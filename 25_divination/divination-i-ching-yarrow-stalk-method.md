# I Ching — Yarrow-Stalk Method (Da Yan Shu)

The **yarrow-stalk method** is the ritually-elaborate consultation procedure for the *Yijing* (Book of Changes / I Ching), distinguished from the abbreviated **three-coin method** (San Bian Fa, a Han-dynasty procedural shortcut). The yarrow procedure is described canonically in the *Xici* (Great Treatise, chapter 9) and codified definitively by Zhu Xi's *Yi Xue Qi Meng* (1186).

## The procedure

The diviner begins with **50 yarrow stalks** (*Achillea millefolium* — the sacred plant of Chinese antiquity, attested in the *Shiji* as growing on Confucius's grave). The procedure for each of the six lines (read from bottom to top) is:

1. Set aside **one stalk** — the "non-use" stalk, symbolizing the unmoved Taiji that originates the Two Modes (yin and yang).
2. Divide the remaining **49 stalks** at random into two heaps (Heaven and Earth).
3. Take one stalk from the right heap and place between fourth and fifth fingers of left hand (Humanity, completing the three-realm cosmology).
4. Count the left heap by fours; the remainder (1, 2, 3, or 4) goes between the third and fourth fingers.
5. Count the right heap by fours; the remainder goes between the second and third fingers.
6. Combine the three remainders — necessarily either 5 or 9.

This is the **first change**. Repeat the procedure twice more using the stalks not held in the hand, producing a **second change** (necessarily 4 or 8) and a **third change** (necessarily 4 or 8). The three results sum to one of four totals:

- **25** = old yang (9) — solid line that will transform
- **21** = young yin (8) — broken line that will remain
- **17** = young yang (7) — solid line that will remain
- **13** = old yin (6) — broken line that will transform

Repeat for six lines to build the **primary hexagram** from bottom to top. Old lines yield a **transformed (resultant) hexagram**. The reading consults the primary hexagram's judgment + image + any changing-line texts + the resultant hexagram.

## Why yarrow specifically

The yarrow (*Achillea millefolium* / *shi cao* 蓍草) is botanically peculiar: it grows in dense colonies, the stems mature woody and straight, and ancient Chinese tradition held it lived for centuries on sacred sites. The *Shiji* (Records of the Grand Historian, ~-90) records that yarrow grew on the grave of Confucius and on the tomb of King Wen. The plant's botanical longevity + sacred-site association made it the canonical material; tortoiseshell (used for the older Shang-dynasty pyromantic divination, see [[scapulimancy-oracle-bones]]) was the alternative.

## Coin method as procedural shortcut

The **three-coin method** (san bian fa) — toss three coins six times, counting heads + tails — produces the same four-line-type distribution as yarrow but with a different probability distribution: the coin method gives equal 1/8 probabilities to the four line types, whereas the yarrow method weights young lines more heavily (3/8 each for young yang/yin, 1/16 for old yang, 5/16 for old yin per Wilhelm's calculation). The yarrow's biased probabilities — fewer changing lines — are widely considered the original Zhou-dynasty distribution. The coin method's procedural ease made it dominant from the Han onward; the yarrow method survived in scholarly + Neo-Confucian circles as the "high" form.

## Cross-tradition position

**Within the Chinese divination spectrum**: the yarrow method is one node on a procedural-elaboration spectrum that runs scapulimancy (Shang) → milfoil/yarrow (Zhou) → coin-toss (Han onward) → spirit-writing (Ming-Qing) → modern coin/app-based methods. Each represents the same underlying divinatory ontology — random mechanism mapped to fixed textual content — at different levels of ritual elaboration.

**Cross-tradition finding — the universal "elaborate vs. abbreviated divination" pattern**: nearly every documented divination system shows the same yarrow/coin polarity:
- **Yoruba [[ifa-odu-divination]]**: the ikin (16 palm-nuts manipulated through 8 passes) vs. the opele (chain with 8 half-shells, single throw) — same odu produced, different procedural complexity
- **Tarot**: full Celtic Cross spread vs. single-card draw
- **Tibetan [[mo-divination-tibetan]]**: full butter-lamp + dice ritual vs. simple dice-toss

The pattern repeats: a "high" elaborate-ritual form preserves the system's full theological framing; an abbreviated everyday form makes it accessible. The high form survives in lineage transmission; the abbreviated form dominates lay practice. The Yijing yarrow/coin polarity is the canonical East Asian instance.

**Confucian-Daoist coexistence**: the Yijing sits uniquely at the Confucian-Daoist boundary. The Ten Wings are Confucian — they read the hexagrams as a moral-cosmological text about the cultivated person. Daoist commentaries (Wang Bi ~+240, the Tao-school Yijing tradition) read the hexagrams as cosmological process. The yarrow-stalk procedure is shared; the interpretive framework branches. This makes the Yijing the principal Chinese instance of a text that scaffolds multiple competing theological-philosophical schools without any single school's exclusive claim.

**Reception in the modern West**: Richard Wilhelm's 1924 German translation (English 1950, foreword by Carl Jung) introduced the I Ching to a 20th-century Western audience that read it through Jungian "synchronicity" — meaningful coincidence as a non-causal organizing principle of psyche-world. The Jung framing reads the divinatory mechanism as a projection-elicitation device rather than a metaphysical claim about cosmic correspondence; this de-metaphysicalized reading underwrites the modern Western I Ching as a psychological self-consultation tool. The Wilhelm-Jung framing is one of the 20th century's most influential cross-cultural transpositions of an Asian divinatory system.
