# Jyotiṣa Praśna (Indian Horary Astrology)

**Praśna** (Sanskrit, "question") is the Indian horary astrological method: a horoscope is cast not for a person's birth but for the **moment a question is asked** of the astrologer, and the chart's structure encodes the answer. The praśna methodology is one of the oldest continuously-practiced horary astrological systems in the world, and represents the principal South Asian instance of the cross-tradition **horary** divinatory genre.

## The praśna procedure

When a consultant approaches a jyotiṣa with a question, the astrologer:
1. Notes the precise time + place of the question
2. Casts the **praśna-kuṇḍalī** — a complete Vedic-astrology chart for that moment, with all 9 grahas (Sun, Moon, Mars, Mercury, Jupiter, Venus, Saturn, Rāhu, Ketu) positioned by sign + nakṣatra + house
3. Analyzes the chart per praśna-specific rules:
   - The **ārūḍha-lagna** (a derived ascendant based on the rising sign's lord's position) signifies the question's manifest face
   - The **bhāva** (house) governing the question's subject area (7th for marriage, 10th for career, etc.) is examined for its lord + occupants + aspects
   - The **chandra-lagna** (Moon-ascendant) gives a parallel reading frame
   - **Yogas** (specific planetary configurations) are checked for activation

The reading produces a directional answer (yes/no, success/failure, timing window) supplemented by qualitative detail. A skilled praśna-vid can answer questions about lost objects' location, traveler's safety, lawsuit outcome, marriage suitability, business venture timing.

## Kerala as the praśna heartland

Within India, **Kerala** is the historical heartland of praśna specialization. The 17th-century *Praśna Mārga* — the canonical Kerala praśna handbook — is one of the most-systematic horary astrological treatises in any tradition, with 32 chapters covering question-types from marriage (chapter 7) through lost objects (chapter 11) to predicting rain (chapter 20). The Kerala tradition produces specialized praśna-practitioners (often hereditary Bhṛgu-lineage diviners) who consult exclusively in praśna mode without doing natal astrology.

## Hellenistic transmission and Tājika overlay

Indian jyotiṣa absorbed Hellenistic Greek astrology in the early centuries CE — the *Yavanajātaka* (~+150) is the principal documented transmission text, with David Pingree's 1978 Harvard critical edition establishing the chain. The 12-house framework, the zodiac-sign divisions, and the planetary aspects all enter Indian astrology from the Hellenistic substrate. Praśna's house-system framework is inherited from this earlier Greek transmission.

A second transmission wave came in the **14th-15th centuries** via Perso-Arabic horary astrology. The *Tājika* tradition (Sanskrit *tājika* = "Tajik/Persian") translated Abū Maʿshar (787-886), al-Kindi (~801-873), and later horary authors into Sanskrit, adapting their doctrines to Indian framework. **Nilakaṇṭha Daivajña**'s *Tājika Nilakaṇṭhī* (~1586) is the principal synthesis, importing the Western horary doctrines of perfection, prohibition, translation of light, and collection of light into Indian praśna practice. This makes praśna unusually **transmission-stratified**: a Vedic-Indian base + Hellenistic-Greek overlay + Perso-Arabic horary import.

## Cross-tradition position

**Cross-tradition finding — horary astrology as a shared genre**: praśna is one node in the cross-tradition **horary astrology** family — the genre of astrological consultation that casts a chart not for a birth but for the moment of inquiry. The family includes:

- **Hellenistic katarchic astrology** (Dorotheus of Sidon ~+75, Hephaistio of Thebes ~+415) — the foundational Greek horary tradition; cast a chart for the moment a question is asked or an action initiated
- **Perso-Arabic horary** (Sahl ibn Bishr ~+820, Abū Maʿshar ~870, al-Kindi ~870) — systematic medieval Arabic-Persian elaboration; the transmission corridor between Hellenistic and Indian + Hellenistic and Latin-medieval traditions
- **Latin-medieval horary** (Guido Bonatti ~+1280, William Lilly *Christian Astrology* 1647) — the Western Renaissance + early-modern horary tradition; William Lilly's 1647 work is the principal English-language horary handbook
- **Modern Western horary revival** (Olivia Barclay 1989, Lee Lehman, John Frawley) — post-1980s recovery from neglect

The cross-tradition horary genre shares: question-moment chart-casting + 12-house frame + planetary lordship-of-houses + specific yes/no-answering rules. The Indian praśna + Western horary lineages share a common Hellenistic ancestor + diverged via Persian intermediation; their contemporary forms (Praśna Mārga's Kerala tradition + Lilly's 17th-c. system) show remarkable structural parallels (same question-typology, similar planet-house significations) that reflect this shared inheritance.

**Cross-tradition finding — praśna vs. natal as a divinatory-ontology divide**: praśna and natal astrology embody two different divinatory ontologies:
- **Natal**: the moment of birth is a cosmic stamp on a person; reading the chart reads the person
- **Praśna / horary**: the moment of asking is a cosmic register of the question's state-of-the-world; reading the chart reads the question's answer

The natal ontology is biographical-determinist; the horary ontology is divinatory-momentary. The same astrological framework (12 houses, 9 planets in jyotiṣa or 7 in Western, zodiac signs, aspects) is repurposed across both ontologies. Praśna's persistence across 1500 years of Indian astrological practice + horary's parallel persistence across the same period in the West testifies to the genre's enduring utility despite repeated dismissal by natal-astrology purists.

**Living practice**: praśna is currently practiced by an estimated 1-2 million jyotiṣa consultants across India, the Indian diaspora, and Indian-influenced Southeast Asia. The Kerala praśna tradition specifically has been recognized as **intangible cultural heritage** by Indian regional bodies. Modern computerized chart-casting (Jagannātha Hora, Parasharas Light, etc.) has democratized praśna access while preserving the interpretive frameworks.
