# Cartomancy — Divination with Playing Cards

Reading fortunes from a pack of cards. The practice is **French, urban, commercial and eighteenth-century**; it is the substrate from which divinatory tarot was grafted, and its documented chronology is the single most economical refutation of the tarot's Egyptian origin-myth.

## The two histories that meet in the pack

**The object is medieval and Islamic.** Playing cards reach Europe in the **1370s** from the Mamluk sultanate of Egypt and Syria; prohibitions and notices cluster in 1371–1379 across Catalonia, Florence, Basel, Paris and Siena, and the Dominican **Johannes von Rheinfelden** wrote a long moralising treatise on card play at Freiburg im Breisgau in **1377**. A near-complete Mamluk pack survives in the Topkapı Sarayı in Istanbul. Its four suits — cups, coins, swords and polo-sticks — become the Italian *coppe, denari, spade* and *bastoni*; the polo-stick turns into a plain baton because Europeans did not play polo and could not read the image (Dummett 1980).

Further back the trail thins. Paper card games are attested in Tang and Song China, and a transmission China → Islamic world → Europe is generally thought probable in outline, but the intermediate links are not documented and Dummett is deliberately cautious about them. **T1 for Mamluk → Europe; the earlier leg is a reasonable inference, not a documented chain.**

**The practice is early modern and French.** Nothing in the medieval or Renaissance record shows anyone telling fortunes with cards. Cards appear in the sources as gambling, as a tax problem, as a moral hazard, and as a game — for four hundred years. The first documented card divination is **[[etteilla-jean-baptiste-alliette]]'s 1770 manual**, teaching a method for the **32-card piquet pack**: the cheapest deck in France.

## The chronology that settles the tarot question

- **1370s** — cards arrive from Mamluk Egypt. A game.
- **1420s–1442** — a fifth suit of allegorical trumps is added in northern Italy; earliest secure record, Ferrara 1442. Still a game. See [[visconti-sforza-tarocchi-deck]].
- **1770** — **Etteilla publishes card divination using an ordinary piquet pack.** No tarot, no Egypt, no Hebrew.
- **1781** — [[antoine-court-de-gebelin]] declares the tarot an Egyptian *Book of Thoth*; [[comte-de-mellet]], in the same volume, maps the trumps to the Hebrew alphabet and prints the first tarot reading-method. See [[event-court-de-gebelin-egyptian-tarot-claim-1781]].
- **1783–1785** — Etteilla extends his existing method to the tarot pack and adopts the Egyptian frame.
- **c. 1788–1789** — Etteilla's *Livre de Thot*: the first deck designed for divination.

**Practice precedes myth precedes tarot divination.** The Egyptian story did not produce the practice; it was applied to a practice that was already thirteen years old and making its inventor a living. This is what the vault means when it says the transmission runs the opposite way to the popular account.

## The Lenormand case — a second invented lineage

The **Petit Lenormand**, a 36-card deck still widely read in Germany, Austria, Brazil and Russia, is named for **Marie Anne Adelaide Lenormand** (1772–1843), the most famous cartomancer of Napoleonic Paris. She never used it. The cards descend from ***Das Spiel der Hoffnung*** ("The Game of Hope"), a **parlour race-game** published by Johann Kaspar Hechtel in Nuremberg around 1799, in which players moved counters along a track of 36 numbered picture cards. After Lenormand's death in 1843 publishers attached her name to the pack and issued it as a fortune-telling deck.

The structure is identical to the tarot case, one rung down and fifty years later: **a real game object + a posthumous prestige attribution + a living practice that loses sight of the join.** Two independent instances of the same pattern, in the same trade, within seventy years, is evidence that the pattern is a feature of how divinatory systems get built — not an accident peculiar to tarot.

## Formal position in the lens

In the typology this lens uses (see [[divination-typology-comparative]]), cartomancy is a **randomiser indexed into a fixed answer-set with interpretive mediation** — the same architecture as the medieval Latin lot-book ([[bibliomancy-sortes]]), the East Asian temple lot-oracle ([[divination-omikuji-kau-cim-lottery-oracle]]), and simple lot-casting ([[cleromancy-lot-casting]]). Where it differs from the sortilege family is that the answer-set is **portable, private, secular in ownership and reusable in combination**: 32 cards yield far more distinguishable outcomes than 36 fixed temple verses, and adjacency between drawn cards generates meaning that no single-lot system can.

That combinatorial property is why cartomancy escaped the church door and the temple courtyard and became a domestic, commercial, and eventually mass-market practice — and why, when [[pamela-colman-smith]] put a readable picture on all 78 tarot cards in 1909, the system became legible without any of the esoteric apparatus that had been built to justify it.
