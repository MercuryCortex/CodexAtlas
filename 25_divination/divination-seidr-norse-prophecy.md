# Seiðr — Old Norse Trance-Prophecy / Sorcery

**Seiðr** (Old Norse *seiðr*; anglicised *seidr*, *seid*) is the trance-based prophecy and sorcery of pre-Christian Scandinavia — the practice of the **völva** (the "staff-bearer" or seeress) who, seated on a raised platform and surrounded by a sung incantation, entered an altered state to contact spirits and the dead, foretell the future, and work the community's fate. It sits on the **natural / inspired** pole of divination: the diviner is not a trained reader of external signs but the living conduit through whom hidden knowledge speaks. In the Atlas it is placed among the divination systems beside its Norse sibling [[runes-divination-norse|rune-divination]] — from which it is deliberately kept distinct — and wired to the cross-tradition [[shamanism]] complex and to the [[greek-oracular-divination|Greek oracular]] practice it structurally mirrors.

## Identity — what seiðr was

Old Norse sources distinguish two strands that modern scholarship groups under seiðr. The first is **oracular** (often called *spá*, "prophecy"): a seeress mounts the *seiðhjallr* (a "seiðr-scaffold" or high seat), assistants sing the *varðlokkur* ("ward-songs" or spirit-songs) to summon the spirits, and in trance she answers the community's questions — about the coming season, the end of a famine, marriages, individual fates. The second is **operative / aggressive** *seiðr*: sending out a spirit-agent (*gandr*) or the practitioner's own mind to gather hidden knowledge, raise storms, blunt weapons, or inflict sickness, madness, and death. The practitioner is titled **völva** (from *vǫlr*, "staff/wand" — hence "wand-carrier"), *seiðkona*, or *spákona* ("prophecy-woman"); the defining implement is the staff, and the practice is overwhelmingly female in the sources. Its etymology is unsettled: Eldar Heide (2006) argues the root image is **spinning** — a mind or spirit spun into a thread and sent out like a snare — connecting *seiðr* to a vocabulary of cord and binding and to the distaff-like form of the völva's staff.

## In the Old Norse sources

The single richest description is **Eiríks saga rauða, chapter 4** — the scene of **Þorbjǫrg lítilvǫlva** ("Þorbjǫrg the Little Seeress") in famine-struck Norse Greenland, c. 1000 CE. The saga lingers on her costume (a blue or black mantle set with stones, catskin gloves, a lambskin hood, a brass-knobbed staff), seats her on a high seat cushioned with hen-feathers, and has a woman named **Guðríðr** — pointedly, a Christian — persuaded to sing the *varðlokkur* so beautifully that the spirits are drawn near; the völva then foretells the lifting of the famine and Guðríðr's own destiny. This is the locus classicus for every reconstruction of the seiðr ritual.

The mythological charter comes from **Snorri Sturluson's *Ynglinga saga*** (c. 1225): **Freyja**, of the Vanir, "first taught seiðr to the Æsir" (ch. 4), making the love-and-fertility goddess also the goddess of trance-sorcery; and **Óðinn** himself is a master of seiðr (ch. 7), able to shift shape, send his spirit abroad, and know men's fates — but Snorri adds that the practice was attended by such *ergi* that it was thought shameful for men, and so was the province of priestesses. The *Poetic Edda* corroborates the divine associations: **Lokasenna** st. 24 has Loki jeer that Óðinn once "beat on the drum as völvas do" and worked seiðr on the isle of **Samsey** — an *ergi* slur — and **Völuspá**, the great cosmological poem, is itself framed as a völva's prophecy recited to Óðinn. A further comparandum outside the saga tradition is the **Historia Norwegiae** (c. 1200), whose Latin account of a Sámi spirit-séance is one of the anchors for reading Norse seiðr against Arctic shamanism.

## The ergi problem — gender and transgression

Seiðr carries an insistent **gender charge**. It is a woman's art; when men practise it — even Óðinn — it attracts the accusation of *ergi*, a loaded term of unmanliness, effeminacy, and sexual passivity that was among the gravest insults in Norse society (an *ergi* / *níð* accusation was actionable under early Icelandic law). That the chief of the gods is both the supreme practitioner of seiðr and the target of Loki's *ergi* taunt is one of the sharpest tensions in Norse religion: the sovereign god of wisdom attains his deepest knowledge by a route his own culture coded as shameful. Modern scholarship (Price 2002/2019) reads this as evidence that ecstatic sorcery occupied a genuinely liminal, boundary-crossing social role; the contemporary Heathen revival (Blain 2002) has in turn reclaimed the *ergi* dimension as a queer and gender-transgressive inheritance.

## Cross-tradition significance (transmission vs convergence)

Seiðr matters comparatively as the **Norse anchor of two distinct debates**, and the two must be kept apart.

**(1) The shamanism question.** Since Dag Strömbäck's *Sejd* (1935), seiðr has been the central European exhibit in the argument that Old Norse religion contained a **shamanic** stratum. The case, developed archaeologically by Neil Price's *The Viking Way* (2002; 2nd ed. 2019), rests on a cluster of shamanic diagnostics: an altered-state specialist, summoned spirit-helpers, the raised platform as an axis between worlds, and a set of Viking-Age female graves furnished with iron **staffs** that Price reads as völva-staves (Birka, Fyrkat grave 4, Klinta). The relationship to the neighbouring **Sámi** (*noaidevuohta*) and wider circumpolar shamanism is the live sub-question: Strömbäck leaned toward Sámi **influence** on Norse practice, while others read the resemblance as **regional convergence** among adjacent northern peoples with no need for a single source. This is genuinely undecided — the cultural contact between Norse and Sámi is real, but whether seiðr is borrowed, ancestral, or parallel cannot be shown from the sources.

**(2) Structural parallel to inspired oracles elsewhere.** Independently of any shamanism claim, the völva on her high seat is a clean **typological twin** of the [[greek-oracular-divination|Delphic Pythia]]: a (usually female) specialist who, in an induced altered state, becomes the mouthpiece of unseen powers — Cicero's *genus naturale*, "natural/inspired" divination, as opposed to the *artificial/inductive* sign-reading of lots and entrails. Here the parallel is **convergent and structural**, not historical: there is no plausible line of transmission between Iron-Age Scandinavia and Archaic Greece, and the resemblance reflects a recurrent human solution — the entranced human conduit — rather than a shared inheritance. This is precisely why seiðr belongs on the [[divination-typology-comparative|comparative-typology]] spine: it fills the Norse slot on the inspired pole, opposite its own sibling [[runes-divination-norse|rune-lots]] on the inductive pole.

**Distinct from rune-divination.** The Atlas keeps seiðr and [[runes-divination-norse|runic divination]] as **separate sibling nodes** rather than merging them. They share a tradition and sometimes a practitioner, but they are different kinds of divination: rune-casting (as in Tacitus, *Germania* 10) is *inductive* — marked lots thrown and read by rule — while seiðr is *inspired* — knowledge delivered through a body in trance. Conflating them would erase exactly the natural/artificial distinction the divination lens exists to expose.

## Disputes

1. **The sources are late and Christian.** Almost everything known about seiðr comes from **13th-century Icelandic prose written by Christians** about a pagan past two-to-four centuries gone — Snorri's *Heimskringla*, the sagas of Icelanders, the compiled *Eddas*. The vivid ritual detail of Eiríks saga is literature, shaped by its authors' distance, moralising, and narrative needs (note that its most sympathetic figure, Guðríðr, is a Christian). **François-Xavier Dillmann** (*Les magiciens dans l'Islande ancienne*, 2006) presses this hard: the reconstruction may tell us more about how medieval Christian Icelanders imagined heathen sorcery than about what Viking-Age people actually did. Any confident account of "how seiðr worked" is a reconstruction, not a transcript.

2. **Is "shamanism" the right word?** Even granting the practice, the **shamanism** label is contested. **Clive Tolley** (*Shamanism in Norse Myth and Magic*, 2009), the most detailed philological study, concludes that the shamanic elements are real but partial and uneven — the fit with classic Siberian shamanism is loose, and the term risks importing a foreign template. This mirrors the broader critique of "shamanism" as a scholarly category (see [[shamanism]], [[shamanism-hypothesis]]): a useful family-resemblance term that can flatten the specifics it is applied to.

3. **The Sámi direction of borrowing.** Whether seiðr derives from, feeds into, or merely resembles Sámi *noaidevuohta* is unresolved and, on current evidence, probably unresolvable; claims of a clean one-way influence in either direction outrun what the sources can support.

The honest position the Atlas takes: seiðr is a **securely attested Old Norse category of trance-prophecy and sorcery**, centred on the völva and mythologically owned by Freyja and Óðinn; its **ritual mechanics are a reasoned reconstruction from Christian-era literature**; its identification as "shamanism" is influential but explicitly debated; and its resemblance to the Greek oracle is convergence, not contact.
