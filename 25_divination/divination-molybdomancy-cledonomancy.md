# Molybdomancy & Cledonomancy — Reading Poured-Metal Shapes and Chance-Heard Words

## Identity

This node holds two minor but tenacious Greco-Roman and European omen-methods that share a single logic: reading a **spontaneous sign** — one plastic, one aural — as a coded message from the powers.

- **Cledonomancy** (from Greek **κληδών**, *klēdōn*, "an ominous utterance"; cf. **φήμη**, *phēmē*, "significant voice/rumour", and Latin ***omen*** in the narrow sense of a portentous word) is divination by a **chance-heard word or sound**. An overheard phrase, a name, a stranger's cry, an off-hand remark is taken — either purely by accident or solicited at a fixed shrine — as the answer to a question or a portent of what is to come.
- **Molybdomancy** (from Greek **μόλυβδος**, *molybdos*, "lead"; German **Bleigießen**, "lead-pouring"; Finnish **uudenvuodentina**, "New Year's tin") is divination by **pouring molten lead or tin into cold water** and reading the shapes into which it instantly solidifies — a ship for a journey, a heart for love, a coffin for death — as omens of the coming year, or, poured over an afflicted person, as a diagnosis of the evil eye.

They are grouped because both read **the sign that arrives unbidden**, and both sit in what Cicero called the *genus naturale* of divination (see [[divination-typology-comparative]]). Two boundaries fix their place in the lens. **Against cleromancy** ([[cleromancy-lot-casting]]): no lot is deliberately cast — the diviner does not control the mechanism but receives what chance (or the god) supplies. **Against scrying** ([[scrying-mirror-water-crystal]]): there is no reflective surface gazed into for a vision — molybdomancy reads a *solidified physical shape*, not an image seen in a medium, and cledonomancy reads *sound*, not sight. Molybdomancy's nearest ancient cousin is therefore **lecanomancy** (oil dropped on water, Mesopotamian; catalogued under scrying), with which it shares the fluid-medium morphology while differing in substance.

## The methods

### Cledonomancy — the *klēdōn* and the *omen*

The chance-word omen is one of the oldest documented Greek divinatory ideas. Its literary root is **Homer, *Odyssey* 20.98–121**: sleepless before the slaughter of the suitors, Odysseus prays to Zeus for both a *teras* (a sign in the sky) and a *phēmē* (a sign in speech). Zeus thunders from a clear sky, and at once a slave-woman grinding grain — the weakest still at work — curses the suitors and prays it be their last feast. Odysseus takes the paired sign as divine assent. The passage fixes the two channels of the *klēdōn*: the ambiguous overheard human word, ratified here by the unambiguous thunder.

Greek religion built the practice into ritual in two forms. **Chance cledonomancy** underlies the pervasive rule of **auspicious silence** — *euphēmeite!* ("keep holy silence"; Latin *favete linguis*) called before sacrifice precisely so that no ill-omened chance word should fall at the wrong moment. **Solicited cledonomancy** had its own shrine: at **Pharai in Achaea**, **Pausanias (7.22.2–4)** describes a working oracle of Hermes Agoraios where the consultant whispered a question into the god's ear, stopped his own ears, walked out through the marketplace, and unstopped them — the **first words he then overheard** were the god's reply. Sarah Iles Johnston (*Ancient Greek Divination*, 2008) treats this as the clearest institutionalisation of an otherwise informal folk method.

Rome carried the same idea under its own ritual law as the ***omen***. **Cicero, *De Divinatione* 1.102–104**, gives the canonical catalogue: Lucius Aemilius Paulus, learning that his little daughter's dog "Persa" had died, read the name as a portent of victory over Perseus of Macedon; the consul whose colleague was named after the auspicious *Caecilia*; the general who would not march until an auspicious word was spoken. A word once "accepted" (*accipio omen*) became binding. In the sceptical second book (2.84) Cicero mocks the whole business with the story of the Syrian fig-vendor whose street-cry **"Cauneas!"** ("Caunian figs!") sounded like *cave ne eas* ("beware, do not go") — a warning Crassus ignored on his way to disaster at Carrhae.

### Molybdomancy — Bleigießen and the evil-eye pour

Molybdomancy's living form is the **New-Year lead-pour**. On New Year's Eve (*Silvester*) across the German-speaking lands, the Nordic countries — Finland especially, where it is *uudenvuodentina*, "New Year's tin" — and historically Greece, a lump of lead or tin is melted in a spoon over a candle and tipped into a bowl of cold water. It freezes instantly into a fantastic solid shape, which is then read (often by its shadow, turned against a wall) for the year ahead. Since EU restrictions on retail lead, the sold kits now use **tin or wax**, but the practice is unbroken; the standard German folklore encyclopedia, the *Handwörterbuch des deutschen Aberglaubens* (s.v. "Bleigießen"), records it as a fixture of the *Silvester* calendar.

A second, apotropaic branch survives around the eastern Mediterranean. In Greece (**μολυβδοσκόπηση**, *molyvdoskopēsē*), Turkey (**kurşun dökme**, "lead-pouring"), and the South Slavic Balkans, molten lead is poured into water held **over the head of a person believed to be under the evil eye** ([[evil-eye]]; Greek *baskania*). The hiss, the bubbles, and the resulting shapes are read both to **diagnose** that the eye has struck and to **break** its power — a divinatory and a healing act at once (see Dundes, ed., *The Evil Eye: A Casebook*, 1981).

## Cross-tradition significance (transmission vs convergence)

The two methods illustrate the Atlas's central distinction — documented transmission versus independent convergence — in opposite ways.

**Cledonomancy: a secure Mediterranean continuity inside a convergent human pattern.** The line from the Greek *klēdōn*/*phēmē* to the Roman *omen* is a **genuine cultural continuity**: Rome inherited and formalised, under its own augural law, an idea it received from the Greek world and shared Italic religion — the same practice, differently codified. Beyond that Greco-Roman corridor, however, the chance-word oracle is best read as **convergence**. Any ambiguous spontaneous utterance can be treated as a message, so the pattern recurs without borrowing: the Hebrew **bat qol** (בַּת קוֹל, "daughter of the voice"), a heavenly or echoing voice used oracularly in rabbinic literature as the residual form of revelation after prophecy ceased, is a **structural parallel**, not a derivation; so too the widespread European folk custom of **eavesdropping for omens** at the crossroads or the church door on charged nights (New Year, St Andrew's Eve). These share the *form* of cledonomancy — the overheard word as sign — without any traceable link to the Greek practice.

**Molybdomancy: a convergent morphomancy, not a documented descent.** Reading chance shapes in a poured or solidified medium — molten lead, molten wax (**ceromancy**), oil on water (**lecanomancy**), egg-white in water (**oomancy**) — is a small, recurrent family of "morphomancy" that appears wherever a substance can be made to freeze or spread into readable forms. The one *documented ancient* member of the family is Mesopotamian **lecanomancy** (the Old Babylonian *Šumma šamnu* oil-omens; see [[scrying-mirror-water-crystal]]). Molybdomancy's own claimed classical pedigree is thin (see Disputes): the securely attested lead-pour is the **early-modern-to-present European folk custom**, most plausibly an independent popular development of the same shape-reading impulse rather than a survival descending from any specific ancient rite. Its cross-tradition interest is therefore as a **convergence case**, and as a still-living practice — one of the very few in the divination lens whose *date-attested-latest* is genuinely the present day.

## Disputes

1. **Is "molybdomancy" an ancient method or a modern label?** The Greek-looking term *μολυβδομαντεία* reads like a **neo-classical coinage** (on the pattern of the many "-mancy" words assembled from Greek roots in the early-modern and modern periods) rather than a securely attested classical practice. Lead was intensely used in Greco-Roman magic — above all for inscribed curse-tablets (*defixiones*) — but the specific rite of **pouring molten lead to read shapes** is documented for certain only in the post-medieval European and Anatolian material. This node treats molybdomancy honestly as a **living folk practice of uncertain antiquity**, whose ancient cognate is lecanomancy, not as a demonstrably classical technique.
2. **Do the two belong in one node?** They are joined here on structural grounds (both read the unbidden sign; both are minor methods without a lens home) and on a shared Greek naming-tradition, not because antiquity linked them. A maximal ontology might split them; the pairing is an editorial convenience, flagged as such.
3. **Home tradition.** Cledonomancy is securely **Greco-Roman**; molybdomancy's living form is **Germanic, Nordic, and Balkan folk**. Per the Atlas's membership-vs-wire rule the node takes a single home tradition — the Greek naming-tradition (*klēdōn*, *molybdos*) — and expresses the Roman and later European-folk reach as **wires and notes**, not as additional home traditions.
