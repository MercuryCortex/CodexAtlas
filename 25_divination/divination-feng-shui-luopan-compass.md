# Feng Shui Luopan Compass Divination

**Feng shui** (風水, "wind-water") is the Chinese tradition of siting human structures — graves, dwellings, temples, cities — in correspondence with the flow of *qi* (vital energy) through the landscape. The **luopan** (羅盤, "Luo-plate") is its precision-instrument: a magnetic compass with concentric rings encoding cosmological correspondences, read at the proposed site to determine optimal orientation + identify auspicious vs. inauspicious topographic features.

## The luopan structure

A full luopan has between 17 and 38 concentric rings around a central magnetic needle. The principal rings encode:

1. **Center**: magnetic needle (the Heaven Pool / Tianchi)
2. **8 Trigrams (bagua)** — the eight Yijing trigrams oriented by direction
3. **24 Mountains (24 shan)** — 24 directional points at 15° each, named via combinations of trigrams + 10 Heavenly Stems + 12 Earthly Branches
4. **64 Hexagrams** — full Yijing hexagram-row mapped to the 24 mountains
5. **28 Lunar Mansions (xiu)** — Chinese astronomical division of the ecliptic for astrological-calendrical correlation
6. **5 Elements (wuxing)** — wood / fire / earth / metal / water assigned to directions + features
7. **Flying Star** numbers (1-9) for temporal-cycle calculation

The luopan is set on a level surface at the proposed site, oriented by magnetic north, then rotated to align with the building's intended facing-direction. The geomancer reads the rings against the site's landscape features (mountains behind, water in front, embracing arms left and right per the canonical Black-Tortoise / Vermilion-Bird / Azure-Dragon / White-Tiger configuration).

## Form School vs. Compass School

**Form School (Xingfa / 形法)** — the Tang-dynasty Jiangxi tradition (Yang Yunsong ~880) — privileges direct visual reading of landscape topography. Auspicious sites have:
- A **protective mountain** behind (the Black Tortoise)
- An **open prospect** in front (the Vermilion Bird) with **flowing or pooling water** that holds qi
- **Embracing arms** to left (Azure Dragon) and right (White Tiger) — flanking hills neither too tall nor too short
- Dragon-vein (long mai) mountain ranges that bring qi from distant sources to the site

The Form School aesthetic is the famous Chinese landscape-painting compositional grammar — every classical Chinese landscape painting (山水 / shanshui) implicitly diagrams feng shui Form School principles.

**Compass School (Liqi / 理氣)** — the Song-dynasty Fujian tradition (Wang Ji ~1078) — privileges the luopan readings + calendrical calculation. The Compass School is more analytical, more textually-codified, and historically more prestigious in the literati-scholar tradition. The two schools are not strictly opposed; most professional geomancers since the Ming dynasty have integrated both.

## Burial siting and the dynasty's mandate

The original and most-historically-consequential application of feng shui is **grave-siting** (yin-zhai feng shui, "yin-dwelling" feng shui). The Burial Book (Zangjing) opens with the canonical principle:

> *"Qi rides the wind and disperses, but is retained when encountering water. The ancients collected it to prevent its dissipation, and guided it to assure its retention. Thus it was called feng shui (wind water)."*

The conviction was that an ancestor properly buried in a qi-accumulating site would transmit good fortune to descendants for generations — and that a dynasty's longevity depended on the founder's tomb being correctly sited. The Ming and Qing emperors maintained court geomancers; the Ming Tombs north of Beijing (built 1409+) and the Qing Eastern + Western Tombs (built 1661+) were all sited by elite feng-shui consultation.

## Reception in the modern West

Pre-1970s Western mention of feng shui was largely dismissive (missionary or colonial sources framing it as superstition). The 1970s-1980s saw two convergent revival vectors:
1. **Lillian Too** (Malaysian-Chinese businesswoman, b. 1946) popularized a simplified Compass-School-derived "Eight Mansions" personal-bazhai system through dozens of books from the late 1980s
2. **Hong Kong real-estate culture** (HSBC building 1985 feng-shui consultation famously, with the cannon-pointed-at-Bank-of-China rivalry) made feng-shui consultation a normal feature of high-end Asian-Pacific commercial architecture

By the late 1990s, "feng shui" had entered Western interior-design vocabulary, often stripped of the luopan + landscape framework into a generic "harmonious arrangement" framing. The simplified Western feng shui is to traditional feng shui what New Age tarot is to occult Lévi-tradition tarot — a popularized derivative recognizable to traditionalists but stripped of much of the technical apparatus.

## Cross-tradition position

**MASSIVE-WIN — sacred siting as cross-tradition pattern**: feng shui is the principal East Asian instance of the **religious-cosmological siting** pattern that recurs across traditions:
- **Vāstu Śāstra** (Indian sacred architecture; see Sanskrit *Mānasāra* + *Mayamatam* texts) — 8×8 or 9×9 vāstu-puruṣa-maṇḍala for site planning + temple orientation
- **Roman augural templum** — the inaugurated rectangular sky-section defining auspicious building space ([[augury-roman-bird-omens]])
- **Etruscan disciplina** — ritual delineation of urban space (sulcus primigenius)
- **Geomancy** (Western, [[geomancy-raml-cross-tradition]]) — random-mark divination but historically applied to siting as well as personal questions
- **Tibetan Sa Che (Earth Examination)** — geomantic siting tradition; Vairocana's lying-supine-demoness diagram is the cosmic prototype for Tibetan temple siting per Buddhist transmission

The cross-tradition commonality: a cosmological grid (compass-rose, mandala, sky-quadrant) projected onto a physical site to determine where to build. The differences are in the cosmological vocabulary (qi vs. vāstu-puruṣa vs. augury vs. mandala) and the precision instrument (luopan vs. gnomon vs. lituus).

**Form School ↔ Chinese landscape aesthetics**: the feng shui Form School is the explicit theoretical basis of classical Chinese landscape painting composition. Every Song-Yuan landscape painting can be read as a diagrammed feng-shui-favorable site. This is one of the most direct cases of a divinatory-cosmological system shaping a fine-art tradition's compositional grammar.
