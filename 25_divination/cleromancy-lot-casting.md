# Cleromancy — Lot-Casting Divination Across Traditions

The cross-tradition divinatory practice of using a randomizing object — drawn lots, cast stones, marked sticks, tokens drawn from a vessel — to resolve a decision-question that has been formally posed to the gods / God / a presiding agency. One of the simplest + oldest + most-broadly-cross-cultural divinatory mechanisms; documented continuously in the Israelite cult from ~-2000 through the modern Moravian Church *Loosung* tradition.

## How it works

The basic structure across traditions:

1. **A question is posed** — typically binary (yes/no) or multi-way (which of N candidates) but formally addressed to the presiding deity/agency
2. **A randomizing mechanism** — the canonical Mediterranean methods include:
   - **Binary stones / lot-stones** drawn from a pouch (the biblical *Urim and Thummim* on the high-priest's breastplate)
   - **Marked sticks or tablets** drawn from a vessel (the Roman *sortes Praenestinae*)
   - **Pebbles** — black + white — drawn from a jar (the Athenian Assembly's voting + cleromantic mechanism)
   - **Marked strips of wood** thrown on a cloth (Tacitus's description of Germanic practice, *Germania* 10)
3. **The result** — read directly as the deity's answer; the cultural-theological framing typically holds that *the random-mechanism is the deity's chosen vehicle for delivering the answer* (Proverbs 16:33 is the locus classicus: "the lot is cast into the lap, but its every decision is from the LORD")
4. **Binding force** — the result is binding on the question-poser; in state contexts (Israelite priesthood, Roman state cult, Greek *kleros*-elections, Moravian Church) the result has institutional force

## Biblical cleromancy — Urim and Thummim and the Matthias election

The Hebrew Bible contains the most-documented ancient cleromantic tradition:

- **The Urim and Thummim** (Exodus 28:30; Leviticus 8:8; Numbers 27:21; Deuteronomy 33:8) — two marked objects in a pouch on the high-priest's breastplate; consulted to determine God's will on yes/no questions. The mechanism's exact nature is unclear (rabbinic + scholarly speculation runs through letter-illumination, stone-drawing, and other reconstructions), but the institutional function as a divinatory binary-lot oracle is clear
- **Achan's identification** (Joshua 7:14-18) — when a sin must be identified, the lot is cast successively (tribe → clan → family → individual) until the guilty party is named
- **Saul's selection** (1 Samuel 10:20-22) — Saul is chosen as Israel's first king by lot
- **Jonah** (Jonah 1:7) — the sailors cast lots to identify who has angered God; Jonah is identified
- **Yom Kippur scapegoat** (Leviticus 16:8) — the lot determines which of two goats is sacrificed and which is sent to Azazel
- **Proverbs 16:33** — the theological formula: "the lot is cast into the lap, but its every decision is from the LORD"

The principal New Testament instance is **Acts 1:26** — after Judas's death, the apostles cast lots to choose his replacement; Matthias is selected. This is the last canonical Christian cleromantic decision; the descent of the Spirit at Pentecost (Acts 2) is widely read as superseding the lot-mechanism in Christian institutional practice (Augustine + later Reformed tradition develop this).

## Greek + Roman cleromancy

The Greek + Roman world institutionalized cleromancy across multiple registers:

- **Athenian Assembly + court** — many offices + jury-selections by lot (*klēros*); the radical-democratic principle was that the lot, being random, treated all citizens equally and prevented oligarchic capture
- **Roman *sortes*** — multiple oracular institutions, most famously the *sortes Praenestinae* (Praeneste / Palestrina; the boy-child drew inscribed tablets from an olive-wood chest). Cicero *De Divinatione* 2.85-87 describes + critiques
- **Astragalus-oracles** — the Pisidian / Pamphylian astragalomancy ([[astragalomancy-knucklebone]]) is structurally cleromantic
- **The Sortes Astrampsychi** — a Greco-Roman + Late-antique question-and-answer lot-book; the consultant chose a numbered question, drew a number-lot, and combined them to find the apodosis in the book; continued in Christian-adapted form into the early Middle Ages

## Christian survival + the Moravian *Loosung*

Despite Augustine's + the Reformed tradition's skepticism, cleromancy survived in Christianity in multiple lineages:

- **Sortes Sanctorum** (Late-antique + early-medieval) — a Christian adaptation of the *Sortes Astrampsychi*-type lot-book; condemned by multiple church councils (Vannes 465; Agde 506) but continued in popular use
- **Bible-and-key + Bible-and-pin** (medieval + early-modern English popular practice) — Thomas 1971 documents
- **Moravian Church *Loosung*** (Count Zinzendorf, 1727 onward) — the most-institutionalized modern Christian cleromancy; lot-drawing for congregational + missionary + marriage decisions; the *Daily Texts* tradition derives from this
- **Mennonite + Hutterite + some Anabaptist communities** — congregational lot-drawing for leadership-selection + disputed decisions continues
- **Catholic papal conclave (variant)** — the conclave's secret-ballot is not strictly cleromantic but the historical *scrutinium per compromissum* + *scrutinium per inspirationem* methods had quasi-cleromantic features

## MASSIVE-WIN — the binary-randomizer convergence

Cleromancy is one of the most-broadly-cross-cultural divinatory practices in world religious history. Every major textual religious tradition + virtually every documented non-textual one practices some form of binary or N-way lot-casting:

- **Mesopotamian** — *pūru* (lot); the term enters Hebrew as *pur* → *Purim*
- **Israelite + Jewish** — Urim and Thummim, Yom Kippur scapegoat, Purim itself (the festival named for the lot Haman cast)
- **Greek** — Assembly + court selection by *klēros*; oracular *sortes*
- **Roman** — multiple *sortes* institutions; Praeneste, Antium
- **Germanic** — Tacitus *Germania* 10 (the Germanic lot-casting precursor of runes-divination)
- **Christian** — Acts 1:26 + medieval Christian *sortes* + modern Moravian/Mennonite
- **Islamic** — *istikhāra* (prayer-and-decision practice; sometimes uses lot-drawing variants) — see [[istikhara-islamic]]
- **Chinese** — Yi Jing yarrow-stalk + coin methods (formally cleromantic) — see [[yi-jing-divination]]
- **African** — multiple traditions (Yoruba 16-cowries, sangoma bones)
- **Mesoamerican** — multiple lot-based divinatory practices alongside the calendrical-divinatory system

The convergence sits on **plausible structural grounds**: cleromancy is the simplest possible divinatory technology (any randomizer + a decision-frame), and it solves a recurrent human problem (binding decision under uncertainty + diffused responsibility) in a structurally minimal way. The cross-tradition convergence is unsurprising on these grounds — but the *theological + institutional embedding* varies dramatically, from full divine-determination ("the lot is the LORD's") to functional-decision-making (Athenian sortition).

## Cross-tradition position

Cleromancy sits at three cross-tradition junctures:

1. **The binary-randomizer divinatory family** — the structural progenitor of [[yi-jing-divination]] (yarrow-stalk + coin), [[ifa-odu-divination]] (opèlè-chain), [[astragalomancy-knucklebone]] (knucklebones), [[runes-divination-norse]] (rune-casting). All these systems are *cleromantic at base* + add a *text-key* layer; raw cleromancy is the simpler progenitor form.

2. **The institutional-decision-making interface** — cleromancy in the Israelite + Greek + Christian-Moravian traditions functions as the *interface between divine will + institutional decision* — the mechanism by which deity-authorized decisions enter the legal-political record. This institutional function distinguishes cleromancy from purely-personal divinatory practices.

3. **The skepticism-survival pattern** — cleromancy has been repeatedly subjected to philosophical + theological + scientific critique (Cicero, Augustine, Reformed Christianity, Enlightenment) + yet has survived continuously, including in modern institutional Christianity. The pattern of *recurring critique + persistent survival* is itself a comparative-religious phenomenon worth noting.
