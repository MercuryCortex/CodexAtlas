# Ogham Divination — Celtic (Irish) Tree-Alphabet Lots

Ogham divination is the practice of drawing or casting the letters of the early-medieval Irish **ogham** script — the *feda* (sing. *fid*, "wood, tree") — as marked lots and reading them against a fixed symbolic key. It must be introduced with its central honesty problem stated first: **the script is genuinely ancient; the oracle is largely modern.** The twenty primary feda are securely attested from c. 350 CE on some four hundred stone monuments, and the medieval Irish learned tradition supplies a real interpretive meaning-layer (the word-oghams). But an actual *drawn-lot fortune-telling deck* of ogham is a twentieth-century reconstruction whose most influential source, Robert Graves's *The White Goddess* (1948), is not a work of Celtic scholarship. This node documents the whole arc — ancient script, medieval learned lore, medieval literary divination-motif, and modern revival oracle — and keeps the tiers apart.

## The ancient script versus the modern oracle

**What is securely attested (T1–T2):**

- The **ogham script** itself: twenty primary letters cut as one-to-five strokes or notches across or along a stemline (*druim*), organised into four *aicmi* (families) of five, later supplemented by five *forfeda* ("over-letters"). Inscriptions run c. 350–700 CE, overwhelmingly in Munster, with clusters in Wales, Scotland, and the Isle of Man; their content is almost entirely memorial ("X son of Y" in the genitive). McManus 1991 is the authoritative treatment.
- Each letter has a **name** (the *fid*), and a rich body of **medieval learned lore** attaches to these names in the Ogham Tract (*In Lebor Ogaim*) and the *Auraicept na n-Éces*, preserved in the Book of Ballymote (c. 1391) and Yellow Book of Lecan.

**What is NOT securely attested:**

- Any **pre-Christian druidic divination** by ogham. No primary source documents it. The inscriptions belong to the already-Christianising period, and their function is memorial and territorial, not oracular (McManus 1991).
- The specific practice of **drawing marked ogham staves from a bag as a fortune-telling method** — the modern "fews" oracle — is a twentieth-century innovation. As with [[runes-divination-norse|rune-divination]], the cleanest tier-disciplined statement is that the ancient culture possessed the script and practised lot-casting, but their combination into the drawn-lot oracle moderns recognise is not documented in antiquity.

## The word-oghams: the genuine medieval meaning-layer

The one authentically medieval interpretive key for the letters is the **Bríatharogam** ("word-ogham"): three sets of two-word kennings, one gloss per letter, preserved in the Ogham Tract and attributed to legendary authorities — the *Bríatharogam Morainn mic Moín*, the *Bríatharogam Maic ind Óc* (of Óengus), and the *Bríatharogam Con Culainn*. These are the closest thing to a "divinatory meaning" the tradition supplies, and every serious modern ogham-oracle draws on them.

Crucially, McManus (1988, 1991) shows that the popular label **"tree alphabet"** (*beith-luis-nin*, after the first letters) overstates the case: only some feda carry genuinely arboreal kennings, and the fully systematic tree-by-tree scheme is a **partly secondary scholastic elaboration**, not the primary meaning of the letters. The word-oghams are learned poetic kennings produced within the *fili* (poet-scholar) curriculum — not a fossilised druidic divinatory code.

## Ogham in the saga literature

Medieval Irish narrative does depict ogham used for divination and for binding, cryptic communication — as a **literary motif**, which is evidence for how learned monks imagined ogham, not for a documented rite:

- **Divination proper** appears in *Tochmarc Étaíne* ("The Wooing of Étaín"), where the druid **Dalán**, unable to find the vanished Étaín, inscribes ogham on wands of **yew** and, using the "keys of divination" (*eochra écsi*) and *imbas* (poetic illumination), determines that she is with Midir in Brí Léith. This is the principal medieval-literary link between ogham and divination.
- **Binding and cryptic use** appears in the *Táin Bó Cúailnge*, where **Cú Chulainn** cuts ogham on a withe-hoop and on branches to impose a *geis* (prohibition) on the invading Connacht army and to send messages only the learned can read.

This saga material sits within the wider **fili / fían** milieu: ogham lore was transmitted in the poets' curriculum (the *Auraicept*), and the fili's own recognised divinatory techniques — *imbas forosnai*, *teinm laída*, *díchetal di chennaib* — belonged to the same learned world. But those techniques are distinct from casting ogham letters, and the sources do not equate them with a letter-lot oracle.

## The modern oracle

The living practice this node documents is essentially a modern one:

- **Robert Graves, *The White Goddess* (1948)** proposed a pre-Christian "Celtic Tree Calendar" of thirteen lunar months mapped onto the ogham letters. It became foundational for Wicca, modern Druidry, and Neopaganism. McManus (1991) and mainstream Celticists document that **the calendar has no attestation in any primary source** and rests on selective misreadings of medieval Irish and Welsh material. It is **T4** — poetic reconstruction, not scholarship.
- On that base, twentieth-century practitioners built the **drawn-lot oracle**: sets of wooden staves or cards (the "**fews**," from *feda*), each bearing one letter, drawn or cast and read via tree-associations and the word-ogham kennings, in spreads modelled on tarot and runes. Liz and Colin Murray's *The Celtic Tree Oracle* (1988) is a representative and influential example; the Order of Bards, Ovates and Druids and other revival bodies teach comparable systems.

The honest statement: modern ogham divination is a **real, meaningful contemporary practice** whose *symbolic content* rests partly on genuine medieval lore (the word-oghams) and partly on a modern invention (Graves's calendar), and whose *method* (drawing letter-lots) is a modern adaptation of a general lot-casting logic rather than a documented ancient rite.

## Cross-tradition significance (transmission vs convergence)

Ogham divination's sharpest comparative value is as the **Celtic twin of [[runes-divination-norse|Norse rune-divination]]**. The two are structurally and historically parallel in a very precise way: each is a **non-Latin regional script of the mid-first-millennium CE** (ogham c. 350 CE; the Elder Futhark c. 150 CE) that carried real learned/mythological lore, and each was **reconstructed in the twentieth century as a drawn-lot oracle** on continuity claims stronger than the evidence supports. The parallel is best read as a shared *modern reception pattern* — the Romantic-to-Neopagan impulse to recover an indigenous European alphabetic divination — rather than as a documented ancient transmission between Celtic and Germanic peoples. Direct Celtic–Norse borrowing of a *divinatory* use is not demonstrable; the two scripts' own relationship (mutual influence versus independent invention) is itself unresolved (McManus 1991).

At the level of **mechanism**, ogham-lots belong to the broadly convergent family of [[cleromancy-lot-casting|lot-casting (cleromancy)]]: a randomiser (the drawn feda) keyed to fixed symbolic content. Marked-wood lot-casting is exactly the mechanism Tacitus ascribes to the Germanic peoples (*Germania* 10) and is one of the simplest and most widely independent of all divinatory technologies. So the underlying *method* is a convergent base-case that needs no borrowing to explain, while the specific *ogham content* is a genuinely Irish inheritance overlaid with modern reconstruction. In the Atlas's comparative typology ([[divination-typology-comparative]]) it therefore sits on the artificial/inductive side, under the cleromancy mechanism, beside runes and astragalomancy.

## Disputes

1. **Was ogham ever a pre-Christian divinatory system?** No primary source attests it. The inscriptions are memorial; the divinatory scenes are saga motifs (Dalán's yew wands) composed in a Christian literary milieu. The mainstream position (McManus 1991) treats a druidic ogham-oracle as undocumented and probably a projection.
2. **Is it a "tree alphabet" at all?** Only in part. McManus (1988) shows the arboreal reading is incomplete and partly a scholastic overlay; the "sacred tree calendar" popularised by Graves compounds this into an outright modern invention.
3. **Graves's status.** *The White Goddess* is enormously influential and enduringly popular, but it is **not evidence for Celtic practice**; it is documented as non-scholarly by McManus and others and is carried here strictly at T4 as reception history.
4. **Authenticity of the living practice.** That the ancient-continuity claims are weak does not make the contemporary practice fraudulent — modern ogham divination is a genuine tradition in its own right. The tier-discipline concerns only the *historical* claims, not the reality or value of current practice (the same distinction the Atlas draws for [[runes-divination-norse|rune-divination]] and tarot).
