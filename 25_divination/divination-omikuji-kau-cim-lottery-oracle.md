# Temple Lottery-Poetry Oracle — qiúqiān / kau cim + o-mikuji

## Identity

The temple lottery-poetry oracle is the everyday, mass-practice divination of East Asian popular religion: a numbered lot is drawn by chance before a temple deity, and its number keys a **fixed, pre-printed corpus of oracle-verses** — one slip per number — which is then read (and often professionally glossed) as the deity's reply. In its Chinese form it is **qiúqiān** (求籤, "seeking the lot"; Cantonese *kau cim / kau chim*): the consultant shakes a cylinder of flat numbered bamboo sticks (*qiān*, 籤) until exactly one works loose and falls out, then exchanges that number for the corresponding **oracle-poem slip** (*qiānshī*, 籤詩; the sets are called *língqiān*, 靈籤, "efficacious lots"). In its Japanese form it is **o-mikuji** (おみくじ), the drawn fortune-slip of Shintō shrines and Buddhist temples. It is one of the most-performed divinatory acts on earth — a daily fixture of temple life from Hangzhou and Taipei to the Sik Sik Yuen (Wong Tai Sin) temple in Hong Kong and the shrines of Kyoto.

Structurally the system is a **cleromancy-plus-text-key**: a randomizer (the falling stick) selects an index; the index maps to a closed, authored verse-corpus. This places it beside — but not inside — the Atlas's other randomizer-plus-text systems, the [[yi-jing-divination|Yi Jing]] and the Mediterranean [[bibliomancy-sortes|Sortes]], and downstream of the raw [[cleromancy-lot-casting|lot-cast]] that has no fixed text at all.

## In the East Asian temple cult

**The Chinese rite (qiúqiān / kau cim).** The consultant kneels before the altar, names the question, and shakes the *qiāntǒng* (籤筒, lot-cylinder) until a single stick falls; if two fall, the throw is void and repeated. The stick bears only a number. Before the number is trusted, most temples require a confirming cast of the **jiǎobēi** (筊杯, "moon-blocks"; Hokkien *bwa-bwei*) — two crescent blocks, each flat on one face and rounded on the other:

- **one up, one down** = *shèngbēi* (聖杯, "sacred") — the deity confirms the number;
- **both flat-side down** = *yīnbēi* (陰杯, "negative") — refused;
- **both rounded-side up** = *xiàobēi* (笑杯, "laughing") — the deity is non-committal.

A refused or laughing throw sends the consultant back to shake again, so the final number is doubly randomized (stick *and* blocks). The confirmed number is then exchanged for the printed **oracle-poem**: a short classical-Chinese quatrain, a graded rating (from *shàng-shàng*, great-luck, down to *xià-xià*, great-misfortune), and topic-by-topic verdicts — marriage, travel, litigation, sickness, the missing person, the year's business. A temple interpreter (*jiěqiān*, 解籤) may unpack the often-cryptic verse. The great corpora are the 100-slip **Guanyin língqiān** (of the bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara) and the **Guandi língqiān** (of the deified general Guan Yu); the **Tiānzhú língqiān**, tied to the Guanyin cult at the Upper Tianzhu monastery near Hangzhou, is a key Song-dynasty witness to the printed genre.

**The Japanese rite (o-mikuji).** At shrine or temple the seeker shakes a numbered stick from a hexagonal box (*mikuji-bako*) — or, today, draws a folded slip or buys one from a dispensing box — and receives a slip graded **daikichi** (大吉, great blessing), *kichi*, *chūkichi*, *shōkichi*, *suekichi*, **kyō** (凶, curse), or **daikyō** (大凶, great curse), with the same life-domain sub-answers. The classical template is the **Ganzan Daishi hyakusen** (元三大師百籤), a set of one hundred Chinese-verse oracles traditionally ascribed to the Tendai patriarch **Ryōgen** (Ganzan Daishi, 912–985) and turned into a mass practice in the Edo period. A distinctive Japanese gesture: an unwelcome slip is knotted (*musubi*) onto a pine bough or a dedicated rack (*musubidokoro*) at the shrine, "tying off" the bad luck so it stays behind.

## Cross-tradition significance (transmission vs convergence)

This node sits at two very different kinds of cross-tradition juncture, and honesty requires keeping them apart.

**A documented transmission: China → Japan.** The Japanese o-mikuji is not an independent Japanese invention. The numbered poem-oracle is a Chinese temple genre that reached Japan through **Buddhist institutional networks**, and Japanese practice preserves its Chinese skin: the classical o-mikuji verses are written in **classical Chinese**, and the flagship corpus is explicitly a "hundred poems" set on the Chinese *língqiān* model. Michel Strickmann's *Chinese Poetry and Prophecy* (2005) — the controlling study — treats the written oracle as a single East-Asian tradition spanning both countries. The one caution is the **Ganzan Daishi attribution**: crediting the corpus to Ryōgen personally is a traditional, largely legendary ascription, even though the underlying China-to-Japan transmission of the genre is secure (see Disputes).

**A striking convergence: the Sortes.** The mechanism — *draw a number, look the number up in a fixed answer-book* — is independently the exact procedure of the late-antique Mediterranean **Sortes Astrampsychi** (see [[bibliomancy-sortes]], [[cleromancy-lot-casting]]): the consultant selected a numbered question, drew a number-lot, and combined them to find a numbered answer in the book. There is **no plausible channel of contact** between a Greco-Roman lot-book and a Song-dynasty temple oracle; the parallel is a convergence on structural grounds — a numbered randomizer married to a closed answer-corpus is a natural, low-cost solution to the same problem (a fixed, deniable, repeatable oracle). This is the same convergence logic the Atlas applies to raw [[cleromancy-lot-casting|lot-casting]] itself.

**A deliberate distinction: not the Yi Jing.** The temple lot-oracle is often lumped with the [[yi-jing-divination|Yi Jing]] because both are Chinese "randomizer-plus-text" systems, but they are structurally different and should not be merged. The Yi Jing *constructs* a hexagram line-by-line (yarrow-stalk or coin) and reads a combinatorial 64-fold symbol-set with layered commentary; the temple oracle *indexes* a flat, closed corpus of authored verses by a single stick-number. The Yi Jing is elite, canonical, and interpretive; qiúqiān / o-mikuji is popular, devotional, and mechanical. They are neighbours in the typology, not the same node.

## Disputes

1. **The Ganzan Daishi ascription.** Attributing the Japanese hundred-poem corpus to Ryōgen (912–985) is a devotional tradition, not a documented fact; the poems' consolidation and mass diffusion are much later (Edo-period), and scholarship treats the ascription as a legitimating legend attached to a genre that was imported, not personally authored.
2. **How old is the Chinese genre?** The *printed, numbered* oracle-slip (*língqiān / qiānshī*) is securely a **Song-dynasty** consolidation (960–1279); claims of solid Tang-dynasty or earlier fixed corpora are debated, since temple lot-drawing as a *practice* is older than the fixed *verse-corpora* that define this system. The Atlas dates the node from the secure Song witness (c. 900–1000) while flagging the precedent question.
3. **One system or two?** Whether Chinese qiúqiān and Japanese o-mikuji are "the same" divination or two related systems is partly a framing choice. This node treats them as one cross-national genre with a documented transmission — the position of Strickmann 2005 — while noting that modern o-mikuji (vending-box slips, simplified grades) has drifted far from the kneeling, moon-block-confirmed temple rite of Sinophone practice.
