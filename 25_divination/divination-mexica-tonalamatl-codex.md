# Mexica Tonalamatl — Pre-Conquest Divinatory Codex Tradition

The **tonalamatl** (Nahuatl, "book of days") is the painted screenfold codex used in pre-Conquest Mesoamerica as the principal divinatory consultation reference. The tonalpouhqui (day-counter / diviner) consulted it at births, marriages, illness, planning of journeys + farming + warfare, reading the consultant's birth-day + the proposed-action day against the codex's painted day-by-day prognostications.

## The codex and the calendar

The tonalamatl is organized by the **tonalpohualli** — the 260-day sacred calendar formed by interlocking 20 day-signs (Crocodile, Wind, House, Lizard, Snake, Death, Deer, Rabbit, Water, Dog, Monkey, Grass, Reed, Jaguar, Eagle, Vulture, Movement, Flint, Rain, Flower) with 13 numbers. Each unique combination (1-Crocodile through 13-Flower) is a tonalli — a day with its own divinatory character. The 260 days are also grouped into 20 trecenas (13-day periods), each beginning with a numbered day-sign and presided over by a patron deity.

The divinatory codex paints each trecena as a multi-figure panel: the patron deity at the head of the trecena, the 13 individual days below, additional auguries + sub-deities + symbolic objects in marginal compartments. The most elaborate surviving examples are the **Borgia Group** — five pre-Conquest screenfolds (Codex Borgia, Codex Cospi, Codex Fejérváry-Mayer, Codex Laud, Codex Vaticanus B) that share a common iconographic vocabulary and are believed to come from the Puebla-Tlaxcala highlands of central Mexico, ~1450-1500.

The post-Conquest **Codex Borbonicus** (~1525) is the most-illustrative for pedagogy: its first 18 pages are the canonical tonalpohualli pages, with the trecenas laid out one per page, the patron deity at the upper-left or right, the 13 days running along two adjacent sides, and the marginal auguries filling the remaining panels. The Borbonicus was painted by indigenous tlacuiloque (codex-painters) in the early colonial period using pre-Conquest pictorial conventions; it preserves the tradition intact.

## How the diviner read

The tonalpouhqui's reading combined multiple inputs:
1. **Consultant's birth-tonalli** — the day-sign + number under which they were born; this fixed lifelong character + fate-trajectory
2. **Proposed-action tonalli** — the day-sign of the contemplated action (journey, marriage, planting, raid)
3. **Trecena patron** — the 13-day period's patron deity, modifying the day's character
4. **Year-bearer + 52-year Calendar Round position** — the slow-moving outer cycle interlocking the 260-day tonalpohualli with the 365-day xiuhpohualli

A child born on 1-Death might receive a name reflecting that tonalli; the parents might delay the naming-ceremony to a more auspicious day a few days later (a documented practice). A raid might be scheduled for the trecena of Itzpapalotl (Obsidian Butterfly), an auspicious warrior-deity period. A marriage might be aligned with a Xochipilli (Flower Prince) trecena for festivity.

Sahagún's *Florentine Codex* Book 4 ("The Art of Divination") provides the contemporary ethnographic account, with day-sign-by-day-sign descriptions of the character + fate associated with each tonalli. It is the principal post-Conquest documentation of how the divinatory codices were actually consulted.

## Cross-tradition position

**Cross-tradition finding — the calendrical-divinatory codex as a Mesoamerican type**: the tonalamatl is a distinctive Mesoamerican divinatory technology that has no direct parallel outside the Mesoamerican culture-area. Its closest cross-tradition parallels are:

- **Mayan tzolkin divination** (the Mayan 260-day calendar, parallel to the tonalpohualli; the Madrid Codex + Paris Codex + Dresden Codex include divinatory pages) — same 260-day calendrical structure, same day-sign-by-day-sign augury approach; the Mesoamerican calendrical-divinatory complex extends from Maya to Mexica
- **Chinese hemerology** (択日, *zeri*) — selecting auspicious days from the sexagenary calendar for marriage, burial, building. The structural parallel is strong: a calendrical system with day-by-day auspicious-vs-inauspicious characterization, consulted for major actions. The Chinese tradition is text-based rather than pictorial; the Mesoamerican is iconographic. But the underlying divinatory ontology — calendar-time as differentially-qualified — is the same.
- **Tibetan astrological consultation** for choosing auspicious days (an *mo* + *kar-tsi* mixed practice)
- **Vedic Indian Pañcāṅga** ([[hindu-panchang-calendar]]) — the daily-almanac tradition

The Mesoamerican distinctive contribution is the **painted-codex format**: the divinatory information is encoded iconographically, not textually. The reading is a *picture-reading* practice, not a *text-reading* practice. This makes the Mesoamerican tradition the principal world instance of fully-iconographic divinatory reference media.

**260-day calendrical mystery**: the 260-day count is independently used by both Mexica and Maya; it has no obvious astronomical referent (it's not lunar, solar, planetary, or eclipse-cycle-derived). The leading hypotheses are: (a) approximation of human gestation (~266 days, close); (b) the interval between zenith-sun passages at ~15° N latitude (Izapa region, ~105 days apart, doubled ~210 days, not quite); (c) ritual elaboration of the 20-day Mesoamerican base count × 13 sacred numbers without astronomical referent. The 260-day calendar is one of the genuinely-unresolved cross-Mesoamerican mysteries.

**Conquest suppression + survival**: nearly all pre-Conquest tonalamatl codices were destroyed during the Spanish Conquest (1519-1521) and the subsequent inquisitorial book-burnings (Bishop Diego de Landa's 1562 Maní burnings destroyed nearly the entire Maya codex corpus; the same suppression hit the central Mexican codices). The 5-codex Borgia Group + a handful of others survive because they were carried to Europe as curiosities + housed in Vatican, Bologna, Liverpool, and Oxford collections. The pre-Conquest divinatory tradition was extinguished as institutional practice within two generations of contact; modern Mesoamerican day-keeping (still practiced among Maya day-keepers in highland Guatemala, the *ajq'ij*) is partly continuous, partly reconstruction.
