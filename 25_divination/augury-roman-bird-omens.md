# Augury — Roman Bird-Flight Divination

The Roman state-religion divinatory institution of reading sky-signs — bird-flight, lightning, thunder, sacred-chicken feeding behavior — within a ritually-marked-off celestial quadrant (the *templum*). Practiced by the *augures*, a college of recognized state priests, prior to any major magistrate act (election, military campaign, legislation, treaty-signing). One of the two foundational Roman divinatory institutions alongside haruspicy.

## How it works

The augur performs the procedure:

1. **Marking the *templum*** — using the *lituus* (a curved staff; the augural symbol of office), the augur marks out a four-quadrant boundary in the visible sky. The same word *templum* is used for both the sky-quadrant and the consecrated ground-quadrant beneath it (which is where the Latin *templum* / English *temple* etymology lives — the temple is the *grounded* templum)
2. **Asking the question** — the augur formulates the *auspicium*: a yes/no question to the gods, often binary ("is it lawful for X to do Y?")
3. **Observation** — the augur watches for bird-flight, thunder, lightning, or feeding-behavior signs within the marked *templum* within a defined time window
4. **Reading** — favorable / unfavorable / null interpretation based on (a) species (eagle, vulture, raven, owl, etc., each with conventional valence), (b) direction (the conventions vary by source — Cicero's *De Divinatione* discusses the Greek vs. Roman left-right inversion), (c) other behavior

The system was **state-institutional** (the *collegium augurum* was a recognized priesthood with legal authority to halt magistrate action via *obnuntiatio* — the declaration of unfavorable signs), **expert-mediated** (decades of training; cooptation into the college), and **politically-consequential** (a negative *auspicium* could legally invalidate an election or military action; the institution's binding force on magistrates was a real feature of Republican politics).

## Founding mythology — Romulus, Remus, and the Aventine augury

Livy 1.6-7 records the foundational Roman bird-omen episode: Romulus + Remus disputed which of the seven hills should be the site of the new city. They resolved the question by augury — Remus on the Aventine, Romulus on the Palatine, each watching for bird-omens. Remus saw six vultures first; Romulus then saw twelve. The contest was disputed (which counted more — earlier-seen or larger-numbered?), leading to the fight in which Romulus killed Remus. The episode functions as the *origin myth of the city of Rome* AND the *origin myth of state augury* — augury is co-foundational with Rome itself in the Roman self-understanding.

## Sacred chickens on campaign — the *tripudium solistimum*

A peculiarly Roman institutional adaptation: military commanders on campaign couldn't always wait for proper bird-flight conditions, so the augural college institutionalized portable divination via sacred chickens carried in cages. The chickens were fed; if they ate eagerly enough that pieces of food dropped from their mouths (the *tripudium solistimum*), the omen was favorable. The institution provided plausible deniability + manipulation potential — the most famous case is the consul Publius Claudius Pulcher at Drepana (-249), who, when the chickens refused to eat (= unfavorable), reportedly threw them overboard saying *"if they will not eat, let them drink"* + fought the battle anyway. He lost (Cicero *De Divinatione* 2.71; Suetonius *Tiberius* 2.2). The episode was preserved as the canonical Roman cautionary tale about ignoring auspices.

## MASSIVE-WIN — bird-omen reading as a cross-tradition convergence

Bird-flight as omen is documented as a divinatory practice across an unusual breadth of unrelated traditions:

- **Greek ornithomancy** — Homeric heroes routinely read bird-flight; Calchas, Helenus, Tiresias all interpret bird-signs (Iliad 2.299-330, 8.247-252, 12.200-229; Odyssey 15.160-178)
- **Etruscan augury** — the Romans explicitly claim Etruscan transmission of the augural discipline; the *Etrusca Disciplina* contained augural-procedural canon (lost; preserved fragmentarily via Cicero, Pliny, Servius)
- **Hittite *kîn*-divination** — bird-omen texts from Hattuša document a parallel institutional bird-divination practice in 2nd-millennium Anatolia (the so-called MUŠEN ḪURRI bird-omen texts; Bottéro 1992)
- **Mesopotamian *šumma ālu*** tablets include extensive bird-omen sections (terrestrial omens)
- **Andean / Inca** — bird-omens documented in early colonial sources (Cobo, Murúa) as state-divinatory practice
- **Indigenous North American** — multiple traditions read bird behavior, flight-patterns, raven-calls as oracular

The cross-tradition convergence sits on **plausible cognitive-ecological grounds** (birds are uniquely visible long-distance moving objects in pre-modern landscapes; their behavior tracks atmospheric, climatic, and seasonal change in ways that *do* carry real predictive information about local conditions), but the *systematized institutional codification* — what species means what, what direction means what, with state-binding legal force — is the cultural-historical particular that varies.

## Cicero's critique (De Divinatione, -44 BCE)

Cicero — himself an augur from 53 BCE — wrote the principal surviving ancient analytical critique. *De Divinatione* book 2 argues that no mechanism connects bird-flight to future events; that the empirical track record of auspices is no better than chance; that the institution survives because it's politically useful, not because it works. The book is the founding document of Western philosophical-skeptical analysis of divination + remains one of the sharpest such analyses in any tradition.

## Christianization terminus

Augury, like haruspicy, was a state-institutional pagan-religious practice. Theodosius I's 391 CE legislation against pagan public rites ended its legal standing. Individual practice persisted longer in private + rural contexts, but the augural college ceased functioning as a state institution by the early 5th c. CE. See [[haruspicy-etruscan-roman]] for the parallel-suppression narrative.
