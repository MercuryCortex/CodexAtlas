# Necromancy — Divination by Consulting the Dead Across Traditions

## Identity

Necromancy is the divinatory consultation of the **dead** for hidden knowledge — the future, a lost secret, a verdict on the living. It is a distinct slot in the Atlas's divination lens: not general spirit-mediumship (the addressee is specifically the deceased), not oneiromancy (the channel is a rite of evocation, not a dream), and not underworld-descent for its own sake (the dead are summoned to answer a question). Two things recur wherever it appears: a ritual technology for reaching the dead — a blood-fed pit, a tomb, a dedicated oracle-of-the-dead, a specialist medium — and a strong ambivalence, since the practice is very often **condemned in the same texts that attest it**. That tension — practice and prohibition bound together — is the node's centre of gravity.

## Necromancy across the traditions

### Mesopotamia — inquiring of the eṭemmu

The oldest secure attestations are Mesopotamian. Akkadian ritual texts describe the raising and interrogation of the **eṭemmu**, the ghost of a dead person, and preserve a late necromantic ritual for making a ghost appear and speak. Irving Finkel's *"Necromancy in Ancient Mesopotamia"* (1983-84) is the classic study; JoAnn Scurlock's *Magico-Medical Means of Treating Ghosts* (2006) sets the full apparatus of contacting, feeding, and interrogating the dead within Mesopotamian magic. Crucially, this ritual death-consultation is older than the Greek material, which matters for the transmission question below.

### The Hebrew Bible — Endor and the prohibition

The Hebrew Bible gives necromancy its most famous single scene and its sharpest prohibition together. In **1 Samuel 28**, King Saul, on the eve of the battle of Gilboa and finding that God "answered him not, neither by dreams, nor by Urim, nor by prophets" (28:6) — the three legitimate channels — seeks out a **ba'alat-'ob**, a "mistress of a ghost," at En-dor. She raises the dead prophet **Samuel**, who prophesies Saul's defeat and death. The Chronicler later names this consultation as the very reason for Saul's death (1 Chr 10:13-14). Yet the same corpus **forbids** the practice in the strongest terms: **Deuteronomy 18:10-11** bans "a consulter with a ghost (*sho'el 'ob*), or a wizard (*yidde'oni*), or one who inquires of the dead (*doresh el-hametim*)"; compare Lev 19:31; 20:6, 27; Isa 8:19 ("should not a people seek unto their God? for the living to the dead?"); and the Deuteronomistic purges of 2 Kgs 21:6 and 23:24. The narrative and the law thus witness to a real regional practice by condemning it. Brian Schmidt's *Israel's Beneficent Dead* (1994) is the standard study.

### Greece — the Nekyia and the nekromanteia

The literary template of Western necromancy is **Odyssey 11**, the *Nekyia*: on Circe's instructions Odysseus sails to the edge of Ocean, digs a pit (*bothros*), pours libations and the blood of black sheep, and the shades throng up — he must hold them from the blood with his sword until **Tiresias** drinks and foretells his homecoming. Beyond epic, Greece maintained dedicated **oracles of the dead** (*nekromanteia* / *psychomanteia*). Daniel Ogden's *Greek and Roman Necromancy* (2001) identifies four principal sites — the **Acheron** in Thesprotia, **Avernus** in Campania, **Herakleia Pontike**, and **Tainaron** — served by *psychagogoi* ("evocators of souls"). Herodotus 5.92 reports the tyrant **Periander** of Corinth sending to the Acheron oracle to consult his dead wife Melissa; Plutarch (*Cimon* 6) has the Spartan regent Pausanias seek out an evocator to appease the ghost of Kleonike.

### Rome — the corpse-oracle

Roman literature pushes necromancy to a gruesome maximum in **Lucan's *Pharsalia* 6**, where the Thessalian witch **Erictho** reanimates a fresh corpse to prophesy to Sextus Pompeius before Pharsalus — the fullest necromancy scene in classical literature. Necromancy also surfaces as a live accusation in Roman political invective (Cicero charges Vatinius with it, *In Vatinium* 14) and in the poetry of Horace (Canidia).

### China — the ancestor-oracle

Independently, Bronze Age China institutionalized death-consultation as its central state divination. On the **Shang oracle bones** (c. -1250 onward), the deceased **royal ancestors** are the primary addressees of inquiry: the living king cracks the bone or shell and asks the dead forebears, who intercede with the high god Di (David Keightley, *Sources of Shang History*, 1978). This is technical rather than evocative — a learned reading rather than a raising — but the logic is the same: the dead are asked. It is cross-linked here to [[scapulimancy-oracle-bones]] and distinguished from Chinese spirit-mediumship (the *wu*).

## Cross-tradition significance (transmission vs convergence)

Necromancy is a case where the honest verdict leans heavily toward **convergence**, with one narrow window for transmission. The underlying premise — *the dead know things the living do not, and the recently dead are still near enough to be reached* — is a near-universal inference from the widespread [[ancestor-veneration|cult of the dead]]. Mesopotamia, Israel, Greece, Rome, and China all arrive at death-consultation, and there is **no secure linguistic or textual chain** binding them together in the way that Hebrew *tĕhôm* is bound to Akkadian *Tiāmat*. The parallels are structural and typological, not genetic: an independent, recurrent solution to a recurrent human wish.

Two qualifications keep the picture honest. First, the **Mediterranean nekromanteion** — a fixed oracular institution of the dead — is a specifically Greek development with no exact counterpart elsewhere, even though the *rite* of raising a ghost is older in Mesopotamia; Finkel's Akkadian ghost-summoning rituals predate the Greek Nekyia, so a diffuse Near-Eastern-to-Greek current of ghost-ritual technique is plausible without being demonstrable. Second, the Israelite material sits on a **shared West-Semitic substrate**: the vocabulary of the '*ob* and the cult of the dead has Ugaritic and broader Levantine analogues, so the biblical prohibition is best read as a polemic against a genuinely regional practice — one the Deuteronomistic writers wanted purged precisely because it was there. Whether Israel *itself* practised an indigenous necromantic ancestor-cult, or merely condemned a foreign one, is the live scholarly dispute (below).

The comparative payoff is that necromancy sits cleanly on the **natural/inspired** pole of Cicero's divide ([[divination-typology-comparative]]): the dead are a *conduit* of knowledge, not a learned sign-system — except in the Chinese oracle-bone case, where the same "ask the dead" impulse is realized as an **artificial/inductive** technique, a revealing split within a single cross-tradition motif.

## Disputes

1. **Did Israel practise necromancy, or only condemn it?** Brian Schmidt (*Israel's Beneficent Dead*, 1994) argues that necromancy and the cult of the dead were **not** an indigenous pre-exilic Israelite institution but a late (Neo-Assyrian-period) development and, in large part, a literary construct of Deuteronomistic polemic. The "ancestor-cult school" — Theodore Lewis (*Cults of the Dead in Ancient Israel and Ugarit*, 1989), Karel van der Toorn, and (archaeologically) Elizabeth Bloch-Smith — argues the opposite: a genuine, indigenous, pre-exilic cult of the dead that the reform literature tried to stamp out. The 1 Sam 28 narrative can be read either way.

2. **Is the Odyssey 11 Nekyia necromancy at all?** The shades come *up* to Odysseus at the pit, which reads as evocation — yet the episode is set at the edge of the underworld and shades into a *katabasis* (a descent). Ogden treats the ambiguity directly; the Atlas wires the node to [[katabasis-and-anabasis]] precisely because the boundary between "bringing the dead up" and "going down to the dead" is porous here.

3. **The philology of *'ob*.** The Hebrew *'ob* has been read as "ghost," as a ritual object/talisman, and (attractively, given the Greek *bothros*) as a "pit" from which the dead are called. The term's exact reference remains debated and bears on how mechanically the En-dor rite resembled the Greek pit-evocation.
