## Upasampada — Theravada Full Monastic Ordination

*Upasampada* (Pali/Sanskrit: "going up to" or "taking up") is the formal ceremony by which a candidate becomes a fully ordained *bhikkhu* (monk) or *bhikkhuni* (nun) in the Theravada Buddhist tradition. The ceremony is the culmination of a process that begins with entry as a *samanera* (novice) after the *pabbajja* ("going forth") ceremony, and typically requires at minimum one *vassa* (rains retreat, three months) in the novice status.

Full ordination creates membership in the *bhikkhu-sangha* — the community of fully ordained monks — with all the rights and obligations of the Patimokkha (227 rules for monks, 311 for nuns). More fundamentally, it creates a claim to an unbroken lineage of ordination extending back to the historical Buddha.

### The Formal Requirements: Quorum as Constitutional Law

The Vinaya (monastic code) specifies precise requirements for a valid upasampada:

**Quorum requirements:**
- Within the "middle country" (Majjhimadesa, the central Ganges plain): minimum **5 fully ordained monks**
- In "border regions" (outside the middle country): minimum **10 fully ordained monks**
- All must be within the *sima* (ritually bounded space, whose boundaries are formally established by the community)

**The candidate must:**
- Be free from certain disqualifying conditions (certain diseases, debt, being enslaved, certain family obligations, being a criminal)
- Have reached the age of 20 (counting from conception, not birth)
- Have the three robes and the alms-bowl present
- Have a formal preceptor (*upajjhaya*) with minimum 10 years of seniority
- Be formally questioned before the assembled sangha — three times — to confirm their eligibility

**The ceremony:**
1. The candidate is presented by a proposer (*kammavacaka*, the "one who performs the formal act")
2. The formal motion is announced: "The Sangha is asked to give the going-forth to [name] who wishes to go forth"
3. Silence = consent; any objection must be voiced
4. The motion is read three times; three silences constitute unanimous consent
5. The ordination is complete at the moment of the third silence

This juridical formalism — the quorum, the threefold public announcement, the legally-binding silence — makes the upasampada one of the most precisely specified ritual acts in the history of religion. The Vinaya devotes extensive commentary to edge cases: what if someone objects after the third silence? What if a monk in the quorum is later found to have been improperly ordained? The validity of every monk's ordination depends on the validity of every ordaining monk's ordination in an unbroken chain back to the first ordination performed by the Buddha himself.

### The Apostolic Succession Parallel

The Theravada upasampada shares its deepest structural logic with three other institutions:

**1. Catholic Apostolic Succession**: The claim that every validly ordained Catholic bishop received his ordination from a bishop who received his from a bishop, in an unbroken chain extending to the apostles ordained by Christ, is structurally identical to the Theravada ordination-chain claim. Both traditions identify:
- A founding ordination event (Christ ordaining the apostles; the Buddha ordaining the first monks)
- An unbroken chain of transmission as the criterion of validity
- A specific quorum/community requirement for valid ordination
- Intense anxiety about chain-integrity (the Catholic Church keeps genealogical records of episcopal ordinations; Theravada traditions keep lineage records)

**2. Sufi Silsila**: The Sufi *silsila* ("chain") is the explicit lineage of master-to-disciple transmission from the founding figure (the Prophet Muhammad, via Ali, via successive masters) to the current teacher. Without a valid silsila, a Sufi master's authority is illegitimate. The silsila is the Sufi equivalent of apostolic succession — a chain whose integrity validates every link and every practitioner within the lineage.

**3. Jewish Rabbinic Semicha**: The ordination of rabbis (*semicha*, "laying of hands") historically claimed a chain back to Moses, who laid hands on Joshua, who laid hands on the elders, and so on. The Talmudic tradition taught that the Sanhedrin's authority — and all rabbinic authority — depended on this unbroken chain. The chain of rabbinic semicha is believed by most historians to have been broken by Roman persecutions in the 1st–2nd centuries CE; the current rabbinate functions on a delegated authority that no longer claims the original *semicha* chain.

The cross-tradition pattern is precise: each of these traditions articulates a **chain theory of sacred authority** in which:
1. Ultimate authority derives from a founding sacred event
2. That authority is transmitted through an unbroken chain of valid acts
3. Chain integrity is the criterion of current authority
4. Breaks in the chain constitute crises requiring institutional response

### The Sri Lanka Lineage Crisis: The Quorum Problem

The most dramatic demonstration of the chain-theory's stakes occurred in 18th-century Sri Lanka. By the early 1700s, Theravada Buddhism in Sri Lanka had declined so severely — through Portuguese and Dutch colonialism, war, and social disruption — that not enough fully ordained bhikkhus remained to constitute a valid ordination quorum. The chain was on the verge of breaking entirely.

King Kirthi Sri Rajasinha (r. 1747–1782) dispatched a delegation to Thailand (Siam) — at that time the strongest Theravada kingdom — requesting a mission of Thai monks to re-establish the ordination lineage in Sri Lanka. The Siamese mission arrived in 1753, led by the Venerable Upali. At a ceremony performed on a raft moored in a river (a deliberately chosen *sima* that could not be contested), the valid Thai ordination lineage was transmitted to Sri Lankan monks. The resulting lineage — the *Siam Nikaya* — is the oldest existing nikaya in Sri Lanka.

The 1753 Siamese mission demonstrates two things simultaneously:
1. The chain-theory is taken completely seriously — the Sri Lanka Buddhists did not simply ordain more monks themselves, because they believed their chain had insufficient links to constitute valid ordination
2. The chain can be repaired by import — the chain doesn't require geographic continuity, only lineage continuity

This crisis has a direct parallel in the history of Catholic apostolic succession: the question of whether certain reforming or splinter groups possess a valid apostolic chain is one of the most contested questions in Catholic ecumenical theology.

### The Temporary Ordination: Liminal Period as Social Institution

A practice that makes Theravada Buddhism unusual among world religions is the widespread custom of **temporary ordination**, particularly in Burma (Myanmar) and Thailand:

- In Burma (*shinbyu*): virtually all boys enter the monastery for a period (ranging from a few days to several months) as a novice monk, typically between ages 10 and 20. The shinbyu ceremony is one of the most important rites of passage in Burmese social life — it is the event at which a boy transitions to manhood.
- In Thailand (*tok tah*): similar brief ordinations are common; many men ordain for a period of weeks or months, particularly during the three-month *vassa* (rains retreat), and then disrobe and return to lay life.

The temporary ordination is understood as generating *merit* (*punna*) for the ordinand and his family, fulfilling a cultural expectation of experiencing the monastic life, and creating a period of liminal transformation that marks the transition to adult male status.

The structural parallel to military conscription in secular societies is exact: a mandatory (or quasi-mandatory) liminal period — separation from ordinary life, submission to a total institution, intense communal discipline — that creates adult male social identity and community solidarity. Both the Burmese shinbyu and the military draft are institutionalized liminal periods (following van Gennep's schema) that convert boys into socially recognized men through a structured ordeal.
