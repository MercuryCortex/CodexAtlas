# Potlatch

## Overview
The potlatch is the central political-religious ceremony of Pacific Northwest Coast peoples: Kwakwaka'wakw, Haida, Tlingit, Tsimshian, Nuu-chah-nulth, and many others. It is an occasion at which a chief or noble family demonstrates status by hosting a feast and *giving everything away* — distributed gifts to guests that can include blankets, food, canoes, copper shields (the most prestigious currency), and in historical records, the deliberate destruction of property. The more one gives (or destroys), the higher one's status. The guests, by accepting gifts, witness and validate the host's claims.

The Canadian government banned the potlatch from 1885 to 1951. This was not incidental — the colonial authorities correctly recognized the potlatch as a competing economic theology, a rival system of value that the capitalist property regime could not absorb.

## The Ceremony — Structure

**Occasions:** Potlatches mark major life events: birth, naming, coming-of-age, marriage, death, and the succession of a chief. They can also be called to challenge rivals (a counter-potlatch escalates the gifting) or to repair social ruptures.

**The gift logic:** In Kwakwaka'wakw tradition (the most extensively documented by Franz Boas), the host chief's prestige is measured not by what he accumulates but by what he distributes. Guests are ranked; each receives gifts calibrated to their social position. To accept the gift is to acknowledge the host's higher status. This is a formal, juridically binding transaction — the witnessing guests are the legal record, before writing.

**The copper shield (*tináa*):** The most prestigious object in potlatch exchange — a shaped copper sheet whose value is established entirely by the history of what was paid for it in previous potlatches. A copper that has been exchanged in many high-prestige potlatches is worth more than a new copper simply because of its transactional history. Destroying a copper ("breaking copper") at a potlatch — publicly smashing it and throwing it into the sea — is the ultimate demonstration of surplus: I have so much that I can destroy the most valuable object.

**The theological dimension:** The potlatch is not charity. It is the enactment of a cosmological principle: the cosmos is maintained by giving, not hoarding. The chief who gives abundantly is aligned with the salmon who gives its body to the people, the tree that gives its wood, the ocean that gives its fish. Hoarding is cosmologically wrong — it disrupts the circulation of gifts that sustains the world.

## The Colonial Prohibition (1885–1951)

The Canadian Indian Act amendment of 1885 explicitly banned the potlatch, with imprisonment for participants. The stated justifications were: economic (the potlatch prevented Indigenous people from accumulating capital); civilizational (it was "wasteful" and "irrational"); and administrative (it concentrated political authority in chiefs whom the colonial government could not control).

The real reason, which some colonial officials stated openly, was that the potlatch was a rival economic logic. A people who measure status by giving cannot be recruited into a system that measures status by accumulation. The potlatch made Indigenous people non-recruitable into wage labor and property ownership as organizing principles of life.

The 1951 revision of the Indian Act removed the ban. Many communities, particularly Kwakwaka'wakw, had continued potlatching secretly throughout the prohibition. The 1921 Dan Cranmer potlatch (Alert Bay, BC) was raided by the RCMP; participants were imprisoned; ceremonial objects confiscated and dispersed to museums. Many of these objects have since been repatriated.

## MASSIVE WIN — Radical Giving as the Mechanism of Spiritual Authority: Four Traditions

**The structural claim:** Four traditions, in complete independence, identified radical giving — not ascetic withdrawal, not intellectual achievement, not moral virtue — as the primary mechanism through which spiritual authority is established and cosmic order is maintained.

| Tradition | Practice | Mechanics | Theological logic |
|-----------|----------|-----------|-------------------|
| Pacific Northwest Indigenous | Potlatch | Chief gives everything away; the more given, the higher the status; hoarding is cosmologically wrong | Cosmos sustained by circulation of gifts; the chief models cosmic generosity |
| Christian / Franciscan | Voluntary poverty (Francis of Assisi) | Stripped off his clothes before his father and the bishop; owned nothing; preached poverty as the path to Christ | Poverty is freedom; radical divestiture imitates Christ's kenosis (self-emptying); the poor man is closest to God |
| Buddhist | *Dāna* (generosity / giving) | Monks own nothing; the community gives food and robes to monks daily; the monk gives the Dharma in return | *Dāna* is the first of the three foundations of merit; generosity is the entry point into the Buddhist path; the gift creates connection |
| Jain | *Aparigraha* (non-possessiveness) | Digambara monks own nothing, not even clothes; householders practice graduated non-attachment to possessions | Possessiveness is the root of *hiṃsā* (harm); releasing attachment to things releases attachment to the ego that causes violence |

**What this means:** Each of these systems identifies accumulation as the problem and giving as the solution — but the theological rationale differs. Francis: poverty is Christ-likeness. Buddhist dāna: giving creates merit and loosens the ego's grip. Jain aparigraha: possession is violence against the world. Potlatch: giving is cosmic alignment. Four independent diagnoses of the same disease (possessiveness), four independent prescriptions (radical giving), four different explanations of why it works.

**The colonial implication:** The Canadian government's instinct was correct. The potlatch is a rival economic theology. If status is measured by giving, the entire apparatus of capitalist motivation (accumulation, property, inheritance, status through possession) becomes incoherent. The potlatch could not be reformed into capitalism — it had to be banned.
