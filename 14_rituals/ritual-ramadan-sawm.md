## Ramadan Sawm — Islamic Fasting and the Acknowledged Prior Tradition

*Sawm* (Arabic: صَوم, "fasting," from *sama*, "to abstain") during Ramadan is the fourth pillar of Islam: a month-long daily fast from pre-dawn (*suhur*) until after sunset (*iftar*), during which food, drink, smoking, and sexual relations are forbidden during daylight hours. The Quran prescribes Ramadan fasting in one of its most remarkable verses — remarkable because it explicitly acknowledges that fasting was already practiced by prior religious communities before Islam.

### The Surprising Find: The Quran's Self-Referential Acknowledgment

Quran 2:183: **"O you who believe! Fasting is prescribed for you as it was prescribed for those who came before you, so that you may achieve taqwa (God-consciousness)."**

This is an extraordinary moment of Quranic self-positioning: Islamic ritual legislation explicitly acknowledges its continuity with prior religious traditions. The phrase "as it was prescribed for those who came before you" (*kama kutiba 'ala'lladhina min qablikum*) is not vague — it is a theological claim that fasting is not a Quranic innovation but the restoration of a universal divine prescription that previous communities received. The Quran does not claim originality here; it claims restoration.

Scholars debate who "those before you" refers to: Jewish fasting (Yom Kippur, Tisha B'Av, four other mandated fasts), Christian fasting (Lent, Wednesdays and Fridays), or the broader pre-Islamic Arabian fasting tradition. The most likely reading is all of them, and the Quran's theological point is precisely that they are continuous.

### Pre-Islamic Ramadan

Ramadan was a sacred month in the pre-Islamic Arabian calendar before the Quran's revelation. Pre-Islamic Arabs observed *tahannuth* — a religious practice of retreating to caves for meditation and spiritual exercises during Ramadan — the same practice Muhammad was performing when the first Quranic revelation came (610 CE). The Prophet's own pre-Islamic spiritual practice of cave retreat during Ramadan predates the Quranic institution of fasting. The month was already sacred; the fasting commandment gave new content to the existing sacred frame.

The shift of fasting month from the pre-Islamic *Ashura* fast (the 10th of Muharram, parallel to Yom Kippur) to Ramadan is itself historically layered: early Islamic practice included Ashura fasting (with explicit parallels to Jewish Yom Kippur cited in hadith), and it was only in year 2 AH that Ramadan fasting became obligatory. The archaeological record of early Islamic practice shows a community negotiating its relationship to Jewish fasting norms before establishing its distinctive calendar.

### Cross-Tradition Fasting: A Universal Ascetic Technology

**Jewish Yom Kippur (25-hour fast)**: The most intense fast in the Jewish calendar — total abstinence from food, drink, leather shoes, bathing, cosmetics, and sexual relations for 25 hours. The theological logic is *kappara* (atonement) and *teshuvah* (return/repentance). The [[ritual-fasting-cross-tradition]] node documents the structural parallels. The Yom Kippur fast is the most complete parallel to Ramadan's intention — both orient fasting toward consciousness of God and purification of conduct, not merely physical discipline.

**Christian Lent (40 days)**: The 40-day fast commemorates Jesus's 40 days in the wilderness (itself mirroring Moses's 40 days on Sinai and the Israelites' 40 years in the desert). Lent's 40 days is a numerological claim: 40 is the biblical duration of transformative ordeal. The [[ritual-advent-lent-liturgical-year]] documents the full Christian fasting calendar. The Eastern Orthodox Lent is more severe: Great Lent prescribes total abstinence from meat, dairy, fish, oil, and wine on most days — an ascetic intensity comparable to Ramadan's daylight restriction but applied to quality rather than timing.

**Buddhist Vassa (3-month Rains Retreat)**: The Theravada *vassa* (Pali: "rain") is a 3-month monastic retreat during the monsoon season when monks do not travel. During vassa, monks traditionally eat only before noon — a restriction derived from the Vinaya rule against eating after midday, which applies throughout the year but is intensified during vassa. This is the oldest institutionalized fasting rule in a world religion: the restriction to morning eating predates the Quran by over a millennium.

**Hindu Ekadashi (bi-monthly fasting)**: Devout Hindus fast on the 11th day of each lunar fortnight — *Ekadashi* — totaling 24 fasting days per year. The fast is associated with Vishnu and is designed to cleanse the digestive system and purify the mind. Some traditions observe water-only or dry fasts; others permit fruits and dairy. The bi-monthly cadence of Ekadashi means that practitioners are in a near-continuous cycle of fast and feast — fasting as a regular discipline rather than an annual event.

### The Unique Logic of Ramadan

What distinguishes Ramadan from other fasting traditions:

1. **Lunar specificity**: Ramadan moves through the solar year (advancing ~11 days annually). A Muslim born in 1980 will fast Ramadan in every season of the year before dying. The fast is not attached to a solar event (winter solstice, spring equinox) but to a lunar month — it is genuinely time-invariant across the solar year.

2. **Collective synchronization**: 1.8 billion people fast simultaneously. The collective dimension is central — *iftar* (breaking the fast) is a communal event. Mosques provide free meals; families gather. The fast creates the most globally synchronized communal experience in human religious history.

3. **Night-inversion**: Ramadan inverts the normal day/night rhythm. Many Muslims sleep during the day and are active at night — eating the *suhur* before dawn, praying *tarawih* (extended night prayers), socializing after *iftar*. The entire social life of Muslim-majority countries restructures around the fasted day. This inversion has a theological logic: Ramadan is the month of the night (*Laylat al-Qadr*, the Night of Power, when the Quran was first revealed) — night becomes primary.

4. **Spectrum of fasting types**: The Quran and hadith distinguish outer fasting (food/drink) from inner fasting — fasting of the tongue (no lying, gossip, obscenity), fasting of the eyes (no forbidden sights), fasting of the ears, fasting of the heart. Al-Ghazali's *Ihya Ulum al-Din* describes three grades: the ordinary fast (food/drink), the special fast (additionally restraining senses and limbs), and the super-special fast (emptying the heart of all except God). This three-level structure mirrors the Buddhist gradation of monastic discipline, Christian mystical gradations, and Kabbalistic levels of fasting intention.
