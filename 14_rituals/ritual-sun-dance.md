# Sun Dance (Wiwáŋyaŋg Wačhípi)

## Overview
The Sun Dance — *Wiwáŋyaŋg Wačhípi*, "dancing looking at the sun" — is the paramount communal ceremony of the Lakota and many other Plains peoples (Cheyenne, Arapaho, Crow, Blackfoot each have cognate forms). Held in summer, anchored to the solstice, the ceremony lasts four days. It is simultaneously a communal renewal rite, a sacrifice offered for the community's benefit, and an embedded vision quest structure in which individual pledgers offer their pain to [[wakan-tanka]] in exchange for visions and community blessing.

Outlawed by the United States government 1883–1934. Revived and restored through [[tradition-lakota]] ceremonial revitalization from the 1970s onward.

## The Ceremony — Structure

**Preparation (four days preceding):** The pledgers (those who have vowed to sacrifice) enter a period of instruction with a *wičháša wakȟáŋ* (holy man). The community gathers. A cottonwood tree is selected, felled with ceremony (it must not touch the ground), and raised as the central pole — the *čaŋwákaŋ*, "sacred tree" — representing the axis connecting earth and sky, the Lakota world-axis.

**The four days:**
- Fasting and dancing from sunrise to sunset, facing the sun as it moves. No food or water throughout.
- Piercing: the pledger's chest (or back) is pierced with wooden skewers by the ceremony's holy man. Rawhide thongs are run through the skewers and attached to the central pole. The pledger dances, leaning back against the ropes, until the skewers tear free through the skin.
- The tearing free is the moment of release — the offering completed, the prayer embodied.
- For some pledgers, the piercing is through the back, with buffalo skulls dragged behind.

**The vision:** The physical extremity — exhaustion, fasting, pain, continuous dance — induces visionary states. The Sun Dance is the ritual container in which individual vision quests (→ [[ritual-vision-quest]]) are embedded within communal ceremony.

**Theologically:** The body's pain is the offering. The pledger does not sacrifice an animal or grain — they sacrifice themselves. In Lakota theology, the self is the most precious thing one can give; therefore, the self is what must be given. This is not punishment or atonement for sin but a cosmic gift-exchange: pain given freely to Wakan Tanka in return for visions, blessings, healing for the community, or cosmic renewal.

## The Colonial Prohibition

The U.S. Office of Indian Affairs banned the Sun Dance in 1883 as part of the Indian Religious Crimes Code, citing "self-torture." The ban remained until 1934 (Indian Reorganization Act). The prohibition is a case study in colonial religious suppression targeting the ceremonial core of Lakota political-spiritual life. Canada banned related ceremonies simultaneously. The Sun Dance continued underground during the prohibition period.

## MASSIVE WIN — Body-as-Sacrifice Across Five Traditions

**The structural claim:** Five traditions, arising in complete independence, arrived at the same theological logic: the body's suffering, freely given, is the highest-value offering to the sacred. The suffering is not the point — it is the *medium* of the gift.

| Tradition | Practice | Mechanics | Theological logic |
|-----------|----------|-----------|-------------------|
| Lakota | Sun Dance | Chest piercing, skewers attached to pole; fasting; tearing free | Self as the offering; cosmic exchange; communal renewal |
| Shiite Islam | Ashura self-flagellation | Chains, blades, striking the chest; public mourning procession | Identification with Husayn's martyrdom; redemptive suffering through the body |
| Hindu / Tamil | Kavadi (Thaipusam) | Metal skewers piercing cheeks, tongue, skin; carrying kavadi frame miles to temple | Fulfillment of a vow to Murugan; the body's ordeal is the proof of devotion |
| Aztec / Mexica | Autosacrifice (→ [[ritual-aztec-autosacrifice]]) | Stingray spines, obsidian blades through tongue, ear, penis; blood collected on paper burned as offering | Blood is the cosmic currency; gods made humans from blood; humans owe blood back |
| Christian mysticism | Flagellant movements; stigmata | Voluntary self-flagellation (Opus Dei discipline, medieval Flagellants); stigmata as involuntary body-inscription | Imitation of Christ's passion; the body as site where the divine wound is replicated |

**What this means:** These practices are not aberrations or cultural curiosities — they are independent solutions to the same theological problem: *what does the sacred require from humans?* Each tradition answers: the body itself, given willingly, through pain. The independent convergence on this answer across five continents and five traditions suggests a deep structure in the logic of sacrifice.

**The Sun Dance's distinctiveness:** It is the most communally-embedded form. The pledger sacrifices for the entire community, not only for personal vision or devotion. The tearing-free is not defeat — it is release. The ceremony is joy as much as ordeal.
