## Fasting — Cross-Tradition Ascetic Discipline

Fasting (voluntary abstention from food, and typically from drink, speech, and sexual activity) is the single most cross-culturally universal ascetic practice in human religious history. It is documented in every major world religious tradition without exception, and in numerous smaller traditional and indigenous practices. The mechanism is consistent: voluntary denial of the body's primary need → heightened spiritual receptivity → proximity to the divine or transcendent. The rationale clusters into five recurring themes across traditions: **purification** (removing physical and spiritual toxins), **humiliation** (reducing ego and bodily claims), **solidarity** (with the suffering, the poor, the divine who suffered), **discipline** (strengthening will against desire), and **altered states** (prolonged fasting produces altered perception and, in extended forms, genuine visions).

### Islamic Ramadan: The World's Largest Collective Fast

The Islamic *Ramadan* fast is the fourth pillar of Islam and the largest single coordinated religious fast in human history — approximately 1.8 billion Muslims worldwide observe it annually. Ramadan is the ninth month of the Islamic lunar calendar, during which the first revelation of the Quran to the Prophet Muhammad in the Cave of Hira is commemorated. The Quranic command (2:183-185) is direct: "O you who have believed, decreed upon you is fasting as it was decreed upon those before you that you may become righteous" — the explicit acknowledgment that fasting was practiced by earlier communities (Jews, Christians) grounds Ramadan in the universal ascetic tradition.

The fast runs from pre-dawn (*fajr*) to sunset (*maghrib*) — complete abstention from food, drink (including water), smoking, and sexual relations during daylight hours. The evening *iftar* (breaking of the fast, traditionally with dates and water following the Prophet's practice) is a communal social event. The pre-dawn *suhoor* meal prepares for the day's abstention. The [[collective-effervescence]] of Ramadan — the shared experience of collective daily discipline, the communal *iftar* tables, the nightly *tarawih* prayers (20-36 additional prayer cycles performed after the obligatory night prayer throughout Ramadan) — produces social bonding of extraordinary intensity. The last ten nights of Ramadan include *Laylat al-Qadr* ("Night of Power," the anniversary of the first Quranic revelation — "better than a thousand months," Quran 97:3), observed with all-night vigil.

### Jewish Yom Kippur: The 25-Hour Total Fast

Yom Kippur ("Day of Atonement") is the holiest day of the Jewish calendar — the culmination of the Ten Days of Repentance (*Aseret Yemei Teshuva*) that begin with Rosh Hashanah (Jewish New Year). The biblical command is stark (Leviticus 16:29): "You shall afflict yourselves [*v'initem et nafshoteichem*]" — the rabbinic interpretation translates this as a 25-hour total fast (beginning before sunset, ending after nightfall the next day). The fast is complete: no food, no water, no bathing, no leather shoes (leather shoes are a luxury), no sexual relations, no cosmetics. The day is spent in continuous synagogue prayer across five services (the Kol Nidre service on the eve, then Shacharit, Musaf, Mincha, and the final Ne'ilah "closing of the gates" service at sunset). The fast concludes with the sounding of the *shofar* (ram's horn) — a single long blast.

The theological content of Yom Kippur fasting is *teshuva* (repentance/return) — the fast as the bodily enactment of the soul's turning back toward God. The body is denied its normal satisfactions to allow the spiritual dimension to predominate. The "Book of Life" (in which God inscribes the fate of each person for the coming year) is sealed at the end of Yom Kippur — the fast is the bodily argument that one deserves inscription in life.

### Christian Lent and Fasting: 40 Days as Sacred Number

Christian fasting has two primary forms: **Lent** (the 40-day preparatory fast before Easter, beginning on Ash Wednesday) and **daily/occasional fasting** as a general ascetic practice. The 40-day duration of Lent is typologically connected to: Moses's 40 days on Sinai (Exodus 34:28), Elijah's 40-day desert journey (1 Kings 19:8), and most directly Jesus's 40-day fast in the desert (Matthew 4:2, Luke 4:2) — which itself is a deliberate reenactment of Israel's 40 years in the desert. The number 40 (Hebrew *arba'im*) carries the meaning of a complete period of testing and transformation.

Lenten fasting was, in early Christianity, an absolute fast (bread, salt, and water only) from Monday through Friday before Easter Sunday — modeled on a pre-baptismal fast by catechumens (candidates for baptism). The fast's connection to [[ritual-baptism]] is direct: the Easter Vigil (the night before Easter Sunday) was the original mass-baptism occasion; the Lenten fast was preparation for initiation. Over centuries Lenten practice was significantly relaxed; current Catholic requirements are strict fasting (one small meal and two smaller collations) only on Ash Wednesday and Good Friday, with meat abstinence every Friday of Lent.

### The 40-Day Convergence: MASSIVE WIN

Perhaps the most striking cross-tradition convergence in the fasting complex:

- **Moses**: 40 days on Sinai (Exodus 34:28 — no bread, no water; receiving the Torah)
- **Elijah**: 40-day journey through the desert to Horeb (1 Kings 19:8 — sustained by a single angel-provided meal)
- **Jesus**: 40-day fast in the Judean desert (Matthew 4:1-11 — tempted three times)
- **The Buddha**: 49 days of meditation under the Bodhi Tree after enlightenment (rounded to 7 weeks = approximately 49 days; the period of post-enlightenment integration)
- **Muhammad**: The annual 30-day fast of Ramadan, climaxing in the Night of Power

Four or five major religious founders/prophets undergo extended periods of complete or near-complete food abstention at the pivotal moment of their spiritual mission. The number 40 appears in three of the Abrahamic cases specifically. Whether this is typological borrowing (Christian writers deliberately shaped Jesus's 40-day fast to echo Moses and Elijah), genuine independent convergence, or something more complex, the pattern is remarkable: the **40-day transformative fast** is the universal format for prophetic commissioning and spiritual breakthrough.

### Buddhist and Jain Fasting: The Ascetic Extreme

The [[tradition-buddhism]]'s relationship to fasting is complex: the Buddha's pre-enlightenment extreme asceticism (documented in Pali sources) included near-starvation fasting so extreme that he could feel his spine through his stomach wall. He abandoned this as one extreme, adopting the "Middle Way" — but Buddhist monastics retain the *vinaya* rule of not eating after noon, and the tradition of *Vassa* (rains retreat) includes additional austerities. The monk's bowl is presented empty each morning; the community's gift of food fills it — a daily material enactment of dana (generosity) and non-attachment.

Jain fasting represents the absolute extreme: the practice of *santhara* (or *sallekhana*) — the voluntary fast unto death undertaken by advanced Jain practitioners as the ultimate act of non-attachment and ahimsa (non-violence, because the dying monk consumes no resources). Approximately 200 Jain deaths annually are attributed to *santhara*. Its legality in India was challenged in 2015 (a Rajasthan High Court ruling declared it illegal) and overturned by the Supreme Court. The Jain *Paryushana* festival (8 days for the Shvetambara sect, 10 for the Digambara) includes collective fasting of varying intensity — the community's simultaneous fast is the year's supreme [[collective-effervescence]] event.

The universal logic: **the body's hunger is the strongest reminder of bodily identity**. By denying hunger, the practitioner asserts that identity is not bodily. Fasting is the simplest possible test of that assertion — and its universal adoption across unconnected traditions suggests it reliably produces the intended outcome.
