# Ardas — The Sikh Standing Prayer

The central congregational prayer of Sikhism. Recited at every gurdwara service, after every communal act, before every significant undertaking. The Ardas is inseparable from Sikh liturgical life — nothing begins or ends without it.

*Ardas* derives from the Persian *'ard dāsht*, meaning "petition" or "supplication addressed to a superior." It is the word spoken to a king while standing in his presence.

## Structure of the Prayer

The Ardas has three mandatory sections, recited in strict order:

**Section 1 — Invocation of the Gurus (fixed text):**
Opens with a verse from the Guru Granth Sahib invoking Pritham Bhagauti (the divine feminine power, sword-of-truth) and then memorializes each of the Ten Gurus by name and deed. This section is fixed — the same words, in the same order, every time. It is the spine of Sikh historical memory.

**Section 2 — Remembrance of the Martyrs (partially fixed):**
The prayer moves through the great persecutions: the Sikhs tortured and executed under Mughal rule; the martyrdom of Guru Arjan Dev (first Guru to be executed, 1606 CE); the martyrdom of Guru Tegh Bahadur (executed for protecting Hindu religious freedom, 1675 CE); the *Chota Ghallughara* and *Vadda Ghallughara* (massacres of 1746 and 1762); the destruction of the Golden Temple by Ahmad Shah Durrani. The martyrs are named. The tortures are described. The congregation receives the full cost of the tradition's existence as liturgical content.

**Section 3 — Petition (variable):**
A representative (usually the *granthi* or any initiated Sikh) voices petitions on behalf of the congregation and any specific needs. Prayers for the sick, the recently departed, the community's projects. Then the universal Sikh petition: *Raj Karega Khalsa* — the Khalsa shall rule — meaning the reign of justice and truth, not political dominion.

**The Closing:**
*Waheguru Ji Ka Khalsa, Waheguru Ji Ki Fateh* — the Khalsa belongs to the Wonderful Lord; Victory belongs to the Wonderful Lord. Spoken three times. The congregation rises from sitting and stands — or remains standing — throughout.

## The Posture: Standing Before a King

**The form of the Ardas is standing.** The congregation stands, hands folded (*anjali mudra*), eyes closed or directed downward. No kneeling. No prostration during the prayer itself. The posture is that of a soldier before a sovereign, a petitioner before a court, a citizen before a king.

This is theologically deliberate. In the Sikh framework, God is *Waheguru* — the Wonderful Lord, the King of Kings. To kneel before a human king while standing before God would invert the hierarchy. The Sikh stands before God in the same posture they maintain before any human authority: upright, not abased.

The prayer is recited aloud by a single representative who speaks *on behalf of the entire congregation* — not as a priest with exclusive access, but as a representative voice. The congregation listens and affirms. Anyone present — any initiated Sikh — may lead the Ardas.

## Cross-Tradition Connections: Posture IS Theology

**Cross-tradition finding:** The posture of standing in prayer is not universal — it is a specific theological statement, and different traditions have chosen different postures with full awareness of the theological implications:

| Tradition | Prayer | Posture | Theological claim |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sikhism** | Ardas | Standing, hands folded | You stand before the King of Kings |
| **Judaism** | [[ritual-mikveh-immersion]] / *Amidah* | Standing, feet together | Before God you stand as at Sinai |
| **Islam** | [[ritual-salat-five-prayers]] | Standing → bowing → prostration cycle | Complete submission enacted in body |
| **Christianity** | Liturgy of the Hours (standing for Gospel) | Standing for Gospel, kneeling for confession | The risen Christ is met standing |

The Amidah (*ha-Amida* = "the standing") is the closest parallel to the Ardas: both are structured petitionary prayers, both are recited standing, both have fixed and variable sections, both are the central prayer of their liturgical system. The Hebrew *Amidah* has 18-19 blessings covering praise, petition, and thanksgiving — the same three-part structure as the Ardas. The parallel is structural, not borrowing: both traditions independently concluded that the correct posture before God is the posture of a dignified supplicant, not a prostrated slave.

Islamic *salat* reaches the opposite conclusion with the same theological logic: if God is the All-Powerful, complete submission (the prostration, *sujud*, forehead on the ground) is the only adequate response. The two positions bracket the same question — what does it mean to stand before infinite power? — with opposite embodied answers.

## The Martyrology as Liturgical Memory

The second section of the Ardas is unique among major world religions' daily prayers: it is a *detailed martyrology recited at every service*. The names of those tortured and killed are spoken aloud. The methods of torture are described. The congregation re-inhabits the trauma of persecution not as historical memory but as present liturgical reality.

This has no parallel in Islamic *salat*, Christian Liturgy of the Hours, Jewish Amidah, or Hindu *puja*. The Ardas ensures that every Sikh, at every service, knows the price at which their tradition was maintained. The prayer is, in part, a communal vow not to waste what was paid for.
