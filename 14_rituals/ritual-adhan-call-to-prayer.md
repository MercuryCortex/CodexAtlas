## Adhan — The Call to Prayer and the Founding Politics of the Sacred Voice

The *adhan* (Arabic: أَذَان, "announcement," from *adhina*, "to permit, to hear") is the Islamic call to prayer, delivered five times daily from the minaret of the mosque by the *mu'adhdhin* (caller, anglicized as "muezzin"). The adhan is not prayer — it is the summons to prayer. The Islamic legal tradition is explicit: the adhan must be delivered in the human voice, without instrumental accompaniment. No bell, no horn, no drum — the unmediated human voice calling across the city rooftops.

### The Text and the Claim

The adhan consists of a sequence of liturgical phrases delivered in a specific order:
1. *Allahu Akbar* (God is greater) — 4 times
2. *Ashhadu an la ilaha illa Allah* (I bear witness there is no god but God) — 2 times
3. *Ashhadu anna Muhammadan rasul Allah* (I bear witness Muhammad is God's messenger) — 2 times
4. *Hayya 'ala al-salah* (Come to prayer) — 2 times
5. *Hayya 'ala al-falah* (Come to success/flourishing) — 2 times
6. [At Fajr only:] *Al-salatu khayrun min al-nawm* (Prayer is better than sleep) — 2 times
7. *Allahu Akbar* — 2 times
8. *La ilaha illa Allah* — once

The phrase *Allahu Akbar* (literally "God is greater/greatest") is theologically precise: not "God is great" but "God is greater" — the comparative, not the superlative. Greater than what you are doing. Greater than your sleep, your work, your immediate concern. The grammar performs the adhan's entire function: interrupting ordinary time to assert divine priority.

The [[music-islamic-adhan-maqam]] documents the musical structure: the adhan is performed in specific maqam (modal) scales, and skilled mu'adhdhins are selected for their ability to move audiences to tears. The adhan is simultaneously a legal act (establishing the prayer time) and a musical-aesthetic event. The tension between the Islamic legal tradition's ambivalence about music and the intensely musical character of the adhan is one of Islamic civilization's productive contradictions.

### The Founding Political Choice: Bilal ibn Rabah

The origin narrative of the adhan is among the most politically significant founding stories in the history of religion. Muhammad, seeking a method to summon worshippers, consulted his companions. Various proposals were debated: a horn (like the Jewish shofar), a bell (like Christian churches), a fire signal. All were rejected — because they were markers of *other* religious communities. The distinctive Islamic call would be the human voice.

Muhammad then appointed **Bilal ibn Rabah** as Islam's first mu'adhdhin — the person whose voice would represent the new religion's most public act, five times a day, to the entire community. Bilal was a formerly enslaved Ethiopian man, dark-skinned, non-Arab, whose African origin was the basis of his enslavement. He was freed by Abu Bakr after being tortured by his enslaver for accepting Islam.

The choice of Bilal as first mu'adhdhin is a deliberately encoded theological statement at the religion's founding moment: the voice of Islam — its most public, most repeated, most recognizable sound — was given to a Black African man from the most marginalized position in Arabian society. The theological claim of Islamic equality before God (*ummah* — the community of believers where no Arab has priority over a non-Arab, no white over a black, except in piety) was enacted, not merely stated, in this appointment.

This is one of the clearest cases in religious history of a founding ritual choice carrying political theology: the sound of the religion teaches the religion's social claim. Every *adhan* heard across the world carries Bilal's voice's structural message.

### The MASSIVE WIN: Every Tradition Chose a Different Sonic Signature

Every major religious tradition that practices communal worship developed a method of summoning the community — and each chose a different sonic instrument. The choice reveals the tradition's theology of sacred sound:

**Jewish Shofar**: The ram's horn blown on Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur. The shofar is the sound of raw, pre-musical nature — an animal horn, not a crafted instrument. Its sound is inarticulate, non-linguistic: it announces without saying. The theological claim: the divine summons is pre-linguistic, older than human speech, rooted in the animal world from which Abraham's substitute ram came. The shofar recalls the Akedah (binding of Isaac): the ram caught in the thicket whose horn became the instrument of divine summons.

**Christian Church Bell**: The cast bronze bell hung in the church tower — a crafted, metallic, resonant sound that carries across distances without linguistic content. Bell-ringing developed into campanology (complex rhythm systems), with different peals for different liturgical occasions. The theological claim (implicit): the sacred summons can be entirely instrumental — words are unnecessary when the community already knows the schedule. The bell presupposes a Christian society; the adhan explains itself.

**Hindu Conch Shell (Shankha)**: The *shankha* blown before puja at home shrines and temple worship is a natural object (a gastropod shell) transformed into a ritual instrument. The resonant drone it produces is associated with the primordial sound *OM* — the first sound of creation. Blowing the conch shell at worship recalls the creation moment. The Mahabharata describes the divine conch shells blown by Krishna, Arjuna, and the Pandavas as having individual names and powers.

**Buddhist Temple Gong/Bell**: Buddhist monasteries use a wooden fish (*mokugyo*), bronze bell (*bonshō*), and gong at different times of day to mark the monastic schedule. The sounds mark time within the monastery; they are not designed for citywide broadcast. The Buddhist sonic summons is inward-pointing (calling a monastery community) rather than citywide (calling a civic community). This structural difference reflects the Buddhist organization as monastic rather than congregational.

**Islamic Adhan — the Human Voice**: The choice of the unmediated human voice is the most theologically loaded of all. It requires a human being to stand in a public position and make a series of truth claims five times daily — personal testimony (*shahada*: "I bear witness"), not just a sound. The mu'adhdhin is a witness, not an instrument. The adhan is simultaneously a theological statement and a personal testimony.

The comparison reveals a spectrum: from natural object (shofar, conch) → crafted instrument (bell, gong) → human voice (adhan). Islam placed the sacred summons at the most personal, most explicitly theological end of this spectrum: not a sound that announces, but a human being who testifies.
