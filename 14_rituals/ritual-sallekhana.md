# Sallekhana — Voluntary Fasting unto Death

The Jain ritual of voluntary, gradual withdrawal from food and water, undertaken as a conscious approach to death. Also called *Santhara* in the Digambara tradition. Not suicide — the practitioner does not actively end their life, but withdraws from the conditions that sustain it, greeting death without attachment, fear, or violence.

Mahavira himself is said to have died this way, as did many of the early Jain saints. The practice is considered the highest form of *ahimsa* (non-violence) applied to oneself: dying without disturbing even the organisms in one's own digestive system.

## Conditions and Structure

**Prerequisites — this is not a casual undertaking:**
- Must be undertaken with full consent of the practitioner (cannot be imposed)
- Requires sanction from the community and spiritual teacher
- Is only considered appropriate when the body can no longer fulfill its dharmic duties — terminal illness, extreme old age, or famine conditions where one's continued life comes at significant cost to others
- Is explicitly prohibited for those with dependents who cannot care for themselves

**The Process:**
1. Public declaration before community and guru: "I take this vow of *sallekhana*."
2. Gradual reduction of solid food over weeks or months.
3. Reduction to liquids only.
4. Reduction to water only.
5. Final withdrawal from water.
6. Throughout: meditation, recitation of the *Namokar Mantra* (the core Jain prayer), and reception of visits from community and family.

**The Spiritual State:** The practitioner is expected to maintain equanimity — *samata* — throughout. No grief, no regret, no attachment to continued existence. The goal is to approach the moment of death with the same peace one would bring to meditation. Anger, fear, or attachment at death moment generates powerful karma; equanimity generates minimal karma, easing the soul toward liberation.

## Theological Core

Jain cosmology holds that the soul (*jiva*) accumulates karma through every action, thought, and intention. Even eating causes karma, because eating involves the destruction of life-forms. Death by sallekhana is the act of reducing karma-accumulation to its absolute minimum at the moment of greatest karmic risk (the death moment).

The Jain distinction from suicide: *suicide* is an act of passion (violence against oneself driven by despair, anger, or attachment). Sallekhana is an act of *non-passion* — the systematic withdrawal of the conditions for continued existence, undertaken with full consciousness and complete equanimity. The body is not destroyed; it is abandoned.

The theological logic is consistent with Jain ontology: the body is not the self. The *jiva* (soul) is bound to the body by karma. Sallekhana is the deliberate attempt to ensure that the karmic binding at the moment of death is as thin as possible, so the soul may move more freely toward liberation (*moksha*).

## Cross-Tradition Connections: Sanctioned Voluntary Death

**MASSIVE WIN:** Four traditions independently legitimize voluntary chosen death under specific spiritual conditions as the highest form of release — each with completely different theological reasoning and zero documented borrowing:

| Tradition | Practice | Theological framing | Conditions |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Jainism** | Sallekhana / Santhara | Minimum karma at death = easier liberation | Terminal illness, old age, or inability to fulfill dharmic duties |
| **Catharism** | *Endura* | After *consolamentum*, the pure soul must not re-contaminate | After receiving the Cathar baptism-of-spirit (*consolamentum*) |
| **Buddhism** | *Parinirvana* (chosen death) | The enlightened being who has exhausted all karma may choose to exit | Only for one who has achieved *nirvana* |
| **Stoicism** | Philosophical suicide (*Cato*, *Seneca*) | Death by one's own choice when continued life becomes incompatible with virtue | When political/personal conditions make virtuous life impossible |

The structural identity: all four traditions define a class of voluntary death that is *not* suicide in the negative sense — it is a spiritually sanctioned exit under conditions where continued life would compromise the highest values of that tradition. All four require a specific spiritual state as precondition. All four frame the act as liberation rather than destruction.

The distinctiveness of sallekhana: it is the only one that makes *non-violence* (ahimsa) its central justification — dying slowly so as to disturb as few organisms as possible, including one's own gut bacteria. The Stoic dies by the sword; the Cathar starves after consolamentum; the Buddhist masters death consciously. The Jain starves slowly out of *care for all beings*.

## Historical Note: Legal Controversy

In 2015, the Rajasthan High Court issued an order banning sallekhana as suicide under Indian Penal Code Section 306. The Jain community challenged the ruling at the Supreme Court of India, which stayed the High Court order pending review. The case surfaced the fundamental jurisprudential question: can a tradition's sanctioned approach to death be legally classified the same as involuntary or passion-driven suicide?

The controversy produced one of the most sophisticated modern legal debates about religious practice and state authority over the body.
