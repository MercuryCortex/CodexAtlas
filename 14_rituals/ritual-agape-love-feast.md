## Agape — The Early Christian Love Feast and Its Pre-Christian Roots

The *Agape* (Greek: ἀγάπη, "love," "charity") was an early Christian communal meal distinct from the Eucharist, though in the earliest period the two were intertwined. The *Agape* was a full communal meal eaten together by the Christian community — rich and poor together, sharing food, sharing resources, expressing the "love feast" dimension of community life. By the 2nd–3rd century, the *Agape* and the Eucharist had been definitively separated: the Eucharist became a short, formalized sacramental rite (bread and wine only) performed in the morning, while the *Agape* remained a fuller evening meal. By the 4th–5th century, the *Agape* had effectively disappeared from mainstream Christianity — the Council of Carthage (397 CE) banned *Agapai* from churches — though it survived in some Eastern Christian communities.

### The Earliest Evidence

**1 Corinthians 11:17-34** is the primary evidence for the early *Agape/Eucharist* complex. Paul's complaint reveals that the Corinthian community's communal meal was *socially stratified* — the wealthy ate their own food and got drunk before the poor arrived from their work; the poor had nothing. Paul calls this a scandal that "proclaims the Lord's death" (i.e., contradicts the eucharistic theology of Christ's self-giving). This text shows the *Agape* and Eucharist were still unified at Corinth c. 55 CE, and that the community meal was socially mixed in theory but violated in practice.

**Jude 12** refers to *agapai* (plural) as a normal feature of community life in which false teachers are a "blemish" — confirming the practice was widespread.

**[[phase-4-061-didache]]** (c. 50–120 CE) provides instructions for the *Eucharist* without apparently distinguishing it clearly from the communal meal; it gives prayers to say at the meal and instructs that only the baptized may eat.

**[[phase-4-063-ignatius-letters]]** (c. 110 CE) uses *agape* as a term for the Christian communal gathering, under the bishop's authority.

## MASSIVE WIN: The Agape as Missing Link — Essene → Christian Liturgy

### Essene Communal Meals at Qumran

The Dead Sea Scrolls ([[phase-3-011-dead-sea-scrolls]]) document the Qumran community's communal meal practices in extraordinary detail. The Community Rule (1QS 6:2-5) describes: all members eating together, the priest blessing the bread and wine *first* (before any other member), the meal eaten in purity, the exclusion of any member under discipline. The messianic texts from Qumran (1QSa 2:11-22) describe an eschatological messianic banquet in which the Messiah of Israel blesses the bread and wine — an anticipation of the messianic meal at the End of Days that the Qumran community may have been ritually *enacting* at every communal meal.

The structural identity is striking:
- Priest/Messiah blesses bread and wine → Jesus blesses bread and wine at the Last Supper
- Pure community gathered together → Early church *Agape* as pure community
- Exclusion of the unworthy → Paul's warning against eating "unworthily" (1 Cor 11:27)
- Eschatological anticipation → "Until he comes" (1 Cor 11:26)

The Essene communal meal is the most parsimonious explanation for the ritual structure of the Last Supper. John the Baptist's connection to Qumran (geographic proximity, ritual immersion, eschatological preaching) has been argued since the Dead Sea Scrolls discovery; the meal tradition may represent a parallel connection. [[tradition-essenes]].

### Pythagorean Communal Meals

The [[tradition-pythagorean]] communities of Magna Graecia (southern Italy, c. 530–400 BCE) practiced communal meals as a central ritual: Pythagoras required his followers to eat together, maintaining dietary restrictions (the famous abstention from beans, likely a ritual purity requirement rather than mere food preference), and organizing the meal as a philosophical and ritual event. Iamblichus (*Life of Pythagoras*, c. 300 CE) describes the Pythagorean meals as structured around the same principles as the *Agape*: equality in sharing, communal purity, the meal as sacred act. The Pythagorean communities were themselves likely influenced by Orphic communal practices ([[tradition-orphic]]).

### Mithraic Sacred Meal

The Mithraic mystery cult (documented from c. 100–400 CE across the Roman Empire) included a *communal sacred meal* of bread and wine in the underground *mithraea*. Christian apologist Justin Martyr (c. 155 CE) noted this directly: *"The wicked demons have imitated [the Eucharist] in the mysteries of Mithras, commanding the same thing to be done."* Mithraic reliefs show the reclining sacred meal of Mithras and Sol (*Mithras and Sol Invictus* banquet), followed by the assembled initiates sharing bread and wine. [[tradition-mithraic-mysteries]].

Justin's claim that Mithraic practice was a *demonic imitation* of the Christian Eucharist is chronologically impossible (Mithraism predates Christianity in the region), but confirms that 2nd-century Christians recognized the structural identity.

### The Convergence

The pre-Christian sacred communal meal tradition — Pythagorean (c. 530 BCE), Essene (c. 150 BCE–68 CE), Mithraic (c. 1st century BCE onward) — all share: a structured communal meal in a separated space, a blessing over bread and wine by an authorized officiant, purity requirements for participants, the meal as enactment of a sacred narrative, and eschatological/cosmic significance. The Christian *Agape* and Eucharist emerge from this pre-existing complex, with the specific Christological identification (the bread and wine = Christ's body and blood) as the distinctive Christian addition.

The *Agape*'s disappearance by the 4th century is itself revealing: as Christianity became the imperial religion ([[event-edict-of-milan-313]]), the radical social equality of the love feast became practically and politically difficult. The Eucharist — formalized, brief, hieratically controlled — survived. The communal meal in which rich and poor ate together — the dangerous, egalitarian practice — was suppressed.
