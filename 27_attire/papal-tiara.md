# Papal Tiara

## Origin and context

The **papal tiara** is the high-conical jeweled crown worn by the Pope from approximately the 8th century until **Paul VI's coronation in 1963** — the last papal coronation. The tiara's distinctive **three superimposed crowns** (whence *triregnum*) make it iconographically unmistakable. It is, with the **Pharaonic pschent double-crown** ([[pharaonic-nemes-and-uraeus]]) and the **Byzantine imperial *stemma***, one of the three most-iconographically-loaded religious-political crowns in world history.

**Origin from the *camelaucum*.** The proto-tiara is the *camelaucum* — a tall conical white cap worn by Byzantine court officials and adopted by Rome's bishops in the early medieval period. The *Liber Pontificalis* records popes wearing a tall white cap from the late 7th c. (Constantine I, r. 708-715, is depicted in something resembling the proto-tiara). By the time of the **Donation of Constantine** forgery (8th c., probably composed under Stephen II or Paul I, c. 750-770), the white conical cap had been theologized: the document claims that Constantine gave Pope Sylvester I "the diadem, that is, the crown of our head, and at the same time the *phrygium* and also the shoulder-band [pallium]" — making the papal cap a Constantinian-imperial inheritance.

**Addition of the crowns.**
- **First crown** added by **Pope Nicholas II** (~1059) — a single circlet at the base, marking the pope's secular-temporal authority.
- **Second crown** added by **Pope Boniface VIII** (~1296-1303), the architect of the medieval theory of papal supremacy. Boniface's bull *Unam Sanctam* (1302) is the high-water mark of the doctrine that "every human creature should be subject to the Roman Pontiff." The second crown materializes the claim.
- **Third crown** added by **Pope Benedict XII** (~1342) or **Pope Urban V** (~1362) — sources disagree. The standard interpretation by the time of the Council of Constance (1414-1418) is that the three crowns signify the pope's threefold authority: **priest, king, and prophet** (the Christological *munus triplex* applied to the papal office) — OR **Church Militant, Church Suffering, Church Triumphant** — OR **Father of Princes and Kings, Ruler of the World, Vicar of Christ** (the inscription used in the coronation formula by the time of Innocent X, 1644).

**Coronation formula.** The papal coronation ritual (used from c. 858 to 1963) culminated with the Cardinal Deacon placing the tiara on the new pope's head with the words:

> "*Accipe tiaram tribus coronis ornatam, et scias te esse Patrem Principum et Regum, Rectorem Orbis, in terra Vicarium Salvatoris nostri Jesu Christi, cui est honor et gloria in saecula saeculorum.*"
> ("Receive the tiara adorned with three crowns, and know that you are the Father of Princes and Kings, Ruler of the World, on earth the Vicar of our Savior Jesus Christ, to whom is honor and glory unto the ages of ages.")

This is one of the most extravagant religious-political claims ever placed in a coronation formula.

## Tradition + use

**Coronation.** Every pope from approximately the 9th century to **Paul VI in 1963** was crowned with the tiara at his accession. The ritual took place in the basilica of St. Peter's (or the Lateran in earlier periods), and the new pope subsequently processed through Rome wearing the tiara — the *possesso* procession.

**Other occasions.** The tiara was worn for solemn processions (the carrying of the pope on the *sedia gestatoria*), for *ex cathedra* solemn definitions (notably the 1854 definition of the Immaculate Conception and the 1950 definition of the Assumption — Pius IX and Pius XII respectively wore the tiara for these pronouncements), and on certain high feasts. It was NOT worn during the Mass itself — for the liturgy, the pope wore the mitre, the standard episcopal headdress.

**Paul VI's renunciation, 1963-1964.** Pope **Paul VI** (Giovanni Battista Montini) was crowned with the tiara at his coronation on **30 June 1963** — the last papal coronation. Within months, in a deliberate ascetic-conciliar gesture coinciding with the Second Vatican Council's emphasis on the church-as-pilgrim-people, Paul VI **descended the steps of St. Peter's altar and laid the tiara upon it as a gift to the poor**. The tiara was subsequently sold (purchased by the American Catholics for $1 million, equivalent to roughly $10 million today, and the proceeds donated to charity); it is now held by the Basilica of the Shrine of the Immaculate Conception in Washington DC.

**Subsequent popes did not resume the tiara.** John Paul I (1978), John Paul II (1978-2005), Benedict XVI (2005-2013), and Francis (2013-) all had **inaugurations** rather than **coronations** — no crown, no enthronement formula, just the bestowal of the pallium and the Fisherman's Ring. Benedict XVI removed the tiara from his papal coat of arms (replacing it with a mitre) — the first pope since the 9th c. without a tiara on his arms. Francis retained the tiara in his arms but has not worn one. The tiara as a worn object is, since 1963, effectively retired.

## Material + form

**Form**: a tall conical or beehive-shaped headdress (variations by period — Boniface VIII's tiara was relatively short and broad; the Renaissance and Baroque tiaras taller and more elaborate). Three superimposed circlets / crowns set with jewels. At the apex a small cross. Two pendant *infulae* (silk ribbons) hanging down the back, embroidered with gold thread, often bearing the papal coat of arms — these are inherited from the **Roman *infulae*** (the priestly headbands of pagan Roman priests, *flamines*) and represent a continuous iconographic survival from pre-Christian Roman religion.

**Material**: silk core (often with cloth-of-gold), gold/silver gilt mounts, with jewels (rubies, emeralds, pearls, sapphires) set in the three crowns and the cross. Many tiaras were gifts from monarchs or wealthy patrons — Napoleon I gifted a tiara to Pius VII in 1804 (deliberately heavy and uncomfortable, in a famous diplomatic insult — the tiara was so heavy the pope could barely stand; modern museum analysis confirms ~8 kg).

**Surviving tiaras.** Several Renaissance and post-Renaissance tiaras survive — the most famous in the Vatican's *Tesoro di San Pietro*. The 1854 tiara of Pius IX (lavishly bejeweled), the Napoleon I-Pius VII tiara, and the 1877 tiara of the Belgian Catholics (gifted to Leo XIII) are among the most-documented surviving examples.

## Cross-tradition parallels

**Cross-tradition finding: Divine kingship — Pharaonic pschent → papal triregnum lineage.** The papal tiara is the principal Christian instance of the **divine-king triple-or-double crown** typology that extends back through:

- **Pharaonic pschent** ([[pharaonic-nemes-and-uraeus]]) — the double-crown of Upper-and-Lower Egypt
- **Hellenistic kingly diadems** (Ptolemaic, Seleucid)
- **Roman imperial diadem** (Constantine onward)
- **Byzantine *stemma*** (the closed imperial crown with pendilia)
- **Sasanian crown** (with prominent ribbons / *taenia*)

The papal tiara's pendant *infulae* directly inherit from Roman priestly headbands. The three-crowns layering is more elaborate than any of the antecedents but sits within the same family — religious-political claim materialized in stacked headgear. The high-conical form, exceptionally, parallels the **Egyptian *hedjet*** (the white crown of Upper Egypt) and the **Bishop's mitre** (which is also a high-conical headdress; the mitre and the tiara descend from a common late-Roman *camelaucum* root). Robert Grimm's *The Papal Tiara: Its Origins and Symbolism* (2005) and the older Joseph Braun, *Die liturgische Gewandung* (1907) trace the lineage.

**Initiatory clothing ceremony — coronation as crowning installment.** The papal coronation is the Christian-papal instance of the cross-tradition pattern: senior ecclesiastical figure places sacred garment / crown on candidate, recites formula of new identity, candidate is incorporated into highest-level lineage. The structural parallel to **Sufi khirqah** ([[sufi-khirqah]]), **Buddhist higher ordination** ([[kasaya-buddhist-robe]]), and **Hindu sannyāsa-dīkṣā** ([[sannyasin-saffron]]) holds — but the papal coronation is unusual in being a one-time elevation rather than an ordinary professional initiation, and in claiming political-temporal as well as spiritual authority. **Byzantine emperor coronations** (in Hagia Sophia, by the Patriarch of Constantinople, with the imperial *stemma*) are the structurally closest cross-tradition parallel — and the papal coronation is partly a Western-Latin response to and reframing of the Eastern imperial coronation.

**The 1963 renunciation as theological signal.** Paul VI's laying-aside of the tiara is a rare and historically significant moment of a religious tradition **publicly retiring an institutional sacred garment**. The closest parallel is **the dissolution of the Japanese imperial regalia's public-religious function in 1946** (Hirohito's renunciation of divine status) and **the laying-aside of the papal *sedia gestatoria*** (the portable papal throne, also retired by John Paul II in the late 1970s). The 1963 gesture explicitly signaled the Catholic Church's repositioning from divinely-mandated political sovereign (the Boniface VIII / *Unam Sanctam* claim) to pilgrim-church-among-the-nations (the Vatican II vision). The tiara is therefore a uniquely well-documented case of a religious garment carrying explicit political-theological meaning into the modern period, then being publicly retired when that meaning was repudiated.
