# Barefoot / Removing Shoes (Cross-Tradition)

Not a garment but the **removal of one** — the recurring rule that the foot must be *bare* before the sacred. Taking off the shoes marks a threshold: outside is the profane world, inside is holy ground.

## The threshold

- **"Put off thy shoes."** At the burning bush God tells Moses, *"put off thy shoes from off thy feet, for the place whereon thou standest is holy ground"* (Exodus 3:5) — the founding text of the practice in the biblical world; priests served barefoot in the Temple.
- **Islam.** Shoes are removed before entering the **mosque** (with ablution, *wuḍūʾ*) — the prayer-space is kept ritually clean for prostration.
- **India.** Shoes are left at the door of every **temple, gurdwara, and Jain derasar** ([[tradition-hinduism]], [[tradition-sikhism]]); barefoot circumambulation is an act of humility.
- **Japan.** Shinto (and the domestic threshold) removes outdoor footwear to keep purity (*kegare* excluded) — a whole architecture of the *genkan*.

## Cross-tradition finding

The shoe is what stands between the body and the ground — dust, the road, the outside world. Baring the foot is a **purity-threshold gesture**: the worshipper arrives *stripped of the road*, in direct, humble contact with holy ground. It is one of the most consistent cross-tradition ritual acts, converging on the same logic of separation between sacred and profane space.
