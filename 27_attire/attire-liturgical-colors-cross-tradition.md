# Liturgical Colours (Cross-Tradition)

Colour as a **coded liturgical language** — where the hue of the garment tells you the season, the mood, or the renunciant's status before a word is spoken.

## The systems

- **The Christian colour cycle.** Western liturgy assigns colours to the calendar: **white/gold** (joy, Easter, Christmas), **red** (martyrs, Pentecost, the Spirit's fire), **green** (Ordinary Time), **violet** (penitence, Advent, Lent), **black/violet** (the dead). Codified in the medieval West (Innocent III described a scheme c. 1200) and worn on the **chasuble** ([[vestment-chasuble]]) and stole. Byzantine practice ([[tradition-eastern-orthodoxy]]) uses a looser bright/dark (feast/fast) contrast.
- **The saffron robe.** For Theravāda monastics the **ochre/saffron** dye of the **kāṣāya** ([[kasaya-buddhist-robe]]) began as the cheapest, humblest colour (earth, spice, jackfruit-heartwood) — poverty made into an identity; Tibetan monastics wear **maroon and saffron**.
- **Hindu saffron.** The sannyāsin's **saffron/ochre** ([[sannyasin-saffron]]) is the colour of **fire** — the world and the self burned away in renunciation.

## Cross-tradition finding

Two opposite logics converge on colour-as-signal. The Christian system is **temporal** — colour marks *when* you are in sacred time. The monastic saffron is **existential** — colour marks *what* you have renounced. Both make the garment legible at a glance: one reads the calendar off the priest, the other reads renunciation off the monk.
