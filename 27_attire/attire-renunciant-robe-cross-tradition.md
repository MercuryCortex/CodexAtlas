# The Renunciant's Robe (Cross-Tradition)

The garment of those who have **left the world** — the single most convergent religious dress-type on earth. Across traditions with no common origin, the renunciant wears a plain, poor, uniform robe that *erases the individual* into the order.

## The robes

- **Buddhist kāṣāya.** Sewn from strips (originally rag-cloth from cremation grounds and dust-heaps — *paṃsukūla*), dyed the humble ochre; the [[kasaya-buddhist-robe]] is poverty patterned into a rice-field of cloth.
- **Hindu saffron.** The sannyāsin's fire-coloured cloth ([[sannyasin-saffron]]) — the world burned away; often a single seamless wrap.
- **The Christian habit.** The [[monastic-habit-benedictine]] and its many order-variants — a uniform of stability, obedience, and the death of self-will; taking the habit *is* entering the order.
- **The Sufi khirqah.** The patched wool robe ([[sufi-khirqah]]) — poverty (*faqr*) worn as the sign of the lineage, conferred at initiation.
- **Jain.** Śvetāmbara white or Digambara nudity ([[attire-jain-monastic-svetambara-digambara]]) — renunciation taken to the limit of possession.

## Cross-tradition finding

The renunciant robe is defined by **subtraction**: rag-cloth, single colour, no ornament, no individuality — the opposite of [[attire-crown-regalia-cross-tradition]]. Where regalia concentrates status, the habit dissolves it. That the same solution — a poor uniform that unmakes the person — arose independently in Buddhist, Hindu, Christian, Jain, and Sufi monasticism is one of the strongest cases of **convergent religious practice** in the vault.
