# The White Garment (Cross-Tradition)

Across unrelated traditions, **white cloth** carries the same cluster of meanings — **purity, equality, death-and-rebirth** — because it reads as the absence of dye, rank, and stain.

## The garments

- **Christian baptismal white.** The newly baptized are clothed in a **white robe** ("Put on the white robe…") — purity and the new self; the priestly **alb** (from *albus*, "white") descends from it. See [[vestment-chasuble]].
- **Islamic iḥrām.** The hajj pilgrim wears **two unsewn white cloths** ([[ihram-pilgrim-garment]]) — erasing rank and wealth so that all stand identical before God; the same cloths become the shroud.
- **Jain Śvetāmbara.** One of the two great Jain orders is literally "**white-clad**" ([[attire-jain-monastic-svetambara-digambara]]) — plain white as the minimum of possession (against the Digambara "sky-clad" nudity).
- **Jewish kittel.** A plain **white robe** worn at Yom Kippur, by grooms, and as a burial shroud — atonement, humility, and mortality in one garment.
- **Shinto.** White (and undyed) cloth marks ritual purity for priests and participants.

## Cross-tradition finding

White is the colour a garment becomes when you subtract everything — dye, decoration, status. That is why the same cloth serves **initiation** (baptism), **equality** (iḥrām), **atonement** (kittel), and **death** (shroud): each is a stripping-away. Note the divergence — white is the funeral colour of much of Asia and of the Jewish shroud, where the modern West mourns in black (the mourning-dress hub).
