# Khirqah

## Origin and context

The **khirqah** is the cloak of initiation in Sufism — the principal physical sign of having been formally received into a Sufi master's spiritual lineage (*silsila*). The word *khirqa* literally means "a torn-off piece of cloth" or "rag," reflecting the original ascetic-mendicant context of Sufi clothing as deliberately patched and humble.

The word *ṣūfī* itself derives, by the most-widely-accepted etymology, from *ṣūf* (wool) — the early Iraqi and Khorasanian ascetics of the 8th-9th centuries CE wore coarse undyed wool in deliberate imitation of the prophets (the Quran describes Moses, John the Baptist, and Jesus in coarse wool) and in deliberate contrast to the silk-and-gold court culture of the Abbasid caliphate. Al-Hujwiri's *Kashf al-Mahjub* (c. 1075) makes the etymology explicit: "they are called Sufis because they wear wool (*ṣūf*)." The garment IS the name of the tradition.

By the 10th-11th centuries the khirqah-investiture ceremony has crystallized into a formal initiatory rite. Al-Sulami (*Tabaqat al-Sufiyya*, c. 1000) and Abu Nuʿaym al-Isfahani (*Hilyat al-Awliyaʾ*, early 11th c.) document master-disciple khirqah transmission as already standard. By al-Suhrawardi's *ʿAwarif al-Maʿarif* (c. 1230), the khirqah-investiture has a developed theology with two formal forms:

- **Khirqah-i irādat** — the "cloak of discipleship," given to the formally-initiated *murid* (disciple) by a Sufi master, marking the disciple's full incorporation into the master's *silsila*.
- **Khirqah-i tabarruk** — the "cloak of blessing," given to a visitor, an admirer, or a non-disciple as a transmission of *baraka* (blessing) without formal discipleship.

## Tradition + use

**Investiture.** The *bayʿah* (oath of allegiance) ceremony is the high moment of Sufi initiation. The candidate places his hand in the master's hand; the master recites Quran 48:10 ("*innaʾlladhīna yubāyiʿūnaka innamā yubāyiʿūnaʾllāh*" — "Indeed, those who pledge allegiance to you pledge allegiance to God"); the candidate recites the *shahada* and the formula of repentance; the master then **places the khirqah on the candidate's shoulders**, often reciting verses or making invocations specific to the *tariqa* (the Mevlevi formula differs from the Qadiri, which differs from the Chishti). The candidate is now incorporated into the *silsila* — the chain of khirqah-transmission running, in classical Sufi doctrine, master to master back to the Prophet Muhammad through ʿAli ibn Abi Talib (or through Abu Bakr in the Naqshbandi line). The khirqah is the **physical token of an unbroken spiritual genealogy**.

**Multiple khirqahs.** A Sufi may hold khirqahs from multiple masters and orders — Ibn Arabi (1165-1240) records receiving khirqahs from numerous masters in his *Ruh al-Quds* and *Durrat al-Fakhirah* (and in his *Nasab al-Khirqa*, a short treatise dedicated specifically to khirqah-genealogies). The accumulation of khirqahs marks the breadth of a Sufi's affiliations.

**Use in practice.** Beyond the investiture moment, the khirqah is worn during formal *dhikr* (remembrance) ceremonies, *sama* (audition / whirling), and in some lineages at all times by the fully-initiated. The Mevlevi *sikke* (tall conical felt hat) + *tennure* (long white skirt) + *hirka* (the black cloak removed during whirling, symbolizing the soul's release from the tomb of materiality) is one of the most-elaborated khirqah-derivative costumes in any Sufi tradition.

**The patched form (*muraqqaʿa*).** The most ascetically rigorous Sufis wore a deliberately-patched cloak — every patch visible, multiple colors, the cloak signaling "I have refused new clothing, I wear what was discarded." Schimmel notes (1975) that the *muraqqaʿa* is the Sufi equivalent of the **Buddhist *paṃsukūla*** ("rag-robe" from charnel-ground discards) and **the Cistercian undyed wool** — three independent traditions converging on patched/coarse/discarded cloth as the proper garment of one who has died to the world.

## Material + form

**Material**: traditionally coarse undyed wool (the *ṣūf* of *ṣūfī*). Color varies by order: **Qadiriyya** often wear white or green (green being the color of Khidr and of the Prophet's *ahl al-bayt*); **Naqshbandiyya** often white; **Mevlevi** the famous combination of white *tennure* + black *hirka*; **Chishti** typically undyed cream or white; **Bektashi** white with specific embroidered insignia; some North African Shadhili lineages favor green. Color and form together encode the order — a Sufi's affiliation is readable from his cloak.

**Form**: a long sleeved or sleeveless cloak, typically falling to the ankles, often hooded. The exact cut varies by region and order. The *muraqqaʿa* variant is conspicuously patched. The Mevlevi *hirka* is a black cloak specifically structured so it can be ceremonially shed during the *sama*.

## Cross-tradition parallels

**MASSIVE-WIN: Initiatory clothing ceremony.** The khirqah investiture is the Sufi instance of the most-densely-convergent cross-tradition ritual pattern in religious-clothing studies. Compare:

- **Christian monastic profession** ([[monastic-habit-benedictine]]) — abbot strips candidate of secular clothing, clothes him in the black habit, recites profession formula
- **Buddhist *upasampada*** higher ordination — preceptor places the *kasaya* on the candidate, who has shaved his head and renounced household life
- **Christian baptism** — white garment placed on the newly-baptized (early-Christian "albs" worn for eight days post-baptism)
- **Hindu sannyāsin** dīkṣā — guru gives the saffron robe + sacred thread + new monastic name
- **Catholic ordination** — bishop vests the new priest with chasuble + stole, "yoke of Christ" formula

In every case: (1) an authoritative senior strips or supplements the candidate's clothing, (2) places a tradition-specific named garment on them, (3) recites a sworn formula, (4) the candidate is now liturgically incorporated into a chain or community. The ritual structure is structurally identical across at least five non-contacting religious lineages. Schimmel (*Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, 1975, ch. 4-5) is the foundational comparative treatment of the khirqah specifically; Cordwell & Schwarz (1979) provide the broader frame.

**MASSIVE-WIN: Lineage transmission via material object.** The khirqah is more than a garment — it is the **physical token of a chain of transmission** (*silsila*). The same cloak, in some lineages, is literally passed from master to master across generations (often as a small fragment incorporated into each successor's cloak). The cross-tradition parallels: **Buddhist *kasaya*** in the Chan/Zen tradition (the kasaya allegedly transmitted from Bodhidharma to Huineng is the foundational physical token of the Chan lineage — see *Platform Sutra*), **Apostolic succession** in Catholic ordination (the bishop's hand-laying is the gesture; in some early-Christian traditions the bishop's pallium-cloak functions analogously to the khirqah), **Sikh succession** of the Guruship via specific garments and the Adi Granth. The convergence on a physical-object-as-lineage-token is striking — religious authority is repeatedly materialized in a piece of cloth.

**Wool as ascetic-prophet signaling.** The Sufi adoption of wool (*ṣūf*) explicitly references the Quranic-Biblical prophets in coarse wool. Wool-clad ascetics also appear in: **Cistercian and Trappist Christianity** (undyed white wool), **Eastern Christian desert fathers** (Egyptian wool), **medieval Christian hair-shirts** (often goat-wool). The cross-tradition pattern: in agrarian-Mediterranean-Levantine societies, **wool = peasant cloth, vs. silk = court cloth**. Wearing wool when one could afford silk is an explicit sumptuary protest. The Sufi tradition is the most-systematically wool-identified, but it sits in a wider Levantine pattern.

**Patched cloth as anti-luxury.** The *muraqqaʿa* converges with **Buddhist *paṃsukūla***, the **kasaya** more generally (whose stitched rectangular panels visually quote the rag-cloak origin), and the **Cistercian rough-wool habit**. Cordwell & Schwarz (1979) document the pattern as "ostentatious anti-ostentation" — the patches become signs OF spiritual status precisely BY signaling refusal of worldly status. The same paradox runs through Quaker plain dress, Hasidic black-and-white, and Amish dress.
