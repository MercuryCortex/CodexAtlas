# The Lots

## What it is

A **lot** is a derived point in a chart — not a planet, not a house cusp, but a degree computed by arithmetic on other positions. The recipe is always the same shape: **measure the arc between two factors, then project that arc from the ascendant.**

The principal one is the **Lot of Fortune** (*klēros tychēs*): the arc from Sun to Moon, projected from the ascendant by day; from Moon to Sun by night, in the sources that reverse by sect. The resulting degree is then treated as a full significator — it sits in a sign and a place, it has a ruler, it receives aspects.

Because the recipe is generic, lots multiply. The Hellenistic sources give a Hermetic set of seven (Fortune, Spirit, Eros, Necessity, Courage, Victory, Nemesis), attributed to [[hermes-trismegistus|Hermes Trismegistus]]; Paulus Alexandrinus lists more; the Arabic tradition expands them enormously, and [[al-biruni|al-Bīrūnī]]'s *Tafhīm* tabulates roughly a hundred — a lot for the father, for illness, for the price of wheat.

## The misnomer, and why the Atlas bothers

In English these are almost universally called **"the Arabic Parts"**. The name is wrong, and it is wrong in a way that is a small perfect specimen of how transmission works.

The chain is:

- **Greek *klēros*** — "a lot, an allotment, an inheritance". The technique is Hellenistic; it is in [[vettius-valens|Valens]] and [[dorotheus-of-sidon|Dorotheus]] in the first and second centuries CE.
- **Arabic *sahm*** (pl. *sihām*) — literally "arrow", hence "share, portion, lot", from the arrows used in pre-Islamic lot-drawing. The Arabic translators chose a word with the same double sense as the Greek.
- **Latin *pars*** (pl. *partes*) — the twelfth-century translators rendering the Arabic.
- **English "part"** — inherited from the Latin.

Latin Europe met the technique **in Arabic**, and named it after where it met it. So the standard name records the route rather than the origin — the same error, in the same direction, as calling the numerals we write "Arabic" when the Arabic tradition itself called them *Indian*.

The Arabic astrologers did earn part of the credit: they systematized and vastly multiplied the lots. But the device is Greek, and the misnomer is worth correcting precisely because it is the kind of thing that makes a transmission chain invisible — a name that quietly attributes the invention to the middleman.

## Not the same "lot" as casting lots

English "lot" covers two unrelated operations and the collision is worth marking explicitly, because it invites a wrong inference.

- **[[cleromancy-lot-casting|Cleromancy]]** — casting objects (knucklebones, sticks, the Urim and Thummim, drawn straws) to obtain an answer by randomization. The answer comes from *outside* the enquirer's control; that is the whole point.
- **The astrological lot** — arithmetic performed on measured celestial positions. There is nothing random in it at all. It is fully determined by the chart.

The Greek word does carry an inheritance metaphor — a *klēros* is what has been apportioned to you, which fits the fatalism of the Hellenistic sources ([[thread-of-fate]]; [[providence-and-fate]]). But the resemblance to lot-casting is lexical, not technical, and this node wires it as such.

## Disputes

- **Does the Lot of Fortune reverse by sect?** [[vettius-valens|Valens]], [[dorotheus-of-sidon|Dorotheus]] and most of the Hellenistic tradition reverse the formula for a night chart. [[ptolemy-astronomer|Ptolemy]] does not, and Ptolemy's authority carried much of the later tradition with him. The disagreement is ancient, unresolved, and consequential — the two methods put the lot in different places in most night charts. It is a good illustration of how a single authoritative text can propagate a minority position for a millennium.
- **How many lots are legitimate?** Ptolemy admits one. Valens uses several structurally. The Arabic tradition admits a hundred. Twentieth-century practice mostly dropped them; the traditional revival restored them. There is no period in which the answer was settled.
- **Were they central or ornamental?** Much twentieth-century writing treated the lots as a curiosity. The Hellenistic sources do not: Valens releases his principal predictive technique from the Lot of Spirit. The reassessment is one of the substantive results of reading the ancient texts in full translation (Brennan 2017), and it is carried here at T2 as a reconstruction of *how the technique worked*, distinct from the T1 fact that the lots are there.
- **What is claimed here.** That the technique is Hellenistic, that the English name misattributes it to the Arabic intermediary, and that the terminology chain runs *klēros* → *sahm* → *pars* → *part*, is Tier-1 documented history. What a lot *signifies* is a claim of the tradition, reported and not endorsed.
