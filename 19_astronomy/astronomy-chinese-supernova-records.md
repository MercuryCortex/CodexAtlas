# Chinese "Guest Star" Records (kèxīng / 客星)

## Identity

The **guest-star records** (客星 *kèxīng*, literally "guest star") are the continuous, dated, positional naked-eye records of transient celestial phenomena — novae, supernovae, and comets — kept by the Chinese imperial astronomical bureau from the Han dynasty (with putative antecedents in Shang oracle bones) through the Qing. A *kèxīng* was a star that "lodged" temporarily among the fixed stars and then departed; comets were separately termed 彗星 *huìxīng* ("broom" or "sweeping star"). Because these apparitions were read as **portents bearing on the Mandate of Heaven**, the throne maintained a standing office — the Taishi 太史 (Grand Scribe/Astrologer), later the Sitiantai 司天臺 and the Ming–Qing Qintianjian 欽天監 (Directorate of Astronomy) — whose duty was to observe, date, and position every anomaly and enter it into the record. The entries survive in the "tianwen zhi" 天文志 (treatises on the heavens) and "wuxing zhi" 五行志 (Five-Phases treatises) of the Twenty-Four Histories, and in compendia such as the *Song huiyao* 宋會要 and the *Wenxian tongkao* 文獻通考. The positions were logged against the [[astronomy-chinese-28-mansions|28 lunar mansions (xiu)]], which makes them uniquely recoverable by modern astronomy.

The corpus is one of the Atlas's clearest cases of an ancient religious-bureaucratic practice yielding hard modern science: the **SN 1054 record is the birth certificate of the Crab Nebula and, through it, of the first identified pulsar**.

## The records and their astrophysical yield

- **SN 185** (December 185 CE) — recorded in the *Hou Han shu* 後漢書 as a guest star in the asterism Nánmén 南門 (near Alpha/Beta Centauri); widely taken as the **earliest reliably attested supernova** in the historical record, its candidate remnant RCW 86.
- **SN 393** — a guest star in the tail of Scorpius (mansion Wěi 尾), recorded in the *Jin shu* 晉書; candidate remnants in the Scorpius region.
- **SN 1006** (April/May 1006) — the **brightest stellar event in recorded history**, entered in the *Song shi* 宋史 near Dǐ 氐 (Lupus). Independently recorded across traditions — in Japan, in the Islamic world by the Egyptian physician **ʿAlī ibn Riḍwān**, and at the abbey of St. Gallen in Europe — making it a rare multi-tradition cross-check. Remnant: SNR G327.6+14.6.
- **SN 1054** (July 1054) — the celebrated case. The *Song shi* and *Song huiyao* record a guest star appearing beside **Tiānguān 天關** (Zeta Tauri), visible in daylight for about **23 days** and at night for some **653 days** before fading. Its remnant is the **Crab Nebula (M1)**, which contains the **Crab Pulsar (PSR B0531+21)**.
- **SN 1181** (August 1181) — a guest star recorded in China and Japan in the region of Chuánshè 傳舍 (Cassiopeia/Cepheus); long tentatively assigned to the remnant 3C 58, an attribution now disputed in favour of the recently-identified nebula Pa 30 (a probable Type Iax white-dwarf merger) — see Disputes.
- **SN 1572 / SN 1604** — recorded by the East-Asian bureaus as well as by Tycho Brahe and Kepler in Europe (see below).
- **Comets** — a continuous cometary series runs from the *Shiji* record of **-240 (Halley's Comet)** onward; the Chinese apparitions have been used to reconstruct Halley's orbit back over two millennia (Stephenson & Yau 1985; Yeomans & Kiang 1981), and the Mawangdui silk manuscript (c. -168) preserves an atlas of comet-tail forms.

Modern astronomers use the records three ways: to **date supernova remnants** (a dated outburst plus a measured expansion rate fixes the remnant's age and distance), to reconstruct **cometary orbits** over a long baseline, and — via eclipse and sunspot entries in the same corpus — to study long-term solar activity and Earth's rotation.

## Cross-tradition significance

The guest-star corpus has two distinct comparative faces, and it is worth keeping them apart.

**Institutional parallel (convergence).** The Chinese bureau records and the Babylonian **Astronomical Diaries** (c. -652 to -61) are the two great **state-sponsored, continuous, portent-driven naked-eye observational archives** of the ancient world. Both are dated, both are positional, both were maintained because celestial anomalies were read as omens touching the ruler. This is best understood as **convergence**: two literate imperial cultures independently discovered that a standing astronomical office producing dated positional records serves both divination and prediction. There is no evidence of transmission between the Babylonian and Chinese record-keeping practices; the Atlas wires this to [[astronomy-mul-apin]] as the Mesopotamian systematic-astronomy anchor, flagging honestly that the true observational counterpart is the Diaries (which has no separate node) rather than MUL.APIN itself.

**The immutability contrast (the sharp cross-tradition point).** The most striking comparison is with the **European** reception of the same phenomenon. Aristotelian–Ptolemaic cosmology held the supralunar heavens to be perfect and unchanging, so when a "new star" blazed out in Cassiopeia in 1572, [[tycho-brahe|Tycho Brahe]]'s demonstration (in *De Nova Stella*, 1573) that it lay beyond the Moon and did not move was **cosmologically revolutionary** — one of the observations that cracked the immutable-heavens doctrine. Yet the Chinese bureau had been recording exactly such transients, as a matter of routine administration, for roughly eighteen centuries by then. The same object (SN 1572, and again SN 1604) appears in both the East-Asian and European records, so this is not a loose analogy but the *same events* read through two radically different cosmologies — changeful-and-ominous in China, impossible-until-observed in Aristotelian Europe.

**The reference frame.** Positionally, none of this is usable without the coordinate grid: guest stars and comets were located relative to the [[astronomy-chinese-28-mansions|28 xiu]] and the named asterisms of the four palaces. The record-corpus and the mansion-system are complementary halves of one observational system — this node supplies the events; the 28-xiu node supplies the sky against which they were plotted.

## Disputes

- **The Shang oracle-bone "new star" (c. -1300).** An oracle-bone inscription mentioning a 新大星 ("new great star") beside Antares (mansion Xīn 心, the "Heart") has been proposed as the earliest nova/supernova record on Earth (Xu, Pankenier & Jiang 2000). The reading and its astronomical interpretation are contested; conservative treatments (Stephenson & Green 2002) begin the *reliable* supernova series with SN 185. The node dates from the firmly-attested -240 comet rather than the oracle bone for this reason.
- **The Crab identification, and who made it.** The SN 1054 ↔ Crab Nebula link was built up in stages: Knut Lundmark (1921) listed the 1054 event among suspected novae; Edwin Hubble (1928) proposed the Crab as an expanding remnant; **Duyvendak (1942)** with **Mayall & Oort (1942)** firmly tied the Chinese/Japanese record to the nebula; and **Xi Zezong (1955/1958)** produced the systematic catalog. The neutron-star remnant was confirmed only with the discovery of the Crab Pulsar in 1968. The record is genuine and precise; the *identification* is a 20th-century scientific achievement, not something the Song astronomers claimed.
- **SN 1181 and its remnant.** The 1181 guest star was for decades matched to the radio remnant 3C 58, but a 2021 identification of the nebula **Pa 30** (surrounding a hot Wolf–Rayet-like central star, likely a Type Iax white-dwarf merger) has displaced that attribution for many workers. The record itself is not in doubt; its physical counterpart is.
