# Bighorn Medicine Wheel (Solstice + Stellar Alignments)

## Identity

The **Bighorn Medicine Wheel alignments** are the archaeoastronomical claims attached to a circular stone monument — a central cairn, six irregularly-placed peripheral cairns, and 28 radial spokes, ~24 m across — on the summit ridge of **Medicine Mountain** (2,940 m) in the Bighorn Mountains of northern Wyoming. In a 1972 field survey published in *Science* in 1974, the solar astronomer [[john-eddy|John Eddy]] argued that sightlines running from the central cairn to specific peripheral cairns are deliberately oriented to the **summer-solstice sunrise and sunset** and to the **heliacal (pre-dawn) risings of three of the brightest stars — Aldebaran (α Tauri), Rigel (β Orionis), and Sirius (α Canis Majoris)**. On Eddy's reading the wheel is a **horizon calendar in stone**, and it is the New-World Plains counterpart to the Old-World solstice circles — [[astronomy-stonehenge-alignment|Stonehenge]], [[astronomy-nabta-playa|Nabta Playa]], [[astronomy-newgrange-solstice|Newgrange]] — and to the other North American programme at [[astronomy-cahokia-woodhenge|Cahokia Woodhenge]].

This node is the **astronomical-phenomenon** entry; the built monument, its ritual life, and its trans-tribal management are treated in the sacred-architecture node [[bighorn-medicine-wheel]], and the cosmological diagram in the symbol node [[medicine-wheel]].

## The alignments (after Eddy 1974)

Eddy measured the angular directions of the central cairn to each of the six peripheral cairns and compared them, with precessional correction for an epoch of c. +1700, to astronomical events:

- **Central cairn → east cairn:** the **summer-solstice sunrise** (~21 June).
- **Central cairn → opposite (south-west) cairn:** the **summer-solstice sunset**.
- **Central cairn → three further cairns:** the **heliacal risings of Aldebaran, Rigel, and Sirius**, in sequence.

The three star-rises are the argument's most distinctive element. At the wheel's latitude and epoch, **Aldebaran** rose heliacally within a day or two *before* the solstice sunrise — a "warning star" announcing that the solstice was imminent; **Rigel** rose heliacally roughly **28 days later**; and **Sirius** roughly **28 days after that** (late July / early August), heralding the approach of autumn and the end of the brief high-mountain season. The ~28-day spacing of the three risings echoes the wheel's **28 radial spokes**, which have separately been read as a lunar month, the 28 ribs of the bison, or the pole-count of a ceremonial lodge — meanings that need not exclude one another.

The site's virtue as an instrument is its setting: an exposed high ridge with sweeping, sharply-defined eastern and western mountain horizons against which rising and setting positions are fixed and repeatable — the same logic that governs the choice of open, horizon-rich ground at Nabta Playa and Stonehenge.

## Cross-tradition significance (convergence, not contact)

The Bighorn wheel is the Atlas's **High Plains occupant of the solstice-aligned stone circle slot**. Set beside its parallels, it makes the comparative point cleanly: mutually-uncontacting societies, thousands of years and whole oceans apart, independently built permanent architecture that observes the sun's turning points. **Nabta Playa** (~-4500, Saharan Egypt) is the earliest; **Newgrange** (~-3200, Ireland) and **Stonehenge** (~-2500, Britain) are the classic northwest-European pair; **Cahokia Woodhenge** (~+1050, Mississippian Illinois) is the other North American programme; and Bighorn (~1700 CE) is the latest major exemplar. No documented contact links Bighorn to any of them, and none is remotely plausible for the Old-World cases. This is **convergent invention** — the recurring human solution to the problem of fixing the seasons before writing — and it is one of the strongest cases in the vault of a genuine cross-cultural regularity rather than a diffused one.

The convergence must be stated at the right grain. What recurs is the **general form** (a circular monument whose sightlines target a solar extreme), not any specific technique or meaning: Stonehenge is a megalithic trilithon architecture, Bighorn a surface arrangement of limestone cobbles; Newgrange targets midwinter dawn *inside* a tomb, Bighorn midsummer sunrise on an open ridge. Even within North America, the Cahokia timber circle and the Plains stone wheel are separate culture-area inventions. The parallel is **structural and typological**, secured by function, not by any shared vocabulary or lineage — which is exactly why it is evidence about human cognition and landscape, not about transmission.

## Disputes

Three caveats keep the claim honest, and the vault states all three:

- **Statistical robustness.** With six peripheral cairns, 28 spokes, and a whole sky of bright stars and solar events to match, the number of possible sightlines is large and the number of "hits" small. Skeptical archaeoastronomers (the methodological tradition represented by Ruggles 2005) warn that such single-site alignment claims are prone to **selection effects** — finding targets after the fact for lines that already exist. The **solstice sunrise/sunset axis is the most defensible** element; the three stellar rises are more contested, because heliacal-rising dates depend on assumed limiting magnitude, horizon altitude, and atmospheric extinction, each of which widens the error bars.

- **Dating and design intent.** The principal construction is placed at **c. +1650-1750** by radiocarbon and lichenometry, yet Eddy's original discussion entertained far older dates, and the wheel's components may not all be contemporary. If the cairns were added or shifted over generations, "the alignment" may be an artefact of a monument that never existed in a single designed state at one epoch. Brumley (1988), who surveyed all ~135 wheels, is cautious precisely because **most Plains wheels show no alignments at all** — Bighorn may be exceptional rather than typical of an astronomical tradition.

- **Cultural continuity.** The medicine-wheel *tradition* is millennia deep (the Majorville Cairn's earliest layers date to c. -3200), but the peoples who used Bighorn in recent centuries are not straightforwardly the same as its builders, and the ethnographic record of an explicitly astronomical purpose is thin. The star-knowledge that makes the alignments plausible is best attested for the **Skidi Pawnee** (Chamberlain 1982), a related but distinct Plains tradition — so the intentional-astronomy reading is supported by analogy, not by direct testimony from the wheel's own builders. The alignments are therefore reported here as **argued (Eddy)**, widely cited and partly accepted, but genuinely contested at the level of the stellar rises, the dating, and the design's unity.
