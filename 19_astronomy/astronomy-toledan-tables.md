# The Toledan Tables

The point where the whole chain lands in Europe. Not a theory and not a discovery — a set of numbers and the instructions for using them, which is what practising astronomers actually need. Assembled at Toledo in the eleventh century out of Arabic sources that were themselves Indian, Persian and Greek, translated into Latin in the twelfth, and, in their Alfonsine successor, still in daily use across Europe when Copernicus was a student.

If you want to see the transmission chain as an object rather than an argument, this is the object.

## What was assembled, and out of what

Toledo in the mid-eleventh century was a centre of learning under Dhūʾl-Nūnid rule, and a group of astronomers gathered there around the qāḍī and historian of science **Ṣāʿid al-Andalusī**. Its outstanding practical astronomer was **Abū Isḥāq Ibrāhīm al-Zarqālī** — Latin *Arzachel* or *Azarquiel* — who began as a metalworker and instrument-maker and became the finest observer in al-Andalus.

What they built was a compilation. Its two principal sources are exactly the two rival Arabic table traditions:

- the **Sindhind** line, Indian in its mean motions, reaching Toledo through Maslama al-Majrīṭī's Córdoban recension of al-Khwārizmī;
- the **Ptolemaic** line, reaching Toledo through al-Battānī.

Onto that they grafted their own observations and their own corrections, and re-based everything on the meridian of Toledo. Al-Zarqālī's most important result continued al-Battānī's: he confirmed and refined the motion of the solar apogee, putting it at roughly one degree in three hundred years against the fixed stars. He also produced the *ṣafīḥa*, a universal astrolabe plate that works at any latitude, which Latin Europe knew as the *saphea Arzachelis* and used for centuries.

## How they crossed

Alfonso VI of Castile took Toledo in 1085. The city kept its Arabic-literate population and, crucially, its books. Over the following century a translation programme moved the contents of those libraries into Latin, and the tables went with them: **Gerard of Cremona**, the most prolific translator of the Middle Ages, rendered the canons, having also translated the *Almagest* itself at Toledo in 1175.

Then the tables did what tables do — they got copied and adapted. Versions were re-based for Marseilles, Toulouse, London, Paris and Novara. An astronomer in twelfth-century England was computing planetary positions with Indian mean motions and al-Battānī's year length without necessarily knowing that either was there.

## The Alfonsine successor

Two centuries later Toledo did it again, now under Christian rule. **Alfonso X of Castile** — *el Sabio*, the Learned — sponsored a new set compiled c. 1263–1272 by **Isḥāq ibn Sīd** and **Yehuda ben Moshe ha-Kohen**, working in Castilian. Around 1320 a Latin version appeared in Paris, in the hands of John of Lignères, John of Murs and John of Saxony, and spread across Europe from there.

How much of the Parisian version descends from the Castilian original is genuinely disputed — Chabás and Goldstein set out the problem — because the Castilian tables themselves largely do not survive, only the canons. What is not disputed is the result: the Alfonsine Tables became **the** working astronomical tables of Latin Europe, and in 1483 at Venice they became the first astronomical tables ever printed.

## The wrong theory that travelled with them

Not everything transmitted is correct, and the vault should say so. Ptolemy's precession rate was badly too slow; al-Battānī's was much better. Comparing them, Arabic astronomers concluded not that Ptolemy had measured badly but that the rate itself *varies* — hence **trepidation**, an oscillation of the eighth sphere, which al-Zarqālī modelled and which these tables carried into Latin astronomy as established doctrine.

It is wrong. It survived for five hundred years because it was embedded in the tables everyone used, and Copernicus was still constructing machinery in *De revolutionibus* to reproduce it — using a Ṭūsī couple to generate the oscillation. Transmission is not a filter for truth; it moves what is in the pipeline.

## The end of the line

The genealogy is continuous and it terminates in the seventeenth century: Toledan Tables, c. 1069 → Alfonsine Tables, c. 1272, printed 1483 → Reinhold's Prutenic Tables, 1551, computed on Copernican models → Kepler's Rudolphine Tables, 1627, computed on elliptical orbits.

Kepler's tables break the chain in substance. In form they are still a zīj: canons and tables, the shape the Arabic astronomical handbook had fixed eight centuries before, itself descended from the Indian *siddhānta* and, behind that, from clay tablets of computed lunar positions in Seleucid Babylon.
