# Cahokia Woodhenge

The **Cahokia Woodhenges** are a series of five timber circles constructed between c. +900 and +1100 at the Mississippian city of [[cahokia-monks-mound|Cahokia]] (modern Collinsville, Illinois) — the largest pre-Columbian city north of Mexico. The most-studied of the five, **Woodhenge III**, comprised 48 cedar posts arranged in a circle 410 feet (125 m) in diameter with a central observation post. Post-mould patterns + horizon geometry (Wittry 1969) demonstrate alignment with sunrise positions on the equinoxes + summer + winter solstices.

The Cahokia Woodhenge is the **principal preserved example of pre-Columbian architectural astronomy in eastern North America** + the timekeeping infrastructure of the Mississippian polity's agricultural-ritual calendar.

## The Mississippian astronomical programme

Cahokia's Woodhenge III is the best-documented but not the only Mississippian solar-observation feature:

- **48 cedar posts** equally spaced around the perimeter (one per ~7.5°)
- A **central observation post** from which alignments to specific perimeter posts mark the equinox + summer-solstice + winter-solstice sunrise positions
- **Five successive Woodhenges** at the site, each rebuilt larger than the last across ~200 years — suggesting an ongoing observational programme refining the alignments

The 48-post count is suggestive (48 = 4 × 12, or 6 × 8) but its exact significance is undetermined. The system would have functioned as a horizon-calendar — predicting equinox + solstice dates for agricultural planning + ritual scheduling.

## The American-architectural-astronomy series

Cahokia Woodhenge sits within a series of large-scale astronomically-oriented Mississippian + later sites (Mound 72 burial alignments; the Emerald Acropolis; Aztalan; the Mound City + Newark earthworks of the older Hopewell tradition). Together they document a **long indigenous Eastern Woodlands tradition of solar-architectural astronomy** independent of Mesoamerican Long-Count astronomy (though direct Mississippian–Mesoamerican cultural contact has been argued for the so-called Southeastern Ceremonial Complex; the case remains contested).
