# The Ṭūsī Couple

A small piece of geometry with an outsized history. Take a circle, and inside it a second circle of exactly half the radius, rolling around the inside of the first without slipping. A point on the rim of the small circle does not trace a curve — it runs back and forth along a straight line, a diameter of the large circle.

Two uniform circular motions produce a linear oscillation. That is the whole device, and it was enough to reorganise four centuries of planetary astronomy.

## The problem it solves

Ptolemy's models are built on a stated principle: heavenly motions are uniform and circular. His **equant** breaks it. The equant is a point, offset from the centre of a planet's deferent circle on the opposite side from the Earth, about which the centre of the planet's epicycle sweeps out equal angles in equal times. It fits the observations beautifully. It also means the epicycle centre does *not* move uniformly about the centre of the circle it is travelling on — the very thing the principle forbids.

For a thousand years this was recognised as an embarrassment and tolerated, because nobody had a replacement that preserved the accuracy. Islamic astronomers named the problem the *shukūk* — the doubts — and the Maragha school made solving it a research programme.

The Ṭūsī couple is the tool that made the programme possible. Once you can generate a straight-line oscillation from legal circular motions, you can decompose the illegal ones: an equant can be replaced by a combination of uniform rotations that produces the same result. Al-Ṭūsī introduced the device in his recension of the *Almagest* in 1247 for the latitude motions, generalised it in the *Tadhkira* of 1261, and Ragep's edition shows he worked out more than one version — a flat two-circle form and a curvilinear form built from spheres.

## Where it travelled inside the Islamic world

The device does not stay at Maragha. Quṭb al-Dīn al-Shīrāzī carries it forward in the generation after al-Ṭūsī. Ibn al-Shāṭir in Damascus builds a whole reformed planetary system on it in the fourteenth century. ʿAlī Qushjī takes it to Samarkand and then to Istanbul; Shams al-Dīn al-Khafrī is still developing the tradition in Safavid Iran in the sixteenth century, contemporary with Copernicus. Bar Hebraeus, who worked at Maragha, carried Maragha astronomy into Syriac.

That diffusion matters for the transmission question. This is not an obscure trick in one manuscript. It was standard equipment across a wide, multilingual, three-century scholarly world with Byzantine contacts — which is why arguing about whether Copernicus could have encountered it is a real argument rather than an obvious one.

## The Copernicus question

The construction appears in *De revolutionibus* — in Book III, generating the slow oscillation of precession and obliquity, and in the Mercury model of Book V — and already in Copernicus's earlier *Commentariolus*. It is geometrically identical to al-Ṭūsī's.

**The argument for transmission.** Identity of construction plus identity of purpose. Hartner added the detail most often quoted: the letters labelling the points on Copernicus's diagram correspond to a transliteration of the Arabic letters on al-Ṭūsī's. Saliba and Ragep supply the routes — Byzantine scholars who studied in Ilkhanid Tabriz, Greek manuscripts carrying Maragha material into Italy, and Copernicus's own years at Bologna and Padua.

**The argument against.** Copernicus cites Arabic astronomers freely — al-Battānī, al-Zarqālī, Thābit — and never mentions al-Ṭūsī. There is no known Latin *Tadhkira*. Di Bono showed the device circulating among sixteenth-century Italian astronomers by routes that do not require Arabic contact. Blåsjö argues the inference from geometric identity to historical contact has been treated as much stronger than it is, and points out that the construction is not hard to arrive at once the equant problem is posed.

**And the awkward third possibility.** Veselovsky argued in 1973 that an equivalent construction already sits in Proclus's commentary on Euclid — that is, in a Greek text that Renaissance Italy owned. The reading is disputed, but its existence is the reason the question stays open: a plausible Greek route is precisely what the transmission argument would need to exclude.

The vault's position is to state what is established — the models and the device are the same, and Copernicus was not first — and to tier the channel at **T2** with both sides attached. Compare [[al-battani-astronomer]], where Copernicus names his source in his own book and no inference is needed at all. Two claims, two evidentiary situations, two tiers.

## Afterlife outside astronomy

The same figure reappears in mechanical engineering as the pair of **Cardan circles** — a hypocycloidal straight-line mechanism, arrived at independently in sixteenth-century Europe and used in linkages and gearing ever since. A device invented to defend the dignity of celestial motion became a way of converting rotary motion to linear in machinery.
