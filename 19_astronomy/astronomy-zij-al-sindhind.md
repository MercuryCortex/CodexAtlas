# Zīj al-Sindhind

Al-Khwārizmī's astronomical tables, made in Baghdad around the 820s. The single most instructive object in the whole chain, because it is a text in which three traditions are visibly stacked on top of one another — and because the way it survives is itself a map of the transmission.

## Three layers in one book

A *zīj* is a working handbook: canons explaining the procedures, then the tables an astronomer actually computes with. Al-Khwārizmī's is not a translation of anything. It is a synthesis, and its strata can be separated.

- **The Indian layer.** The mean motions of the Sun, Moon and planets follow the *Brāhmapakṣa* parameters that reached Baghdad through al-Fazārī's Sindhind translation of the 770s. The title says so: *Sindhind* is Arabic for *siddhānta*.
- **The Greek layer.** The structure of the equations — the corrections applied to mean motion to get true position — and the equation of time follow Ptolemaic models.
- **The Persian layer.** Chronology, era conversion, and some parameters come from the Sasanian *Zīj al-Shāh*, itself a text that had already absorbed Indian astronomy at Gondēshāpūr in the sixth century.

And the trigonometry is Indian rather than Greek. Ptolemy computes with chords; al-Khwārizmī computes with sines, the Indian half-chord. That choice is permanent: every subsequent astronomer in the Islamic world and then in Europe uses sines, and the word carries the route it came by — Sanskrit *jyā*, transliterated into Arabic as *jiba*, read as the ordinary Arabic word *jayb* meaning a fold or bosom of a garment, and then translated literally into Latin as *sinus*.

## How it survives — a transmission chain in the manuscript history

There is no Arabic manuscript of this work. What exists is:

1. Al-Khwārizmī's Arabic original, Baghdad, c. 820s — **lost**;
2. A recension by **Maslama al-Majrīṭī** of Córdoba (d. c. 1007) and his school, adapting the tables to al-Andalus — **lost in Arabic**;
3. A **Latin translation by Adelard of Bath**, c. 1126, of that Andalusian recension — **this is the text we have**, edited by Suter in 1914 and analysed by Neugebauer in 1962.

So the book reaches us only because Latin Europe wanted it. It is the first complete set of Arabic astronomical tables to cross into Latin, arriving a generation before the Toledo translation programme reached full pace, and the Indian mean motions inside it were still being used in Europe centuries after anyone there knew where they came from.

## The rivalry that shaped Islamic astronomy

Ninth-century Baghdad has two competing table traditions, and the vault should keep both visible because the winner determines what Europe later received.

The **Sindhind** line is Indian-parameter and sine-based — al-Fazārī, al-Khwārizmī, and the Andalusian school after them. The **mumtaḥan** ("verified") line is Ptolemaic and observation-driven — al-Maʾmūn commissioned fresh observations in Baghdad and Damascus, producing the *Zīj al-Mumtaḥan* of Yaḥyā ibn Abī Manṣūr.

The Ptolemaic line eventually prevails on accuracy, which is why the Arabic astronomer Latin Europe cites by name is al-Battānī and not al-Khwārizmī. But the Indian contribution does not disappear; it is absorbed. The trigonometry stays Indian, the decimal arithmetic al-Khwārizmī propagated separately becomes the arithmetic of the world, and the Toledan Tables that Europe computed with for two centuries are built from the Andalusian Sindhind recension *and* al-Battānī together.
