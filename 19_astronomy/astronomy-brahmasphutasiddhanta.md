# Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta

Brahmagupta's *Correctly Established Doctrine of Brahmā*, composed in 628 CE at Bhillamāla in western India. It is simultaneously the major astronomical treatise of the *Brāhmapakṣa* school, the first text in the world to give the arithmetic of zero and of negative numbers, and — through a translation made in Baghdad a century and a half later — the object through which Indian mathematical astronomy entered the Islamic world.

Three separate things travel out of this one book: a planetary system, an arithmetic, and a school name.

## The book

Twenty-four chapters of Sanskrit verse. The first ten are the standard siddhāntic curriculum — mean motions, true positions, the three problems of diurnal motion, eclipses, heliacal risings, the lunar crescent, planetary conjunctions. Chapter 11, the *Tantraparīkṣā*, is a critique of earlier treatises. Chapters 12 and 18, the *gaṇita* and the *kuṭṭaka*, are the mathematical chapters, and they are why the book is famous outside astronomy.

Brahmagupta writes as a partisan. He works within the *Brāhmapakṣa*, whose parameters descend from the *Paitāmahasiddhānta*, and he defends it against rivals — above all against Āryabhaṭa, whom he names and attacks for asserting that the Earth rotates. Chapter 11 is a polemic, and it is a useful one for the vault, because it shows Indian astronomy as a live argument between schools rather than a single tradition, and because the competing parameter sets those schools produced were then transmitted separately into Arabic.

## Zero, and the honest failure

Chapter 18 states the rules. A quantity added to zero is unchanged; zero added to zero is zero; a positive divided by a positive or a negative by a negative is positive; a positive divided by a negative or a negative by a positive is negative. Positives are *dhana*, "fortunes"; negatives are *ṛṇa*, "debts" — a commercial metaphor that makes the sign rules intuitive.

Then it fails. Brahmagupta asserts that zero divided by zero is zero, and gives no satisfactory rule for a nonzero quantity divided by zero. The error is worth recording rather than smoothing over: it stood in Indian mathematics until Bhāskara II proposed (in the 12th century) that a quantity divided by zero is an infinite quantity, and it was not rigorously resolved anywhere until the 19th-century arithmetisation of analysis. The vault's interest is in what a tradition actually said, including where it was wrong.

## The road to Baghdad

The transmission has a date, a city, and named translators, which is what makes it a transmission rather than a resemblance.

Arabic sources — the historian Ibn al-Ādamī, quoted in al-Qifṭī's *Taʾrīkh al-ḥukamāʾ*, and al-Bīrūnī — report that in 771 or 773 CE an Indian delegation arrived at the Baghdad court of the caliph al-Manṣūr bringing a Sanskrit astronomical work. **Muḥammad ibn Ibrāhīm al-Fazārī**, assisted by **Yaʿqūb ibn Ṭāriq**, translated it into Arabic as the *Zīj al-Sindhind al-kabīr* — *Sindhind* being the Arabic rendering of Sanskrit *siddhānta*. From that point the word *Sindhind* names an entire tradition in Arabic astronomy: the Indian-parameter line, as against the Ptolemaic line.

Two cautions the vault keeps visible:

- **Which Sanskrit text it was is not settled.** Al-Bīrūnī says the *Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta*; the transmitted parameters are Brāhmapakṣa, which fits; but Pingree allows that the exemplar may have been another work of the same school. The *school* is certain, the *exemplar* is probable.
- **Brahmagupta's later handbook travelled separately.** The *Khaṇḍakhādyaka* (665 CE) became the Arabic *Zīj al-Arkand*, and al-Bīrūnī produced his own version of it. There were several Indian-to-Arabic channels, not one.

What arrives in Baghdad in the 770s therefore predates the great Greek-to-Arabic translation wave. Islamic astronomy is Indian before it is Greek — a sequence that is easy to lose when the chain is told as a straight line from Ptolemy.
