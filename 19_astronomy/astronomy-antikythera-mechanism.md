# The Antikythera Mechanism

The **Antikythera Mechanism** is a Hellenistic Greek geared astronomical computer recovered in 1900–1901 from a Roman-era shipwreck off the Greek island of Antikythera. The mechanism dates c. -150 to -50 (with the wreck itself dated c. -70 to -50). It is the **most sophisticated mechanical artefact preserved from the ancient world** — a hand-cranked geared device with at least 37 interlocking bronze gears whose function was to compute, display, and predict:

- **Calendar and lunisolar dates** (Egyptian + Greek calendars)
- **Moon's position + phase** (with a pin-and-slot variable-speed device modelling lunar anomaly)
- **Saros-cycle eclipse predictions** (lunar + solar, by date + time-of-day)
- **Olympiad cycle**
- **Five planetary positions** (per the 2021 reconstruction)
- **Positions on the zodiac**

The mechanism is the principal physical evidence that Hellenistic Greek mathematical astronomy was being **technologically embodied in precision geared instruments**, ~250 years before Ptolemy's Almagest systematized the same astronomy in mathematical-textual form.

## What it tells us

The mechanism's existence shifts our picture of Hellenistic technical-scientific civilization:

- **Geared technology** of precision sufficient to compute astronomical positions was available c. -100 — the level of mechanical sophistication is not matched in surviving European technology until the medieval astrarium tradition ~1300 years later
- **Astronomical modelling** of the Hellenistic period included variable-speed lunar motion (the Hipparchan eccentric-or-epicycle model) implemented physically — not just mathematically abstract
- **Saros-cycle eclipse prediction** was a precision-engineered function — the Babylonian eclipse-period data Hipparchus uses appears physically gear-encoded
- **The Hellenistic-Babylonian astronomical-parameter inheritance** is documented in the mechanism's gear-ratios (planetary-period values matching Babylonian System-A + System-B)

## The recovery + decoding

Discovered in 1900–1901 in a sponge-divers' salvage of a Roman-era shipwreck; partially cleaned + studied through the 20th century; subjected to high-resolution X-ray tomography in the 2000s (Freeth et al. 2006), which revealed the previously-illegible inscriptions inside the corroded bronze fragments. The decoding is ongoing — the 2021 Freeth-et-al. reconstruction of the front-dial planetary model is the most recent major advance.
