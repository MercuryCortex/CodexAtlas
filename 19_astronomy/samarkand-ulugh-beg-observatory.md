# Samarkand Observatory (Ulugh Beg)

The **Samarkand Observatory** was founded in 1424 by the Timurid prince + later sultan **Ulugh Beg** (1394–1449) in his capital Samarkand. It operated for 25 years until Ulugh Beg's assassination in 1449. Its centrepiece was the **Fakhri sextant** — a 40-metre-radius vertical meridian arc partly built into bedrock, the largest pre-telescopic observational instrument ever constructed. The principal output was the **Zīj-i Sulṭānī** (1437) — the most accurate astronomical handbook + star catalogue produced anywhere in the world between Ptolemy (+150) and Tycho Brahe (+1580).

Samarkand is the direct institutional + technical heir of the [[maragha-observatory|Maragha Observatory]] (1259) + the immediate predecessor of [[tycho-brahe|Tycho Brahe]]'s European observational programme. It is the high point of the Islamic state-observatory tradition.

## The institution

Ulugh Beg himself was a serious astronomer — not just a royal patron. The Samarkand staff included three of the leading mathematician-astronomers of the period:

- **Jamshīd al-Kāshī** (d. 1429) — the leading Islamic mathematician of his generation, author of the *Miftāḥ al-ḥisāb* ("Key to Arithmetic"), computer of π to 16 decimal places. Senior mathematician until his death.
- **Qāḍīzāda al-Rūmī** (c. 1364–1436) — from Bursa in Anatolia, the senior astronomer in residence; succeeded al-Kāshī as senior mathematician.
- **ʿAlī Qushji** (c. 1403–1474) — student + later head of the observatory after Qāḍīzāda's death; after the dispersal of the institution following Ulugh Beg's assassination, Qushji took the Samarkand astronomical tradition to Istanbul where he taught at the Hagia Sophia + transmitted Samarkand methods into Ottoman astronomy.

## The Fakhri sextant

The defining instrument was a **40-m-radius vertical meridian arc** — about a third of a full circle, partly constructed into bedrock to ensure stability. The Fakhri allowed unprecedented precision in measuring **solar + planetary altitudes at meridian transit**: angular precision of ~2 arcseconds, far exceeding any prior pre-telescopic instrument. The size + precision are what allowed the Zīj-i Sulṭānī's star catalogue + planetary tables to substantially improve on Ptolemy.

## The Zīj-i Sulṭānī star catalogue

Ulugh Beg's catalogue lists **994 stars** with positions reduced to the epoch 1437. It is the **first significant independent reobservation of the Ptolemaic star catalogue** since Ptolemy himself (c. +150) — closing a **1287-year gap**. Mean error ~0.5° (vs. Ptolemy's ~1.0°). The catalogue passes directly to Europe in the 17th century (Hyde's 1665 Latin edition), where it is used + improved on by Tycho Brahe + then by John Flamsteed.

## The Samarkand → Tycho transmission

The chain is documented: Ulugh Beg's star catalogue is published in Persian + Arabic copies; reaches Europe via Ottoman channels; published by Hyde at Oxford in 1665 (Latin translation); Tycho Brahe explicitly cites Ulugh Beg as a predecessor + comparator for his own catalogue. The Samarkand observational programme is **the immediate technical antecedent** of European observational astronomy's Renaissance breakthrough.
