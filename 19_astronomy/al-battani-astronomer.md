# al-Battānī

The most accurate observational astronomer of the ninth century and, for six hundred years, the Arabic astronomer Latin Europe actually used. He observed at al-Raqqa on the Euphrates from 877 to 918 — a forty-year baseline, which is what makes his numbers good — and set the results in a handbook, the *Kitāb al-Zīj*, that Europe knew as *De motu stellarum*.

If the vault wants one clean, uncontested case of Islamic astronomy passing into European astronomy, this is it.

## What he found

Al-Battānī did not try to overturn Ptolemy. He worked inside the *Almagest*'s geometry and attacked its numbers, which was the harder and more useful job.

- **The tropical year**: 365 days, 5 hours, 46 minutes, 24 seconds — about two and a half minutes short of the true value, and far better than anything before him.
- **The obliquity of the ecliptic**: about 23°35′, a genuine improvement on Ptolemy.
- **Precession**: about 54.5 arcseconds a year — roughly one degree in sixty-six years — against Ptolemy's one degree per century, an error of nearly a third that had stood unchallenged for seven hundred years.
- **The motion of the solar apogee.** This is the real discovery. Ptolemy had treated the line of solar apsides as fixed with respect to the equinoxes. Al-Battānī measured the solar apogee and found it some seventeen degrees from where Ptolemy had it — too much to be error. The apogee moves. Turning one of Ptolemy's constants into a variable is a structural result, and it is the finding that al-Zarqālī in Toledo, and eventually Copernicus, take up.
- **Method**: he computes in sines and cotangents, tabulating the cotangent as the *umbra versa*, the "turned shadow" of a gnomon, and states relations for solving spherical triangles that Ptolemy's chord-based methods handled far more clumsily.

## Why he, and not the others, reached Europe

Two ordinary practical reasons, and they are worth naming because transmission usually turns on exactly this kind of thing.

First, **his book was translated early**. Plato of Tivoli rendered the zīj into Latin at Barcelona around 1116, as *De motu stellarum* — before Gerard of Cremona's Toledo programme was properly under way. A Castilian version followed under Alfonso X. It was printed at Nuremberg in 1537, which put it on the desks of the generation that included Copernicus, and again at Bologna in 1645.

Second, **his parameters got into the tables**. Al-Zarqālī built the Toledan Tables from al-Battānī and the Andalusian Sindhind together; the Toledan Tables became the Alfonsine Tables; the Alfonsine Tables were the working computational instrument of European astronomy until the seventeenth century. A European astronomer who had never heard of al-Battānī was still using his year length.

## The reference case for a contested question

The vault holds two Islamic-to-Copernicus claims, and they are not on the same evidentiary footing. Keeping the difference sharp is the point of having tiers at all.

**Al-Battānī → Copernicus is documented.** Copernicus names him in *De revolutionibus*, uses his parameters, and could have got the book from a printed Latin edition. Swerdlow and Neugebauer's 1984 reconstruction shows the borrowings parameter by parameter. There is no gap in the chain.

**Maragha → Copernicus is inferred.** Copernicus names nobody, no Latin translation of Ibn al-Shāṭir or of al-Ṭūsī's *Tadhkira* is known, and the case rests on the mathematical identity of the constructions. It is a strong argument and a serious body of scholarship, but it is an argument, and the vault labels it as one. See [[astronomy-tusi-couple]] and [[ibn-al-shatir-astronomer]] for that debate with both sides attached.

A note on the long tail: because al-Battānī recorded eclipse timings with unusual care, his observations remained live scientific data for centuries after his astronomy was obsolete, entering the eighteenth- and nineteenth-century work on the Moon's long-term acceleration. Very few medieval texts stay useful as *measurements*.
