# Stonehenge Solstice Alignment

The **Stonehenge solstice axis** is the architectural-astronomical alignment of the Late Neolithic / Early Bronze Age sarsen monument on Salisbury Plain. The axis runs from the **Heel Stone** outside the circle to the centre — framing **midsummer sunrise** (and reciprocally **midwinter sunset** in the opposite direction). The sarsen circle + trilithons (the iconic Stonehenge architecture) are constructed c. -2500 organized around this axis.

Stonehenge is one of the two most-studied solstitially-aligned monuments of Neolithic northwest Europe (the other being [[astronomy-newgrange-solstice|Newgrange]], ~-3200) and one of the earliest preserved examples of large-scale **architectural astronomy** — the integration of astronomical observation into permanent ritual landscape.

## The axis

The Heel Stone (a single ~30-tonne sarsen, leaning at ~30° from vertical, ~75 m north-east of the circle centre) marks the **midsummer-sunrise direction** when viewed from the centre of the sarsen circle. The opposite-direction view (south-west) frames the **midwinter sunset** through the gap of the great south-western trilithon.

Pearson 2012 argues that the **midwinter alignment was the load-bearing one for the Neolithic builders** — multiple lines of evidence (the Durrington Walls midwinter feasting deposits, the geographic relation to Woodhenge, the funerary context of the Stonehenge dead) point to midwinter as the ritual focus, with midsummer as the reciprocal axis-direction rather than the primary observational target. The popular modern association of Stonehenge with midsummer sunrise is partly a Druid-revival overlay.

## Astronomical scope

Beyond the solstice axis, additional putative alignments have been proposed (Aubrey-Hole lunar alignments, station-stones marking lunar standstills, etc.). Ruggles 1999 is the standard critical sifting: the **solstice axis itself is robust** + a few station-stone alignments are statistically credible; the more elaborate "Stonehenge eclipse-predictor" interpretations (Hoyle 1977; Hawkins 1965) have not survived statistical scrutiny.
