# Maragha Observatory

The most influential observatory of the medieval Islamic world. Founded in 1259 CE under Mongol-Ilkhanate patronage from Hulagu Khan + organized by [[al-tusi-astronomer|Naṣīr al-Dīn al-Ṭūsī]] in northwest Iran. The Maragha observatory was the institutional + intellectual locus of the **Maragha school of astronomy** — the program of reforming Ptolemaic planetary theory whose technical content transmits via Renaissance Italy into Copernicus's *De Revolutionibus*.

## The institution

Maragha was the most well-resourced observatory in the world at the time of its founding. The Mongols supplied lavish funding (a percentage of pious endowments across the Ilkhanate), elaborate physical infrastructure (a large library, instrument workshops, residential quarters for scholars + students), and a multilingual + multinational staff that included Arabic-speaking + Persian-speaking + Chinese astronomers (Fao Mun-ji / Fu Mengchi).

The principal output was the **Zīj-i Īlkhānī** (see [[astronomy-zij-i-ilkhani]]) — the astronomical handbook completed c. 1272 — and a sustained program of theoretical-mathematical innovation around the **Tūsī couple** + related geometric constructions for replacing Ptolemy's equant.

## The Maragha → Copernicus transmission (the load-bearing claim)

The Maragha school's technical content reaches Copernicus's *De Revolutionibus* (1543) — documented as one of the cleanest documented Islamic→Renaissance scientific-transmission cases in the history of science. The chain runs:

**Tusi (Maragha, c. 1260)** → al-'Urdi + al-Shirazi (Maragha-school 13th-14th c.) → Ibn al-Shatir (Damascus, c. 1350) → unknown transmission to Italy (16th c.) → Copernicus's *De Revolutionibus* (1543).

The key evidence:
- Copernicus's lunar model is mathematically equivalent to Ibn al-Shatir's (Roberts 1957)
- Copernicus's Mercury model uses an identical mathematical construction (Hartner 1973)
- Copernicus's use of the Tūsī couple appears in *De Revolutionibus* III.4 in geometric form identical to Tusi's

Copernicus does not cite the Islamic sources. The detailed mathematical identity is too dense to be coincidence; the transmission must have happened, even if the precise channel is still being investigated (Saliba 2007 ch. 7).
