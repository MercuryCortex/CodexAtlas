# The Saros Cycle

The **Saros** is the Babylonian discovery that eclipses **recur**: after a period of **223 synodic months** — about **18 years, 11⅓ days** (6585⅓ days) — the Sun, Moon, and lunar nodes return to nearly the same geometry, so a very similar eclipse repeats. It is the pivot on which astronomy turns from *reading* the sky to *predicting* it. Where the older Mesopotamian tradition treated an eclipse as a portent to be interpreted and warded off, the Saros makes an eclipse a **calculable, periodic event** — arguably the first great achievement of quantitative predictive astronomy, and one the Atlas tracks downstream through Hipparchus, Ptolemy's *Almagest*, and the geared Saros dial of the [[astronomy-antikythera-mechanism|Antikythera Mechanism]].

## Why 223 months works

An eclipse can happen only when a syzygy (new or full Moon) falls near a lunar **node** — one of the two points where the Moon's tilted orbit crosses the ecliptic. The Saros works because a single interval is very nearly a whole number of *three* different lunar months at once:

- **223 synodic months** (new-Moon to new-Moon: sets the phase) ≈
- **242 draconic / nodical months** (node-return: sets the eclipse geometry) ≈
- **239 anomalistic months** (perigee-return: sets the Moon's speed and thus eclipse magnitude)

Because all three cycles close together after 223 months, the *whole configuration* repeats, and so does the eclipse. The match is not perfect — the anomalistic term drifts slightly — so eclipses in one "Saros series" gradually change magnitude and eventually die out over ~1,200–1,500 years, but across a human lifetime the recurrence is strikingly reliable.

The leftover **⅓ of a day** matters: in that third of a day the Earth turns roughly **120°**, so each successive eclipse in a series is visible about a third of the world further west. Three Saros cycles — the **Exeligmos**, 669 synodic months ≈ **54 years 33 days** — return the eclipse to nearly the same longitude, which is exactly why the Antikythera Mechanism carries a subsidiary Exeligmos dial giving a 0-, 8-, or 16-hour correction.

## In the Babylonian astronomical record

The Saros is not a text but a *scheme* recovered from Babylonian tablets. Its precondition is the **Astronomical Diaries** — the continuous night-by-night observational program run at Babylon from **-652** onward (Sachs & Hunger) — which built the long, dated record of eclipses that alone can reveal an 18-year periodicity. From this baseline the Late-Babylonian scribes produced **eclipse tables organized in 18-year (223-month) cycles** — the so-called **"Saros Canon"** and related texts edited by Aaboe, Britton, Henderson, Neugebauer & Sachs (1991). These tabulate eclipse *possibilities* by month across successive 18-year cycles, distinguishing months when an eclipse could occur from those when it could not. Lunar eclipses (visible from a whole hemisphere) are predicted well; solar eclipses, whose visibility depends on the observer's exact location, are flagged as *possible* rather than guaranteed — the same probabilistic character the Maya eclipse table shows independently.

This is the birth of prediction out of divination. The eclipse-omen series [[phase-1-019-enuma-anu-enlil|Enuma Anu Enlil]] read each eclipse as a message about the king and the state; the Saso­s scheme, produced by the same scribal institution, computes *when the next one is due*. The two coexist — the same experts who tabulate the cycle still perform the substitute-king ritual — but the intellectual center of gravity has shifted from interpretation to calculation.

## Cross-tradition significance (transmission and convergence)

The Saros anchors two very different kinds of cross-tradition link, and they must be kept apart.

**Documented transmission — Babylon to the Greek world.** The Babylonian 223-month eclipse period passes, by a well-traced route, into Hellenistic Greek astronomy. **Hipparchus** (2nd c. BCE) works with the Babylonian eclipse records and period-relations; **Ptolemy** records the relation explicitly in *Almagest* IV.2 (223 synodic = 239 anomalistic = 242 draconic months) and calibrates his lunar theory on 8th-c.-BCE Babylonian eclipse observations (IV.6 ff.). Around **-100** the same 223-month cycle is physically **gear-encoded** on the Antikythera Mechanism's back-dial spiral, with the Exeligmos correction on a subsidiary dial. This is one of the most secure transmission chains in the history of science: the *same numbers*, with the same origin, moving from cuneiform tablet to bronze gearwork to Greek text.

**Independent convergence — Babylon and the Maya.** The Maya eclipse-warning table in the **[[phase-8-003-dresden-codex-maya|Dresden Codex]]** (405 lunar months = 11,960 days) encodes eclipse recurrence through node-return periodicity with **no contact** with the Old World. Here the parallel is *convergent*, not inherited: two civilizations, watching the same sky with patient record-keeping, independently discovered that eclipses run on a fixed lunar-month cycle. The Saros and the Dresden table together make the strongest possible case that eclipse-cycle prediction is a natural attractor of any long-lived naked-eye astronomy — reached twice, without borrowing.

The [[sacred-eclipse|sacred eclipse]] hub catalogs the near-universal *mythic* response to eclipses (the devouring demon, the ritual noise, the sacrificed substitute). The Saros is the quantitative substrate under that symbol: the moment one tradition learned that the devouring was on a timetable.

## Disputes

- **The name "Saros" is a modern misnomer.** The Babylonians had no special word for the 18-year eclipse cycle. The term was applied to it by **Edmond Halley in 1691**, who took the word *sáros* from the 11th-century Byzantine *Suda* lexicon — where it actually renders a Babylonian *šár* (3600) and does **not** denote 223 months. **Neugebauer** (1975) flags the usage as an established but historically inaccurate convention. The cycle is genuinely Babylonian; the label "Saros" is not. The Atlas retains the conventional name while flagging its provenance here.
- **How the Babylonians *derived* it.** Whether the 223-month period was found by direct inspection of the eclipse record, or built up from shorter component periods (the "Saros" as a combination of Goal-Year-type period-relations), is a technical debate in the specialist literature (Aaboe et al. 1991; Steele 2000; Brack-Bernsen). What is not in doubt is that the mature scheme is attested in the tablets and used predictively.
- **Solar-eclipse prediction.** The Saros predicts lunar eclipses reliably but only flags solar-eclipse *possibilities*, because solar visibility is local. Claims that the Babylonians (or the Antikythera Mechanism) "predicted solar eclipses" precisely overstate the case; both give danger-windows, corrected by the Exeligmos for time-of-day.
