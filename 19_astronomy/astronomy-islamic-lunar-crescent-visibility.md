# Islamic Lunar Crescent Visibility (Ruʾyat al-Hilāl)

## Identity

**Ruʾyat al-hilāl** ("sighting of the crescent") is the Islamic science and religious practice of determining when the thin new lunar crescent will first become visible in the western twilight after conjunction — the observation that legally opens each month of the [[islamic-hijri-calendar|Hijri calendar]]. Because the Hijri calendar is purely lunar, the first sighting of the *hilāl* fixes the start of Ramadan (the fast), of Shawwāl (Eid al-Fitr), and of Dhū al-Ḥijjah (the Hajj and Eid al-Adha); a single day's error moves the observance of hundreds of millions of people. This made the *astronomical* problem of first crescent visibility one of the few points where naked-eye astronomy is bound directly to religious law — and it drove a continuous tradition of visibility criteria from the 9th century CE to the present.

The phenomenon is genuinely hard. Just after conjunction the Moon is a razor-thin arc, low over the horizon, drowned in twilight, and visible for only minutes before it sets. Whether it can be seen depends on the **age** of the Moon (time since conjunction), the **lag time** (interval between sunset and moonset), the **arc of vision** (ARCV, the Moon–Sun altitude difference), the **elongation** (arc of light, ARCL), the **crescent width**, and atmospheric clarity. Below an elongation of roughly **7° — the Danjon limit** — the crescent cannot be seen at all, because too little of the illuminated limb is turned toward the observer.

## The medieval criteria (in the Islamic astronomical tradition)

The predictive science begins not with an Islamic invention but with an **inherited "Indian criterion"**: the Moon is judged visible if it *sets a sufficient interval after the Sun* — a threshold on the difference in setting times, equivalent to a condition on the oblique-ascension difference. This rule reached Arabic astronomy through [[al-khwarizmi|al-Khwārizmī]]'s *Zīj al-Sindhind* (c. 830 CE) and appears in the tables of Ḥabash al-Ḥāsib (fl. c. 850). Kennedy's *Survey of Islamic Astronomical Tables* (1956) catalogued dozens of such **visibility tables** (*jadāwil al-ruʾya*) across the *zīj* literature; Hogendijk (1988) analysed the early examples in detail.

Islamic astronomers then tested and refined the criterion against observation:

- **Al-Battānī** (d. 929, the Latin *Albategnius*), in his *al-Zīj al-Ṣābiʾ*, gave a visibility rule keyed to the arc between Sun and Moon at sunset.
- **Ibn Yūnus** (d. 1009), in the great Fatimid *al-Zīj al-Ḥākimī al-Kabīr* of Cairo, supplied further visibility material.
- **[[al-biruni|Al-Bīrūnī]]** (d. c. 1048) discussed the problem in his *al-Qānūn al-Masʿūdī*, treating the several astronomical factors explicitly.

By the later Middle Ages, dedicated crescent-visibility tables were a standard chapter of the *zīj* — the same handbooks (such as the [[astronomy-zij-i-ilkhani|Zīj-i Īlkhānī]]) whose computed lunar and solar positions the calculation-based prediction of first sighting actually requires.

## The transmission chain and the modern criteria

The Islamic crescent science sits in the middle of a long, largely **documented transmission chain**, not a case of isolated invention:

- **Babylon.** The Babylonian month began at first crescent sighting, and Babylonian mathematical astronomy already *computed* first visibility through the "lunar six," chiefly **NA₁**, the sunset-to-moonset interval (Neugebauer 1975). This is the deep ancestor of both the practice and the problem — the [[babylonian-calendar|Babylonian calendar]] endpoint.
- **India.** The setting-time-difference criterion of siddhāntic astronomy (the [[astronomy-surya-siddhanta|Sūrya-Siddhānta]] tradition) is the immediate source of the earliest Arabic rule.
- **Islam.** Transmitted via al-Khwārizmī, refined by al-Battānī, Ibn Yūnus and al-Bīrūnī, and tabulated across the *zīj* genre.
- **Modern astrophysics.** **Bruin (1977)** reframed the question in terms of crescent width and twilight contrast; **Ilyas (1994)** proposed altitude/azimuth-difference criteria and an **International Lunar Date Line**; **Yallop (1997)** introduced the widely adopted **q-test** (from ARCV and crescent width, calibrated on 295 sightings); and **Odeh (2004)** produced the large-dataset criterion behind today's global visibility maps.

Ptolemy had already flagged the problem in the *Almagest* but, as Bruin notes, offered no more than the Babylonian rule of thumb that the Moon cannot be seen less than about a day after conjunction — so the substantive advance runs through the Indian and Islamic line.

## Calculation vs sighting (the jurisprudential debate)

The distinctively Islamic feature is that the crescent is a matter of **law**, not only astronomy. The governing hadith — *"Fast when you see it and break your fast when you see it; if it is obscured from you, complete thirty days"* (Bukhārī, Muslim) — is read by the majority of classical jurists to require **actual visual sighting** (*ruʾya*), with calculation (*ḥisāb*) inadmissible as a legal basis. A **minority juristic tradition** held that astronomical calculation could establish or exclude the possibility of sighting.

The debate is unresolved and very much live. Modern practice splits: some authorities require local naked-eye sighting; others accept "sighting anywhere in the Muslim world"; others adopt calculation-based tabular calendars (e.g. the Saudi **Umm al-Qurā** civil calendar, and the Fiqh Council of North America / ISNA's 2006 shift to a calculated criterion). The recurring result is that Ramadan and the two Eids begin on **different days in different countries** — the practical face of a thousand-year-old dispute between the empirical and the computational.

## Cross-tradition significance

The honest reading here is **transmission, not convergence**. Unlike the [[astronomy-lunar-mansions|lunar mansions]] — where independent invention on a shared sky is a serious possibility — the Islamic crescent-visibility *criterion* is demonstrably **descended** from a specific Indian rule (via al-Khwārizmī's *Zīj al-Sindhind*), which itself stands downstream of the older Babylonian first-visibility computation. Kennedy (1956) and Hogendijk (1988) trace the Indian → Islamic step through the surviving tables; the Babylonian → Indian step is more reconstructed but astronomically continuous. The line then runs *forward* into modern astrophysics, since Bruin, Ilyas, Yallop and Odeh are self-consciously solving the same problem the medieval astronomers posed.

The genuine *parallel* — as opposed to inheritance — is with the [[hebrew-calendar|Hebrew calendar]]: rabbinic Judaism also began each month on the testimony of witnesses who had *seen* the new crescent before the court (Mishnah, Rosh Hashanah), before eventually replacing sighting with calculation. Islam and Judaism thus represent the two great Near-Eastern crescent-sighting month-starts that diverged on the same question Islamic jurists still debate: **when may calculation stand in for the eye?** No other tradition made naked-eye first-crescent detection a binding legal act for an entire community's ritual year in quite this way — which is why the science and the fiqh grew up entangled.

## Disputes

- **Where the criterion originates.** That the earliest Arabic rule is the transmitted "Indian criterion" is well established (Kennedy 1956); how far back beyond India it runs — and how directly the Babylonian NA₁ computation fed the Indian rule — is less securely documented and is treated here at T2.
- **Which modern criterion is best.** Bruin, Ilyas, Yallop and Odeh give measurably different visibility boundaries; the discrepancies at the margin (the "may need optical aid" zone) are exactly where disputed sightings occur, and no single criterion is universally accepted.
- **Ḥisāb vs ruʾya.** The legal admissibility of calculation is genuinely unsettled across the schools and modern councils; the naming and dating of the classical minority position permitting *ḥisāb* is itself debated (see self-flags).
- **Claimed sightings below the Danjon limit.** Reports of crescents seen at elongations under ~7° are treated by astronomers as observational error or misidentification, but they periodically drive calendar disputes.
