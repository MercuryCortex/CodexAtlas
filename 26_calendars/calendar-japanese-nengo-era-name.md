# Japanese Nengō (Era-Name) Calendar System

The **nengō** (年号, "year-name") or **gengō** (元号) system is the Japanese tradition of designating eras of historical time by named reigns, with year-numbering restarting at 1 with each new era. Modern Japan uses nengō dating in parallel with Gregorian dating — Japanese passports, currency, government documents, and many newspapers list both. The current era is **Reiwa** (令和, "beautiful harmony"), beginning **1 May 2019** with the accession of Emperor Naruhito. As of 2026 CE, the current Japanese date is **Reiwa 8**.

## Historical origin: 645 CE Taika

The first Japanese nengō, **Taika** (大化, "great change"), was proclaimed in 645 CE following the Isshi Incident (a coup that brought the Soga regent family down). The choice of nengō system was a direct adoption of the Chinese imperial practice — Chinese emperors had used era-names since the Han dynasty (specifically Emperor Wu's *Jianyuan* era, 140 BCE). The Japanese adoption marked a deliberate political-cultural alignment with the Chinese imperial model.

Pre-1868, era-names changed for various reasons:
- Accession of a new emperor (sometimes)
- Inauspicious events (earthquake, fire, comet) — the era-name was changed to mark a fresh start
- Auspicious omens (rare bird sightings, lucky calendrical events) — the era-name was changed to celebrate
- Calendrical-cycle thresholds (changes at sexagenary-cycle thresholds in some periods)

Some emperors had **multiple era-names** during their reign; one nengō (Engi, 901-923) lasted 22 years; another (Eihō, 1081-1084) only 3. The pre-1868 era-name change frequency averaged ~5-7 years.

## Ichi-sei ichi-gen — the Meiji rule

The **Meiji Restoration** (1868) instituted the **"one reign, one era-name"** rule (一世一元 / *ichi-sei ichi-gen*). From the Meiji era forward, the era-name changes only at imperial accession or death. The first four ichi-sei ichi-gen eras:

- **Meiji** (明治, "enlightened rule"): 1868-1912 — Emperor Mutsuhito
- **Taishō** (大正, "great righteousness"): 1912-1926 — Emperor Yoshihito
- **Shōwa** (昭和, "enlightened harmony"): 1926-1989 — Emperor Hirohito
- **Heisei** (平成, "achieving peace"): 1989-2019 — Emperor Akihito
- **Reiwa** (令和, "beautiful harmony"): 2019-present — Emperor Naruhito

The era-name choice is significant. Reiwa is the first nengō drawn from a Japanese source (the Manyōshū, ~759 CE) rather than a Chinese classical text — a deliberate post-2019 nationalist gesture. The choice was made by a panel of literary, historical, and political experts and announced by Prime Minister Shinzō Abe on 1 April 2019, one month before the actual accession.

## Calendrical reform: 1873 Gregorian adoption

In 1873 (Meiji 6), Japan adopted the **Gregorian calendar** for all civil purposes, displacing the previously-used Chinese-derived lunisolar calendar (the Senmyō calendar used 862-1684, the Jōkyō 1684-1755, the Hōryaku 1755-1798, the Kansei 1798-1844, and the Tenpō 1844-1872). The transition occurred at the new year: **3 December Tenpō 12 (Meiji 5) was followed directly by 1 January Meiji 6** (= 1 January 1873 CE), eliminating the rest of the lunisolar year.

This was one of the most-dramatic single-step calendrical transitions in history. The Japanese state explicitly adopted Western timekeeping for Westernization-modernization signaling. The traditional lunisolar calendar (kyūreki, 旧暦, "old calendar") survives in marginal use for agricultural-festival scheduling and lunar-new-year observances in some communities.

## How dual dating works

Modern Japanese documents typically show both Gregorian and nengō dating:
- Driver's licenses: nengō dating primary
- Passports: both shown
- Newspapers: vary by publication; conservative outlets use nengō, internationally-oriented outlets Gregorian, most use both
- Public-school history textbooks: both
- Corporate documents: typically both or Gregorian only

Computing nengō from Gregorian: subtract the era's start-year and add 1.
- 2026 Gregorian = 2026 - 2019 + 1 = **Reiwa 8** (start of Reiwa = 2019 May 1, so any 2026 date is Reiwa 8 except dates 1 Jan - 30 Apr 2019 which were Heisei 31)
- 1995 Gregorian = 1995 - 1989 + 1 = **Heisei 7**
- 1945 Gregorian = 1945 - 1926 + 1 = **Shōwa 20** (the year of the WWII surrender, recurringly cited as "Shōwa 20-nen")

## Cross-tradition position

**Era-naming as East Asian shared convention**: the nengō system is a Sinitic-cultural-sphere shared practice. Era-names were used by:
- **China** (140 BCE through 1911 fall of Qing dynasty) — the originating tradition
- **Korea** (Three Kingdoms period through 1910 Japanese annexation) — adopted era-names following Chinese model, sometimes Korean-coined, sometimes imitative of Chinese
- **Vietnam** (Đại Cồ Việt period onward) — adopted era-names following Chinese model
- **Japan** (645 CE onward) — adopted era-names; the only one of the four still in active use

The nengō system is the **only surviving traditional East Asian era-name calendar in active state use**. The People's Republic of China uses Gregorian since 1949; the ROC (Taiwan) uses Republic of China dating (民國紀年 / *Mínguó jìnián*, Year 1 = 1912 founding of the Republic). North Korea uses Juche dating (Year 1 = 1912 Kim Il-sung's birth). South Korea + Vietnam use Gregorian. Japan's persistence with nengō is uniquely traditional.

**Cross-tradition era-naming**: the nengō pattern of restart-with-each-reign era-naming parallels:
- **Roman regnal dating** (Augustus 14 AD = Year 41 of Augustus) — used in some inscriptions but not the dominant Roman dating convention
- **Egyptian regnal dating** — every pharaoh's reign restarted at Year 1; the dominant ancient Egyptian dating
- **British "regnal year" dating** in legal documents — "in the 4th year of the reign of King Charles III" is still used in formal British legal contexts
- **Thai Buddhist calendar regnal-rama overlay** — kings of the Chakri dynasty are numbered Rama I through Rama X

The nengō is the most-fully-elaborated and most-currently-used regnal-style dating system. Its persistence in the Reiwa era reflects the Japanese cultural-political investment in the imperial institution as a continuity-marker.

**Era-name semantic conservatism**: nengō are chosen from classical texts and chosen for auspicious-poetic resonance. The 248 eras since Taika 645 show remarkable semantic conservatism — recurring themes include peace, harmony, light, virtue, prosperity, gentleness. The era-name is read as a state-prayer for the era. This makes the nengō system one of the few persistent state-level **performative-utterance** practices — the act of naming an era is a state speech-act with implicit hope-content.
