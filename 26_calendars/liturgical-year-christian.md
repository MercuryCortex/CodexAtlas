# Christian Liturgical Year (Advent → Pentecost → Ordinary Time)

The annual ritual cycle of Christian worship — a calendrical SUPERSTRUCTURE laid over the underlying civil calendar (Julian, then for most Western churches Gregorian after 1582) that organizes the Sunday-by-Sunday rhythm of Christian sacramental + scriptural-reading + commemorative life. Distinct from a "calendar" in the narrow sense — it is a **ritual rhythm** of two interlocking annual cycles: the **TEMPORALE** (movable feasts dependent on Easter) and the **SANCTORALE** (fixed feasts of saints + Mary + the Lord).

## The two cycles

**TEMPORALE — movable, Easter-dependent**:
- **Advent** — 4 Sundays before December 25, penitential preparation for the Nativity
- **Christmastide** — Dec 25 to Epiphany (Jan 6) or to the Baptism of the Lord
- **Ordinary Time I** — between Epiphany and Lent (varies in length depending on Easter date)
- **Lent** — 40 days of fasting before Easter, beginning Ash Wednesday (West) or Clean Monday (East), recalling Christ's wilderness fast
- **Holy Week** — Palm Sunday, Maundy Thursday (institution of the Eucharist), Good Friday (Crucifixion), Holy Saturday (descent into Hell), Pascha
- **Eastertide** — 50 days from Easter to Pentecost
- **Ordinary Time II** — the long "green" season after Pentecost, lasting until Advent

**SANCTORALE — fixed, civil-calendar-anchored**:
- Mary's feasts (Annunciation Mar 25, Assumption/Dormition Aug 15, Nativity of Mary Sep 8, Immaculate Conception Dec 8)
- Apostles' and saints' feast days (each saint commemorated on a fixed civil date — usually the death date as *dies natalis* "birthday into heaven")
- Fixed Christological feasts (Transfiguration Aug 6, Exaltation of the Cross Sep 14)
- All Saints (Nov 1) followed by All Souls (Nov 2)

The annual liturgical experience is the **superposition** of both cycles: a single Sunday might fall in Lent (temporale) while also being the feast of an apostle (sanctorale) — and the liturgical books contain priority rules for which observance wins.

## The Easter computation (Computus Paschalis)

The most-mathematically-sophisticated element of the Christian liturgical year is the **Computus** — the algorithm for computing Easter's date. By [[event-council-of-nicaea-325]] canon, Easter is:

> the first Sunday after the first ecclesiastical full moon that occurs on or after March 21 (the conventional spring equinox)

This requires:
1. A **lunisolar calculation** (find the relevant ecclesiastical full moon)
2. A **Sunday-finding calculation** (find the next Sunday after that)
3. Synchronization with the underlying solar calendar

**Dionysius Exiguus's 525 CE codification** of the 19-year Metonic-style Paschal cycle made the computation standardizable across Western Christendom. The Computus was a major driver of medieval mathematical-astronomical work — including Bede's *De Temporum Ratione* (725), the Carolingian computus revival, and Roger Bacon's calls for calendrical reform that anticipated the [[gregorian-calendar]] reform of 1582.

The **Eastern Orthodox liturgical year** uses the same Nicaea 325 Paschal canon but computes it within the [[julian-calendar]] framework — generating the recurring 1-, 4-, or 5-week gap between Eastern and Western Easter dates.

## The December 25 Nativity question

The fixed Nativity date of **December 25** has two main scholarly explanations:

- **Older "history-of-religions" thesis** (Frazer, then Bauer / Usener): Christmas was set to December 25 to compete with the Sol Invictus *dies natalis Solis Invicti* festival instituted by Aurelian in 274 CE — a Christianization of the Roman winter-solstice pagan festival
- **Newer "computation" thesis** (Talley 1991, Hijmans 2009, Roll 1995): Dec 25 was independently arrived at via a tradition that placed Christ's death at March 25 (Annunciation date) and applied a "perfect-life" / *concordatio* assumption identifying conception with death-date; 9 months from Mar 25 = Dec 25 Nativity

The two theses are not mutually exclusive — the [[event-council-of-nicaea-325]]-era fixing of Dec 25 may have leveraged BOTH the computational tradition and the pagan-substitution opportunity. The historiographical debate is one of the cleanest cases of revisionist scholarship overturning a "consensus" Frazerian-substitution story.

## Cross-tradition position

The Christian liturgical year sits at multiple cross-tradition junctures:

1. **Jewish-Passover inheritance** — Pascha (Easter) inherits Passover; the Quartodeciman controversy (2nd c.) was a dispute over whether to keep Pascha on 14 Nisan (the Jewish Passover date) or on the following Sunday. The Nicaea 325 canon settled it in favor of Sunday with computational rather than lunar-Jewish anchoring
2. **Roman solar-religion encounter** — whether Dec 25 represents substitution of Sol Invictus or independent computation, the Christian liturgical year emerged in dialogue with Roman solar religion
3. **Mediterranean cyclical-festival tradition** — the temporale/sanctorale structure parallels other Mediterranean ritual-year systems (e.g., the Egyptian + Greek + Roman ceremonial calendars), generalized into a Christological frame
4. **Mathematical-astronomical knowledge transmission** — the Computus drove medieval European mathematical-astronomical work for ~1,000 years, eventually motivating the Gregorian reform of 1582
5. **Eastern + Western divergence** — the 1054 schism, the 1582 Gregorian reform, and the 1923 Revised Julian adoption produced the modern situation of multiple Christian liturgical calendars in parallel
