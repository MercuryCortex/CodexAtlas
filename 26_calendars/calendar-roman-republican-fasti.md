# Roman Republican Calendar & the Fasti (pre-Julian, to 46 BCE)

## Identity

The Roman Republican calendar is the **lunisolar civil-religious calendar of Rome before Julius Caesar's reform** — a 355-day year of twelve months whose reconciliation with the solar seasons was entrusted not to an astronomical cycle but to the **discretion of the pontifices**. It is the direct ancestor of the [[julian-calendar|Julian]] and hence the [[gregorian-calendar|Gregorian]] calendar: Caesar's 46 BCE reform kept its month-names and its distinctive day-reckoning (Kalends, Nones, Ides) while replacing everything that made it lunar and priest-regulated. In the Atlas it is a distinct node from the Julian calendar — the **pre-reform system plus the fasti** — wired to the Julian node as ancestor, not merged into it.

Two things make it a significant cross-tradition object. First, its **intercalation mechanism**: an intercalary month, **Mercedonius**, inserted at pontifical discretion, whose political abuse let the civil calendar drift roughly three months out of phase with the seasons by 46 BCE. Second, the **fasti** — the published calendar that marked each day with a character-letter (F, N, C, NP, EN) declaring whether legal and public business was religiously lawful that day. The fasti make the Republican calendar the Roman member of the cross-tradition family of **calendars whose days carry a determinative character** governing action.

## Structure: the 355-day year

The Republican year ran twelve months totalling 355 days: four "long" months of 31 days (Martius, Maius, Quintilis, October), February of 28, and the remaining seven of 29. The religious year began with **Martius** (March, the month of [[mars-roman|Mars]] and of the opening of the campaigning season); the numbered late months **Quintilis, Sextilis, September, October, November, December** ("fifth" through "tenth") preserve the memory of an older year that began in March — a fossil the Roman antiquarians read as evidence of a still earlier ten-month "Romulan" calendar of 304 days. The **civil** new year was moved to the **Kalends of Ianuarius (1 January)** in 153 BCE, when the consuls began entering office on that date; January itself is the month of [[janus|Janus]], god of thresholds and beginnings.

Days were not numbered forward from the 1st. Each month had three fixed anchor-days — the **Kalendae (Kalends, the 1st)**, the **Nonae (Nones)**, and the **Idus (Ides)** — and every other day was named by **counting backwards and inclusively** toward the next anchor. In the four long months the Nones fell on the 7th and the Ides on the 15th; in the other eight, on the 5th and the 13th. These three markers are the vestige of the calendar's **lunar origin**: the Kalends was once the new moon (when the *pontifex minor* "called out," *calare*, the day of the Nones), the Nones the first quarter, the Ides the full moon. By the historical Republic the link to the actual moon was long broken and the month-lengths were fixed conventions.

## Intercalation and its abuse

A 355-day year loses about ten days a year against the sun, so the calendar required regular correction. The Republican solution was the intercalary month **Mercedonius** (mensis intercalaris): in an intercalary year, **February was cut short after the Terminalia (23 February)** and a month of **27 days** was inserted, producing a year of **377 or 378 days**. Crucially, **whether to intercalate in any given year was decided by the college of pontiffs** under the Pontifex Maximus — not by any published rule.

This discretion was **notoriously abused**. Because Roman magistracies and tax-farming contracts ran by the civil year, pontiffs (themselves often serving politicians) lengthened years to keep allies in office or shorten enemies' terms, and neglected intercalation during the disruptions of the late Republic. Cicero, Censorinus, Macrobius, and Suetonius all record the resulting chaos: by **46 BCE the civil calendar was roughly three months ahead of the seasons**, with harvest festivals falling in spring. Caesar, as Pontifex Maximus, resolved it by decreeing the **annus confusionis** — a single year of **445 days** (46 BCE), the longest in recorded history — to realign the calendar, then instituting the solar [[julian-calendar|Julian]] scheme from 1 January 45 BCE.

## The fasti: lawful and unlawful days

The **fasti** were the calendar as a public document — a grid listing, for each day, its **character-letter**:

- **F — dies fastus**: a day *lawful* for civil legal business; the praetor could pronounce the three solemn words of jurisdiction (*do, dico, addico*).
- **N — dies nefastus**: an *unlawful* day, on which such acts were religiously forbidden.
- **C — dies comitialis**: a day on which the popular assemblies (*comitia*) could be convened.
- **NP** and **EN (endotercisus / intercisus)**: further classes — NP marking major festival days (its exact sense debated), EN a "split" day, nefastus in the morning and evening but fastus in the middle.

Also entered were the fixed **feriae** (festivals) and the **dies religiosi / atri**, ill-omened anniversary days — most famously the *dies Alliensis* (18 July, the anniversary of Rome's defeat at the Allia) — on which no public business was undertaken. This knowledge had originally been the guarded preserve of the pontiffs; the scribe **Gnaeus Flavius** is said to have **published the fasti in 304 BCE** (the *ius Flavianum*), making the calendar of lawful days public property. The only substantially surviving **pre-Julian painted calendar** is the **Fasti Antiates Maiores** (from Antium, c. 84–55 BCE); dozens of Julian-era stone fasti survive thereafter, and Ovid's poem *Fasti* versifies the festival year (January–June extant).

The word *fasti* — from *dies fasti* — also came to name the year-lists themselves: the **Fasti Consulares** (the roll of eponymous consuls by which Romans dated their years) and the **Fasti Triumphales**. Time-reckoning and the record of civic history were, in Rome, the same document.

## Cross-tradition significance (transmission vs convergence)

**Secure transmission — the ancestor of the Julian/Gregorian line.** The strongest cross-tradition claim about this calendar is genealogical and undisputed: it is the **documented parent of the [[julian-calendar|Julian]] calendar**, which is the parent of the [[gregorian-calendar|Gregorian]]. Caesar's reform was an *edit*, not a replacement — it retained the Roman month-names (later augmented by *Iulius* for Quintilis and *Augustus* for Sextilis) and the entire Kalends/Nones/Ides day-reckoning, changing only the lunar month-basis and the discretionary intercalation. The month-names on a smartphone in 2026 are the Republican calendar's month-names. This is pure transmission-with-refinement, the same lineage relation the Atlas records for Egyptian → Coptic → Ethiopian and for Julian → Gregorian.

**Convergence — the lunisolar type, solved institutionally.** As a 355-day lunisolar calendar the Republican scheme belongs to the same broad **type** as the [[babylonian-calendar|Babylonian]], Greek, [[hebrew-calendar|Hebrew]], and pre-Islamic Arabian calendars: all keep lunar-ish months and periodically insert an intercalary month to chase the solar year. But this shared type is **convergence on a real astronomical constraint, not evidence of borrowing**. What is distinctively Roman — and the reason the calendar failed — is that Rome solved the intercalation problem by **priestly discretion** rather than by a fixed cycle. The [[hebrew-calendar|Hebrew]] and Babylonian calendars pinned intercalation to the 19-year **Metonic cycle**; there is **no evidence Rome ever adopted the Metonic cycle**, and its conspicuous absence is exactly why the Roman year could drift three months off the sun. The parallel to the other lunisolar calendars is typological; the contrast in *mechanism* is the point.

**The fasti as juridical hemerology.** The dies fasti/nefasti system makes Rome a member of the cross-tradition phenomenon catalogued under the [[divinatory-calendar]] theme — calendars in which each day carries a **determinative character** governing what may be done. Yet the Roman case is distinctive: where the Babylonian hemerologies (*Iqqur īpuš*) and the Mesoamerican [[divinatory-calendar|tonalpohualli]] mark days as astrologically or ritually **auspicious/inauspicious**, the Roman marks are primarily **juridical and religious-legal** — they encode *fas*, divine law, determining when the machinery of courts and assemblies may lawfully turn. The resemblance is convergent (independent institutions binding action to the character of the day), not a transmitted omen-science.

## Disputes

- **The intended intercalation cycle.** Michels (1967) reconstructed the *intended* scheme as an alternation of common (355) and intercalary (377/378) years yielding a four-year mean of ~366.25 days — which *over*-corrects the ~365.25-day solar year, so that even had the pontiffs intercalated faithfully the calendar would have drifted the other way. The precise correction cycle the Romans intended (and whether a longer 24-year adjustment was ever contemplated) is debated and cannot be settled from the sources.
- **The meaning of NP and the day-marks.** The sense of the letter-mark **NP** is genuinely unresolved — proposals include *nefastus publicus*, *feriae publicae*, and *fastus principio/nefastus posterior* — and the fine distinctions among the day-classes are reconstructed from limited evidence (Michels 1967; Rüpke 2011).
- **The Numan attribution.** The tradition crediting Numa Pompilius with the 12-month 355-day calendar (and Romulus with a prior ten-month year) is **antiquarian legend**, not documentary history; the calendar's real origin — including possible Etruscan influence — is undetermined. The dates in this node's header therefore mark a *traditional* rather than an attested starting point.
- **Gnaeus Flavius and the "secret" calendar.** The story that the fasti were a pontifical secret published only in 304 BCE is transmitted by Cicero and Livy but has been questioned by modern scholars as partly aetiological; how genuinely restricted the calendar-knowledge had been is debated.
