# Iranian Jalali Calendar (Omar Khayyam Reform, 1079 CE)

The solar-anchored Iranian calendar — astronomically more accurate than the Gregorian calendar — commissioned in 1074 + completed in 1079 CE by a Seljuk-court commission led by **[[omar-khayyam]]** (1048-1131; mathematician, astronomer, philosopher, and Persian-poetic figure of the *Rubaiyat*) under Sultan Jalal al-Din Malik Shah. Replaced the older Zoroastrian-era solar calendar that had drifted significantly + corrected the Islamic Hijri-lunar's seasonal drift problem in Iranian civil contexts.

## MASSIVE-WIN — mathematical accuracy

The Jalali calendar's average year length is **365.2422 days** — corresponding to the astronomical tropical year. The Gregorian calendar (designed 503 years LATER, 1582) has an average year of **365.2425 days**. The Jalali is therefore *more astronomically accurate* than the Gregorian by ~0.0003 days/year = ~26 seconds/year less drift.

The mathematical achievement reflects Khayyam's stature as one of the most-significant pre-modern mathematicians. The Jalali leap-year rule is more flexible than Gregorian's mechanical "divisible-by-4-except-centuries-not-divisible-by-400" — Khayyam's rule was observational-astronomical (leap year if vernal-equinox-to-vernal-equinox > 365.5 days), with the 33-year-cycle 8-leap-year mathematical approximation as the working algorithm.

Hubble Space Telescope-grade arguments are not needed to appreciate the achievement: Khayyam achieved (1079) calendrical precision that Catholic Europe would not match for another five centuries (Gregory XIII 1582).

## Vernal-equinox anchor + Nowruz

The defining structural feature: **the year begins at the vernal equinox** (Farvardin 1 = March 21 ± 1 day in the Gregorian, depending on equinox timing). This anchor preserves the **pre-Islamic Zoroastrian celebration of Nowruz** — the New Year festival celebrating Ahura Mazda's defeat of Angra Mainyu + the renewal of cosmic order at the spring equinox. The Jalali reform under a Muslim Seljuk Sultan, designed by a Muslim mathematician, deliberately preserved the Zoroastrian-substrate Nowruz festival within the new Islamic-political framework.

This is a cross-tradition continuity case: **Zoroastrian-substrate calendar-festivals survive the Islamic conquest + are institutionalized within Islamic-Iranian state-calendars**. Nowruz today is celebrated by:
- Iranians (Muslim, Zoroastrian, Bahá'í, secular)
- Afghans
- Tajiks + Uzbeks + Kazakhs + Turkmen (Central Asian Persianate)
- Kurds (across Turkey, Iraq, Iran, Syria)
- Azerbaijani + Iranian Diasporas
- UN-recognized as international holiday since 2010

## Cross-tradition position

The Jalali calendar sits at multiple cross-tradition junctures:

1. **Zoroastrian-Islamic continuity** — the calendar preserves Zoroastrian-substrate festival anchoring (Nowruz, Yalda, Mehregan) within Islamic-political framework. The cleanest single Iranian case of pre-Islamic religious-cultural continuity through Islamic-era institutional adoption
2. **Mathematical-astronomical achievement** — Khayyam's 365.2422-day average year is more accurate than Gregorian. The cross-tradition math-comparison favors medieval Iranian astronomy over Renaissance European astronomy on this specific metric
3. **Bahá'í Badíʿ calendar inheritance** — the Bahá'í calendar (see `bahai-badi-calendar.md`) inherits the Iranian vernal-equinox-anchored solar structure; Bahá'u'lláh's Iranian context made the equinox-Nowruz anchoring natural

## Modern usage

The Solar Hijri calendar (the Jalali system with the Hijri-era anchoring) is the **official civil calendar of Iran + Afghanistan** today. Most other Islamic countries use Gregorian-civil + Hijri-religious; Iran's continued use of the Jalali solar calendar is a distinctive Persian-cultural-continuity fact. Current Solar Hijri year 1404 (= 21 March 2025 - 20 March 2026 Gregorian).
