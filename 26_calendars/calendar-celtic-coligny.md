# Coligny Calendar

## Identity

The **Coligny calendar** is a fragmentary bronze **lunisolar** calendar engraved in the **Gaulish** language, recovered in 1897 near Coligny in eastern France and dated by its Roman letter-forms to the **late 2nd century CE**. It is the **single longest surviving text in Gaulish** and the principal direct evidence for pre-Christian Celtic timekeeping. The original plate — about 1.48 m wide and 0.9 m high — was broken in antiquity; roughly forty per cent survives as dozens of fragments now in the Gallo-Roman museum at Lyon. Written in a hybrid **Gallo-Roman** script (Gaulish words, Latin capitals, Roman numerals), it lays out a repeating **five-year cycle of 62 months** and marks every month as either **MAT** ("good/complete") or **ANM(AT)** ("not good/incomplete"). In the Atlas it is placed in the calendar lens as the **Celtic member of the lunisolar type**, beside the [[babylonian-calendar|Babylonian]], [[hebrew-calendar|Hebrew]] and other lunisolar systems on the comparative spine [[calendars-comparative]].

## Structure and text (the bronze)

The plate is ruled into columns, one per month. Each **month** is headed by its name and by the tag **MAT** or **ANM**: the twelve regular months run **Samonios, Dumannios, Riuros, Anagantios, Ogronios, Cutios, Giamonios, Simivisonna, Equos, Elembivios, Edrinios, Cantlos**. Months alternate broadly between **30 days (MAT / "full")** and **29 days (ANM / "hollow")**, tracking the synodic lunation. Each month is cut in two by the word **ATENOUX** ("returning night" / renewal); the days of each half are numbered in Roman numerals, so a date is a month, a half, and a numeral. Individual days carry notations (IVOS, D, N, and others) whose full meaning is only partly recovered.

The **five-year cycle** contains **62 months**: sixty regular months plus **two intercalary months** of 30 days — one inserted *before Samonios* at the head of the cycle and one *between Cutios and Giamonios* in the third year. These extra months drag the lunar count back toward the sun, exactly the **lunisolar** manoeuvre the calendar lens is organised around. The two halves of the year are headed by **Samonios** and **Giamonios** — names built on the Celtic roots for *summer* (*samo-*) and *winter* (*giamo-*) — each opened by a multi-day festival, including the **TRINVX SAMO(nii)**, the "three nights of Samonios."

Because 62 lunations (~1831 days) exceed five solar years (~1826 days) by several days, the plate's own lustrum drifts slowly against the sun, implying a **longer correcting cycle** that the surviving bronze does not preserve. Garrett Olmsted's *The Gaulish Calendar* (1992) reconstructs a **30-year** super-cycle and reads the whole as a precise lunar-solar predictor — a reconstruction often connected to the "thirty-year age" the Gauls are said to reckon (see Disputes). The authoritative text edition remains **Duval & Pinault's *RIG* III** (1986), which also publishes the closely related **Villards-d'Héria** fragment, a second Gaulish calendar plate in the same system.

## Cross-tradition significance

The Coligny calendar is the **Celtic instance of the lunisolar problem** — how to keep months lunar yet the year solar — and its value on the comparative spine is precisely that it solves that problem by its **own arithmetic**. Where the [[babylonian-calendar|Babylonian]] (and, through it, the [[hebrew-calendar|Hebrew]]) calendar reconciles moon and sun on the **19-year Metonic cycle** (seven intercalary months in nineteen years), Coligny inserts **two intercalary months in every five years**. There is **no evidence** that the Gaulish scheme derives from Babylonian or Greek intercalation; the resemblance is **typological convergence** on a real astronomical constraint, not documented transmission. This makes it a clean contrast case against the Metonic family: the same sky, a genuinely different answer.

Two features give it a further comparative reach. First, its **night-first reckoning** and lunar month-count match the classical reports that the Gauls (Caesar, *De Bello Gallico* 6.18) and their druids (Pliny, *Natural History* 16.95) "counted by nights, not days" and measured time by the moon — a rare case where a surviving indigenous document can be set against Greco-Roman ethnography of the same culture. Second, its **MAT / ANM** marking of whole months as favourable or unfavourable places it, at least structurally, near the **hemerological** (good-day/bad-day) calendars of the ancient world — the Mesopotamian day-omens and the Mesoamerican [[divinatory-calendar|divinatory count]] — though whether MAT/ANM is a *religious* auspicious/inauspicious judgement or merely a *technical* full-vs-hollow label is itself unresolved (see Disputes).

## Disputes

- **Samonios = Samhain / the New Year?** The most-cited claim about the calendar is that **Samonios** corresponds to the Irish **[[ritual-samhain|Samhain]]** and marks the Celtic new year. Both names may descend from Proto-Celtic *samon-*, and the **TRINVX SAMO** ("three nights of Samonios") is tempting beside Samhain's own festival nights. **But the equation is contested:** *sam-* means *summer*, and **Giamonios** (*winter*) sits directly opposite Samonios in the year, so a "Samonios = November winter-festival" reading has to explain why the *summer*-named month opens it. Ronald Hutton (*Stations of the Sun*, 1996) and cautious Celtic scholarship warn against projecting the later Irish ritual year onto the Gaulish bronze. The Atlas frames this link as **debated (T3)**, not secure.
- **How "druidic" is it?** The calendar is routinely called a druidic astronomical instrument, on the strength of Pliny's druids and their lunar reckoning. This is **inferential**: nothing on the plate names druids, and the object is a **Gallo-Roman** artefact (Latin script, Roman numerals) that may equally reflect a civic or temple calendar shaped by Roman calendrical culture. The druidic-astronomy framing is a reasonable hypothesis, not an established fact.
- **Olmsted's reconstruction.** Olmsted (1992) reconstructs a full 30-year, highly accurate lunar-solar predictor and a complete text. The reconstruction is **influential but idiosyncratic**; much of it goes beyond what the ~40%-preserved fragments secure, and specialists treat the fuller super-cycle and "predictor" claims with reserve. The conservative baseline is the *RIG* III edition (1986), which reconstructs less.
- **What do MAT and ANM mean?** That **MAT** ~ *matu-* ("good") tags 30-day months and **ANM** ~ *anmatu-* ("not good") tags 29-day months is agreed; **why** is not. The labels may be a purely technical **full-vs-hollow** month notation, an **auspicious-vs-inauspicious** hemerological judgement, or both at once. The correlation with month-length is imperfect, which keeps the question open.
