# Zoroastrian Calendar — Fasli, Shenshai, and Kadmi Recensions

The Zoroastrian religious calendar is a **365-day solar calendar** of 12 thirty-day months + 5 supplementary Gatha days at year-end, with a complex history of recensions resulting in **three modern variants** practiced by different segments of the Zoroastrian community: **Fasli** (Iranian Zoroastrians + 20th-century Parsi reform), **Shenshai** (Indian-Parsi mainstream), and **Kadmi** (Indian-Parsi minority + some Iranian).

## Structure: 12 × 30 + 5 Gatha days

Each of the 12 months bears the name of an Amesha Spenta (Divine Immortal) or yazata (revered being). Each of the 30 days within a month is **also** named after an Amesha Spenta or yazata — so each calendar day has both a month-name and a day-name. When the month-name and day-name coincide (e.g. the day of Mehr within the month of Mehr = the 16th of the 7th month), the day is a **jashan** (festival), celebrated with the relevant divinity's ritual. This produces the **12 jashans of name-coincidence** that structure the Zoroastrian devotional year.

The 5 days at year-end are the **Gatha days**, named after the 5 *gāthās* (hymn-clusters) of Zoroaster's own poetic compositions in the Avesta. These are the most-sacred liturgical period of the year, devoted to recitation of the Gathas + remembrance of departed souls (fravashis). The 5-day epagomenal period is structurally identical to the Egyptian 5-day extra-month (the *heriu renpet* / Coptic *nasi*) and the Mayan *Uayeb* — the cross-tradition pattern of a 365-day calendar with 360 days organized + 5 days set aside for liminal observance.

## The three recensions and the calendar-drift problem

The 365-day Zoroastrian calendar has **no native intercalation** in its Shenshai and Kadmi forms. Without leap-day insertion, the calendar drifts through the seasons by one day every four years (the same drift that the Egyptian civil calendar exhibited). Over centuries, Nowruz (originally a spring equinox festival) drifted away from spring entirely.

**Shenshai (शहंशाही, "imperial / royal")** — the calendar of the Parsi mainstream in India. Uses the Yazdegerdi (Y.Z.) era starting from Yazdegerd III's accession (632 CE, the last Sasanian emperor's accession year). Has drifted ~7 months from the true solar year. Nowruz in Shenshai is currently observed in August, not March.

**Kadmi (कदमी, "ancient")** — a recension instituted by Parsis from Iran (Jamasp Vilayati, 1746) who arrived in India claiming their calendar was "ancient" (whence the name) and one day ahead of Shenshai. This recension also has no intercalation. Kadmi celebrates Nowruz one day before Shenshai. The Kadmi vs. Shenshai split caused major communal conflict in 18th-19th c. Bombay Parsi community.

**Fasli (फसली, "seasonal")** — a 20th-c. reform recension. Adopts a leap-day intercalation to keep Nowruz fixed at the vernal equinox (March 21). The Fasli recension is the standard among **Iranian Zoroastrians** (Iran has used a fixed-Nowruz calendar in various forms since the Sasanian period) and was adopted by **Parsi reformers in India** in the 20th century. The Fasli was officially adopted by the Iranian state in 1925 as the basis of the Iranian Jalali calendar.

## The Yazdegerdi era

All three recensions number years from **Yazdegerd III's accession in 632 CE**. So 2026 CE = approximately Y.Z. 1394 (Shenshai/Kadmi) or Y.Z. 1395 (Fasli, depending on exact reference). The Yazdegerdi era is a rare surviving instance of a calendar-era starting from a specific deposed monarch's accession; it was preserved by Zoroastrian diaspora as a continuity-marker after the Arab conquest dissolved the Sasanian state.

## Cross-tradition position

**Egyptian-derived 365-day structure**: the Zoroastrian 365-day-no-intercalation pattern closely parallels the **Egyptian civil calendar** (also 360 days + 5 epagomenal). Both originate as solar-approximation calendars without leap-year intercalation; both drift through seasons over centuries. The structural similarity has prompted scholarly debate about possible Egyptian-Iranian transmission in the Achaemenid period (Egypt was part of the Achaemenid empire 525-404 BCE + 343-332 BCE), though direct documentation of transmission is absent. The cleanest hypothesis is parallel astronomical reasoning (365 days = best round-number approximation of the solar year) producing convergent calendar structures.

**Cross-tradition calendar-drift cases**: the Zoroastrian Shenshai/Kadmi drift is one of the historically-best-documented cases of a religious community accepting calendar-season divergence rather than reforming the calendar. The Indian Parsi community's persistence with the drifting Shenshai through the 18th-19th-20th centuries reflects a theological-ritual conservatism — the calendar is part of the religious inheritance + reforming it would break continuity with ancestors. The same pattern appears in:
- **Julian-vs-Gregorian Eastern Orthodox** ([[julian-calendar]]) — Old Calendarist communities preserve the Julian calendar against the Gregorian reform; calendar-drift is accepted as the price of continuity
- **Ethiopian Coptic calendar** ([[ethiopian-geez-calendar]]) — preserves the Coptic 365-day structure
- **Coptic Christian** ([[coptic-calendar]]) — preserves the Egyptian calendar legacy

The Parsi Shenshai-Kadmi-Fasli three-way is the most-fractured contemporary religious-calendar community, with active debate within the Parsi community about reform vs. continuity.

**Nowruz as the longest continuous festival**: the Iranian-Zoroastrian Nowruz, celebrated at the vernal equinox, is one of the longest continuously-attested religious festivals in the world — documented in Achaemenid (550-330 BCE) inscriptions + Avesta + Pahlavi books + medieval Persian + modern Iranian state observance. UNESCO inscribed Nowruz as Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2009. Its survival across Zoroastrian, Sasanian, Islamic-Persian, and modern Iranian periods testifies to the depth of the agricultural-cycle festival within Iranian culture.
