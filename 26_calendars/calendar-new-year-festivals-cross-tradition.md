# New-Year Festivals — the Cross-Tradition Year-Renewal Hub

## Identity

This is the **comparative hub** for the year-renewal festival: the rite by which a tradition marks the seam of its year and, in doing so, claims to renew the world. It is not itself a festival and has no calendar of its own — it is a scholarly survey, the *festival* companion to the *structural* survey in [[calendars-comparative]]. Where that node compares calendars by how they reconcile the moon and the sun, this one compares the **festival each calendar produces at its year-boundary**, and states, for each resemblance across traditions, whether it reflects **documented transmission** or **independent convergence**.

The organising idea is **Mircea Eliade's**: in *The Myth of the Eternal Return* (1949), Eliade argued that the New-Year festival is the archaic rite *par excellence* — the moment when the accumulated chaos and "sins" of the expiring year are expelled, the cosmogony is ritually re-actualized, and time itself is **regenerated**. The old year dies into disorder; the festival re-enacts the original ordering of the world (a combat, a creation-recitation, a divine judgment, a purification); the new year begins in restored order. Eliade called this the abolition of profane time and the return to the pure time of origins (*in illo tempore*). The Atlas adopts the **pattern** as an organising lens while flagging, in the Disputes section, that the very universality Eliade claimed for it is the point later scholarship most sharply contests.

## The festivals compared

The members cluster by **where in the solar year each places the boundary** — and the striking fact is that they do *not* agree, which is itself the strongest evidence for convergence over a single diffused festival:

- **Spring equinox.** The **Babylonian Akitu** ([[ritual-akitu-new-year]]) — the flagship — was the twelve-day festival of Nisannu (late March–April): priests recited the *Enūma Eliš* in full (Marduk's combat with Tiamat, the making of the world from her body), the king was stripped of his regalia, struck, and made to recite a negative confession before Marduk, and the divine assembly "fixed the destinies" (*šīmātu*) for the year. The Persian/Zoroastrian **Nowruz** (carried here by [[iranian-jalali-calendar]]; see [[babylonian-calendar]] for the Akitu's own calendar) falls at the vernal equinox and is, uniquely among the ancient members, **still living** — a spring festival of renewal linked in Zoroastrian thought to *Frashōkərəti*, the final renovation of the world. Hindu **Ugadi/Gudi Padwa** (lunisolar Chaitra, Deccan) sits near the same equinoctial seam.
- **Solar entry into Aries (mid-April, *Mesha Sankranti*).** **Songkran** (Thai) and its cognates — Lao Pi Mai, Khmer Chaul Chnam Thmey, Burmese Thingyan — and the north-Indian **Vaisakhi**, Tamil **Puthandu** and Kerala **Vishu** mark the sun's passage into Aries with water-pouring purification. These are carried in the vault by [[hindu-panchang-calendar]] pending their own nodes.
- **Autumn (1 Tishrei).** **Rosh Hashanah** (carried by [[hebrew-calendar]]) opens the Ten Days of Awe that close on Yom Kippur ([[ritual-yom-kippur-atonement]]): it is *Yom ha-Din*, the Day of Judgment, when the divine court reviews and inscribes each person's fate for the coming year in the Books of Life and Death (*Yom Teruah*, the day of the shofar, Lev 23:24; Num 29:1).
- **First new moon of early spring.** The **Chinese New Year / Spring Festival** ([[ritual-chinese-new-year]]) — lunisolar, between late January and mid-February — wards off the year-devouring beast *Nián* with red and firecrackers, reunites the family, and renews the household through ancestor veneration.
- **Winter / 1 January.** The Roman **Kalendae Ianuariae** presided over by [[janus]], the two-faced god of doorways who looks back on the old year and forward to the new (Ovid, *Fasti* 1), with vows and *strenae* (good-omen gifts) — the direct ancestor of the Gregorian civil New Year.

Related annual-renewal processions that share the structure without being calendrical New Years are wired here as parallels: the Egyptian [[ritual-opet-festival]] (annual re-investiture of the king's divine *ka*) and the Japanese festival cycle ([[ritual-matsuri]], whose Shōgatsu is the New Year rite).

## Cross-tradition significance (transmission vs convergence — the honest map)

The intellectual payload is the same as in [[calendars-comparative]]: keep apart what scholarship can **trace** from what merely **rhymes**. The renewal pattern is powerfully attractive precisely because the year-boundary is a **universal** human need — every agricultural society must somewhere cut the ring of the seasons — so the temptation to read a shared *festival* behind a shared *function* is strong.

**Genuinely documented transmission:**

- **The Southeast Asian solar New Year descends from the Indian solar New Year.** Songkran, Pi Mai, Chaul Chnam Thmey and Thingyan all fall at *Mesha Sankranti* (the sun's entry into Aries, mid-April) because they were carried into mainland Southeast Asia with Indic astronomy and calendrics during the Indianisation of the region (mid-first-millennium CE). This is real diffusion from a single source, not convergence.
- **The Babylonian calendar frame reached the post-exilic Jewish calendar.** The very name of Rosh Hashanah's month — *Tishrei* — is a Babylonian month-name adopted after the Exile (Stern 2012). What transmitted is securely the **calendar frame**; whether the *festival theology* travelled with it is a separate and weaker claim (see below).
- **Nowruz diffused across the Persianate world** — Central Asia, the Caucasus, Kurdistan, Mughal India — from an Iranian core (Boyce 1975). A real spread within one cultural sphere.

**Contested transmission (the famous case):** Sigmund **Mowinckel** reconstructed an autumn Israelite "**Enthronement of YHWH**" New-Year festival from the enthronement psalms (47, 93, 95–99) and argued it was **modelled on the Babylonian Akitu** — the divine king enthroned, the destinies fixed, the cosmos renewed. It is one of the most influential hypotheses in twentieth-century biblical studies and one of the most disputed: many scholars accept that Rosh Hashanah's Day-of-Judgment theology *structurally resembles* the Akitu's fixing of destinies while rejecting the claim of direct **modelling**, since the festival is reconstructed rather than attested and the resemblance may be convergent (both cultures imagining the year's turn as a cosmic assize).

**Independent convergence (the bulk of the resemblance):** That Akitu, Nowruz, Rosh Hashanah, the Kalends and the Spring Festival all frame the year-turn as **chaos expelled and order restored** does *not* make them genetically related. The clinching evidence is that they cannot even agree *when the year turns* — spring equinox, mid-April Aries-entry, autumn, midwinter, first spring new moon. A single diffused festival would carry its date; these do not, because each culture independently sacralised the boundary its own calendar happened to draw. The shared "renewal" is convergence on a real human and agricultural constraint — the seam of the year — not a borrowed rite.

The honest summary: **the renewal pattern is a typology of a universal function, not a family tree.** Two or three specific lineages (the Indian → Southeast-Asian solar New Year; the Babylonian calendar frame into Judaism; Nowruz across the Persianate world) are genuine transmission; almost everything else that looks alike is convergence — and one celebrated lineage (Akitu → Israelite enthronement festival) remains unproven.

## Disputes

- **The "pattern" itself is over-generalised.** The idea of a single ancient-Near-Eastern New-Year *pattern* (divine combat → death/humiliation of the king → sacred marriage → enthronement) is the thesis of the **Myth and Ritual school** — S.H. Hooke's *Myth and Ritual* (1933) and the "Patternists," standing on Frazer's dying-and-rising king. Later scholarship regards it as a template imposed on diverse and only partly comparable festivals. **Jonathan Z. Smith** (*To Take Place*, 1987) is the decisive dissent: he dismantles both the universal-pattern assumption and **Eliade's** archaic-ontology, arguing that ritual is irreducibly local and that the "eternal return" reads a modern nostalgia back into ancient practice. This node uses Eliade's pattern as an organising *lens* while recording that its universality is exactly what is contested — the Smith-vs-Eliade split is the live debate here, as Tsumura-vs-Gunkel is for [[tehom]].
- **How much of the Akitu can be reconstructed.** The flagship rite of the king's humiliation rests heavily on a **single Seleucid-period ritual text**; whether the humiliation was a regular annual archaic rite or a late elaboration is debated (Bidmead 2002). Even the best-documented member is partly a reconstruction.
- **Eliade's cyclical/linear dichotomy.** Eliade set archaic *cyclical* time against Judeo-Christian *linear* time. Subsequent scholarship (as [[cosmic-cycles]] and [[eternal-return]] note) has substantially complicated the opposition: much pre-modern cosmology is both cyclical *and* linear, with renewal cycles nested inside larger frames or ended by a terminal event — and Judaism's own Rosh Hashanah is a cyclical annual renewal inside a linear salvation-history.
