# French Republican (Revolutionary) Calendar — 1793-1805

The **French Republican Calendar** (calendrier républicain français) is one of the most-documented and historically-significant cases of a religious calendar being **deliberately replaced by a secular calendar** for explicit ideological reasons. Instituted by the National Convention on **5 October 1793** and applied retroactively from the proclamation of the First Republic on 22 September 1792 (Vendémiaire 1, Year I), it was used as France's official civil calendar until **Napoleon abolished it on 1 January 1806** (the official act dated 9 September 1805 / 22 Fructidor Year XIII).

## Structure: 12 months × 30 days + 5/6 sansculottides

The calendar's structure was designed by a committee chaired by **Charles-Gilbert Romme** (mathematician, deputy of the Convention) including the astronomer **Jérôme Lalande**, the mathematician **Gaspard Monge**, the chemist **Louis-Bernard Guyton-Morveau**, and the poet-playwright **Philippe-François-Nazaire Fabre d'Églantine** (who supplied the month names + 360 day-names).

- **12 months of 30 days each** = 360 days
- **5 supplementary "sansculottide" days** at year-end = 365 days total
- **A 6th sansculottide ("Festival of the Revolution")** in leap years (years III, VII, XI, XV per Romme's plan)
- **Year starts on the autumnal equinox in Paris** — astronomical-observation-based year-start, not fixed calendar date

The decade (10-day week) replaced the 7-day Christian week. The 10th day (décadi) was the official rest day, replacing Sunday. The 5th day was sometimes treated as a half-rest. The 30-day month was divided into three décades.

## The day-names: Fabre d'Églantine's poetic project

Fabre d'Églantine's most-elaborate contribution was the **naming of every day of the year** after agricultural-natural-rural objects:
- The first day of each month: a plant (Raisin = grape on Vendémiaire 1, Pomme = apple on Vendémiaire 1 in some years)
- The 5th day: a domestic or wild animal (Cheval = horse, Brebis = sheep)
- The 10th day (décadi): a farm tool (Cuvée = vat, Pressoir = press)
- Most other days: plants, vegetables, fruits, minerals, agricultural objects, in a structured seasonal progression

So instead of "St. Nicholas Day" (December 6), the Republican date was "21 Frimaire — Genièvre [Juniper]." The replacement of saints'-day naming with agricultural-naturalist naming was the explicit anti-Catholic project of the Convention's de-Christianization phase (Year II, ~1793-1794). Every Catholic feast was replaced with a botanical or zoological reference; the calendar literally re-named time away from the Christian framework.

## Decimal time

In parallel with the Republican Calendar, the Convention briefly mandated **decimal time** (decimal hours, minutes, seconds; 10 hours per day, 100 decimal-minutes per hour, 100 decimal-seconds per decimal-minute). Decimal time was officially adopted 5 October 1793 + officially abandoned 7 April 1795 — proving impractical because no clocks existed to display it and the public showed no interest. The Calendar continued but decimal time was the first reform-collapse.

## Why it failed: the 10-day week

The Calendar Round, the month-naming, and the year-start convention all survived the public's adaptation period reasonably well. What killed the Republican Calendar was the **10-day week**:
- Workers got 1 day off per 10, not 1 per 7 — 36 décadis per year vs. 52 Sundays. The implicit wage-cut + labor-time-increase was unpopular.
- Catholic and rural populations continued observing Sunday Mass regardless of the official calendar.
- The 10-day week broke the lunar-cycle-derived 7-day pattern that had structured most Mediterranean and European labor for two millennia.

Napoleon's 1806 abolition was politically welcomed because it restored the 7-day week and Sunday rest. The calendar's de-Christianization project had failed not for theological reasons but for labor-rhythm reasons.

## Brief revival under the Paris Commune

The **Paris Commune** (March-May 1871) briefly revived the Republican Calendar during its 72-day existence. Decree of 18 Floréal Year LXXIX (8 May 1871) restored Republican dating for Commune documents. The revival ended with the Commune's bloody suppression in late May 1871.

## Cross-tradition position

**MASSIVE-WIN — secular calendar reform as case study**: the French Republican Calendar is the principal world-historical instance of an **anti-religious calendar reform**. Comparison with other religious-to-secular calendar transitions:

- **Soviet 5-day continuous workweek (1929) + 6-day workweek (1931)** — Stalinist calendar reform aimed at suppressing Orthodox Sunday observance; collapsed 1940 because of labor-coordination problems analogous to the French failure
- **Khmer Rouge year-numbering (Year Zero, 1975)** — Pol Pot's regime used a Year-Zero ideological convention; reverted with regime collapse 1979
- **Iranian Islamic Calendar / Pahlavi imperial calendar shift (1976-1979)** — Mohammad Reza Shah's 1976 shift from the Hijri-Shamsi (Islamic solar) calendar to an "Imperial calendar" backdating to Cyrus the Great's accession (2500-year-empire framing); reverted within years and explicitly reversed by the 1979 Islamic Revolution
- **Gregorian reform of 1582** ([[gregorian-calendar]]) — a calendar reform but explicitly *preserving* the religious framework rather than overthrowing it; the structural opposite of the French Republican project

The French Republican Calendar is the longest-running (~12 years) and most-fully-developed secular-calendar replacement attempt in history. Its failure has been read as evidence for the deep social-cultural embedding of weekly-cycle religious time; calendar systems are not arbitrary technical conventions but encode centuries of labor-coordination, market-rhythm, and devotional pattern that resist top-down reform.

**Anti-clerical signaling vs. agricultural-romanticism**: the calendar's design embeds a curious tension. The structure is anti-Catholic (suppressing saints' days, replacing Sunday); but the day-name vocabulary (grape, apple, juniper, sheep, vat) is rural-agricultural in a way that **the actual Revolutionary urban Jacobin constituency was not**. The calendar is a Parisian-bureaucratic projection of a rural-agricultural-idealized France. This tension — the urban revolutionary project naming its days after country agricultural objects — is one of the cleanest examples of Enlightenment anti-religious reform retaining a residual rural-agrarian-Catholic substrate it explicitly intended to overthrow.
