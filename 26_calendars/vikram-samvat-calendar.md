# Vikram Samvat Calendar (North Indian Hindu Lunisolar)

The most widely-used regional Hindu lunisolar calendar — anchored to the **Vikrama Era (VS)** beginning in 57 BCE, traditionally the year of King Vikramāditya of Ujjain's victory over invading Śaka peoples. In 2026 CE, Vikram Samvat is 2082-2083. The official calendar of Nepal (since 2007 VS, alongside the more-recent Bikram Sambat solar version), the regional calendar of much of North India (especially Gujarat, Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh, Bihar, Uttar Pradesh), and one of the most widely-circulated Hindu liturgical-civil calendars in the modern Indian-diaspora world.

## Structure

The Vikram Samvat is a **lunisolar calendar** with the same basic structure shared across all major Indian Hindu lunisolar reckonings:

- **12 lunar months** named for the nakshatra (lunar mansion) that approximately corresponds to the month's full moon
- **Each month split into two pakṣas** (fortnights): śukla pakṣa (waxing, new-to-full) and kṛṣṇa pakṣa (waning, full-to-new)
- **30 tithis (lunar days) per month** — but a tithi can be slightly shorter or longer than a solar day, so some calendar days carry no tithi (kṣaya) and some carry two (vṛddhi); this is the source of the festival-date variations across regional almanacs (pañcāṅga)
- **Intercalation**: an extra month (adhika māsa) added every 32-33 months on average to reconcile the lunar 354-day year with the solar 365-day year

### Pūrṇimānta vs Amānta reckoning

A critical regional variation: **the month boundary is reckoned differently in North vs South India**:

- **Pūrṇimānta (full-moon-ending)** — the lunar month ENDS with the full moon. This is the **North Indian Vikram Samvat convention** (also used in Bihar, UP, MP)
- **Amānta (new-moon-ending)** — the lunar month ENDS with the new moon. Used in Gujarat, Maharashtra, Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, and most South India

The two conventions are off by half a month for any given lunar event — generating ongoing minor festival-date variation between North and South Indian almanacs even when both nominally use the Vikram Samvat era-count.

## Festival anchoring

The Vikram Samvat calendar structures the major Hindu festival year. Key festival-calendar logic:

- **Chaitra (Mar-Apr)** — the spring New Year in most pūrṇimānta reckonings; Rāma Navamī commemorates Rāma's birth on Chaitra 9 śukla
- **Śrāvaṇa-Bhādrapada (Jul-Sep)** — the monsoon-festival cluster: Rakṣā Bandhan, Janmāṣṭamī (Kṛṣṇa's birth), Gaṇeśa Chaturthi
- **Āśvina (Sep-Oct)** — autumn Navarātri culminating in Vijayadaśamī (the most major North Indian festival)
- **Kārtika (Oct-Nov)** — Diwali on the new moon ending Kārtika kṛṣṇa pakṣa; functions as New Year in **Gujarati Vikram Samvat reckoning**
- **Phālguna (Feb-Mar)** — Holi on the full moon

The same Vikram Samvat calendar can therefore have **multiple "New Year" interpretations** depending on regional convention:
- **Chaitra Śukla Pratipada** = pan-Indian Vikram Samvat New Year (most common)
- **Diwali** = Gujarati Vikram Samvat New Year
- **Vaiśākha Śukla Pratipada** = Tamil + Malayalam New Year (different calendrical era but same month)

## The 57 BCE epoch

The traditional anchoring of the Vikrama Era to 57 BCE poses scholarly puzzles:

- The **earliest unambiguous epigraphic attestation** of the Vikram Samvat era is roughly **9th century CE** — over 900 years after the supposed founding date
- The 57 BCE date corresponds to no securely-attested historical event in well-documented sources
- The likely scenario per Kane 1958 and modern scholarly consensus: the era-count was **back-projected from the Gupta-period emperor Chandragupta II (380-415 CE)** who adopted the Vikramāditya title; the back-projection assigned a 57 BCE founding to a legendary predecessor of the Gupta dynasty

This makes the Vikrama Era one of the cleanest cases of **legendary-historical era-counting**: the era-count is canonical and universally used, but its founding-event historicity is contested.

## Cross-tradition position

The Vikram Samvat calendar sits at multiple cross-tradition junctures:

1. **Indian lunisolar family** — structurally related to [[hindu-panchang-calendar]], the Śaka Era calendar, and regional Tamil + Bengali + Malayalam + Assamese calendars; all share the same basic Metonic-style + nakshatra + saṅkrānti structure with regional adjustments
2. **Nepali state calendar** — the **Bikram Sambat** (a solarized version of Vikram Samvat) was instituted by Nepali king Surendra in 1903; Nepal is the only country whose official civil calendar is the Vikram Samvat tradition
3. **Buddhist-Jain shared use** — Jain communities in the Vikram-Samvat-using regions use the same calendar with Jain-specific festival overlays; some Buddhist-Hindu transitional communities (e.g., Newar Buddhists in the Kathmandu valley) also use Vikram Samvat
4. **Gupta-period back-projection** — exemplifies the broader pattern of legendary-historical era-anchoring in religious calendars (parallel to the Bhārata era ~3102 BCE in [[hindu-panchang-calendar]] cosmic-cycle reckoning, or the Diocletian-era epoch in [[coptic-calendar]])
