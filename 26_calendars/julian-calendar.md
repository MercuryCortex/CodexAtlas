# Julian Calendar (Caesar 46 BCE reform)

The first Mediterranean-world calendar with a regular 4-year leap structure and a constant 365.25-day average year. Promulgated by Julius Caesar during his sole consulship in 46 BCE, technically authored by the Alexandrian astronomer Sosigenes, and surviving without structural change for 1,628 years until the [[gregorian-calendar]] reform of 1582. Still the liturgical calendar of most Eastern Orthodox churches in the 21st century — the longest-continuously-used calendrical structure in Christian history.

## The 46 BCE reform

Pre-Caesarean Rome used a 355-day lunisolar calendar with intercalary months inserted at the discretion of the *pontifices*. This discretion had been abused for political purposes — extending magistrates' terms by adding intercalations, shortening terms by omitting them — and by Caesar's time the civil calendar was ~3 months out of phase with the seasons. Caesar's reform did two things:

1. **The "year of confusion"** — 46 BCE was extended to 445 days (the longest year in recorded history) by adding two intercalary months, realigning the calendar with the solar seasons
2. **Permanent 365.25-day structure** — adopted Sosigenes's recommendation of a 365-day year with one intercalary day every fourth year, drawing on the [[egyptian-civil-calendar]]'s 365-day structure but adding the leap-year correction that Egyptian civil reckoning lacked

The technical author Sosigenes of Alexandria represents the **Hellenistic-Egyptian astronomical inheritance** entering Roman civil administration — a clean case of the broader pattern of Mediterranean knowledge-transmission running through the Library of Alexandria.

## Structural features

- **Average year = 365.25 days**, exactly 11 minutes 14 seconds longer than the astronomical tropical year (~365.2422 days)
- This 11-minute error accumulates to roughly **1 day every 128 years**, or ~3 days every 400 years
- By 1582 CE the Julian calendar had drifted ~10 days behind the astronomical seasons — the gap that motivated the Gregorian reform
- The drift was a known problem from at least the Venerable Bede (8th c.) onward; Roger Bacon flagged it explicitly to Pope Clement IV in 1267 ("De Reformatione Calendarii")

## The Christian inheritance

The Julian calendar passed seamlessly into Christian civil-liturgical use as Christianity became the Roman state religion in the 4th century. The [[event-council-of-nicaea-325]] established the **Paschal computation** — Easter falls on the first Sunday after the first full moon on or after the vernal equinox — within the Julian framework. This embedded the Julian calendar permanently in Christian liturgical practice.

When the Gregorian reform of 1582 was issued, **Protestant and Orthodox Christianity refused or delayed adoption** for theological-political reasons:

- **Protestant Europe** adopted Gregorian gradually 1700-1752 (Britain + colonies in 1752, the "Give us back our 11 days" episode)
- **Russian Empire** stayed on Julian until the 1918 Bolshevik civil-calendar reform (the "October Revolution" of November 1917 reflects Russia still on Julian)
- **Most Eastern Orthodox churches** still use Julian liturgically: Russian Orthodox, Serbian Orthodox, Georgian Orthodox, Jerusalem Patriarchate, Mount Athos. Hence Russian-Orthodox Christmas falls on January 7 in civil-Gregorian dates (the 13-day Julian-Gregorian gap as of 1900-2100)
- **Coptic Orthodox + Ethiopian Tewahedo** use Julian-derived calendars ([[coptic-calendar]] + [[ethiopian-geez-calendar]]) with their own era-counts but Julian leap-year structure

## Cross-tradition position

The Julian calendar's 1,628-year structural lifespan (46 BCE to 1582 CE) and continuing 2,072-year liturgical lifespan (46 BCE to 2026 CE in Eastern Orthodox use) make it **the longest-continuously-operating solar calendrical structure in human history**. It is also the institutional framework within which:

- The Christian Easter computation (the Paschalion) was canonized at Nicaea 325
- The Eastern Christian liturgical year (Triodion, Pentecostarion, Octoechos) was developed in the patristic-Byzantine period
- The Coptic and Ethiopian Tewahedo calendars derive their leap-year structure
- The medieval Western Christian liturgical year operated until 1582
- The Diocletianic "Era of Martyrs" (the Coptic + Ethiopian epoch, counting from 284 CE) is reckoned

The 11-minute-per-year error that motivated Gregorian reform is also the structural reason for the persistent **Julian-Gregorian gap in Eastern Easter dates**: Eastern Orthodox Pascha is computed on Julian-anchored astronomical conditions, often falling 1, 4, or 5 weeks after Western Easter.
