# Athenian (Attic) Calendar

## Identity

The **Athenian (Attic) calendar** is the lunisolar calendar of the classical Greek city of Athens: twelve lunar months, each nominally begun at the first sighting of the new crescent (the *noumenia*), with the year opening at midsummer in the month **Hekatombaion** — the first new moon after the summer solstice. Its twelve months are named for their principal festivals (Hekatombaion, Metageitnion, Boedromion, Pyanopsion, Maimakterion, Poseideon, Gamelion, Anthesterion, Elaphebolion, Mounichion, Thargelion, Skirophorion), so it is at root a **sacred calendar**: the frame on which the city's rites — the Panathenaia, the City Dionysia, the Anthesteria, the Eleusinian Mysteries — were hung. It is the Greek civic occupant of the lunisolar slot in the Atlas's calendar lens, and it is notorious for two features: an **intercalation managed by hand rather than by rule**, and the fact that it ran **alongside a second, entirely separate calendar** — the democratic prytany calendar of the Council.

Athens kept no continuous era-count; a year was identified by the name of its **eponymous archon** ("in the archonship of Apseudes"), which is why Athenian chronology is a chronology of magistrate-lists.

## The festival calendar

The Attic month was tied to the moon. It ran from one *noumenia* to the next and alternated **"full" (30-day)** and **"hollow" (29-day)** months, giving an ordinary year of roughly 354 days — about eleven days short of the solar year. To keep Hekatombaion near midsummer, a **thirteenth month** was periodically inserted, normally a repeated **Poseideon** (*Poseideon deuteros*). But there was **no fixed intercalation cycle**: the decision lay with the archon, taken *ad hoc*, and the archon could equally **insert or suppress individual days** — to move a festival, to avoid an ill-omened day, or for frank political convenience.

The consequence is the single most-quoted fact about this calendar: the civic day-count and the actual moon **drifted apart**. Inscriptions occasionally date an event twice over — once **"according to the god"** (*kata theon*, i.e. by the true moon) and once **"according to the archon"** (*kata archonta*, i.e. by the manipulated civic count) — and the two can differ by several days. Aristophanes makes a joke of exactly this in the *Clouds* (615–626): the Moon complains that the Athenians have muddled the days so badly that the gods go home from their festivals unfed, because the sacrifices are being offered on the wrong date.

Within the month the days were counted in a distinctive way: the first ten **forward** as days "of the waxing month" (*histamenou*), the middle ten through, and the **last ten backward** as days "of the waning month" (*phthinontos*) — so that "the fourth of the waning month" counts down toward the month's end. The first day was the *noumenia*; the last was **hene kai nea**, "the old and the new," a day reckoned as belonging to both the closing and the opening month, a reform Plutarch credits to **Solon** (*Solon* 25).

## The prytany (conciliar) calendar — the democratic double

Running in parallel with this sacred calendar was a wholly separate civic one: the **prytany** or **conciliar calendar**, the working year of the **Boule (Council of 500)**. The democratic year was divided not into months but into **prytanies** — the successive terms during which each of the ten Cleisthenic **tribes** (*phylai*) held the presidency of the Council. In the fourth century, an ordinary year had **ten prytanies** (four of 36 days and six of 35 days = 354 days, matching the festival year), lengthened when the festival year was made intercalary; the arrangement is described in the Aristotelian **Constitution of the Athenians** (*Ath. Pol.* 43.2). The count changed whenever the number of tribes changed — to **twelve prytanies** after the new tribes of 307/6 BCE, and to thirteen at points in the Hellenistic and Roman periods.

Because Athenian **decrees were dated by both systems at once** — "in the *n*th prytany of [tribe]… on the *m*th day of [festival month]" — and because the two counts were managed independently, their published double-dates frequently **fail to line up by simple arithmetic**. Those mismatches are both the great gift and the great headache of the field: they are the raw material from which the calendars have been reconstructed, and the reason the reconstruction is disputed (see **Disputes**).

## Cross-tradition significance (transmission vs convergence — the Metonic thread)

The Athenian calendar sits at the origin of a term everyone in comparative calendrics uses: the **Metonic cycle**. In **432 BCE** the Athenian astronomer **Meton** (with Euctemon) observed the summer solstice at Athens — Ptolemy dates the observation to 27 June 432 (*Almagest* III.1) — and published a **19-year cycle**: 235 lunar months equal almost exactly 19 solar years (the difference is about two hours), so seven intercalary months in nineteen years hold a lunar month-count to the sun. Callippus refined it a century later into the **76-year Callippic cycle**.

Two honest qualifications belong on this cluster. **First**, the 19-year cycle is **Babylonian before it is Greek**: the seven-in-nineteen intercalation was already regularized in Babylonian astronomy (see **[[babylonian-calendar]]**), and Meton's fame has left a Babylonian achievement wearing a Greek name. Whether Meton **learned** the cycle from Babylonian sources or arrived at it by independent Athenian observation is genuinely debated (see Disputes) — the cross-tradition edge here is drawn as *influence with a live dissent*, not as settled borrowing. **Second**, and importantly, **Athens itself never adopted Meton's cycle for its civic festival calendar.** The cycle lived on **parapegmata** (astronomical star-almanacs) and in scientific astronomy; the city's festival calendar went on being intercalated by the archon's hand for centuries afterward. Meton's cycle is thus a scientific product *of* Athens that the *Athenian state calendar ignored* — one of the sharpest illustrations anywhere of the gap between astronomical calendrics and civic timekeeping.

Downstream, the same 19-year cycle became the arithmetic backbone of the fixed **[[hebrew-calendar]]** (seven Adar-II months in nineteen years) and, through the Christian *computus*, of the calculation of Easter. The parallel between the Attic and Hebrew lunisolar calendars — new-moon months, a thirteenth intercalary month, the Metonic ratio — is best read as **convergence on the same astronomical problem reinforced by a shared Babylonian-derived cycle**, not as any direct Athens-to-Jerusalem line. The Athenian scheme's true mechanical heir is the **[[astronomy-antikythera-mechanism]]**, which turns the Metonic and Callippic cycles into bronze gearwork — though tellingly its calendar dial carries the month-names of a **Corinthian/Epirote**, not Attic, city, a reminder that Greeks shared the lunisolar *structure* while every polis kept its **own** month-names.

## Disputes

- **How free was the archon, and which calendar was the flexible one?** The central technical controversy of the field pits **W. K. Pritchett and O. Neugebauer** (*The Calendars of Athens*, 1947) against **B. D. Meritt** (*The Athenian Year*, 1961). On the Pritchett–Neugebauer view the **festival calendar** was the manipulated one — the archon shifted festival days freely, which is why *kata theon* and *kata archonta* diverge — while the prytany calendar ran comparatively regularly. Meritt, reconstructing from the double-dated decrees, argued for a much more **regular festival calendar** and located the play in the prytany year, proposing detailed year-by-year schemes. Many of Meritt's specific reconstructions are now doubted, and Pritchett's picture of an **ad-hoc, tamperable festival calendar** is closer to the current consensus (Hannah 2005), but individual Athenian years remain genuinely uncertain.
- **Did Meton get the 19-year cycle from Babylon?** That the seven-in-nineteen cycle was in Babylonian use before Meton is not in doubt; whether Meton **transmitted** it from Babylonian astronomy or **rediscovered** it independently is debated. B. L. van der Waerden and others argued for transmission; Neugebauer was cautious, noting that a competent observer could reach the cycle from solstice observations alone. The Atlas records the edge as influence-with-dissent rather than as proven borrowing.
- **When does the calendar "end"?** There is no clean terminus. Attic festival months keep appearing in Athenian inscriptions into the Roman imperial period; the date given here (267 CE, the Herulian sack) is a conventional epigraphic cut-off, not an abolition.
