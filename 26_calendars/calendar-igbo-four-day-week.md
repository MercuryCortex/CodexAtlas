# Igbo Four-Day Market Week (Izu — Eke, Orie, Afọ, Nkwọ)

## Identity

The **izu** is the indigenous week of the Igbo peoples of southeastern Nigeria: a recurring cycle of **four named days — Eke, Orie (also Oye), Afọ, and Nkwọ** — that has structured markets, personal naming, and ritual time in Igboland from before the colonial period into the present. It belongs to Igbo traditional religion (*Odinani*; see [[tradition-igbo-religion]]) but functions as a civil-and-market timekeeping frame for all Igbo, not only ritual specialists. Within the Codex Atlas calendar lens it is catalogued as a **week-cycle** — a fixed, socially-defined rotation of days that, unlike the lunar month or the solar year, has **no astronomical referent at all**. That is precisely what makes it interesting: it is a fully-formed, living **week** independent of the seven-day planetary week that now dominates the globe.

The four-day week is the small, robust, universally-attested unit (sometimes called *izu nta*, "small week"). Two of them make an **eight-day "great week" (*izu ukwu*)**, which in some communities is the primary market-and-ritual cycle. Above the week sits a standardised superstructure — seven four-day weeks to a 28-day lunar **month (*onwa*)** and thirteen months to a ~364-day **year (*afọ*)** — but this fuller annual apparatus is a later systematisation and varies locally; the four-day market rotation is the part that is everywhere and manifestly old.

## In Igbo life (market, name, and ritual)

**The market engine.** The four days are, first, **market days**. Neighbouring towns hold their markets on different days of the izu, so that a cluster of communities keeps a market open somewhere every day while no two adjacent markets clash. The four-day cycle is thus a piece of **economic infrastructure**: it coordinates trade across a landscape of decentralised, kingless communities (the Igbo were famously stateless outside the Nri sacred-kingship). The staggering of periodic markets on the day-week is the classic subject of West African market-geography (Hodder & Ukwu 1969).

**The names people carry.** The week is stamped into Igbo **personal names**: a child is often named for the market day of birth — *Nweke* or *Mgbeke* (born on **Eke**), *Okoye* or *Mgboye* (**Orie/Oye**), *Okafọ* or *Mgbafọ* (**Afọ**), *Okonkwọ* or *Mgbonkwọ* (**Nkwọ**). The protagonist of Achebe's *Things Fall Apart* — **Okonkwo** — is literally "a male born on Nkwọ." This is a week made visible in a society's roster of names, comparable to (but independent of) the Akan day-names or the Latin planetary day-names embedded in European naming.

**Ritual and the alusi.** Each market day carries **ritual weight**: particular days are auspicious or forbidden for specific sacrifices, shrine visits, and undertakings, and in local practice each day is associated with particular ***alusi*** (the cult-spirits of Odinani — e.g. [[ala-igbo|Ala]] the earth, [[amadioha|Amadioha]] the thunderer, [[chukwu-igbo|Chukwu]] the high god, [[anyanwu|Anyanwu]] the sun). The day-names themselves also double as ordinary Igbo words (notably *eke*, which also names the royal python and the act of creation; *afọ*, which also means "belly" and "year"), and the [[ifa-odu-divination|Afa]] diviner (*dibia*) reads the day-cycle when timing rites. Nri oral tradition, recorded by Onwuejeogwu (1981), derives the four day-names from four spirit-beings encountered by the founding culture-hero, after whom the four markets were named — an aetiology, not a datable history.

## Cross-tradition significance (a living indigenous week, convergent not transmitted)

The Igbo izu is the Atlas's clearest case of an **indigenous week reckoned entirely apart from the seven-day cycle** that European colonialism, Islam, and Christianity later carried worldwide (see the seven-day week embedded in the [[gregorian-calendar|Gregorian civil calendar]]). Its comparative importance rests on a point made most sharply by Eviatar Zerubavel in *The Seven Day Circle* (1985): of all calendrical units, **the week alone has no natural anchor**. The day is the earth's rotation, the month the moon's phases, the year the sun's circuit — but a "week" is a purely **social** cycle, a rhythm a society simply chooses. Because it is chosen rather than given, different societies have chosen **different lengths**: the Judaeo-Babylonian/planetary **seven**, the Roman *nundinal* **eight**, the Soviet experimental **five**, and — across the West African forest belt — the market **four**.

The Igbo four-day week and the global seven-day week are therefore **independent solutions to the same social problem**, not evidence of contact. This is a **convergence** claim, and an unusually secure one: the izu is embedded in a non-literate, non-astrological society lying entirely **outside** the Mediterranean–Near Eastern sphere within which the seven-day planetary week diffused. There is no plausible transmission route, and no scholar proposes one. The resemblance — a fixed, astronomically-unanchored, ritually-loaded cycle of **named** days — is structural, arising because market coordination and the human appetite for a sub-monthly rhythm are general, not because anyone borrowed anything.

The nearest **genuine relatives** are other West African market weeks. The **Yoruba** four-day week (with days classically dedicated to *orisha*) is the closest structural sibling; the broader region shows four-day and eight-day market cycles among the Edo, Nupe, Tiv, and others, all documented in the market-periodicity literature (Hodder & Ukwu 1969). These West African weeks may share a **regional culture-area** background, but that is a separate, intra-African question from any link to the seven-day week. Further afield, the Mesoamerican [[tonalpohualli|260-day tonalpohualli]] offers a purely **typological** echo — another cycle of named days bearing ritual value with no solar or lunar anchor — but the two are wholly independent inventions on different continents, and the comparison is offered only to locate the izu within the general class of **non-astronomical day-counts**, never as diffusion.

## Disputes

- **Antiquity is inferred, not documented.** There is no pre-colonial written record of the Igbo week. Its age is reasoned backward from the continuity of Igbo ritual culture through the Nri kingdom (c. 10th–11th c. CE) and the Igbo-Ukwu bronze horizon (c. 9th c. CE), and from its deep embedding in language and naming. The four-day cycle is very probably old, but any specific pre-1500 date is an inference, and the frontmatter's earliest-date should be read as an inferential floor, not an attestation.
- **The week is solid; the year is standardised.** The four-day market rotation is attested everywhere and is unquestionably indigenous. The neat superstructure often quoted — 4-day week × 7 = 28-day month × 13 = 364-day year — is in significant part a **20th-century systematisation** (associated with modern Igbo writers seeking to present a unified "Igbo calendar"), and local communities historically varied in how (and whether) they carried the week up into a formal month-and-year scheme. The Atlas records the four-day week as the secure object and the annual apparatus as a later, variable overlay.
- **Terminology and day-order vary.** Sources differ on whether the primary unit is counted as the **four-day** small week (*izu nta*) or the **eight-day** great week (*izu ukwu*), and the order and exact names of the days (Orie vs Oye; sequence variants) differ regionally. The demand that "four days make an eight-day great week" conflates the two counts; the accurate relation is that **two four-day weeks make the eight-day izu ukwu**.
- **Day–deity pairings are local, not a fixed pan-Igbo table.** That each market day carries ritual meaning and *alusi* associations is well attested; a single rigid, Igbo-wide day-to-deity assignment is **not**, and this node deliberately avoids asserting one.
