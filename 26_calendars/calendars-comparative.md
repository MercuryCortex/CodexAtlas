# Comparative Calendar Systems (the calendar lens, by type)

## Identity

This is the **comparative spine** for the calendar lens (folder `26_calendars/`): a meta-node that organises the lens's two dozen individual calendars by **structural type** and states, for each cross-calendar resemblance, whether it reflects **documented transmission** or **independent convergence**. It is not itself a calendar — it has no epoch, no months, and no festivals of its own — but a scholarly survey, modelled on the typological organisation of E.G. Richards's *Mapping Time* (1998) and the common-day-number arithmetic of Reingold & Dershowitz's *Calendrical Calculations* (2018).

The single fact that organises the whole field is an **incommensurability**: the synodic (lunar) month averages ~29.5306 days and the tropical (solar) year averages ~365.2422 days, and **no whole number of lunations equals a whole number of solar years** (12 lunations fall ~11 days short of a solar year; 12.368 lunations fit a year). Every calendar humans have built is a different answer to this one problem. Sorting the lens's calendars by *which answer they give* yields four types:

- **Lunar (pure)** — keep the month exactly lunar, refuse to reconcile with the solar year, and let the year drift through the seasons. Exemplar: the [[islamic-hijri-calendar|Islamic Hijri calendar]] (12 lunations ~354 days, drifting ~11 days earlier each solar year).
- **Lunisolar (with intercalation)** — keep months lunar but periodically insert a thirteenth (intercalary) month to drag the year back into step with the sun. Exemplars: the [[hebrew-calendar|Hebrew]], [[hindu-panchang-calendar|Hindu pañcāṅga]], [[chinese-sexagenary-calendar|Chinese]] and [[tibetan-phugpa-calendar|Tibetan Phugpa]] calendars.
- **Solar** — abandon the month-as-lunation; make the months arbitrary divisions of the solar year and intercalate *days* (a leap day) rather than months. Exemplars: the [[egyptian-civil-calendar|Egyptian civil]], [[julian-calendar|Julian]], [[gregorian-calendar|Gregorian]], [[coptic-calendar|Coptic]], [[ethiopian-geez-calendar|Ethiopian Geʿez]] and [[iranian-jalali-calendar|Iranian Jalali]] calendars.
- **Interlocking counts (Mesoamerican)** — decline the solar-year correction altogether and instead run two (or more) independent counts of different length whose coincidence defines a longer composite cycle. Exemplars: the [[tonalpohualli|260-day tonalpohualli]], the 365-day vague-solar count, their 52-year [[calendar-mesoamerican-calendar-round|Calendar Round]], and the Maya [[mayan-calendrical-system|Long Count]] layered on top.

## The four types in detail

**Lunar (pure).** The simplest possible calendar: count lunations and ignore the sun. The cost is that the year is ~354 days and slides backward through the seasons by roughly eleven days a year, completing a full circuit of the solar year in about 33 lunar years. The [[islamic-hijri-calendar|Hijri]] calendar is the only major surviving pure-lunar *civil* calendar; it deliberately **abolished** intercalation. Pre-Islamic Arabia had used an intercalated (lunisolar) calendar with an inserted month called *nasiʾ*; Qur'an 9:36–37 condemns *nasiʾ* and fixes the year at twelve lunar months, which is why Ramadan migrates through all seasons over a Muslim lifetime.

**Lunisolar (with intercalation).** The most widespread historical solution, and the one whose recurrence across cultures most tempts a transmission story. The reconciliation can be pinned to a cycle: **235 lunations ≈ 19 solar years** (the difference is under two hours), so inserting **7 intercalary months every 19 years** keeps a lunar month-count in step with the sun. This is the **Metonic cycle**, named for the Athenian astronomer Meton (432 BCE) but demonstrably **known earlier in Babylonian practice** and adopted into the [[hebrew-calendar|rabbinic Hebrew calendar]] (a doubled month, Adar II, in 7 of every 19 years). Not every lunisolar system uses the Metonic cycle, however: the [[hindu-panchang-calendar|Hindu pañcāṅga]] computes its intercalary *adhika māsa* from siddhāntic planetary astronomy; the [[chinese-sexagenary-calendar|Chinese]] calendar keys its leap month to the 24 solar terms (*jiéqì*); and the [[tibetan-phugpa-calendar|Tibetan Phugpa]] calendar manages the gap through skipped and doubled lunar days (*tithi*) drawn from the Kālacakra astronomy. Same problem; several genuinely different solutions.

**Solar.** Cut the link between month and moon. The [[egyptian-civil-calendar|Egyptian civil calendar]] did this first and most starkly: twelve 30-day months plus five epagomenal days = a flat **365 days with no leap day at all**, so it drifted a full day against the seasons every four years and a complete year every ~1460 years (the *Sothic cycle*, measured against the heliacal rising of Sirius). The [[julian-calendar|Julian reform]] (46 BCE) supplied the missing correction — one leap day every four years, fixing the mean year at 365.25 days — and the [[gregorian-calendar|Gregorian reform]] (1582) refined that to 97 leap years per 400 for a 365.2425-day mean. The [[coptic-calendar|Coptic]] and [[ethiopian-geez-calendar|Ethiopian]] calendars are the **Alexandrian descendants** of the Egyptian scheme (the Egyptian 365 days plus an Augustan leap day), and the [[iranian-jalali-calendar|Jalali]] calendar reaches the highest accuracy of all by anchoring the year directly to the **observed vernal equinox** rather than a fixed arithmetic rule.

**Interlocking counts (Mesoamerican).** A structurally different idea entirely: instead of one count corrected against the sky, run several uncorrected counts in parallel and read the date as their *combination*. The [[tonalpohualli|260-day count]] (20 day-signs × 13 numbers, no astronomical referent) runs simultaneously with a 365-day vague-solar count; because the least common multiple of 260 and 365 is 18,980 days, any particular pairing recurs only once every **52 years** — the [[calendar-mesoamerican-calendar-round|Calendar Round]]. The Maya additionally layered an absolute linear day-tally, the [[mayan-calendrical-system|Long Count]], on top, so a full Classic-Maya date fixes both a cyclic position and an absolute one. This architecture is **independent of the Old World** in both origin and design.

## Cross-tradition significance (transmission vs convergence — the honest map)

The intellectual payload of this node is to keep two things apart that look alike: lineages that **scholarship can actually trace document-to-document**, and resemblances that arise because **bright people independently solved the same astronomical problem**. The lunisolar type is precisely where the temptation to over-read transmission is strongest, because the intercalation problem is **convergent** — it has been solved independently many times — yet a *few* specific lineages are genuinely historical.

**Securely documented transmission (genetic lineages):**

- **The Metonic 19-year cycle: Babylon → Greece and Babylon → Judaism.** The 19-year intercalation cycle was in use in Babylonian astronomy before Meton's Athenian publication (432 BCE), and the rabbinic Hebrew calendar's fixed 7-in-19 intercalation descends from this Babylonian-derived practice (Stern, *Calendar and Community*, 2001; *Calendars in Antiquity*, 2012). This is a **traceable transmission**, not a coincidence.
- **The Egyptian → Coptic → Ethiopian solar line.** The [[coptic-calendar|Coptic]] calendar is the Alexandrian reform of the [[egyptian-civil-calendar|Egyptian civil]] calendar (with an Augustan leap day, 25 BCE), and the [[ethiopian-geez-calendar|Ethiopian Geʿez]] calendar is structurally the Coptic calendar transmitted south (Neugebauer, *Ethiopic Astronomy and Computus*, 1979). A single 365-day skeleton carried down three traditions.
- **The Julian → Gregorian reform.** The [[gregorian-calendar|Gregorian]] calendar is not a new calendar but an **edit** of the [[julian-calendar|Julian]] one: same months, same leap-day mechanism, a tightened leap rule (drop three leap days every 400 years) plus a one-time ten-day correction (Coyne, Hoskin & Pedersen, *Gregorian Reform*, 1983). Pure transmission-with-refinement.

**Independent convergence (the same problem solved separately):**

- **The lunisolar intercalation problem itself.** That the [[hindu-panchang-calendar|Hindu]], [[chinese-sexagenary-calendar|Chinese]] and [[tibetan-phugpa-calendar|Tibetan]] calendars are all lunisolar does **not** make them genetically related to the Babylonian–Hebrew Metonic family. Each computes its intercalation by its own astronomy (siddhāntic, *jiéqì*-based, Kālacakra-based). The shared *type* is convergence on a real astronomical constraint, not borrowing.
- **High-accuracy solar reckoning.** The [[iranian-jalali-calendar|Jalali]] calendar's equinox-anchored year is *more* accurate than the Gregorian and was achieved **independently** under Omar Khayyam's commission (1079 CE), centuries before Gregory (Aminrazavi 2005). Two cultures reaching near-perfect solar precision by different routes.
- **Interlocking composite cycles.** The Mesoamerican 52-year [[calendar-mesoamerican-calendar-round|Calendar Round]] and the [[chinese-sexagenary-calendar|Chinese]] 60-year sexagenary cycle are both **composite cycles built from shorter interlocking counts**, a striking structural parallel — but they are universally accepted as **independent inventions** (Aveni 2001). The resemblance is typological, not genetic.

**Fully independent (no Old-World relation at all).** The Mesoamerican **260-day count** ([[tonalpohualli]]) has no astronomical referent and no Old-World analogue; its very period (260 = 13 × 20) is a New-World construct (variously linked to the ~260-day human gestation interval, the maize cycle, or the interval between solar zenith passages at Mesoamerican latitudes, all debated). The Maya [[mayan-calendrical-system|Long Count]] double-counting architecture has no parallel in any ancient Old-World calendar. This is the cleanest case in the whole lens of a sophisticated calendrical system invented entirely on its own.

The honest summary: **lunar / lunisolar / solar / interlocking is a typology, not a family tree.** Within it, three lineages (Metonic, Egyptian-Coptic-Ethiopian, Julian-Gregorian) are real transmission; almost everything else that looks similar across the lens is convergence on the same sky.

## Disputes

- **Where did the Metonic cycle originate?** The cycle is named for Meton of Athens (432 BCE), but cuneiform evidence shows the 19-year intercalation scheme in Babylonian use earlier; whether Meton learned it from Babylonian sources or rediscovered it is debated, and the precise date at which Babylonian intercalation became fully regular (rather than ad hoc royal decree) is itself contested (Stern 2012). What is *not* in dispute is that the Hebrew calendar's fixed cycle ultimately derives from the Babylonian-derived tradition rather than from an independent Jewish computation.
- **The origin of the 260-day count.** That the tonalpohualli is independent of the Old World is not in dispute; *why its period is 260 days* is. Gestation-interval, agricultural-cycle, and zenith-passage explanations all have scholarly advocates and none is decisive (Aveni 2001) — a genuinely open question internal to Mesoamerican studies, and unrelated to any transmission claim.
- **Hyperdiffusionism is rejected.** Fringe claims of Old-World-to-Mesoamerican calendrical transmission (e.g. the Atlantis-diffusion literature) are uniformly rejected by mainstream scholarship; the Mesoamerican counts are independent. This node records that rejection so the comparison cannot be mis-read as a diffusion argument.
- **A missing upstream node.** The Babylonian luni-solar calendar — the historical *source* of the Metonic transmission into the Greek and Hebrew calendars — does not yet have its own node in this lens; it is referenced here in prose pending creation. This is a known wire-endpoint gap rather than a scholarly dispute.
