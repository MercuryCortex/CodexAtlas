# Monastic medicine

The medieval Latin Christian medical tradition of the cloister: medical care organized around the monastic infirmary, knowledge transmitted through scriptoria-produced medical manuscripts, materia medica grown in dedicated cloister gardens. Between the 6th c. (the Benedictine Rule's chapter 36 made care for the sick a constitutive monastic duty) and the rise of the medical universities in the 12th-13th c. (Salerno, Montpellier, Bologna, Padua), Latin monasticism was the principal institutional carrier of Western medical practice.

## Benedictine Rule chapter 36

The Rule of St Benedict (c. 530-560), chapter 36: "The care of the sick must rank above and before all else, so that they may truly be served as Christ" (*infirmorum cura ante omnia et super omnia adhibenda est*). The constitutive Benedictine commitment to in-house care of the sick produced the *infirmarius* (infirmary-keeper) as a specialized monastic office, the dedicated *infirmaria* (infirmary building, often separate from the main monastery), and the *hortus sanitatis* (cloister garden of medicinal plants).

## The medical scriptoria

Carolingian and Ottonian monasteries assembled the medical manuscript corpus that preserved Greek and Latin medical knowledge through the early medieval period. The Lorscher Arzneibuch (c. 795), the Bald's Leechbook in Anglo-Saxon England, the Pseudo-Apuleius herbal in many continental scriptoria, and the multi-volume medical anthologies produced at Monte Cassino preserved Late-Antique Greek and Latin medical authors (Galen, Dioscorides) at a time when the secular medical schools had largely lapsed. The principal channel by which Hellenistic medicine reached the high medieval Latin West was *monastic scribal preservation*.

## Hildegard of Bingen

Hildegard of Bingen (1098-1179), Benedictine abbess of Rupertsberg, wrote two medical works (*Physica* and *Causae et Curae*) that are the most-developed medical texts of the high monastic tradition. *Physica* is a materia medica organized by natural-kind (plants, elements, trees, stones, fish, birds, animals, metals) with each entry giving medicinal use; *Causae et Curae* is a more theoretical work on physiology and disease. Hildegard's medicine combines orthodox Galenic humoral theory with a strong cosmological-theological framing and an extensive original materia medica.

## Salerno transition

The Schola Medica Salernitana (active c. 9th c., rose to prominence 10th-12th c.) was the transitional institution: founded outside the monastic system but drawing heavily on monastic medical manuscripts, integrating new Arabic-Greek medical translations (Constantine the African, c. 1020-1087, monk of Monte Cassino, translated Arab medical texts into Latin), and training a non-cloistered medical class. Salerno's *Regimen Sanitatis Salernitanum* (a verse health-manual) was the most-popular medical text in medieval Europe.
