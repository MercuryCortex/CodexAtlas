# Acupuncture & Moxibustion

## Identity

Acupuncture-moxibustion — Chinese **針灸** *zhēnjiǔ*, a compound that literally binds *zhēn* (針, "needle") to *jiǔ* (灸, "cautery/moxa") — is the signature therapeutic technique of Chinese medicine: the insertion of fine metal needles, and the burning of dried mugwort (*Artemisia*, moxa) at or near defined points on the body, in order to regulate the circulation of **qì** (氣) through the **jīngluò** (經絡, "channel/meridian network"). It is the operative counterpart to the herbal pharmacopeia within the broader [[medicine-chinese-traditional|Traditional Chinese Medicine]] system, and it is a **technique**, not a whole medical system: where TCM as a hub embraces qì-theory, yin-yang, five-phase diagnosis and the *materia medica*, zhēnjiǔ is the hands-on procedure that acts on the channels themselves.

The channel network on which it operates comprises **twelve primary channels** (*jīngmài* 經脈), each associated with an organ system, together with the **eight extraordinary vessels** (*qíjīng bāmài* 奇經八脈) — of which the governing vessel (*dū mài* 督脈) and conception vessel (*rèn mài* 任脈) carry their own points. On these fourteen point-bearing channels the modern standard fixes **361 canonical points** (WHO 2008), each with its own name, location and range of indications. Needling and moxa are complementary rather than alternative: needling is generally used to move or disperse qì, moxa to warm and tonify — "where the needle does not reach, moxa is fitting," as the classical maxim has it.

## In the Chinese medical corpus

The textual history complicates the popular image of acupuncture as primordially a needling art. The oldest recovered medical manuscripts — the silk and bamboo texts from **Mawangdui** and **Zhangjiashan** (c. 186–168 BCE) — already describe a system of **eleven vessels** and prescribe **cauterization (moxa)**, but contain **no reference to fine-needle insertion** (Harper 1998). On the paleographic evidence, therefore, **moxibustion is the older technique**, and systematic needling was elaborated afterward.

That elaboration is the achievement of the **Huángdì Nèijīng** (黃帝內經, "Yellow Emperor's Inner Canon," compiled c. 1st c. BCE–1st c. CE), whose **Língshū** (靈樞, "Spiritual Pivot") half is the founding canon of needle therapy: it sets out the "**nine needles**" (each shaped for a different purpose — some for insertion, some for lancing or pressure), the completed channel network, and the indications of the points (Unschuld 2016). The first work devoted *specifically* to the technique is **Huángfǔ Mì's Zhēnjiǔ Jiǎyǐ Jīng** (針灸甲乙經, "A-B Classic of Acupuncture and Moxibustion," c. 256–282 CE), which systematized the points into an ordered manual that remained authoritative for a millennium. In the Song dynasty, **Wáng Wéiyī** cast the famous **bronze acupuncture figures** (*tóngrén* 銅人, 1027 CE) — hollow mannequins pierced with point-holes and filled with water, used to standardize locations and to examine students. The theory reached its classical maturity, and its most influential point-formulae, in the Ming (e.g. Yáng Jìzhōu's *Zhēnjiǔ Dàchéng*, 1601).

## Cross-tradition significance

Zhēnjiǔ presents two very different kinds of cross-tradition link, and honesty requires keeping them apart.

**Secure historical transmission — the East Asian sphere.** From the 6th–7th c. CE, Chinese needle-and-moxa therapy was carried, through Korean intermediaries and direct Tang-court contact, into [[medicine-kampo-japanese|Japan]] (where it became *hari* 鍼 / *kyū* 灸, later developing Waichi Sugiyama's insertion-tube for fine, near-painless needling), into [[medicine-korean-hanbang|Korea]] (*chimgu* / hanbang, with its own Saam five-phase and Sasang-constitutional lineages), and into Vietnam (*châm cứu*). This is documented transmission of an actual technique — the strongest kind of cross-tradition tie — and it makes acupuncture-moxibustion the **most widely transmitted single procedure of East Asian medicine**. Within China it also shares its entire physiological substrate with [[qigong-energy-cultivation|qìgōng]]: the needle regulates qì through the jīngluò from without; qìgōng cultivates and circulates the same qì through the same governing and conception vessels from within.

**Structural convergence — the Indic parallel.** The Chinese qì / jīngluò body (an invisible vital force flowing through a network of channels, regulable at nodal points) is strikingly parallel to the Ayurvedic-yogic **prāṇa / nāḍī** body (a vital breath flowing through subtle channels, gathered at *cakra* nodes) — the same parallel the Atlas records for [[medicine-ayurveda]] under the qì ↔ prāṇa heading, and for [[pranayama-yogic-breath|prāṇāyāma]] as a breath-discipline. **Joseph Needham** (Lu & Needham 1980) raised the question of whether Buddhist intermediaries carried any actual technique between India and China, but the connection is unproven and most scholars read the two channel-physiologies as **independent, convergent solutions** to the shared problem of imagining the living body as a system of flowing vital force. The parallel is therefore **typological, not a demonstrated borrowing** — genuine at the level of body-conception, speculative at the level of transmission.

## Disputes

**Moxa or needle first?** The Mawangdui evidence (cautery, no needling) has made the priority of moxibustion over acupuncture the mainstream scholarly position (Harper 1998; Lu & Needham 1980), against the older folk-image of the needle as the original instrument. Related is the debate over the Língshū's "nine needles": some were clearly **not** for deep insertion but for lancing, bloodletting or pressure — so early "acupuncture" was a broader family of skin-and-flesh interventions than the fine filiform needling that later came to define the art (Kuriyama 1999).

**What are the channels?** The jīngluò have no exact correlate in gross anatomy, and the relation between the classical channel-body and the dissected body has been a live question since the tradition met Western anatomy (Kuriyama 1999; Unschuld 1985). In the modern biomedical setting the mechanism and the clinical efficacy of acupuncture beyond certain pain and nausea indications remain contested, with sham-needle controlled trials at the center of the argument. The Atlas records the technique as a major cross-tradition **medical system-component and its transmission history**, and states plainly that its physiological model (channels carrying qì) is a traditional theory, not an anatomically verified one.
