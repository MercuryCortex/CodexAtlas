# African traditional medicine

The collective term for a number of distinct, regionally-diverse, continuously-practiced indigenous medical traditions across sub-Saharan Africa. Unlike Ayurveda or Chinese medicine — each of which has a single substantial canonical textual tradition — African traditional medicine is principally oral, lineage-transmitted, and varies substantially by language family and ecological zone. The WHO estimates that approximately 80% of sub-Saharan African populations use traditional medicine as a principal or supplemental healthcare resource; the practitioner population is well over a million.

## Principal regional traditions

- **Yoruba (Nigeria/Benin)**: the *babaláwo* (Ifá-divination priest-physician), the *onísegùn* (herbalist), the *adáhunṣe* (specialist in spiritual-causation healing). The *Ifá* corpus (256 *odu* + thousands of *ese*) frames diagnosis as much as the materia medica frames treatment.
- **Igbo (Nigeria)**: the *dibia* (diviner-healer); strong integration of dream and ancestor consultation with herbal pharmacopoeia.
- **Zulu / Xhosa / other Nguni (Southern Africa)**: the *sangoma* (diviner, ancestrally called) and the *inyanga* (herbalist); the threshold-illness initiation called *thwasa* is a documented psychological pattern (called-by-the-ancestors illness only resolved through accepting the sangoma path).
- **Akan (Ghana)**: the *ɔkɔmfoɔ* (priest-diviner) + *odunsini* (herbalist) division.
- **Kongo / Bakongo (Central Africa)**: the *nganga* (diviner-healer) tradition that crossed the Atlantic to become the *nganga* of Cuban Palo Mayombe, *zé pelintra* of Brazilian Candomblé, and other Afro-Atlantic continuations.

## Diagnostic structure

A common feature across these traditions: illness causation is *multi-axial*. The practitioner asks not only "what physical process is producing the symptoms" but also "what social, ancestral, or witchcraft cause has positioned this person to be sick now." Diagnosis often combines pulse and clinical observation with divination (cowrie shells, palm-nuts, casting bones — see [[ifa-odu-divination|Ifá]]). The biomedical-only framing of disease is conceptually narrower than the African-traditional framing.

## State integration

Recent decades have seen substantial state-recognition of African traditional medicine: the South African Traditional Health Practitioners Act of 2007 established a formal practitioner registry; Ghana, Zimbabwe, Mali, and others have parallel frameworks; the WHO Decade of African Traditional Medicine (2001-2010, extended 2011-2020) institutionalized formal cooperation between traditional practitioners and biomedical systems.
