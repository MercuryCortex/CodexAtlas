Mesopotamian temple medicine is the oldest systematically documented healing tradition in the world, attested in cuneiform tablets from as early as 2700 BCE and continuous through the Neo-Babylonian period (c. 600 BCE). It is the foundational case study for the universal pattern: **healing is a priestly function before it becomes a scientific one**.

## The Two Healer Classes

Mesopotamian medicine operated through two distinct but complementary practitioners — one of the most analytically important institutional facts in the history of medicine:

**The Āšipu (exorcist-healer)**: diagnosed disease as a supernatural event — divine punishment, demonic attack, or witchcraft. His tools were incantations, rituals, amulets, and diagnostic omen series. The *Diagnostic Handbook* (**Sakikkû**, c. 1000 BCE, compiled by Esagil-kīn-apli) is the most sophisticated surviving Āšipu text: 40 tablets, 3,000 prognosis entries organized anatomically from head to feet, with ritual prescriptions for each condition.

**The Asû (herbalist-physician)**: the practical healer who applied plant remedies, bandages, enemas, and surgeries. His pharmacopoeia — the **Uruanna** plant series — lists hundreds of medicinal plants with their curative applications. The Asû was empirical within a supernatural framework: the plant worked because of its inherent qualities AND because its preparation was ritualized.

The two practitioners **worked in tandem**, not opposition. The Āšipu diagnosed via omens and supernatural causation; the Asû treated via material means. This division — spiritual diagnosis + material treatment — is the deepest structural pattern in early medicine across all traditions.

## The Diagnostic Handbook — 1000 BCE

The **Sakikkû** represents the world's first systematic clinical manual. Compiled by the scholar Esagil-kīn-apli in Borsippa, it:
- Organizes symptoms anatomically (head → toe) — the same sequence used in modern clinical training
- Records expected prognosis (will live / will die) with the *Āšipu's* systematic success tracking
- Identifies which god or demon is responsible for each symptom cluster
- Prescribes both ritual counter-measures and material treatments

The causal theory (supernatural) is wrong by modern standards. The empirical methodology (systematic symptom-recording, expected outcome documentation) is the *same* methodology. The Sakikkû is a **proto-clinical trial record** embedded in a divine causation framework.

## Sacred Pharmacology

The **Uruanna** plant list and related cuneiform medical recipes document a working pharmacopoeia of striking sophistication:
- Willow bark (*Salix*) — used for pain and inflammation; the active compound is salicin, precursor to aspirin
- Castor oil — purgative, widely prescribed
- Opium — the Sumerians called it *hul gil* ("joy plant"); the oldest documented medicinal use of opium is Sumerian, c. 3400 BCE
- Garlic, onion, thyme — antimicrobial properties confirmed by modern analysis
- Beer (fermented grain, probiotic) — standard vehicle for herbal medicines

The **ritual preparation** of these remedies was inseparable from their efficacy: incantations pronounced during compounding were understood to activate the plant's healing power. Modern pharmacology separated preparation from ritual; Mesopotamian medicine made them one act.

## MASSIVE WIN: The Surgeon's Fees in Hammurabi's Code

**Hammurabi's Code** (c. 1754 BCE) devotes sections 215–225 to surgeons — the oldest legal regulation of medical practice in the world. The fees are graduated by patient class (nobleman, freedman, slave); the penalties for malpractice are severe (if a noble patient dies from surgery, the surgeon's hand is cut off). This is the first evidence that:
1. Surgery was a recognized profession with standardized fees
2. Medical malpractice was a legal concept
3. Medical practitioners were held accountable to written law

The same structure — fee scales, malpractice liability, professional accountability — appears in Hippocratic tradition 1,300 years later. The institutional frame preceded the rationalist transformation.
