# Kampo

*Kampo* (漢方) — literally "the Han method" — is the Japanese tradition of Chinese-derived herbal medicine, which entered Japan in the 6th-7th c. through Korean intermediaries and direct Tang-court contact, and which gradually crystallized into a distinct Japanese form during the Edo period (1603-1868). Today Kampo is unusual among traditional medical systems for its formal integration into the modern Japanese state medical insurance system — 148 Kampo formulations (mostly multi-herb decoctions and granular extracts) are covered as prescription medicines, and most Japanese physicians prescribe at least some Kampo alongside biomedical interventions.

## Edo-period synthesis

The Edo period produced the principal Kampo schools that survive into modern practice:

- **Gosei-ha** (the *neoclassical* school): grounded in Song-Ming Chinese internal-medicine theory; sophisticated diagnostic frameworks.
- **Koho-ha** (the *ancient methods* school): a 17th-18th c. movement (Goto Konzan, Yoshimasu Todo) that rejected later Chinese theoretical accretions and returned to the Han-dynasty *Shang Han Lun* (傷寒論, "Treatise on Cold Damage") of Zhang Zhongjing (c. 200 CE) as the source text. This school's emphasis on direct symptom-pattern matching is the dominant strand of modern Kampo.

## The Meiji compression and recovery

The Meiji Restoration (1868) imposed Western biomedicine as the official Japanese medical system; Kampo lost its formal status. The tradition survived through Edo-period family lineages of practitioners and through scholarly editions of the classical texts. A 20th-century revival — driven partly by scientific pharmacological investigation of Kampo herbs at Japanese universities — led to formal re-recognition. By 1976 the Japanese Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare had approved insurance coverage for the standard Kampo formulations, the first such integration of a traditional system into a modern national insurance framework.

## Diagnostic distinctiveness

Kampo diagnosis uses pulse, abdominal palpation (*fukushin* — an Edo-Japanese specialty), tongue, and a sho (証, syndrome-pattern) framework that names specific multi-symptom complexes for which specific formulations are indicated. The abdominal-palpation technique is particularly distinctive — it is the most-developed in any East Asian medical tradition and is the cornerstone of clinical Kampo practice today.
