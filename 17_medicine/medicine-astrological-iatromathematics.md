# Astrological medicine (iatromathematics)

## Identity

**Iatromathematics** (Greek *iatromathematiká*, ἰατρομαθηματικά — "medical mathematics," where *mathēmatikē* is the ancient word for astrology) is the doctrine and practice that the heavens govern the human body and the course of disease, and that the physician must therefore read and time treatment by the stars. It is not a free-standing medical system but a doctrine woven *into* other systems — the Galenic-humoral medicine of the Greek, Roman, Byzantine, Arabic and Latin-European worlds, and, in its own idiom, the Jyotiṣa astrology attached to Āyurveda. Its four working parts are: **melothesia** (the mapping of the signs and planets onto the parts and organs of the body — the "zodiac-man," *homo signorum*); the **critical days** (*dies critici*, *krísimoi hēmérai*, the turning-points of a fever timed by the Moon); **electional and decumbiture astrology** (choosing an astrologically sound moment to bleed, purge, dose or cut, and casting a chart for the moment a patient fell ill); and the **planetary rulership of organs, humours and herbs**, which lets a remedy be matched to a disease by its ruling planet.

In the Atlas, iatromathematics sits at the crossing of the medicine, astrology, and correspondence lenses: it is the medical application of [[planetary-correspondences]], the concrete clinical form of [[microcosm-macrocosm]], and the branch of [[tradition-western-astrology]] that touches the body directly.

## The doctrine

**Melothesia — the zodiac-man.** The core image is a human figure with the twelve zodiac signs inscribed on the body-parts they rule, running head-to-foot in zodiacal order: **Aries–head, Taurus–neck, Gemini–arms/shoulders, Cancer–chest, Leo–heart, Virgo–belly, Libra–kidneys/loins, Scorpio–genitals, Sagittarius–thighs, Capricorn–knees, Aquarius–shins, Pisces–feet.** Alongside this *zodiacal* melothesia runs a *planetary* melothesia in which the seven planets rule organs and humours (Saturn–spleen and black bile, Jupiter–liver, Mars–gall, Sun–heart and vital spirit, Venus–the kidneys and generation, Mercury–brain and nerves, Moon–the lungs, stomach and bodily fluids). The scheme is codified in Ptolemy's *Tetrabiblos* (III.12–13) and elaborated across the pseudo-Hermetic *Iatromathematica* and the Nechepso–Petosiris astrological corpus; the zodiac-man diagram becomes one of the most reproduced images of the late-medieval world, standard in Books of Hours and printed almanacs.

**Critical days.** Hippocratic medicine already held that fevers reach their crisis on set days; the astrological version times those crises by the Moon. Galen's *De diebus decretoriis* (On Critical Days, Book III) explains the pattern of medical crises by the Moon's passage through the zodiac — the "medical month" of roughly 26–27 days — and Galen, the arch-rationalist, there endorses a genuinely astrological lunar doctrine. This is arguably the most important bridge between orthodox humoral medicine and astrology: it let learned physicians treat star-timing not as superstition but as continuous with Galen.

**Elections, decumbiture and phlebotomy.** From the Moon's position the physician chose *when* to act. The governing rule of the "bloodletting man" (the vein-man of the almanacs) was that one must **not cut with iron a member ruled by the sign the Moon currently occupies** — so surgery or venesection on the head waited until the Moon left Aries, and so on. **Decumbiture** astrology cast a chart for the *decubitus*, the moment the patient took to bed, to diagnose and prognose the illness; it was systematized in Arabic practice, carried through the medieval Latin summae (Guido Bonatti's [[phase-5-066-bonatti-liber-astronomiae|Liber Astronomiae]]), and given its fullest vernacular form by William Lilly's [[phase-6-054-lilly-christian-astrology|Christian Astrology]] (1647) and Nicholas Culpeper's *Semeiotica Uranica* (1651).

**Planet-ruled herbs.** Since each planet ruled organs, humours *and* plants, therapy could be organised planet-by-planet: a disease of a Saturnine (cold, dry, melancholic) organ was met either by Saturnine simples (sympathy) or by the herbs of the opposing planet (antipathy). This is the working logic of Culpeper's *English Physitian* (1652) and of the Renaissance doctrine of signatures, which reads a plant's planetary ruler off its form and colour.

## Cross-tradition significance (transmission vs. convergence)

The **Mediterranean core is a documented transmission, not a coincidence.** Melothesia, the lunar critical days, and the electional apparatus are a single Hellenistic invention — assembled in Greco-Egyptian Alexandria (c. 2nd–1st c. BCE), codified by Ptolemy and endorsed within Galen — that then travels as a connected package: **Hellenistic Greek → Arabic → Latin → Renaissance European.** The Arabic phase (al-Kindī, Māshāʾallāh, Sahl ibn Bishr, Abū Maʿshar; the *ikhtiyārāt* or elections literature) preserved and extended it, and the twelfth- and thirteenth-century Latin translations carried it into the university medical curriculum, where a physician was *expected* to know astrology (French 1994; Siraisi 1990; Boudet 2006). This is the same secure line of descent that carries [[planetary-correspondences]] and the [[medicine-galenic-humoral|Galenic]] corpus itself; iatromathematics rides along with them.

The **Indian counterpart is the harder, more interesting case.** Jyotiṣa medical astrology times treatment, surgery and drug-taking by **muhūrta** (electional astrology), and the **Kālapuruṣa** — the "Time-Man" or cosmic zodiac-body — maps the twelve *rāśis* onto the human frame head-to-foot in the identical order as Western melothesia (Meṣa/Aries–head … Mīna/Pisces–feet). Because Greek astrology demonstrably entered India in the early centuries CE (the *yavana-jātaka*, "Greek astrology," and Varāhamihira's explicit debt to the *yavanas*), the framework of Indian horoscopic astrology — and with it, plausibly, the zodiac-body — is in significant part a **Hellenistic transmission** eastward (Pingree 1981). But the Indian doctrine is then fused with an indigenous and independent medical theory (the Āyurvedic *tridoṣa*, the *nakṣatra* lunar mansions, the *graha* deities), so the medical *application* is a local elaboration, not a straight import. The honest statement: **the horoscopic frame and very likely the zodiac-body travelled from the Hellenistic world; the doṣa-based medical content it was married to is Indian.** The head-to-foot melothesia is therefore a genuine cross-tradition parallel that is *partly* shared inheritance and *partly* convergent structure — not the independent typological coincidence it can look like at first glance, but not a pure transmission of the whole doctrine either.

A separate caution guards against over-reading the humoral resemblance: that both Galenic and Āyurvedic medicine are astro-medical *balance* systems is real, but the tridoṣa–humoral parallel is itself structural (there is no documented genealogical link between the four humours and the three doṣas), so the astrological overlay sits on top of two medical systems that are only analogically related.

## Disputes

- **Was Galen's lunar doctrine "astrology"?** Galen distanced himself from natal astrology yet plainly times medical crises by the Moon in the zodiac (*De diebus decretoriis* III). Scholars divide over whether this is a limited, empirically-motivated lunar theory or a full astrological commitment; Cooper (2011) reads it as a genuine, if bounded, astral doctrine that later authorities used to license the whole enterprise.
- **How independent is the Indian zodiac-body?** The mainstream position (Pingree) derives Indian horoscopic astrology, and with it the Kālapuruṣa melothesia, from Hellenistic transmission via the *yavana-jātaka*; a minority stresses indigenous *nakṣatra* astronomy and doṣa medicine as prior and formative. The order-identity of the head-to-foot sign-body mapping is usually taken as weighing toward transmission for the *frame*, with Indian development of the *medical* content.
- **The learned attack on astro-medicine.** Giovanni Pico della Mirandola's [[phase-6-053-pico-disputationes-adversus-astrologiam|Disputationes adversus astrologiam divinatricem]] (1496) launched the influential humanist assault on judicial astrology, medical included; over the seventeenth century the rise of anatomy, iatrochemistry and the mechanical philosophy detached astrology from learned medicine in the West, though it survived in the almanac tradition and, in the Jyotiṣa idiom, continues in South Asian practice to the present.
