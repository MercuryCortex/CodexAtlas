# Ṭibb al-Nabawī — Prophetic Medicine

## Identity

**Ṭibb al-Nabawī** (Arabic **الطب النبوي**, "the Medicine of the Prophet") is the Islamic genre of medical guidance derived from the ḥadīth — the reported sayings and practices of the Prophet Muḥammad — concerning health, illness, diet, and healing. It is the **scripture-derived, traditionist strand** of medicine in Islamic civilisation: its authority rests not on Greek natural philosophy but on the transmitted word of the Prophet and, secondarily, on the Quran. Characteristic remedies include **honey** (Quran 16:69 calls it "a healing for people"), **black seed** (*ḥabba sawdāʾ*, *Nigella sativa* — "in the black seed is healing for every disease except death," Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5688), **cupping** (*ḥijāma*), dates, olive oil, and dietary and hygienic counsel, together with **ruqya** (Quranic recitation over the sick) and remedies against **the evil eye** (*al-ʿayn*).

Ṭibb al-Nabawī must be distinguished from the **Galenic-philosophical medicine** — the [[medicine-unani|Yūnānī (Greco-Islamic)]] tradition of the physicians and the broader [[medicine-islamic-golden-age|Islamic Golden Age medical science]]. Both flourished in the same civilisation, but Prophetic medicine is a religiously grounded, ḥadīth-authenticated corpus, produced largely by traditionist scholars (*muḥaddithūn*) and jurists rather than by practising court physicians, and at points asserted **against** the autonomous authority of the Greek-derived learned tradition.

## In the genre

The material has two lives. First, as **scattered ḥadīth**: the earliest layer is the medical reports embedded in the canonical collections — the "Book of Medicine" (*Kitāb al-Ṭibb*) of Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim gathers the Prophet's statements on cupping, cautery, honey, black seed, contagion, the evil eye, and incantation. Second, as a **self-standing literary genre**, which crystallised only in the 13th–14th centuries, when scholars extracted, organised, and commented on this material as an alternative to the falsafa-medicine of Avicenna's *Canon*:

- **al-Dhahabī** (d. 748/1348) — an early systematic *al-Ṭibb al-Nabawī*.
- **Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya** (d. 751/1350) — the most influential treatment, forming a large section of his *Zād al-Maʿād*; it selectively absorbs the humoral framework of the physicians while subordinating it to revelation.
- **Jalāl al-Dīn al-Suyūṭī** (d. 911/1505) — later Egyptian systematisations that helped fix the genre.

The genre's therapeutic logic layers three registers: (1) **dietary and regimen counsel** framed increasingly in the hot/cold, dry/moist qualities borrowed from [[medicine-galenic-humoral|Galenic humoralism]]; (2) **physical interventions** attested in ḥadīth — above all *ḥijāma* (wet cupping), together with cautery, which the Prophet is reported to have used but discouraged; and (3) **spiritual-incantatory medicine** — *ruqya*, the recitation of Quranic verses (notably Sūrat al-Fātiḥa and the two "seeking-refuge" chapters, al-Falaq and al-Nās) over the sick, and remedies against *al-ʿayn*, the evil eye, held in the ḥadīth to be a real cause of harm.

## Cross-tradition significance

Ṭibb al-Nabawī is a **genuine headwater**: it names the Islamic instance of the recurring pattern in which a **revealed / scriptural medicine** develops in parallel to, and in tension with, a **learned naturalistic medicine** — the same fault-line that runs between temple-and-scripture healing and Hippocratic naturalism elsewhere. Its most important cross-tradition relationships are **within** Islamic civilisation, and the honest framing is one of **negotiation, not derivation**:

- Against [[medicine-unani|Unani]] and the [[medicine-islamic-golden-age|Golden-Age medical science]], the relationship is not inheritance but **competition and partial accommodation**. Perho (1995) shows the genre was largely the work of *traditionist* scholars asserting a religiously authoritative medicine over against the Greek-derived learned tradition; where a ḥadīth and a Galenic doctrine conflict, the ḥadīth is made to prevail. Yet Ibn Qayyim freely uses the Galenic four-qualities vocabulary to *explain* the Prophet's recommendations — so the two systems are entangled in practice even as they are distinguished in principle.

- Against the pan-Mediterranean and Near-Eastern **evil-eye complex** ([[evil-eye]], and the apotropaic [[hamsa|hamsa]] hand), Prophetic medicine supplies the Islamic scriptural warrant: the ḥadīth "the eye is real" (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5740) and the prescription of ruqya and washing. This is a **convergent / shared-motif** relationship — the belief in the harmful gaze is far older and geographically wider than Islam, and Ṭibb al-Nabawī inherits and Islamises it rather than originating it.

The transmission question is therefore inverted from the usual cross-tradition case: the borrowed material (humoral theory) flows **into** Prophetic medicine from Greek science, while the genre's own distinctive claim is its **independence** from that science. It is a case of a scriptural tradition selectively domesticating a naturalistic one.

## Disputes

Scholars debate how much Ṭibb al-Nabawī was ever a **practised** medicine as opposed to a **literary and legitimising** genre. Perho (1995) frames it as primarily the creation of traditionist scholars — a way of grounding medicine in revelation — rather than a rival clinical practice; on this reading its opposition to Galenic medicine is more ideological than therapeutic, since in the clinic the two toolkits overlapped heavily. There is also the modern question of **revivalism**: in the contemporary period Ṭibb al-Nabawī has been actively promoted (black-seed and honey remedies, ḥijāma clinics) as an authentic Islamic medicine, sometimes in explicit opposition to biomedicine — a modern reframing distinct from its medieval, scholarly origins. Finally, the boundary with Unani is genuinely fuzzy: because Ibn Qayyim and others absorbed humoral theory, some material sits in both traditions, and the "distinct-from" edge marks a doctrinal-authority distinction, not a clean separation of content.
