# Traditional Chinese Medicine

## Identity

Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM / 中醫 *Zhōngyī*) is one of the world's oldest and most continuous medical systems, with documented practice spanning approximately 2,700 years and living practitioner traditions today. Its foundational texts — the **Huangdi Neijing** (Yellow Emperor's Canon of Internal Medicine, c. 200 BCE–100 CE) and the **Shennong Bencao Jing** (Divine Farmer's Materia Medica, c. 200 CE) — established the theoretical framework and pharmacopeia that underpins TCM practice to this day.

TCM is built on five theoretical pillars:
1. **Qi** (氣) — vital force or life-breath that flows through the body via meridian channels; disease = blocked, deficient, or excess qi
2. **Yin-Yang** — dynamic complementary opposites structuring all phenomena; diagnosis identifies which polarity is out of balance
3. **Five Phases** (wuxing 五行: wood/fire/earth/metal/water) — five-element cycle governing organ systems, seasons, flavours, emotions, and their pathological interactions
4. **Zang-Fu** theory — internal organ system with functions broader than Western anatomy (e.g., *kidney* governs fear and the bones, not just urine filtration)
5. **Meridians** (jīngluò 經絡) — 12 primary channels connecting organ systems through which qi flows; acupuncture and moxibustion act on these channels

## MASSIVE WIN: Five Phases ↔ Four Humors

The most structurally precise cross-tradition convergence in ancient medicine: both TCM's five-phase system and the Hippocratic four-humour system are independent solutions to the same deep problem — encoding the body as a cosmos in miniature and diagnosing disease as elemental imbalance. Both emerged c. 400–200 BCE with no documented contact.

| TCM | Hippocratic |
|-----|-------------|
| Wood / spring / liver / anger | Yellow bile / summer / fire |
| Fire / summer / heart / joy | Blood / spring / air |
| Earth / late summer / spleen / worry | Phlegm / winter / water |
| Metal / autumn / lung / grief | Black bile / autumn / earth |
| Water / winter / kidney / fear | — |

Both mapped body-states onto seasonal/environmental cycles; both prescribed correction of elemental excess or deficiency as therapy; both trained practitioners in sensory diagnosis (pulse, tongue, colour, odour). The structural parallel is not coincidental — it reflects the universal human insight that the body participates in the same order as the cosmos, and that medicine is the practice of restoring that participation.

## Qi ↔ Prana

The TCM concept of **qi** and the Ayurvedic concept of **prana** are independent formulations of the same foundational insight: the body is animated by an invisible vital force that circulates through channels, can be blocked or depleted, and can be restored through specific interventions. Both traditions also developed comprehensive **pulse-diagnosis** systems (Chinese: *mai qie* / Ayurvedic: *nadi pariksha*) as the primary diagnostic method.

Buddhist missionaries (4th–8th c. CE) partially merged the two traditions, transmitting Ayurvedic texts into China where they influenced Chinese pharmaceutical thinking.

## The Pharmacopeia

The **Shennong Bencao Jing** classified 365 substances (plants, minerals, animals) into three tiers:
- **Upper tier** (120 entries): tonics for long life, non-toxic, for sustained use (ginseng, liquorice, astragalus)
- **Middle tier** (120 entries): therapeutic for specific conditions, mildly toxic, short-term use
- **Lower tier** (125 entries): powerful symptom-specific medicines, toxic, short-term use only (aconite, rhubarb)

This three-tier classification — the earliest systematic pharmacopeia in East Asia — anticipates modern pharmacological categories (nutritional supplements / therapeutic drugs / high-potency medications).
