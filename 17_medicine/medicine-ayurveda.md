Ayurveda ("the science of long life" — *āyus* = life, *veda* = knowledge) is the oldest continuously practiced comprehensive medical system in the world. Its written tradition spans c. 600 BCE to the present; its oral/ritual roots extend into the Atharva Veda (c. 1200 BCE); its practice is still legally recognized and formally taught in India today.

## The Vedic Roots — Atharva Veda Medicine

Before Ayurveda's rationalization, Vedic healing was embedded in the **Atharva Veda** (c. 1200–900 BCE) — a collection of healing hymns, plant-medicine invocations, and disease-expulsion rituals. The Atharva Veda practitioner (the *bhishaj*) was a ritual healer: disease was caused by demons, ancestral curses, or violated cosmic order (*rta*), and healing required both plant medicines and sacred performance.

The transition from Atharva Veda medicine to classical Ayurveda mirrors the Hippocratic rationalization in Greece: natural-cause theories supplemented (but did not replace) supernatural frameworks. The result is a system that is simultaneously more rationalist than its shamanic predecessor and more holistic than the purely naturalistic Hippocratic corpus.

## The Tridosha System — Three Body Forces

The organizing framework of Ayurveda:
- **Vata** (wind/air): movement, nervous system, breath, circulation — deficiency → weakness; excess → anxiety, pain
- **Pitta** (fire/water): transformation, digestion, metabolism, intellect — deficiency → poor digestion; excess → inflammation, anger
- **Kapha** (water/earth): structure, cohesion, lubrication, immunity — deficiency → weakness; excess → lethargy, congestion

**Prakriti** (constitutional type): each person has a unique baseline ratio of the three doshas — their natural constitution. Diagnosis involves determining both the prakriti and the current doshic imbalance (*vikriti*). Treatment restores the individual's prakriti.

This is **personalized medicine 2,500 years before genomics**: the recognition that optimal health looks different for different constitutional types — and that universal treatments are inadequate.

## The Great Classical Texts

**Charaka Samhita** (compiled c. 100 BCE–200 CE): the primary internal medicine text. 8 books, 120 chapters, covering physiology, pathology, diagnosis, treatment of internal diseases, pharmacology (600+ plant substances, 200 animal substances, 80 mineral substances), and medical ethics. Charaka's therapeutic approach is **primarily lifestyle, diet, and herbal medicine** — surgery is acknowledged but secondary.

**Sushruta Samhita** (c. 6th century BCE–3rd century CE): the foundational surgical text. Contains **surgical procedures of astonishing sophistication**:
- **Rhinoplasty** (nose reconstruction) — described in detail, using skin flaps from the cheek or forehead; the same technique was rediscovered in Europe in the 15th century and is standard modern reconstructive surgery
- **Cataract surgery** (couching and extraction)
- **Cesarean section** (described for fetal extraction in cases of maternal death)
- Detailed anatomical dissection instructions; classification of surgical instruments (127 types)

Sushruta classified surgical training as requiring practice on models (vegetables, leather bags, dead animals) before human patients — the first documented simulation-based surgical training.

**Ashtanga Hridayam** (c. 600 CE, Vagbhata): the synthesis text that united the Charaka and Sushruta traditions. Most widely taught Ayurvedic text in Kerala today.

## MASSIVE WIN: Rhinoplasty — India to Britain

**Sushruta's rhinoplasty technique** (c. 500 BCE–200 CE):
1. Measure the defect with a leaf template
2. Cut a matching flap from the cheek or forehead, leaving one end attached for blood supply
3. Fold the flap onto the nose defect, sew in place
4. Keep the pedicle attached until the flap establishes its own blood supply (2+ weeks)
5. Cut the pedicle

This procedure was being performed by the Kumhar caste (brick-makers, known as "nose surgeons") in Pune, India, in 1794 when **British surgeons documented it in the *Gentleman's Magazine*** (October 1794). The article transmitted the technique to Europe. Graefe, Tagliacozzi, and the founders of modern reconstructive surgery acknowledged the Indian origin. The most common cosmetic surgery in the world today descends directly from Sushruta.

## MASSIVE WIN: Tridosha ↔ Humors — Independent Convergence

The Hippocratic humoral system and Ayurvedic tridosha system reached the same conclusion through independent reasoning:
- Health = dynamic equilibrium of physiological forces
- Constitution = baseline ratio (prakriti / natural temperament)
- Disease = departure from equilibrium
- Treatment = diet + exercise + targeted intervention to restore balance

Both systems were codified c. 400–300 BCE. There is no documented contact at the time of initial codification. The parallel is either: (1) independent discovery of the same proto-scientific framework for thinking about homeostasis, or (2) earlier contact via Persian intermediaries that remains undocumented. Either conclusion is extraordinary.
