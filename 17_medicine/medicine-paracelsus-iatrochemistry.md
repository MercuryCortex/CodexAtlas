# Iatrochemistry

The early-modern school of medical practice grounded in alchemical-chemical theories of body and disease, founded by Paracelsus (Theophrastus von Hohenheim, 1493/4-1541) in the 1520s and continued by van Helmont, Crollius, Glauber, Sylvius, Sennert, and others into the early 18th c. Iatrochemistry is the transitional movement between [[medicine-galenic-humoral|Galenic humoral medicine]] and modern biomedical chemistry — and is the principal channel by which alchemical practice converted into pharmaceutical chemistry.

## The Paracelsian rupture

Paracelsus's medicine was a calculated rupture with the academic Galenic-Avicennian framework that dominated 16th-c. European university medicine. In a public 1527 confrontation at the University of Basel he burned a copy of Avicenna's *Canon* on St John's Eve. The Paracelsian program:

- **Substance over balance**: disease is not humoral imbalance but the action of specific *causal entities* in the body — the *tria prima* (sulfur, mercury, salt) describing the constitutive chemical principles of every substance.
- **Specific remedies for specific diseases**: each disease has a distinct chemical cause; the remedy must target the cause, not restore an abstract balance. This is the conceptual ancestor of modern targeted pharmacology.
- **Mineral and metallic remedies**: Paracelsus introduced antimony, mercury, sulfur, and other inorganic preparations (alongside specific botanicals) as remedies — many of which became standard pharmacy items.
- **The doctrine of signatures**: a Renaissance-Hermetic principle (each plant displays through its form the disease it treats) which Paracelsus integrated systematically. The doctrine is empirically wrong but produced extensive trial-and-error pharmacological discovery.
- **Theurgic-cosmological framing**: medicine is the practitioner's participation in the divine maintenance of the world; physical, spiritual, and astrological forces all act on the patient.

## Van Helmont and the second generation

Jan Baptista van Helmont (1580-1644), Flemish Catholic Paracelsian, gave the school its mature form. His introduction of the concept of *gas* (he coined the word — from Greek *chaos*), his quantitative experimental methods (the famous willow-tree experiment showed water alone could account for the mass increase of a growing plant, falsifying the Aristotelian elemental theory), and his theory of the *archeus* (a vital organizing principle for each organ) are landmarks. Van Helmont is one of the principal figures by whom early-modern chemistry developed as a discipline.
