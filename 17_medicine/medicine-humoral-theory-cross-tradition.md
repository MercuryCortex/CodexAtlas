# Humoral theory — the cross-tradition framework

## Identity

*Humoral theory* is the name given to a family of pre-modern medical systems that share one governing idea: **health is the balance, and disease the imbalance, of a small set of bodily principles**, each principle paired with elemental qualities (hot/cold, wet/dry) and treated by restoring equilibrium through diet, regimen, and drugs of opposite quality. It is not a single tradition but a comparative *slot* filled, under different names and different enumerations, across the medical cultures of the Mediterranean, the Islamic world, the Indian subcontinent, and the Tibetan plateau.

This node is the **comparative hub** sitting above the Atlas's individual humoral systems — [[medicine-hippocratic|Hippocratic]] and [[medicine-galenic-humoral|Galenic]] medicine (Greek), [[medicine-unani|Unani]] (Greco-Islamic), [[medicine-ayurveda|Ayurveda]] (Indian), and [[medicine-tibetan-sowa-rigpa|Sowa Rigpa]] (Tibetan). It exists to hold the honest comparative claim these individual nodes each make in passing: that the *documented* Greek→Arabic→Latin transmission and the *debated* Greek↔Indian parallel are two very different kinds of relationship, and must not be collapsed into a single origin story.

## The systems compared

| Tradition | Name of the principles | The principles | Number |
|---|---|---|---|
| Greek (Hippocratic-Galenic) | *chymoi* / humours | blood, phlegm, yellow bile, black bile | four |
| Islamic (Unani) | *akhlāṭ* | dam, balgham, ṣafrāʾ, sawdāʾ | four |
| Indian (Ayurveda) | *doṣa* (tridoṣa) | vāta (wind), pitta (bile), kapha (phlegm) | three |
| Tibetan (Sowa Rigpa) | *nyes pa* | rlung (wind), mkhris pa (bile), bad kan (phlegm) | three |

Two structural features recur across all four: (1) each principle is bound to elemental qualities (the Greek hot/cold/wet/dry; the Indian mahābhūta associations), so that therapy is a logic of opposites; and (2) each individual has a *constitutional* baseline — the Greek/Unani *krasis* / *mizāj* (temperament), the Ayurvedic *prakṛti* — that the physician's task is to read and maintain, an early form of constitutional or "personalised" medicine.

The **number mismatch is the honest crux**: the Mediterranean-Islamic line is a tetrad, the Indian-Tibetan line a triad. This is the first reason the two families cannot simply be identified as one inheritance.

## The documented spine: Greek → Arabic → Latin

The one relationship in this cluster that is *genealogical and secure* runs Greek → Arabic → medieval Europe. Galen's humoral synthesis was translated almost in its entirety into Arabic in the 8th-9th c. at the Abbasid Bayt al-Ḥikma (the circle of Ḥunayn ibn Isḥāq), producing [[medicine-unani|Unani]] — a name that simply means *"Greek."* The four *akhlāṭ* are Galen's four humours; the *mizāj* is his *krasis*. Avicenna's *al-Qānūn fī al-Ṭibb* (c. 1025) canonised the system, was translated into Latin by Gerard of Cremona at Toledo in the 12th c., and became the standard European university medical textbook into the 17th c. — so that Renaissance Europe received Galenic humoralism *through Arabic mediation* before it recovered the Greek originals after 1453. This spine is not a typological parallel; it is one continuous doctrine carried across three languages, and it is documented at every step (Pormann & Savage-Smith 2007; Nutton 2013).

## The debated parallel: Greek ↔ Indian

The relationship between the Greek humours and the Ayurvedic **tridoṣa** is of a completely different order — a *structural* parallel whose historical cause is contested. Both systems were codified in roughly the same window (c. 400-100 BCE), both make health a dynamic balance of bodily forces, both treat by diet, regimen, and opposite-quality drugs. But tridoṣa is a triad, it is embedded in the Vedic-Atharvavedic and Buddhist-ascetic matrix of Indian thought, and its earliest classical statement (the Caraka and Suśruta Saṃhitās) shows no dependence on Greek texts.

The mainstream position (Kenneth Zysk 1991; Dominik Wujastyk 2003) is **independent convergence**: two proto-scientific cultures reached the same homeostatic model of the body separately, tridoṣa growing out of an indigenous Indian (partly Buddhist-monastic) medical milieu. The classic **dissent** is Jean Filliozat's *La doctrine classique de la médecine indienne* (1949), which pressed the Greek-Indian parallels (including *pneuma* ↔ *prāṇa/vāyu*) and left open the possibility of contact via Persian/Achaemenid intermediaries in the centuries of Greco-Indian exchange after Alexander. The honest verdict: the parallel is real and striking, the *contact is undocumented*, and the differing enumerations (four vs three) weigh against a single origin. The Atlas therefore wires Greek↔Indian as a **debated parallel**, not as transmission.

## The Tibetan derivation

Tibetan [[medicine-tibetan-sowa-rigpa|Sowa Rigpa]] is the clearest *within-family* transmission on the Indian side: its three *nyes pa* — *rlung* (wind ≈ vāta), *mkhris pa* (bile ≈ pitta), *bad kan* (phlegm ≈ kapha) — are a triad closely modelled on the Ayurvedic tridoṣa during the 8th-12th c. compilation of the *rGyud bzhi* (Four Tantras). Sowa Rigpa is a deliberate synthesis tradition, and its foundational histories also record *Greek-via-Arab-Persian* humoral inputs (the semi-legendary foreign physician "Galenos" among those said to have served the early Tibetan court), making the Himalayan system a meeting point of both the Indian and, more faintly, the Mediterranean lines (Yoeli-Tlalim, in Akasoy et al. 2010).

## Cross-tradition significance

The humoral hub is one of the Atlas's cleanest demonstrations of *why the transmission-vs-convergence distinction matters*. Within it sit **two categorically different kinds of connection**: a documented, multilingual, genealogical descent (Greek → Unani → Latin Europe), and an undocumented but structurally arresting parallel (Greek ↔ Ayurveda), with the Tibetan triad derived from the Indian side. To narrate all of these as "the ancient world's shared humoral wisdom" would be to flatten a secure philological fact and a contested comparative hypothesis into the same register — exactly the over-claim this hub is built to prevent. The framework's cross-cultural reach is genuine and remarkable; its *unity* is partly inheritance and partly independent human convergence on the same homeostatic intuition, and the two must be kept distinct.

## Disputes

- **Common origin vs independent convergence (Greek ↔ Indian).** Filliozat (1949) is the classic argument for parallels and possible contact; Zysk (1991) and Wujastyk (2003) hold for independent Indian development. No text or trade record documents transmission at the codification stage, and the four-vs-three enumeration cuts against identity. Unresolved; the mainstream leans convergence.
- **How "scientific" the systems were.** Modern integrative-medicine advocacy sometimes reads humoralism as prescient holism; historians of medicine (Nutton) caution against retrojecting present values onto a theory whose therapeutic core — bleeding, purging, opposite-quality drugging — was frequently harmful.
- **Whether a comparative "humoral theory" is one thing at all.** Some scholars resist treating the tetrad and the triad as a single object, precisely because it invites the flattening this hub warns against; the node's answer is to name the family while keeping its internal relationships typed and distinct.
