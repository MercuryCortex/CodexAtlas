# Sangoma + Inyanga — the Nguni diviner-healer complex

The southern-African Nguni-language medico-religious tradition — practiced by *izangoma* (singular *isangoma*, the ancestrally-called diviner-healer) and *izinyanga* (singular *inyanga*, the herbalist) — is one of the best-documented diviner-healer complexes in sub-Saharan Africa and the principal traditional medical resource for a substantial portion of the Zulu, Xhosa, Swazi, Ndebele, and other Nguni-speaking populations. The South African Traditional Health Practitioners Act 22 of 2007 formally recognizes the *isangoma*, *inyanga*, traditional birth attendant, and traditional surgeon (*ingcibi*) as four legally-licensable categories.

## The institutional distinction — sangoma vs inyanga

The Nguni tradition makes a sharp role distinction that pan-African summaries often elide:

- ***isangoma*** — the diviner-healer; called by the ancestors (*amadlozi*) through the *thwasa* initiation-illness; works principally through divination (the bone-throwing oracle, dream consultation, ancestral mediumship); diagnoses both natural and supernatural causation.
- ***inyanga*** — the herbalist; trained through apprenticeship (not ancestral calling); works principally through materia medica (*muthi*) and physical therapeutics; the medical-pharmacological specialist.

Many contemporary practitioners are dual-credentialed (both *isangoma* and *inyanga*), but the analytical distinction matters: the *isangoma* is selected by the ancestors, the *inyanga* by training.

## The thwasa initiation-illness

The most ethnographically distinctive feature of the tradition is *ukuthwasa* — the "illness of calling." A future *isangoma* is "selected" by the ancestors through a recognized syndrome: persistent unexplained illness (often with somatic, dissociative, and visionary features), dreams of ancestors and water-spirits, restlessness, and recovery only on accepting the call. The candidate then apprentices to a senior *isangoma* (the *gobela*) for one to several years; graduation is marked by the *ukuphothula* ceremony.

The medical anthropology literature (Ngubane 1977; Wreford 2008) documents *thwasa* as a culturally-patterned response that is simultaneously a psychological-spiritual transition AND a recognized professional-vocational pathway. The pattern is structurally parallel to other globally-attested "shamanic-illness" recruitment routes ([[medicine-shamanic|Siberian shamanism]], the Korean *sinbyeong* sickness-of-the-spirits), but in the Nguni case it is institutionalized within a continuous, state-licensed professional class.

## Bone-throwing divination — the diagnostic core

The *isangoma*'s principal diagnostic instrument is the *amathambo* — a set of divinatory objects (animal bones, shells, coins, dice, seeds) cast onto a mat. Each object has a fixed symbolic register; the configuration formed when cast is read against the client's presenting question. The reading produces both a diagnosis (which ancestor, which transgression, which envious witchcraft) and a treatment prescription (which sacrifice, which *muthi*, which behavioural change).

The bone-throw is structurally a randomization device that surfaces information the *isangoma* may already have (the client's circumstances, family history, presenting complaint) and frames it through an ancestral interpretive lens that the client recognizes as authoritative. The cognitive-anthropology literature (Werbner; Janzen) treats the cast as a structured-interview device with strong ethnographic continuity to [[medicine-african-traditional|other African divination systems]] (Ifá, Bamana, Lozi).

## Muthi pharmacopoeia

The Nguni materia medica (*muthi*, sometimes *imithi*) draws on roughly 3,000 documented plant species, plus animal products and minerals. South African ethnobotany has produced substantial scientific work on muthi compounds; several biomedically-active substances (e.g. *Sutherlandia frutescens* / cancer bush; *Hypoxis hemerocallidea* / African potato) are continuously prescribed muthi materia that have undergone clinical-trial evaluation.

## The colonial suppression + post-1996 recognition

Under the colonial and apartheid regimes the *izangoma* were criminalized through the Witchcraft Suppression Act of 1957 (technically still on the books). The post-1996 democratic government has progressively reversed this: the South African Constitution recognizes traditional health practitioners, the THPA 2007 establishes a registration mechanism, and the THPA 2014 Council regulates the profession parallel to the Health Professions Council of South Africa. The dual-system integration is one of the most-developed in any African state — an institutional parallel to the [[medicine-tibetan-sowa-rigpa|Sowa Rigpa]] and [[medicine-korean-hanbang|Hanbang]] state-licensing models.
