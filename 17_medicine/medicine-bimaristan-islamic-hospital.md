# Bimaristan — the Islamic hospital

The bimaristan (Persian *bīmār* = sick + *-stān* = place; Arabic *māristān*) is the medieval Islamic charitable hospital — a public, free-of-charge, multi-specialty inpatient and outpatient medical institution attached to a *waqf* (religious endowment), with separate wards by disease type, salaried physicians, attached medical schools, and integrated pharmacy. The institution's mature 12th-14th c. form is the closest pre-modern analogue to a modern teaching hospital — preceding the European university hospital by roughly four centuries.

## Origins and the Jundīshāpūr model

The institutional ancestor was the medical school + hospital complex at Jundīshāpūr (Khuzestan, southwestern Iran), a late Sasanian foundation (6th c. CE) that survived the Arab conquest. Nestorian-Christian physicians from Jundīshāpūr — most famously the Bukhtīshūʿ family — were summoned to the Abbasid court at Baghdad beginning under al-Mansur (754-775). The first dedicated bimaristan in the Islamic world is usually dated to the Umayyad foundation under al-Walid I in Damascus (c. 707 CE), originally for leprosy; the canonical Baghdad bimaristan was founded under Harun al-Rashid (c. 805) and run by the Bukhtīshūʿ.

## The Mansuri Bimaristan (Cairo, 1284)

Founded by the Mamluk sultan al-Mansur Qalawun in 1284, the *al-Bimaristan al-Mansuri* is the most extensively documented medieval Islamic hospital and the archetypal institution. Endowment documents specify:

- **Separate wards** by disease class: febrile illnesses, ophthalmic disease, surgical cases, dysentery, mental illness, women's wards (with women physicians and attendants), convalescence.
- **Free treatment** to all without distinction of religion, rank, or means; patients discharged with a stipend to cover lost wages during recovery.
- **Salaried staff**: physicians, surgeons, oculists, pharmacists, cooks, nurses, administrators — all paid from the *waqf* endowment.
- **Integrated medical school** and library — the major route by which the [[medicine-islamic-golden-age|Islamic medical synthesis]] was transmitted to the next generation.
- **Pharmacy** (*shifākhāna*) compounding the materia medica described in [[medicine-unani|Unani]] / Ibn Sīnā's *Canon*.
- **Music therapy** for psychiatric patients in the mental-illness ward — one of the earliest documented institutional uses.

The Mansuri operated continuously from 1284 to the early 20th c., a 600+ year run.

## The mental illness ward — a distinctive institution

The bimaristan's institutional treatment of mental illness is one of its most distinctive features. The Nuri Bimaristan (Damascus, 1154), the Mansuri (Cairo, 1284), the Bayazid II Bimaristan (Edirne, 1488), and the Granada Maristan (1367) all had dedicated psychiatric wards with humane regimens — music, baths, garden access, occupational therapy — at a period when the comparable European response to mental illness was confinement or judicial process. The Edirne bimaristan's psychiatric wing is one of the best-preserved physical examples (now a museum of medical history).

## Cross-tradition connections

- **[[medicine-islamic-golden-age]]**: the medical-knowledge content; the bimaristan is the institutional vehicle.
- **[[medicine-unani]]**: the medical doctrine practiced inside the bimaristan.
- **[[medicine-monastic-european]]**: the parallel + partial successor — European hospitals (the Hôtel-Dieu of Paris, the Hospital of San Giovanni in Florence, the Crusader hospital of the Knights Hospitaller) borrowed bimaristan organizational features through contact in the Crusader Levant.
- **[[medicine-asklepion-cult|medicine-asklepion-cult]]**: distant ancestor in the temple-hospital lineage.
- **[[ibn-sina]]**: the canonical Unani theorist whose *Canon* was the bimaristan's medical textbook.
- **[[al-razi]]**: bimaristan-affiliated clinician; his *Continens (al-Hawi)* is partly composed of bimaristan case records.
