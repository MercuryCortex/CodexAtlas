# Exorcism and Possession-Healing — Illness as Spirit-Invasion

## Identity

Across an unusually wide span of unconnected cultures, a specific medical theory recurs: some illness is not a disorder of the body's own substance but the presence inside the body of a wilful, personal, non-human agent — a demon, ghost, jinn, zār, gui, or lwa — that has invaded, seized, or "struck" the sufferer. On this aetiology the cure is not pharmacological but relational and performative: the intruding agent must be named, confronted, and either **expelled** (exorcism) or **appeased and accommodated** (the possession-cult model). This node treats the medical face of spirit-possession — illness understood as invasion and healed by ritual address to the invader — as a cross-tradition pattern in its own right, distinct from possession sought as oracle, initiation, or blessing (for which see [[possession-ritual]]).

The pattern has a datable headwater in Mesopotamian temple medicine and a continuous living presence in Islamic ruqya, the East African zār, Haitian Vodou, and charismatic Christian deliverance. It is placed in the medicine lens because in every one of these settings it is, in native terms, a **theory of disease and a technique of cure**, practised by a specialist healer.

## The invasion aetiology and its two cures

**Mesopotamia (āšipu medicine, c. 2700–500 BCE).** The oldest systematic instance. The Babylonian diagnostic-prognostic series *SA.GIG* ("Symptoms") reads the sick body as a field of signs pointing to a supernatural agent — the "Hand of a god", the "Hand of a ghost" (*eṭemmu*), the "Hand of the demoness Lamashtu". The *āšipu* (exorcist-diagnostician) works beside the *asû* (herbalist), addressing the invading agent by incantation, apotropaic figurine, and ritual, while the *asû* treats the body's substance (Scurlock & Andersen 2005; Geller 2010). Here the dyad of spiritual and material medicine is institutionally explicit (see [[healer-priest]]).

**New Testament (1st century CE).** In the Synoptic Gospels, [[jesus-of-nazareth|Jesus]]' exorcisms are narrated squarely as **healings**: the Capernaum demoniac (Mark 1:23–27), the Gerasene "Legion" (Mark 5:1–20), the mute demoniac whose speech is restored when the demon leaves (Matt 12:22), and the convulsing boy whose symptoms read as epilepsy (Mark 9:14–29). Casting out the spirit and curing the affliction are one act; Jesus commands the demon by authoritative word rather than by the elaborate adjurations of contemporary Jewish and Greco-Egyptian practice (Twelftree 1993). Second Temple Judaism had already developed a rich demonology of illness (Tobit; the Qumran texts; the Genesis Apocryphon, where Abram exorcises Pharaoh's plague).

**Medieval and early-modern Christianity.** The energumen (the possessed) is a recognised clinical category, and discernment of whether a sufferer is possessed, ill, bewitched, or a fraud becomes a formal diagnostic problem (Caciola 2003; Sluhovsky 2007). The rite is codified in the *Rituale Romanum* of 1614, whose *De exorcizandis obsessis a daemonio* lists the signs distinguishing true possession from natural disease. This tradition survives in the contemporary Catholic exorcist and in Protestant charismatic "deliverance" ministry.

**Islam (ruqya, jinn-possession, the evil eye).** Illness is attributed to three overlapping supernatural causes: sorcery (*siḥr*), the touch of a *jinn* (possession, colloquially *ṣarʿ*, "seizure"), and the [[evil-eye|evil eye]] (*al-ʿayn*). The Qur'anic cure is *ruqya* — therapeutic recitation, above all of the two "seeking-refuge" sūras al-Falaq (113) and al-Nās (114) and the Throne Verse (2:255). Classical jurists (Ibn Taymiyya, Ibn al-Qayyim) treated jinn-possession and its cure as real medicine; the practice is vigorous in the present day (Sengers 2003 on Egyptian zār-and-ruqya; Rothenberg 2004 on Palestine).

**East Africa (the zār, 18th c.–present).** In Ethiopia, Sudan, and Egypt, the *zār* is a class of spirit that afflicts (predominantly) women with chronic, undiagnosable illness. Crucially, the cure is **not exorcism**: the spirit cannot be expelled, only identified, propitiated with the offerings, music, and dress it demands, and thereafter managed. The sufferer becomes a permanent host and a member of the cult (Lewis 2003; Boddy 1989 on the northern-Sudanese zār-bori). The neighbouring Hausa *bori* (see [[ritual-bori-spirit-possession]]) works the same accommodationist logic.

**China (exorcistic and Daoist medicine).** Popular and Daoist medicine long held that many illnesses are caused by *gui* (ghosts), demonic infestation, or sorcerous attack, cured by written talismans (*fu*), spells, and the interventions of Daoist ritual-masters and spirit-mediums. Michael Strickmann's *Chinese Magical Medicine* (2002) documents this therapeutic exorcism as a mainstream, textually elaborate strand of Chinese healing, from the *fangxiangshi* and *nuo* rites to Zhengyi Daoist ritual (see [[medicine-chinese-traditional]]).

**Haitian Vodou.** Affliction may be sent by a displeased *lwa* or by *maji* (malign magic); the *houngan*/*mambo* diagnoses the spiritual cause and heals by ritual accommodation of the lwa (the *wanga*), not by expulsion — again the propitiatory pole of the pattern (see [[ritual-vodou-healing]]; [[tradition-vodou-haitian]]).

## Cross-tradition significance (transmission vs convergence)

The pattern is overwhelmingly one of **independent convergence**, with pockets of documented **transmission**. That mixture is the whole interest of the node and must be stated exactly.

**Convergence.** The core aetiology — illness as invasion by a personal agent, cured by authoritative address to that agent — arises independently in cultures with no contact: Mesopotamian āšipu medicine, Amazonian and Siberian shamanism (see [[medicine-shamanic]]), and Chinese exorcistic medicine cannot be derived one from another. The recurrence is best explained as a convergent human response to affliction that is chronic, invisible, or psychiatric — a point Erika Bourguignon's cross-cultural survey and I.M. Lewis's comparative work both press.

**Transmission, where it is real.** Some links are genuinely historical, not merely typological. New Testament exorcism inherits directly from Second Temple Jewish demonology, which in turn had absorbed elements often traced (contestably) to Iranian dualism. Islamic ruqya stands in explicit continuity with biblical and pre-Islamic Near-Eastern practice. And the zār is a case of **documented diffusion**: the cult spread across the Red Sea region — Ethiopia and the Horn to Sudan, Egypt, and the Arabian coast — during the 18th and 19th centuries, carried along slave-trade and pilgrimage routes (Boddy 1989; Lewis 2003). So the Ethiopian, Sudanese, Egyptian, and Arabian zār are connected by transmission, even while the zār-as-a-whole converges typologically on Chinese or Mesopotamian exorcism to which it is unrelated.

The honest summary: **the aetiology is convergent; particular lineages within it are transmitted.** Secure historical descent (Second Temple → Gospel exorcism; the Red-Sea zār diffusion) must not be inflated into a single diffused world-tradition, and independent parallels (China, Amazonia, Mesopotamia) must not be flattened into borrowing.

## Disputes

**Is "possession" one category?** Bourguignon distinguished *possession-trance* (an altered state) from *possession-belief* (an attribution of cause without trance); later cognitive and psychological anthropologists (e.g. Emma Cohen, Rebecca Seligman) argue that "spirit possession" lumps distinct phenomena — executive-possession, pathogenic-possession, oracular-possession — under one Western folk-label. On this view the node names a family resemblance, not a natural kind. The counter-position (Lewis, Boddy) is that the affliction-and-cure complex is coherent enough cross-culturally to be a genuine object of comparison.

**Expulsion vs accommodation.** The single sharpest internal division is therapeutic, not aetiological: Christian and Islamic exorcism **expel** the invader as an enemy, whereas the zār and bori cults **retain** it as a lifelong guest. Lewis's influential "peripheral possession" thesis reads the accommodationist cults as an idiom of protest by the socially marginal (especially women); Boddy's *Wombs and Alien Spirits* criticises that functionalist reduction and insists the zār be read as a cosmology in its own right. The debate is live and bears directly on how the node frames "cure".

**Illness or affliction?** Whether these phenomena should be assimilated to modern psychiatric categories (dissociative and conversion disorders, epilepsy misread as demonic in Mark 9) or respected as native medical systems is itself contested; the medieval discernment literature (Caciola; Sluhovsky) shows the "natural disease vs true possession" boundary was already an anxious diagnostic problem long before biomedicine.
