# The Plague Response — Medicine and Religion Facing Epidemic Death

## Identity

The **plague response** is the double-register reaction to epidemic disease that took its fullest documented form in the **Black Death** of 1347-51 — the pandemic that killed perhaps a third to a half of the population of Europe and the Near East. Faced with mass, inexplicable death, the learned traditions of Afro-Eurasia responded on **two causal levels at once**: a *medical* register inherited from Greek medicine (plague as **miasma**, corrupted air), and a *theological* register in which the pestilence was an act of God — punishment, test, or mercy, depending on the tradition. These two explanations were not rivals but a stacked causal chain: God the ultimate cause, corrupted air the proximate one. The Paris medical faculty's *Compendium de epidemia* (October 1348) states the structure plainly — the plague's first cause lies in the heavens (a conjunction of Saturn, Jupiter and Mars) and thence in the poisoning of the air, while its final cause is divine will.

In the Atlas this node sits among the medicine traditions but is a **cross-tradition comparative node**: it is the point where Galenic humoral medicine, Latin-Christian penitential theology, Islamic plague-law, and the anthropology of scapegoating all address a single event. It completes two theme hubs — [[destroyer-healer]] (the same power kills and cures) and [[scapegoat-mechanism]] (the surrogate victim) — and draws on [[medicine-galenic-humoral]] for its medical vocabulary.

## The double register — miasma and divine will

The **medical explanation** was Galenic and astrological. Plague was *miasma*: air corrupted by putrefaction, swamp-vapours, or a malign celestial conjunction, which entered the body and unbalanced the humours. The therapeutic corollaries were flight ("flee early, flee far, return late"), fumigation with aromatics, purging and bloodletting, and — where the rival **contagion** theory prevailed — isolation. Person-to-person contagion and quarantine were the practical counter-doctrine: Ragusa (Dubrovnik) imposed a thirty-day *trentino* in 1377, extended to the forty-day *quarantino* (whence *quarantine*) at Venice and Marseille, and Venice built its lazarettos. Miasma and contagion coexisted uneasily for centuries until Fracastoro's *De contagione* (1546) gave contagion a theory of *seminaria* (seeds of disease); see Nutton, "The Seeds of Disease" (1983).

The **theological explanation** ran underneath. In Latin Christendom the dominant reading was **divine punishment** for collective sin, demanding collective penance — processions, masses, penitential preaching. In Islam the same event was read, canonically, in the opposite key.

## The three responses

**Latin Christendom — penance, saints, scapegoats.** The Christian response produced (a) the **flagellant movements** of 1348-49 (the German *Geissler*, the Brethren of the Cross), who processed town to town scourging themselves in a thirty-three-and-a-half-day penitential circuit mirroring Christ's years, until Pope **Clement VI** condemned them by his bull of 20 October 1349; (b) the cult of the **plague-saints**, above all **St Sebastian** — whose arrow-pierced body made him the plague-intercessor because arrows were the ancient sign of pestilence — and **St Roch** (Rocco of Montpellier), the plague-pilgrim shown with a bubo on his thigh; and (c) **votive plague-churches and processions** that continue the tradition long after 1351, from Venetian *scuole* to Palladio's **Il Redentore** (vowed after the 1575-76 plague) and Longhena's **Santa Maria della Salute** (vowed after 1630-31). Its darkest form was the **scapegoating of Jews**: accusations of well-poisoning and mass burnings — the Strasbourg massacre of 14 February 1349 killed some two thousand — which Clement VI's own bulls (July and September 1348) explicitly condemned, noting that Jews died of the plague like everyone else.

**The Islamic Mediterranean — martyrdom and submission.** Islamic plague-doctrine, systematised from ḥadīth, ran on three principles (Dols 1977): the plague-dead among the faithful are **martyrs** (*shuhadāʾ*) and the plague itself a **mercy** for believers (a torment only for the unbeliever); a Muslim should **neither enter nor flee** a plague-stricken land ("if you hear of it in a land, do not go to it; if it breaks out where you are, do not flee from it"); and there is **no contagion** (*lā ʿadwā*) — disease comes directly from God, not by transmission between bodies. The great juristic treatise is **Ibn Ḥajar al-ʿAsqalānī's** *Badhl al-māʿūn fī faḍl al-ṭāʿūn* (c. 1430s); **Ibn al-Wardī**, who died of the plague at Aleppo in 1349, left an eyewitness *Risāla*. Yet the no-contagion orthodoxy met an empirical dissent: **Ibn al-Khaṭīb** of Granada (d. 1374) argued from observation that contagion plainly existed, a position that cost him (see Disputes; Stearns 2011).

**The victims.** In the Christian response the Jewish communities of the Rhineland were not participants but the **surrogate victims** of the crisis — the plague read through the lens of [[scapegoat-mechanism]].

## Cross-tradition significance (transmission vs convergence)

The plague response mixes **genuine transmission** with **independent convergence**, and it is worth keeping them apart.

The *medical* substrate is **transmission**: miasma-humoralism is one Greco-Arabic-Latin inheritance, the shared Galenic corpus that Christian, Muslim and Jewish physicians all read (see [[medicine-galenic-humoral]], [[medicine-islamic-golden-age]]). When the Paris faculty and an Andalusian physician both diagnose corrupted air, they are drawing the same well. The **plague-archer iconography** is likewise a continuity: St Sebastian's arrows descend from **Apollo** the *loimos*-archer of *Iliad* I — Marshall (1994) traces the image intact across a millennium and a half, which is why this node wires to [[apollo]] and to the [[destroyer-healer]] hub as the monotheist instance of an old Mediterranean pattern.

The *theological* readings, by contrast, are **convergence and even inversion**: Latin Christendom and Islam took the very same event and one made it **punishment** demanding penance, the other **mercy** conferring martyrdom. Neither borrowed the response from the other; each generated it from its own scripture and law. And the **scapegoat pattern** — pogrom and self-flagellation as two faces of surrogate victimage — is claimed by Girard (1986) not as a borrowed idea but as a **cross-cultural mechanism** that surfaces wherever a community cannot explain its own catastrophe. Here the honest frame is: *a shared medical vocabulary, independently opposed theologies, and a convergent social mechanism*.

## Disputes

**Miasma vs contagion — and did contagion "lose"?** The older story that medieval medicine was blind to contagion until Fracastoro is now heavily qualified. Practical contagion-thinking (quarantine, cordons, isolation) was in force from the 1370s, and in Islam the no-contagion doctrine was contested from within: **Ibn al-Khaṭīb** wrote that "the existence of contagion is established by experience … and by trustworthy reports," setting empirical observation against the ḥadīth — a striking case of a physician resisting a theological datum. Stearns (2011) cautions against reading this as a simple science-vs-religion contest: the debate was internal to Islamic law and reason.

**Were the pogroms a reaction to the plague?** Samuel Cohn (2007) overturns the intuitive account. Examining the chronology town by town, he finds the well-poisoning accusations and Jewish burnings **often preceded the plague's arrival** — the violence was less a maddened response to mass death than a pre-emptive, organised persecution licensed by the coming terror. This sharpens rather than softens the [[scapegoat-mechanism]] reading: the victim is chosen *before* the crisis fully lands.

**Did the plague "cause" the flagellants and the saints, or reveal them?** Norman Cohn (1970) situates the flagellant surge within a longer apocalyptic tradition it did not invent; the plague was the trigger, not the origin. Likewise the plague-saints predate 1347 (Sebastian was invoked in the Roman plague of 680, per Paul the Deacon) — the Black Death intensified and spread cults already present.
