# Hanbang (Korean traditional medicine)

The Korean codification of East Asian medicine — *hanbang* (한방, "Korean prescription") or *hanui-hak* (한의학, "Korean medicine science") — is one of four state-licensed traditional medical systems in East Asia (alongside [[medicine-chinese-traditional|Chinese TCM]], [[medicine-kampo-japanese|Japanese Kampo]], and [[medicine-tibetan-sowa-rigpa|Tibetan Sowa Rigpa]]). Unlike Kampo, which preserves a Han-dynasty formulary almost intact, Korean medicine self-consciously diverged from the Chinese mainstream in the 15th-17th c. through the *Hyangyak* (indigenous-medicine) movement and Heo Jun's encyclopedic *Donguibogam*.

## The Hyangyak indigenous-medicine project

In 1433 the Joseon court published the *Hyangyak jipseongbang* (鄕藥集成方, "Compilation of Native Korean Prescriptions") — a 85-volume catalogue of 703 plant, mineral, and animal medicines native to the Korean peninsula, together with 10,706 prescriptions. The motivating logic was practical and proto-nationalist: foreign Chinese drugs (*tangyak*) were expensive and slow to obtain; Korean drugs (*hyangyak*) were locally available, climatically matched to Korean bodies, and could be standardized through royal pharmaceutical surveys. The *Hyangyak* movement is one of the earliest documented programmatic efforts to construct an indigenous national pharmacopoeia.

## The Donguibogam (1610)

Heo Jun's *Donguibogam* (東醫寶鑑, "Precious Mirror of Eastern Medicine") is the canonical Korean medical text — 25 volumes, completed 1610, published 1613. Heo synthesized 80+ Chinese, Korean, and Japanese medical sources into a single integrated framework organized around the *jeong-gi-shin* (essence-energy-spirit) triad rather than the Chinese five-phase scheme. The text was widely exported to Qing China and Tokugawa Japan, where it became influential — a rare case of a Korean medical text being adopted by both larger neighbors. In 2009 UNESCO inscribed the *Donguibogam* on the Memory of the World Register.

## Sasang typology (Yi Je-ma, 1894)

A distinctive late-Joseon innovation: Yi Je-ma's *Donguisusebowon* (1894) introduced *sasang-uihak* (四象醫學, "four-image medicine"), classifying all humans into four constitutional types — *Taeyangin*, *Taeumin*, *Soyangin*, *Soeumin* — based on the relative sizes of the lung, liver, spleen, and kidney systems. Each type requires distinct dietary, pharmacological, and lifestyle prescriptions. Sasang typology is a personalized-medicine framework — parallel to the [[medicine-ayurveda|Ayurvedic]] *prakriti* / Hippocratic temperament traditions, but developed independently in late-19th-c. Korea.

## Practice today

Korean Medicine Doctors (KMD, *hanuisa*) are licensed by the Ministry of Health and Welfare through a separate 6-year curriculum and national examination, parallel to but distinct from biomedical (MD) licensing. There are 12 colleges of Korean medicine in South Korea, and the Korea Institute of Oriental Medicine (KIOM, founded 1994) conducts state-funded research. The KMD profession's structural separation from biomedicine — unique in East Asia — has produced both a more empirically rigorous traditional-medicine research culture (the *Donguibogam* clinical-evidence project; the standardized Sasang questionnaire) and ongoing institutional tension with biomedical professional bodies.
