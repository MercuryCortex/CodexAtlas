# Unani medicine

The name *Yūnānī* means "Greek" in Arabic and Persian — the medical tradition is named for its Hellenic ancestor, the Hippocratic-Galenic humoral system. The synthesis was constructed at the Abbasid Bayt al-Ḥikma in 9th-c. Baghdad (and earlier in 8th-c. Jundīshāpūr) by translating Greek medical texts into Syriac and Arabic, integrating them with Persian (Sasanian) medical practice, and absorbing pharmacological knowledge from [[medicine-ayurveda|Ayurveda]] and from contact-zone trade networks. Under the canonical Avicenna (Ibn Sīnā, *al-Qānūn fī al-Ṭibb*, c. 1025) the system reached its mature form.

## The Four Humors

Like its Greek parent, Unani is fundamentally a humoral system: health is the dynamic balance of four humors (*akhlāṭ*) — blood (*dam*), phlegm (*balgham*), yellow bile (*ṣafrāʾ*), black bile (*sawdāʾ*). Each humor has two of the four primary qualities (hot/cold, dry/moist). Each body has a constitutional balance (*mizāj*) that the practitioner is responsible for restoring when illness perturbs it. The therapeutic toolkit: pharmacopoeia (sometimes hundreds of compound drugs per condition), regimen (*ʿilāj bi'l-tadbīr* — diet, exercise, sleep, climate), and specific physical interventions (cupping, leeching, surgery).

## The Indian survival

The Unani tradition entered South Asia through Persian-Mughal contacts and was sustained under Mughal court patronage (the *ḥakīms* of the imperial physicians). After Mughal collapse and the British medical system's expansion in the 19th c., Unani survived through the Sharifi family (Delhi) and the Tibbiya College (founded 1889). After Indian independence Unani was given formal state recognition; today there are Unani medical colleges in India, Pakistan, and Bangladesh awarding the BUMS (Bachelor of Unani Medicine and Surgery) degree. The South Asian Unani tradition has continuously integrated [[medicine-ayurveda|Ayurvedic]] materia medica, making the South Asian system distinct from any pure Greek inheritance.

## The Avicenna canon

Ibn Sīnā's *al-Qānūn fī al-Ṭibb* (the *Canon*) was translated into Latin in the 12th c. (Gerard of Cremona) and was the standard medical textbook in European universities into the 17th c. — meaning the Hippocratic-Galenic system reached Renaissance Europe through Arabic mediation, not Greek direct transmission. The Greek originals returned to European awareness only later, through Byzantine Greek manuscripts arriving after the fall of Constantinople (1453).
