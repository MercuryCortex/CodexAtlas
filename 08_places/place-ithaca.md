# Ithaca

## Identity

Ithaca (Greek **Ἰθάκη**, *Ithakē*) is the island kingdom of [[odysseus]] and the goal of the *Odyssey*'s ten-year *nostos* (homeward return) — the fixed pole toward which the whole epic of wandering bends. In the poem it is a small, rugged island in the western sea, "clear-seen" (*eudeielos*) beneath its forested Mount Neriton, with the neighbouring islands Dulichium, Same, and wooded Zacynthus lying close about it (Od. 9.21–26). Odysseus's own verdict on it is a classic Greek statement of the bond between a man and his *patris*: a rugged island, "but a good nurse of young men" (Od. 9.27), than which he can imagine nothing sweeter to see. The traditional identification — made in antiquity and retained by most modern scholarship — is the modern island of **Ithaki (Thiaki)** east of Cephalonia, though the identification has been formally contested twice since 1900 (see Disputes). In the Atlas, Ithaca is the place-lens anchor of the nostos: the homecoming-goal that gives the *Odyssey*'s geography its meaning, and — through Porphyry's allegoresis of its cave — a place that late antiquity converted into an image of the soul's own homeland.

## In the Odyssey

More than half of the *Odyssey* is set on Ithaca. The poem opens there (Books 1–2): Athena visits Telemachus in a palace occupied by the hundred and eight suitors of Penelope (the catalogue at Od. 16.245–253 counts them by island), and the Ithacan assembly convenes for the first time since Odysseus sailed. The entire second half (Books 13–24) is the return itself: the sleeping Odysseus is set ashore by the Phaeacians at the **harbour of Phorcys**, beside the **cave of the Nymphs** with its two entrances — one toward the north wind for mortals, one toward the south for the gods (13.96–112); Athena mists the island so that the king fails to recognise his own kingdom (13.187 ff.) before revealing it and helping him hide the Phaeacian treasure in the cave. The island's poetic topography is precise and repeatedly invoked: leaf-shaking **Mount Neriton** (9.21–22), wooded **Neion** above the town (1.186; 3.81), the spring **Arethusa** and the **Raven's Rock** where Eumaeus pastures the pigs (13.407–410). Telemachus declines Menelaus's gift of horses because Ithaca is goat-pasture, with no broad running-grounds or meadow (4.601–608). On this ground the poem closes: the beggar's disguise, the bow-contest and the slaughter in the hall (Books 21–22), the recognition by Penelope at the immovable olive-trunk bed (23), and the reunion with old Laertes at his farm (24).

## Cult and archaeology (the cave at Polis Bay)

Excavations by **Sylvia Benton** for the British School at Athens (1930–32) in a collapsed cave at Polis Bay, on the north-west coast of Ithaki, produced a votive sequence running from the Bronze Age into the Roman period. Its two celebrated components: the remains of a dozen or more **bronze tripod-cauldrons of the ninth–eighth centuries BCE** — objects that scholars since Benton have connected with the tripods Odysseus brings home from Phaeacia and hides in the cave of the Nymphs (Od. 13.13–15, 217–218; at 8.390–391 the givers are twelve kings and Alcinous the thirteenth) — and, from the Hellenistic levels, a terracotta fragment (usually described as part of a mask) inscribed **ΕΥΧΗΝ ΟΔΥΣΣΕΙ**, "a votive to Odysseus." The sherd is the hard epigraphic evidence of a **hero-cult of Odysseus on Ithaca**; a Hellenistic inscription is also cited as attesting games called the *Odysseia*. Which came first is a genuine chicken-and-egg problem: a cult older than the epic's panhellenic spread (local tradition feeding the poem) or a cult generated by the poem's fame (the epic feeding local practice). **Irad Malkin** (1998) reads cult and epic as interlocking in the ninth–eighth-century world of Euboean and Corinthian seafaring toward Italy, for which Ithaca is the natural last harbour before the open crossing — the island's epic role and its position on the westward sea-route reinforcing one another.

## Cross-tradition significance

Ithaca's reach beyond Greek epic is almost entirely a matter of **documented literary transmission**, and should be stated as such. Downstream, the island passes with the *Odyssey* into Latin (the kingdom of Ulysses) and the whole Western canon; Dante's striking inversion (Inferno 26) has Ulysses refuse the return and sail past the pillars to his death — Ithaca renounced; and C.P. Cavafy's "Ithaka" (1911) fixed the modern proverbial reading, the destination as the pretext of the journey. None of this is convergence; it is the reception-history of a single Greek poem.

Perhaps the most consequential ancient transformation is **Neoplatonic**: Porphyry's *On the Cave of the Nymphs* takes the Ithacan cave of Od. 13.96–112 — two-gated, sacred to the Naiads, wet with ever-flowing water — and reads it, drawing on Numenius and Cronius, as an image of the **cosmos**: souls descend into embodiment through the northern gate (Cancer) and ascend, freed, through the southern (Capricorn). In this reading the return to Ithaca becomes the **soul's return to its origin**, and the island is promoted from epic homeland to metaphysical homeland — a founding document of the homeland-of-the-soul allegory in later Platonism (Lamberton 1986). This too is transmission — a named text interpreting a named passage — not parallel invention.

The only genuinely typological comparison worth recording is the **homecoming-pole** itself: the epic that ends where its hero began. The closest structural analogue is Gilgamesh's return to Uruk, whose walls close the ring of the poem (*Gilgamesh* XI) — and the *Odyssey*–*Gilgamesh* relationship is elsewhere the vault's strongest *argued* (not proven) case for Near-Eastern-to-Greek epic transmission (West 1997; see [[odysseus]] and [[phase-2-008-homeric-epics]]). But no one derives Ithaca from Uruk: the homeward-bound ring-structure is a widespread narrative form, and the parallel is structural, not genetic.

## Disputes

- **The Ithaca question (the identification dispute).** Odysseus says Ithaca "lies low, furthest out to sea toward the dark [the west], while the others face the dawn" (Od. 9.25–26) — yet modern Ithaki is mountainous and lies *east* of the larger Cephalonia. The traditional identification with Thiaki is ancient (a polis named Ithaca stood on the island in the classical period, and coins attributed to it bear Odysseus's head). **Wilhelm Dörpfeld** (*Alt-Ithaka*, 1927) argued that Homeric Ithaca was **Leukas (Lefkada)**; the theory won no lasting following. **Bittlestone, Diggle, and Underhill** (*Odysseus Unbound*, 2005) argued that the **Paliki peninsula of western Cephalonia** was once a separate island (a marine channel through the Thinia isthmus later filled by rockfall) and was Homeric Ithaca; the geological test has not confirmed the channel and the thesis has not won mainstream assent. The mainstream position (Heubeck et al.) retains Thiaki and allows the poet imprecise geography — a caution as old as **Eratosthenes**, who mocked attempts to map Odyssean geography (the cobbler-of-the-wind-bag quip, apud Strabo 1.2.15): if the wanderings resist mapping, Od. 9.25–26 should not be pressed as a survey datum either.
- **Cult before epic, or epic before cult?** As above: whether the Polis-cave tripod deposit attests an Odysseus-cult older than the epic's diffusion, or a cult created by the epic, is debated; Malkin (1998) is the standard discussion.
- **The Mycenaean question.** Bronze-Age occupation of Ithaki is attested but modest, and no palace has been found; press-level claims (c. 2010) of an "Odyssean palace" at the so-called School of Homer site at Agios Athanasios have not been accepted in the peer-reviewed literature.
