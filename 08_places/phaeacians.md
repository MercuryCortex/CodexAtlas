# The Phaeacians — Scheria (Court of Alcinous)

## Identity

Scheria (Σχερίη) is the island of the Phaeacians (Φαίηκες), the last stop of Odysseus's wanderings and the stage of Books 6–8 and 13 of the *Odyssey*. This node deliberately folds the place, the people, and the royal court into one entry: the island, King Alcinous and Queen Arete, the princess Nausicaa, the court singer Demodocus, and the founder-king Nausithous have no independent cult or corpus outside this single Homeric episode and its reception, and none carries enough separate demand for a node of its own. (The Homeric Alcinous is unrelated to the 2nd-century CE Middle-Platonist Alcinous, author of the *Didaskalikos*, discussed in the vault's Platonism node — the shared name is coincidence, and this node's slug avoids the collision.)

The Phaeacians are a people deliberately placed at the edge of the human map: they once lived in Hypereia beside the overbearing Cyclopes, until Nausithous, son of [[poseidon]], led them away to Scheria, "far from men who eat bread" (Od. 6.4–10); Nausicaa says of them that they live "far apart in the surging sea, the farthest of men, and no other mortals mix with us" (Od. 6.204–205). They are Homer's threshold community — human, yet so near the gods that the gods attend their feasts undisguised (Od. 7.201–206).

## In the Odyssey

**The reception (Od. 6–8).** Shipwrecked, naked, and stripped of every companion, Odysseus is washed ashore on Scheria and met by Nausicaa, whom [[athena]] has steered to the beach (Od. 6.13–47). What follows is the fullest hospitality sequence in Greek epic (Reece 1993): the stranger is clothed and fed on the beach, directed to supplicate Queen Arete (Od. 7.53–77), seated in the place of honour, feasted, entertained with games and with the three songs of Demodocus — the quarrel of Odysseus and Achilles, the loves of Ares and Aphrodite, and, at the guest's own request, the wooden horse — and loaded with gifts, all *before* his name is asked (Alcinous puts the question only at Od. 8.550). The palace itself is a marvel: bronze walls with a cobalt frieze, golden doors with silver posts, gold and silver watchdogs wrought by Hephaestus (Od. 7.84–102); and beside it the famous orchard where pear ripens on pear, apple on apple, and fig on fig the whole year round, the West Wind breeding one crop as it ripens another (Od. 7.112–132).

**The frame of the Apologoi (Od. 9–12).** The entire first-person account of the wanderings — Cyclops, Aeolus, Laestrygonians, Circe, the Nekyia, the Sirens, Scylla and Charybdis, the cattle of the Sun, Calypso — is a tale told at Alcinous's court. Scheria is thus not one adventure among the others but the narrative frame of the poem's most famous books: the fantastic world exists in the *Odyssey* only as a story performed in the Phaeacian hall.

**The conveyance and the petrified ship (Od. 13).** Phaeacian ships need no steersmen or rudders; they "know the thoughts and minds of men" and cross the sea shrouded in mist (Od. 8.557–563). Their crossing to Ithaca is wrapped in a sleep "most like death" (Od. 13.80), and Odysseus is laid on his home shore still sleeping, beside his treasure. Poseidon, resenting the painless escort — of all comers, and of Odysseus above all — obtains Zeus's leave and turns the returning ship to stone, rooted in the sea within sight of the harbour (Od. 13.125–187), fulfilling half of Nausithous's old prophecy that the god would one day smite an escort ship and hide the city under a mountain (Od. 8.564–571). Alcinous orders the escort-custom ended and bulls sacrificed to Poseidon; whether the mountain ever falls, the poem never says — the narrative cuts away to Ithaca and does not return.

## Cross-tradition significance

**The ideal-xenia panel.** Within the vault's cross-tradition hub on sacred hospitality ([[moral-sacred-hospitality-cross-tradition]]), Scheria is the Greek narrative exemplar: the complete performance of the host's duties to a stranger who cannot reciprocate. Steve Reece (*The Stranger's Welcome*, 1993) analyses the Scherian sequence as the fullest positive realisation of the Homeric hospitality type-scene, against which the poem's violations are measured — Polyphemus, the anti-host who eats his guests, and the suitors, the anti-guests who devour their host. The symmetry runs through Poseidon: the monster who breaks xenia is his son, the people who perfect it are his descendants, and the god strikes both the blinded hero and the perfect hosts. The resemblance between Greek xenia and the Hebrew, Islamic, and Indic hospitality institutions catalogued in that hub is typological — at most a shared eastern-Mediterranean guest-culture — and no descent is claimed here.

**The paradise-island slot.** Alcinous's ever-bearing garden and the Phaeacian life of feast, lyre, dance, warm baths, and bed (Od. 8.248–249) place Scheria beside Elysium (Od. 4.561–569) and Hesiod's Isles of the Blessed (*Works and Days* 167–173) as the *Odyssey*'s living image of effortless ease — the closest a mortal community stands to the gods anywhere in Homer, wired here to [[theme-heroes-paradise-island]]. The within-Greek family relation (Scheria ~ Elysium) is a matter of shared formulaic language and shared imaginative geography; the resemblance to the Celtic blessed isles across the western sea ([[celtic-otherworld]]) is independent convergence on the island-otherworld type, and is claimed only as such.

**The threshold crossing.** Charles Segal's classic reading (*Arion*, 1962; developed in *Singers, Heroes, and Gods in the Odyssey*, 1994) takes Scheria as the liminal land between the fantastic world of the wanderings and the real world of Ithaca, and the mist-wrapped, death-like sleep of the crossing (Od. 13.79–92) as a symbolic death-and-rebirth re-entry — the reason the node also carries a cautious parallel to [[katabasis-and-anabasis]]. Pierre Vidal-Naquet (*The Black Hunter*, 1986) reads the Phaeacians on the poem's grid of humanity: golden-age agriculture without toil and correct sacrifice with the gods present, the structural inverse of the Cyclopes. On the Near Eastern side, M. L. West (*The East Face of Helicon*, 1997) sets the *Odyssey*'s wandering-and-return frame beside [[gilgamesh-king]]'s journey, where a hero likewise crosses waters no mortal ship can cross by means of a supernatural ferryman to reach the far shore of the known world; Bruce Louden (*Homer's Odyssey and the Near East*, 2011) reads the episode's hospitality narratives against Near Eastern and biblical parallels. As with the Gilgamesh–Odyssey question generally, the transmission is argued, not secured.

**Reception.** Classical Corcyra claimed the Phaeacian past outright: Thucydides grounds Corcyraean naval pride in it (1.25.4) and mentions a precinct of Alcinous on the island (3.70.4). Apollonius of Rhodes (*Argonautica* 4) sets the sheltering of Jason and Medea — and their wedding — at Alcinous's court on Drepane, a generation before Odysseus's visit, fixing the Phaeacia = Corcyra equation for the Hellenistic tradition.

## Disputes

**Where is Scheria?** The ancient identification with Corcyra (Thucydides; Apollonius) competes with the poem's own insistence that the Phaeacians live beyond the reach of ordinary seafaring, "farthest of men." Ancient geography already split on the question: Eratosthenes' famous quip — that you will find the scene of Odysseus's wanderings when you find the cobbler who sewed the bag of the winds (quoted by Strabo 1.2.15) — was aimed precisely at such localisations. The modern commentary consensus (Hainsworth in Heubeck et al., vol. 1) treats Scheria as mythic geography, a never-land constructed to be the last threshold before home; the Corcyra claim is reception history, not Homeric fact.

**Does the mountain fall?** At Od. 13.158 the transmitted text has Zeus advise Poseidon to petrify the ship *and* "hide the city under a great mountain"; an ancient variant attributed to Aristophanes of Byzantium in the scholiastic tradition reads the line as advice *not* to bury the city. The poem's silence — it never returns to Scheria — leaves the Phaeacians' fate genuinely open, and the variant shows ancient readers already divided over whether the perfect hosts are destroyed for their hospitality (see Heubeck–Hoekstra ad Od. 13.125–187).

**Ferrymen of the dead?** The strong form of the symbolic reading — that the Phaeacians are, mythically, ferrymen of the dead and Scheria a land of the departed — has been repeatedly proposed on the strength of the death-like sleep, the mist, and the crossing no living ship performs. The moderate Segal position (threshold and symbolic rebirth) is widely accepted; the strong allegorical identification is resisted by the standard commentaries as over-reading. This node states the moderate position and flags the strong one as a minority reading.
