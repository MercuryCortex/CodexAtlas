# Amarna

The brand-new capital city of [[akhenaten|Akhenaten]] (reigned ~ -1353 to -1336), founded in his regnal year 5 on previously unoccupied desert ground in Middle Egypt, dedicated exclusively to the worship of [[aten|the Aten]]. The city's full Egyptian name is **Akhetaten** — 'Horizon of the Aten'. Amarna is one of the shortest-lived major capital cities in human history: founded ~ -1346, abandoned ~ -1332 — approximately 14 years of occupation.

## The Atenist religious revolution

Akhenaten's Atenist program is one of the most radical theological-political experiments in the ancient world. The historical-academic interpretation of *what exactly Akhenaten was doing* has shifted significantly over the past century:

- **19th-c and early 20th-c reading**: Akhenaten as 'the first monotheist'; the founder of pure-religion-of-light; the proto-Moses (Freud 1939 *Moses and Monotheism* is the canonical statement of this position)
- **Mid-20th-c reading (Redford 1984)**: Akhenaten as political reformer — the Atenist program is principally directed against the **politico-economic power of the Amun-priesthood at Thebes**, not against polytheism in the abstract
- **Late-20th-c reading (Assmann 1995, 1997; Hornung 1999)**: Akhenaten as radical solar-monotheist — a real theological revolution, but one rooted in *Egyptian* solar theology (the cult of Ra-Horakhty), not a foreshadowing of Mosaic religion

The modern consensus is closer to the Assmann/Hornung reading: Atenism is **a genuine monotheism**, but emerges from within Egyptian solar theology and remains theologically distinct from the later Mosaic monotheism of the Hebrew Bible.

## The Great Hymn to the Aten

The **Great Hymn to the Aten**, inscribed in the tomb of Akhenaten's vizier Ay at Amarna, is the canonical articulation of Atenist theology. Its parallel with **Psalm 104** is one of the most-discussed textual parallels in the Hebrew Bible: both are extended sun-creator hymns with striking phrase-level resemblances. The Atenist hymn predates the Psalm by ~600 years.

| Great Hymn to the Aten | Psalm 104 |
|---|---|
| "Thou risest beautifully in the horizon of heaven" | "Bless the LORD, O my soul... clothed with honour and majesty" |
| "When thou settest in the western horizon, the land is in darkness like death" | "Thou makest darkness, and it is night" |
| "The fish in the river leap before thy face" | "There go the ships; there is that leviathan whom thou hast made to play therein" |
| "Thou makest the seasons" | "He appointed the moon for seasons" |

The mainstream-academic position (Sarna 1986, Day 2013) is that **direct textual borrowing is possible but unproven**; the structural-thematic parallel is uncontroversial. The Hymn and the Psalm articulate independently — or via mediated transmission of unknown route — the same fundamental theological perception: the sun as the all-creating, all-sustaining divine presence.

## The Amarna Letters

The **Amarna Letters** — 382 surviving cuneiform tablets discovered at Amarna in 1887 — are the diplomatic correspondence of the Amarna royal court with foreign rulers: Mitanni, Hatti (Hittite Empire), Babylonia, Assyria, Cyprus (Alashiya), and the Canaanite vassal city-states (Jerusalem, Tyre, Sidon, Byblos, Ugarit, Megiddo, Lachish, and dozens of others). They constitute:

- The single most important corpus of Late-Bronze-Age international diplomacy
- Our primary source for the political geography of the 14th-c BCE Near East
- Among the principal early attestations of place-names later familiar from the Hebrew Bible (Jerusalem appears as *Urusalim*; the *ʿApiru* / *Habiru* outlaw-bands appear here — the much-discussed Habiru/Hebrew etymological question is anchored here)

The Amarna Letters were written predominantly in Akkadian (the international diplomatic lingua franca of the period), even by the Egyptian court — a striking testimony to the international cosmopolitanism of the era.

## Nefertiti, Tutankhamun, and the collapse

[[nefertiti]] is the principal consort of Akhenaten and one of the most-prominently-depicted queens in Egyptian art; the iconic bust of Nefertiti from the workshop of Thutmose at Amarna (now in Berlin's Neues Museum) is one of the most-recognised pieces of Egyptian portrait sculpture. Nefertiti's role in Atenist theology is unusually elevated — she is depicted offering directly to the Aten on equal terms with Akhenaten, an iconographic move with no clear precedent in Egyptian royal art.

[[tutankhamun]] (originally **Tutankhaten** — 'Living Image of the Aten') was born at Amarna and was a child during the city's final years. After Akhenaten's death and the brief reigns of Smenkhkare and Neferneferuaten (a queen-regent figure, possibly Nefertiti herself; the identification is contested), the boy-king Tutankhaten changes his name to Tutankhamun ('Living Image of Amun') and the court abandons Amarna, returning to Thebes and restoring the traditional Amun cult.

The Atenist religious revolution is reversed within ~5 years of Akhenaten's death. Tutankhamun's **Restoration Decree** (preserved on the Karnak stela) explicitly repudiates the Amarna religion. Akhenaten and the Amarna pharaohs are subsequently *damnatio memoriae* — their names are chiseled out of monuments, omitted from the Abydos King List, and replaced in king-list chronologies. Amarna itself is systematically dismantled; its blocks are reused as fill in pylons at Karnak and Hermopolis, where they remain in situ until the 20th-c discovery of the **Talatat blocks** that allows partial reconstruction of Amarna's relief decoration.

## The Akhenaten-Moses thesis

The hypothesis that Akhenaten's monotheism is a *historical source* of Mosaic monotheism is one of the principal speculative-historical positions in 20th-c religious studies. The thesis traces to:

- **Manetho** (~ -300) preserves a Moses-as-Egyptian-renegade-priest tradition (possibly drawing on a Memphite memory of the Amarna disaster)
- **Sigmund Freud, *Moses and Monotheism* (1939)** — the most famous modern articulation; argues that Moses was an Atenist priest who fled the post-Amarna reaction and transmitted Atenist monotheism to the Hebrew slaves
- **Jan Assmann, *Moses the Egyptian* (1997)** — the principal mediating position; reads Western monotheism as bearing Akhenaten's *traumatic memory* through a 'mnemohistorical' (rather than textual-transmissive) channel

The mainstream-academic position rejects direct Akhenaten → Moses transmission as unprovable, but the structural-thematic parallel between Atenist monotheism, the Hymn to the Aten / Psalm 104 resonance, and the broader Egyptian-Israelite cultural-religious zone is genuine and acknowledged. See [[theme-akhenaten-moses-monotheism-thesis]] for fuller discussion.
