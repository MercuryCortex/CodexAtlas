## Gregorian Modes: The Tonal Grammar of Western Sacred Music

The medieval church modes (Modes I–VIII, also called the Gregorian modes or ecclesiastical modes) are the tonal system within which **virtually all Western sacred music was composed for approximately 1,000 years**. They were codified in the Carolingian period (c. 800–900 CE), transmitted through the Boethian educational framework, and remained the primary tonal language of Western music until the triumph of major/minor tonality in the 17th century.

## The Eight Church Modes

The medieval system organized its modes in **four pairs**, each pair consisting of an **authentic** mode (the fundamental form) and a **plagal** mode (derived by transposing the authentic form down a fourth). Each mode has:
- A **final** (the home note — like a modern tonic)
- A **tenor** or **dominant** (the reciting tone used in psalm tones)
- A characteristic **modal color** derived from its pattern of intervals

| Mode | Number | Final | Character | Greek name claimed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dorian | I (authentic) | D | Grave, virile, sacred | "Dorian" |
| Hypodorian | II (plagal) | D | Humble, submissive | "Hypodorian" |
| Phrygian | III (authentic) | E | Mystical, passionate | "Phrygian" |
| Hypophrygian | IV (plagal) | E | Tender, harmonious | "Hypophrygian" |
| Lydian | V (authentic) | F | Happy, joyful | "Lydian" |
| Hypolydian | VI (plagal) | F | Devout, tearful | "Hypolydian" |
| Mixolydian | VII (authentic) | G | Angelic, uniting | "Mixolydian" |
| Hypomixolydian | VIII (plagal) | G | Perfect, reserved | "Hypomixolydian" |

**Critical note:** The Greek names are misleading. Thanks to [[boethius]]'s transmission error, the medieval "Dorian" (on D) does not correspond to the ancient Greek Dorian mode. The names were inherited and reapplied. The actual scale structures are different from the classical Greek modes, though the ethos theory (each mode has a specific emotional character) was faithfully preserved.

## Gregory I: The Legendary Codifier

Pope **Gregory the Great** ([[gregory-the-great]], r. 590–604 CE) is the traditional namesake of the Gregorian chant system. Medieval tradition held that he personally assembled and organized the repertoire of liturgical chant, that a dove whispered the melodies in his ear, and that he established the Schola Cantorum in Rome to train singers.

Modern scholarship (principally Peter Jeffery's work) has significantly revised this picture:
- The system now called "Gregorian" was codified in the **Frankish kingdom**, not Rome, in the 8th–9th century under Charlemagne
- What Gregory actually did was probably standardize the *assignment* of chants to the liturgical calendar, not compose or collect the melodies
- The "Frankish-Roman synthesis" created the repertoire during the Carolingian period

Nevertheless, Gregory's name attached to the system because he was credited with organizing it, and "Gregorian chant" remains the standard term.

## The Ethos Theory in Christian Dress

The Greek ethos doctrine — each mode produces specific emotional and moral effects — was directly inherited by medieval music theory, adapted for Christian theological purposes:

| Greek tradition | Medieval Christian adaptation |
|---|---|
| Dorian = martial/virtue | Mode I = grave dignity, suited to solemn feasts |
| Phrygian = ecstatic | Mode III = mystical, suitable for penitential texts |
| Lydian = soft/sensuous | Mode V = joyful, suited to Easter and celebratory texts |
| Modes affect the soul's proportions | Modes align the soul to specific devotional states |

Medieval theorists did not cite Plato directly — they worked from Boethius, who worked from Plato. But the underlying logic is identical: **music shapes the soul's disposition**, and therefore the choice of mode for a specific liturgical occasion is a theological act, not merely an aesthetic one.

This is the Christian reception of the Platonic music-ethics argument. Plato banned certain modes from his ideal city; the medieval church assigned specific modes to specific liturgical purposes, mapping mode to theological season, feast, and affect.

## The Mode System's 1,000-Year Dominance

From approximately 800 CE to 1600 CE, virtually every piece of composed Western music used the modal system. This is an extraordinary durability — comparable to the Sanskrit raga system's persistence in India.

The reasons for the modes' staying power:
1. **Institutional entrenchment**: The church mode system was the curriculum in every European music education institution
2. **Theological authorization**: Modes were not merely musical conventions — they had theological meaning
3. **The monophonic repertoire**: The entire existing chant repertoire was in the modes; you couldn't change the system without invalidating everything already composed
4. **The Boethian framework**: As long as Boethius remained the quadrivium music textbook, the modal system was the theoretical apparatus every trained musician knew

The transition away from modes toward major/minor tonality was gradual and contested:
- **Josquin des Prez** (c. 1450–1521): still primarily modal but beginning to show tonal tendencies
- **Palestrina** (c. 1525–1594): classic modal polyphony
- **Monteverdi** (c. 1567–1643): self-consciously transitional
- **J.S. Bach** (1685–1750): completes the transition; *Well-Tempered Clavier* is a manifesto of equal temperament

## Connection to Byzantine Ison and Sacred Drone

The Byzantine chant tradition maintained its own parallel mode system (the Octoechos, eight echoi or modes) independently of the Western Gregorian tradition, carrying the unbroken Greek practice through the Eastern church. The characteristic Byzantine practice of the **ison** — a sustained drone note held beneath the melody — is the Eastern church's equivalent of the Western modal tonal center.

This makes the medieval church modes and the Byzantine ison two branches of the same ancient Greek tradition, diverged after the 4th century but sharing common roots. The ison connects to the broader cross-tradition [[music-sacred-drone]] pattern (Hindu tanpura, Tibetan singing bowls, Sufi zikr drone) — a further cross-tradition finding: monophonic sacred chant over a sustained drone appears to be **a universal sacred-sound technology** that Christian, Hindu, Buddhist, and Sufi traditions independently discovered.

## Equal Temperament and What Was Lost

The triumph of equal temperament (12 equally-spaced semitones) in the 17th–18th century eliminated the modal system's distinctive tonal colors. In equal temperament, all half-steps are identical; the distinctive interval patterns of the modes collapse into the two surviving modes (Ionian = major; Aeolian = minor) plus theoretical constructs.

What was lost:
- The **characteristic thirds** of each mode (the Phrygian's minor second above the final, the Lydian's raised fourth, the Dorian's distinctive natural sixth) — these created the modal "color" that medieval listeners responded to as distinct emotional-theological spaces
- The **justly-tuned intervals** — Pythagorean pure fifths and major thirds sound physically different (richer harmonic series) from equal-tempered approximations
- The **theological grammar** — Gregorian chant uses specific modal choices for theological reasons that became inaudible once the mode system collapsed into major/minor

The 20th-century **Solesmes restoration** of Gregorian chant (beginning with Dom Prosper Guéranger, c. 1830s) deliberately recovered the modal system as a living liturgical practice. Solesmes editions of chant remain the standard.
