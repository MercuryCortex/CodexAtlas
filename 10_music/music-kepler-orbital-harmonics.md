*Harmonices Mundi* (1619) is one of the most misrepresented books in the history of science. It is routinely described as Kepler claiming the planets sing — a romantic mysticism on his way to modern astronomy. What he actually did was more precise and more interesting: he measured the ratio between each planet's fastest speed (at perihelion) and slowest speed (at aphelion), calculated the interval this ratio corresponds to, and found that every planet produces a musical interval. Saturn's orbital velocities produce a minor third (5:6 ratio). Jupiter sweeps an interval between a minor and major third. Mars covers a perfect fifth. Earth sweeps an almost exact minor second. Venus, the most circular orbit, produces a nearly pure tone — just a diesis (81:80) between its extremes.

These ratios are measured, not stipulated. Kepler derived them from Tycho Brahe's decades of planetary observations — the most accurate naked-eye astronomical data ever compiled. His calculation of the orbital velocity ratios is correct by modern standards. The question is what they mean: do they reflect a designed harmonic structure, or are they coincidence given that any ratio close enough to a simple fraction can be called an interval? Kepler was aware of this problem and argued that the closeness of the matches was too consistent to be random, while acknowledging that the intervals are approximate, not exact.

The most important Keplerian finding that is almost never cited: the planetary intervals, taken together, form chords. At specific moments in their orbits, Saturn and Jupiter simultaneously produce a complete chord. Earth and Venus together produce a chord (albeit a dissonant one). The ensemble of all six planets (as Kepler knew them) produces a complex but analyzable harmonic structure. This is not the static Pythagorean claim that the planets are at distances corresponding to intervals — that claim Kepler refuted. This is the dynamic claim that the planets' changing speeds produce continuous musical change, like voices in a complex polyphony. Kepler's cosmos is not a chord; it is a motet.

The significance for the vault: Kepler's position is categorically different from both Pythagoras's (pure mathematics, no physical mechanism) and Fludd's (symbolic theology, no physical measurement). Kepler is doing physics. His orbital velocity ratios are real. The intervals they produce approximate (but do not exactly match) simple harmonic ratios. Whether this is evidence of harmonic design, a consequence of orbital mechanics producing approximately integer-ratio resonances as stable equilibria (as modern celestial mechanics suggests), or coincidence, is still genuinely undecided. The music-cosmos claim, in Kepler's precise version, has never been definitively refuted — only reframed.
