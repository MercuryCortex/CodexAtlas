## What Just Intonation Is

Just intonation names any tuning system in which every interval between pitches is expressed as a simple whole-number ratio — a ratio drawn directly from the harmonic series. The perfect fifth is 3:2. The perfect fourth is 4:3. The major third is 5:4. The minor third is 6:5. These numbers are not arbitrary: they appear in precisely this order as the natural overtones of any vibrating string or air column.

When a string vibrates at 100 Hz, it simultaneously vibrates at 200 Hz (octave, ratio 2:1), 300 Hz (octave + fifth, 3:2 against the fundamental), 400 Hz (two octaves), 500 Hz (major third above two octaves, 5:4), and so on. Just intonation is the claim that intervals derived from these natural overtones are acoustically, mathematically, and in many traditions *cosmologically* superior to any tempered approximation.

The operative experience: when two notes in a just ratio sound together, their overtones align and the interval "locks" — there is a stillness in the sound, a cessation of beating. Equal-tempered intervals produce audible beating because their overtones are slightly misaligned. This is not aesthetic opinion. It is measurable in cycles per second.

## The Pythagorean System

The oldest documented just-intonation system in the West is Pythagorean tuning. The tradition attributes its discovery to Pythagoras's observation at a blacksmith's forge: hammers whose weights stood in ratios of 2:1, 3:2, and 4:3 produced the octave, fifth, and fourth respectively. Whether or not the story is historical, the ratios are real.

Pythagorean tuning generates its scale by stacking pure 3:2 fifths. Beginning at any pitch and ascending twelve pure fifths returns you almost — but not exactly — to the starting pitch seven octaves higher. The gap between where you arrive and where you began is the Pythagorean comma: roughly 23.5 cents. This comma is not a flaw in the mathematics; it is a structural fact about the universe, reflecting the incommensurability of powers of two and powers of three. No finite sequence of pure fifths will ever produce a pure octave. Equal temperament's great move is to distribute this comma invisibly across all twelve fifths, making each one 2 cents flat.

## Cross-Tradition Convergence

What is remarkable is the cross-cultural convergence on just ratios. Indian music theory from the Natyashastra (c. 200 BCE) describes the shruti system — 22 microtonal steps within an octave, producing intervals that correspond closely to just ratios. The 3:2 fifth (pancham) is sacrosanct in raga practice and cannot be tempered. Al-Farabi (c. 872–950 CE) and Ibn Sina (c. 980–1037 CE), working in the Islamic philosophical synthesis, derived their modal scale systems from just ratios derived from the monochord. Ptolemy's Harmonics (2nd century CE) catalogues multiple genus systems, all built on small-integer ratios, and explicitly argues that the ratio is not a human convention but a property of nature.

The convergence across Greek, Indian, Persian-Islamic, and medieval European theorists on the same small-integer ratios is the investigative pressure point: these traditions had no musical contact with each other, yet all arrived at 3:2, 4:3, 5:4. The harmonic series is the same structure in every medium that vibrates — it is genuinely universal physics, not cultural convention.

## What Is Lost in Leaving Just Intonation

The move to equal temperament sacrifices the acoustic purity of every interval except the octave. The equal-tempered major third is 14 cents sharp relative to the just 5:4 — wide enough to produce audible beating on sustained chords. The equal-tempered minor third is 16 cents sharp relative to 6:5. The equal-tempered fifth is 2 cents flat — narrow enough that choir singers trained by ear routinely sing it pure and must be corrected downward for piano accompaniment.

From the Pythagorean-cosmological perspective, the loss is more profound: the tuning system ceases to participate in the mathematics that governs vibrating physical systems. Every chord in equal temperament is slightly wrong in a precisely calculable way.
