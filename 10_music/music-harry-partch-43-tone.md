## Who Harry Partch Was

Harry Partch (1901–1974) was an American composer who did something almost unprecedented in 20th-century music: he built his own instruments, wrote his own theoretical justification for a wholly alternative tuning system, and then composed a substantial body of music — operas, dance theater, ritual drama — entirely within that system. His theoretical monument is Genesis of a Music (1949, expanded 1974), a book that opens with Pythagoras, moves through Helmholtz's acoustics, and arrives at a 43-tone octave built from the ratios of the first eleven overtones of the harmonic series.

Partch was working in the United States in the 1930s–1960s, moving between California and the Midwest, largely outside academic music institutions. He was not a fringe eccentric: Genesis of a Music is a rigorous theoretical text, and his instruments — the Chromelodeon (adapted reed organ), the Adapted Viola (with a cello neck fitted to a viola body to enable wider just intervals), the Cloud Chamber Bowls (Pyrex carboys from Berkeley's Manhattan Project radiation lab, hung and struck), the Quadrangularis Reversum, the Spoils of War — are engineering achievements that survive and are still performed today.

## The 43-Tone System

Partch's scale divides the octave into 43 unequal steps, each defined by a ratio involving integers no larger than 11. The generating principle is the otonality/utonality framework he derived from Helmholtz: the major chord arises from taking the 4th, 5th, and 6th partials of the harmonic series (4:5:6 — a C major chord); the minor chord arises from inverting this relationship (taking the same ratios in descent, generating what Helmholtz called undertones). Partch extended this framework through the 11-limit — meaning ratios involving the primes 2, 3, 5, 7, and 11 — producing intervals including the harmonic seventh (7:4, substantially flatter than the equal-tempered seventh) and the 11th partial (an interval roughly halfway between a perfect fourth and a tritone, with no equal-tempered equivalent).

The result is a scale with no equal-tempered equivalents except the octave. It cannot be played on any standard Western instrument without retuning or rebuilding. This was not a problem to be worked around but the entire point: Partch believed that equal temperament had so thoroughly colonized Western musical perception that the only way to hear just intervals clearly was to build instruments that could play nothing else.

## The Pythagorean Claim

Partch was explicit about his intellectual lineage. Genesis of a Music opens with a sustained critique of equal temperament and positions the book as a recovery project — an attempt to reconnect Western music to the Pythagorean insight that interval ratios are not human conventions but physical facts. He cited Ptolemy's Harmonics as a key source, valued Al-Farabi's modal theory, and drew on Helmholtz's acoustical demonstrations that just intervals produce stable, beating-free sonorities while tempered intervals do not.

The claim is large: that the adoption of equal temperament in the 18th–19th centuries was not a neutral technical improvement but a philosophical severance — a decision to value unlimited transposability over acoustic correctness, convenience over cosmological fidelity. Partch's 43-tone system is a counterproposal that treats this severance as reversible.

## The Parallel with Indian Raga

The most striking cross-tradition parallel is with Indian classical music. The raga system, which Partch knew but did not directly cite as a source, operates from an almost identical premise: the pancham (fifth, ratio 3:2) is inviolable, the precise intonation of every note in a raga is cosmologically significant, and the equal-tempered compromise is simply not available because the tradition never adopted it. Indian classical music never passed through the equal-temperament normalization that occurred in Western music, preserving just ratios not as a recovered alternative but as the unbroken default.

The investigative pressure point: two traditions — one a radical 20th-century American art project, one a 2,000-year-old South Asian performance tradition — arrive at the same practical conclusion: the pure whole-number ratio is not a preference but a requirement. Partch built new instruments to get there; Indian classical music never needed to rebuild anything because it never left.

## Legacy

Partch's instruments are now held at Montclair State University (New Jersey) and the Harry Partch Institute. His music has been performed and recorded by Newband, the Partch Ensemble, and other specialist groups. Composers including Lou Harrison, Ben Johnston, and La Monte Young extended the just-intonation project he catalyzed, and the microtonalism of the early 21st century — including xenharmonic composition and just-intonation revival — acknowledges him as a foundational figure. The dispute he crystallized — whether equal temperament is a liberation or a loss — remains unresolved.
