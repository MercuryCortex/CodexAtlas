## The Problem Equal Temperament Solves

By the early 18th century, Western music had a structural crisis. Keyboard instruments — harpsichords, organs — had to be tuned before performance and could not be retuned mid-piece. Just intonation produces pure intervals in one key at the cost of unusable intervals in distant keys: if you tune an instrument to play perfectly in C major, the interval of G-sharp to E-flat (enharmonically equivalent to A-flat to E-flat) becomes a "wolf fifth," wildly sharp and unplayable. Meantone temperament, the dominant practice from c. 1500–1700, distributed the syntonic comma across four fifths to produce a pure 5:4 major third in the central keys — but still produced wolf intervals.

Equal temperament solves this by dividing the octave into exactly twelve equal semitones, each of size 100 cents. Every fifth is 700 cents — 2 cents narrower than the pure 3:2 fifth of 701.96 cents. Every major third is 400 cents — 14 cents wider than the pure 5:4 of 386.31 cents. No interval except the octave is acoustically pure. But every key is equally usable.

## What Is Gained

The gains are real and should not be minimized. Equal temperament enables:

**Unlimited transposition:** A piece written in C major sounds identical in structure (though at different pitch) when played in F-sharp major. All keys are interchangeable.

**Chromaticism and modulation:** Composers can move freely through all twelve keys without encountering unplayable intervals. The harmonic language of Beethoven, Wagner, Brahms, and Mahler — the complex chromatic progressions, the distant key modulations — depends on equal temperament. This is genuinely new expressive territory.

**Universal notation and ensemble tuning:** All equal-tempered instruments share a common pitch framework. An orchestra's oboe (which sets the tuning A) can be matched exactly by a piano, a flute, a trombone.

J. S. Bach's Well-Tempered Clavier (1722), often cited as equal temperament's manifesto, is more precisely a demonstration of well temperament — a system that makes all keys usable while preserving some variability in interval color. Full equal temperament became standard only in the 19th century with the industrial standardization of piano manufacturing.

## What Is Lost

**Acoustic purity.** Every sustained chord in equal temperament beats. Sing a sustained major chord a cappella and the overtones align: the chord "opens." Play the same chord on a piano and the interference creates audible pulsing. This is not inaudible theory — it is the reason choral singers, string quartets, and baroque ensembles frequently adjust intonation away from equal temperament when playing without keyboard support.

**Interval color differentiation.** In meantone and other historical temperaments, different keys had different intervallic characters: C major was serene and stable (its thirds nearly pure), B major was tense and difficult (its thirds wide and bright). This was not a flaw but a feature — keys had affective identities corresponding to their actual acoustic properties. Equal temperament erases these differences. The "key character" that composers like Mattheson (1713) and Schubart (1784) described corresponds to real acoustic variation, not projection.

**The Kepler connection.** Johannes Kepler in Harmonices Mundi (1619) showed that the angular velocities of the planets at perihelion and aphelion produce ratios that closely approximate just harmonic ratios — Venus and Earth together approximate a minor sixth (5:8), Jupiter and Saturn together approximate a minor third. Kepler was working from observational data, not numerology. The ratios he found are approximately just. Equal temperament, by deforming those ratios uniformly across all intervals, moves Western music's pitch vocabulary further from the ratios that Kepler found embedded in orbital mechanics. This is the investigative pressure point: if Kepler's empirical result holds, then just intervals are not a Greek preference but a discovery about the universe, and equal temperament is a deliberate retreat from cosmological accuracy.

## The Living Dispute

The debate is not settled. Early music performance on period instruments, the "historically informed performance" movement from the 1960s onward, has restored meantone and various historical well temperaments to concert practice. Harry Partch's 43-tone just system, Lou Harrison's use of just intonation, La Monte Young's just drones, and the extended intonation of many non-Western musicians who train on equal-tempered instruments but adjust toward just intervals in practice — all represent the continuing dissent. The claim of the dissent: equal temperament is a historically specific compromise that Western culture has naturalized into an invisible assumption, and its adoption severed Western music from the mathematical structure of physical reality.
