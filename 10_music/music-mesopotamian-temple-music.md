## Mesopotamian Temple Music

The temple music of Sumer and Babylonia (c. 3000–539 BCE) is the earliest documented professional music institution in human history. It predates any other recorded musical profession by centuries and produced the oldest surviving written music theory — not as a philosophical exercise but as working technical documentation used by working musicians.

## The Gala Priests — World's First Professional Musicians

The **gala** (later Akkadian: **kalû**) were a professional priestly class whose sole function was performing lamentation music in Sumerian temples. Ur III period administrative tablets (c. 2100–2000 BCE) from Ur, Nippur, and Lagash record temple musician rosters with salaries, ration allocations, and duty schedules — the world's first documented music payroll. The Kesh Temple Hymn is among the earliest surviving examples of the sacred repertoire they maintained.

The repertoire centered on **eršemma** ("wailing of the heart") — lamentation songs performed to appease deities, mourn destroyed temples, or intercede during crisis. The Lamentation over the Destruction of Ur is a surviving full example: a lengthy ritual lament for the fall of the city, composed in a form and performance style that the gala performed professionally. Similarly, the Shulgi Royal Hymns and Enheduanna's temple hymns give evidence of the tradition's early sophistication.

## Gender-Liminality as Performance Requirement

The gala were required to perform their lamentation repertoire using **emesal** — a dialect of Sumerian normally used by women and female deities. Male-bodied priests used grammatically feminine forms while voicing divine lamentation. This is not accidental or peripheral: it is structurally embedded in the tradition. The gala were the world's first documented institution to formalize gender liminality as a ritual performance requirement — occupying a liminal third-gender position that connected them specifically to Inanna, whose mythology extensively features boundary-crossing between gendered domains.

This gender-liminality as sacred function runs parallel to later traditions of shamanic gender-crossing as cosmological access, castrato singers in Christian sacred music, and the hijra tradition in South Asian ritual contexts — but the Mesopotamian case is the earliest documented version.

## The Tuning Tablets — World's First Music Theory Documents

Before the Hurrian Hymns were deciphered, a parallel cache of tablets provided abstract music theory documents. The **CBS 10996** tablet (c. 1800–1600 BCE, held at the University of Pennsylvania) and **UET VII 126** from Ur describe:

- Tuning procedures for a nine-string instrument using a **cycle of fifths** — the same mathematical procedure rediscovered (or learned) by Pythagoras 1,200 years later
- **Named scale positions** that appear in the same names in the Hurrian notation
- Mathematical procedures for calculating consonant intervals

The oldest surviving written music theory predates the Greek tradition by over a millennium. When Pythagoras allegedly discovered that musical harmony follows mathematical ratios, he was either rediscovering what Babylonian scribes had written down on clay 1,200 years earlier — or, as ancient Greek sources actually claim, he learned it during his studies in Babylon.

## MASSIVE WIN: Documented Transmission Origin

The Mesopotamian tuning tablets are the earliest documented node in the vault's scale transmission chain. The chain runs:

**Babylonian tuning** (c. -1800, Mesopotamia) → **Hurrian Hymns** (c. -1400, Ugarit) → **Greek modes** (c. -600, allegedly via Pythagoras's Babylonian studies) → **Arabic maqam** → **Church modes** → **Western tonal system**

The gala priests who sang temple laments to Inanna are the professional predecessors of this entire tradition. Their technical vocabulary — interval names, scale names, tuning procedures — traveled through the Hurrian tablets and eventually into the Greek theoretical corpus.
