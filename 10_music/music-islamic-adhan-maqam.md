## Islamic Adhan and Maqam — Time-of-Day Modal Conventions in the Call to Prayer

The Islamic *adhan* (أَذَان, call to prayer) is performed five times daily — at Fajr (pre-dawn), Dhuhr (noon), Asr (afternoon), Maghrib (sunset), and Isha (night) — marking the five obligatory prayer times that structure Muslim daily life. In Ottoman, Egyptian, and Syrian tradition, each call has developed strong regional conventions associating specific *maqamat* (melodic modes) with specific prayer times, tuned to the emotional register appropriate to that moment.

This practice positions Islamic adhan tradition at an analytically important position in the music-time convergence spectrum: it is a *living practice*, not a philosophical prescription — the muezzin actually selects different maqamat at different times. But it is a *preference* rather than a theological obligation, and the cosmological theory connecting the maqam's scale structure to the cosmic state of the hour is not formalized.

## The Five Prayer Times and Their Maqam Conventions

Ottoman and Egyptian traditions document consistent associations (regional variations apply):

| Prayer | Time | Maqam Convention | Emotional Register |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fajr (فجر) | Pre-dawn, ~90 min before sunrise | Rast or Hijaz | Solemnity, awakening; the liminal pre-light moment |
| Dhuhr (ظهر) | Solar noon | Bayati | Clarity, full presence; the height of the day |
| Asr (عصر) | Afternoon (~mid-afternoon) | Saba or Rast | Contemplative, slightly melancholic; the day descending |
| Maghrib (مغرب) | Just after sunset | Rast or Hijaz Kar | Gratitude, completion; the closing of the day |
| Isha (عشاء) | Night, ~90 min after sunset | Hijaz Kar or Bayati | Depth, mystery, devotional stillness |

These are not canonical rules — they do not appear in the Quran or hadith as prescriptions. They are the accumulated practice of centuries of muezzin tradition, particularly in the Ottoman court tradition where music theory was highly developed and the muezzin's selection was considered an art form.

## The Maqam's Emotional Logic

The *maqam* system (Arabic: مقامات, Turkish: makam) is not simply a scale. Like the Indian raga, a maqam includes:

1. **Tonal center and hierarchy**: which notes are emphasized and avoided
2. **Characteristic melodic phrases**: specific ornaments and motions that identify the maqam
3. **Ethos (ṭāb')**: an emotional-spiritual quality specific to each maqam

The emotional associations are well-documented in Islamic music theory. Maqam **Saba** (صبا) is widely described as evoking sadness, longing, and existential depth — the reason for its placement at Asr, the hour when the afternoon light falls and the day begins its descent. Maqam **Rast** (راست) is considered balanced and natural — neither too dark nor too bright — making it suitable for the dawn's clarity and the sunset's equilibrium. Maqam **Hijaz** carries an edge of yearning that marks the pre-dawn as a moment of spiritual aspiration.

## Between Theory and Practice: The Diagnostic Position

The Islamic adhan maqam conventions occupy an analytically distinct position between the Greek ethos doctrine and the Indian raga system:

| System | Is it practiced? | Is it obligatory? | Is there a cosmological theory? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Greek ethos theory | No — philosophical prescription | N/A | Partially (Music of Spheres, planetary modes) |
| Islamic adhan maqam | **Yes** — actual muezzin selection | No — strong convention, not law | No — emotional appropriateness only |
| Catholic Liturgy of Hours | Yes — obligatory chant schedule | Yes — monastic obligation | No — commemorative, not cosmological |
| Indian raga cosmology | Yes — performance practice | Yes — cosmological obligation | Yes — Nada Brahma + rasa + Natya Shastra |

The Islamic tradition gets further than Greece (it is a living practice, not just theory) and shares with the Liturgy of Hours the feature of actual time-structured obligatory practice. But it lacks the cosmological theory that the raga system's Nada Brahma doctrine provides.

The Islamic theological framework has a specific reason for not building this theory out further: *tawhid* (divine unity) creates a strong current against claims that particular musical structures have privileged access to the divine. The doctrine that mode X *participates* in the cosmic state at dawn hour sits awkwardly with the strict Islamic distinction between Creator and creation. The raga system's Nada Brahma doctrine — that sound IS Brahman — is only available within a non-dualist metaphysical framework. Islamic theology does not permit it.

## The Muezzin as Artist

In the Ottoman tradition, the muezzin's selection of maqam was considered a refined art. The best muezzins were trained musicians who could modulate between maqamat within the adhan itself, building emotional arc. The Topkapi Palace maintained professional muezzins selected for this skill. This is a tradition where the modal choice at each prayer time is deliberate, emotionally intelligent, and aesthetically evaluated — far closer to the raga performance context than the Liturgy of Hours.

The Cairo tradition (documented by Racy in the 1980s) still shows strong conventions: the Fajr adhan from Al-Azhar Mosque uses specific maqam choices that have been maintained for generations, and listeners notice and evaluate the appropriateness of the muezzin's selection.
