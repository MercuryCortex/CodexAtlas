## Sufi Sama — Music as the Path to Divine Union

*Samāʿ* (Arabic/Persian: سماع — "listening," "hearing") is the Sufi ceremony in which music, poetry, and movement are used as a technology for *fanāʾ* — the annihilation of the ego-self in divine presence. It is the Islamic world's most developed theology of music as a path to union with God.

The primary theoretical problem sama addresses: **Is music permitted in Islam?** This is the most contentious question in Islamic music theology, and sama is both the answer and the controversy.

## The Halal/Haram Debate: Music in Islamic Theology

No verse of the Quran explicitly forbids music. The prohibition derives from:
- **Hadith sources**: Several hadith (attributed sayings of the Prophet) describe musical instruments as forbidden (*lahw wa laʿib*, "vain play and amusement")
- **Juristic interpretation**: Classical jurists differed; the Maliki and Shafi'i schools tend toward prohibition of instruments; the Hanafi school is more permissive
- **Al-Ghazali's resolution** (c. 1095 CE): The most influential medieval position — music is not inherently forbidden; its permissibility depends entirely on *intention* and *effect*. Music that arouses passion for God is not just permitted but praiseworthy; music that arouses worldly passion is prohibited.

Al-Ghazali's *Ihya Ulum al-Din* devotes an entire chapter (*Kitab al-Sama' wa al-Wajd* — Book of Listening and Ecstasy) to this question. He concludes: "The effect of music on the heart is to excite its spiritual states; it reveals what is latent there, and amplifies what is already present." If what is latent is love of God, music amplifies it. If what is latent is lust or vanity, music amplifies that instead.

This is one of the most sophisticated analyses of music's moral ambiguity in any religious tradition.

## Rumi and the Ney: The Opening of the Masnavi

The *Masnavi-ye Maʿnavi* (Spiritual Couplets) of Jalal al-Din Rumi (c. 1258–1273 CE) — the most celebrated mystical poem in the Persian language — opens with 18 verses about the ney flute:

> "Listen to the ney, how it tells the story of separations:
> 'Ever since I was cut from the reed bed,
> men and women have wept at my lament.'"
> — Masnavi, verses 1–3

The ney (*ney*, from Persian for "reed") is a simple end-blown reed flute, the same material as the reed pen that writes — and in Sufi theology, the same material as the human soul cut from its divine origin. Rumi's doctrine:
- The ney cries because it has been cut from the reed bed (the origin)
- The human soul cries because it has been cut from God (*ruh al-quds*, the divine breath)
- Music — specifically the ney's sound — is *the cry of longing itself*, not a symbol of it

The ney does not *represent* the soul's longing; it *is* the longing made audible. This is the Islamic parallel to the Vedic doctrine that sound IS Brahman — not a symbol of the divine but the divine in sonic form.

## MASSIVE WIN: The Universal Cry of Separation

| Tradition | Image / Sound | The Separation | The Return |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sufi (Rumi) | Ney flute, cut from reed bed | Soul cut from God at creation | Sama ceremony as return |
| Hindu | AUM / Nada Brahma | Cosmic sound becomes manifest world | Meditation/chant returns to source |
| Jewish | Niggun (wordless melody) | Torah's letters fall into time | Wordless singing ascends above language |
| Christian | "The Word became flesh" | Logos enters limitation | Eucharist as re-membering |
| Gnostic | Sparks of divine light | Pneuma trapped in matter | Gnosis returns sparks to Pleroma |

**All five traditions use the same structure**: the divine externalizes/separates into a limited form (ney cut from reed / sound becomes form / light becomes flesh / Logos becomes human / pneuma becomes material). The return path involves *reversing the medium of creation*: sound returns to silence, ney longs back to reed bed, sparks rise back to light.

## The Mevlevi Order and Whirling (Sema)

Rumi's son Sultan Walad founded the Mevlevi Order (Turkish: *Mevlevî*, from Mevlana = "our master," Rumi's honorific) in Konya (present-day Turkey) in the late 13th century. The Mevlevi ceremony — called *sema* in Turkish, *sama* in Arabic/Persian — is the most famous Sufi ritual in the world, known for the *semacı* (whirling dervishes):

**Structure of the Mevlevi sema ceremony:**
1. **Naʿt-i Şerif**: Eulogy poem praising the Prophet, sung solo
2. **Taksim**: Improvised ney solo — the instrumental "prayer before prayer"
3. **Devr-i Veled**: Slow procession in three circles
4. **Four Selams**: Four sections of turning (*dönme*), each with specific maqam and tempo
5. **Taksim and closing**: Return to stillness

The turning (whirling) is a precise cosmic metaphor: the dervish extends the right arm upward (receiving divine grace) and the left arm downward (channeling it into the earth). The body becomes a cosmic axis, rotating as the planets rotate. This is Sufi cosmological architecture made bodily — the music provides the temporal framework, the rotation enacts the cosmic structure.

## Hazrat Inayat Khan and the West (20th Century)

The most significant 20th-century transmitter of Sufi music theology to the West was **Hazrat Inayat Khan** (1882–1927), an Indian Sufi musician of the Chishti order who came to Europe and America beginning in 1910. His synthesis:
- Classical Indian music trained (studied with Maula Bakhsh, his grandfather, a court musician)
- Sufi initiation in the Chishti order (the order most associated with *qawwali* music)
- Developed a universal Sufi movement in the West

His text *The Mysticism of Sound and Music* (assembled from his lectures) is the most systematic English-language statement of Islamic sacred music theology. Key claim: "There is nothing in this world that is not music. The whole of life is music. The art of music is the most direct and the most powerful because it is the exact expression of the divine law."

## The Qawwali Tradition (Chishti Order)

The Chishti Sufi order (founded in Afghanistan, most active in South Asia) developed *qawwali* — a distinctive devotional musical practice used in Sufi shrines (*dargah*). Qawwali:
- Uses Arabic, Persian, Urdu, and Hindi-language devotional poetry
- Features call-and-response structure; the lead singer (*qawwal*) is supported by chorus and harmonium, tabla, and clapping
- Is performed at *ʿurs* (death anniversaries of Sufi saints) at shrines
- The most famous modern qawwal was Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan (1948–1997)

The Chishti order explicitly valued music more than most Sufi orders. Its founder Muinuddin Chishti (c. 1141–1236 CE) is reported to have said: "Music is the food of the soul, and the soul must eat."
