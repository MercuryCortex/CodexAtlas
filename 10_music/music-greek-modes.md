## Greek Modal System

The Greek modal system is one of the foundational architectures of Western music — a set of named scales, each tied to a specific **ethos** (ἦθος): an emotional character, a moral effect, a cosmological resonance. The system predates Plato by at least two centuries and survived, transmuted, into the Gregorian church modes and ultimately into all Western tonal theory.

## The Modes: Names and Characters

The Greek modes (τρόποι, *tropoi*) were originally defined by which pattern of tones and semitones a scale used, and crucially by where you *started* within that pattern. The canonical list, with their traditional emotional assignments:

| Mode | Character | Plato's Verdict | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Dorian** | Grave, martial, austere | **PERMITTED** | Mode of warriors; Apollo's mode |
| **Phrygian** | Ecstatic, passionate, Dionysian | **PERMITTED (for war songs)** | Bacchic rites; imported from Phrygia |
| **Lydian** | Soft, plaintive, relaxing | **BANNED** | "Funeral lamentations" — said to make men weak |
| **Mixolydian** | Mournful, intense | **BANNED** | "Passionate" — associated with Sappho |
| **Ionian** | Relaxed, convivial | **BANNED** | Too soft; later became the major scale |
| **Aeolian** | Sorrowful | **BANNED** | Later became the natural minor scale |
| **Locrian** | Unstable, dissonant | Seldom mentioned | Theoretical; rarely used in practice |

The system was not merely descriptive. It was **prescriptive**: each mode had a specific effect on the soul, and an ethical government had to regulate which modes its citizens heard.

## The Ethos Theory: Music as Technology of the Soul

The central claim — elaborated across Plato, Aristotle, and the Pythagorean tradition — is that **music directly shapes character**. This is not metaphor. The Greek word *ethos* means both "character" and "habit": modes produce habits of soul.

Plato articulates this most forcefully in *Republic* Book III. Socrates is designing the ideal city, and he bans most music:

> "What modes of music represent courage and temperance — those we keep. The Dorian, I believe, expresses bravery; the Phrygian, the spirit that endures. We need no more than these."

The philosophical logic: the soul is constituted by proportion (this is the *Timaeus* claim). Music that embodies the *right* proportions reinforces virtue; music in the wrong proportions corrupts the soul by habituating it to disharmony. This is essentially an application of Pythagorean harmony theory to ethics.

Aristotle (*Politics* VIII) is more permissive: different modes serve different legitimate purposes — Dorian for education, Phrygian for religious ecstasy, the softer modes for relaxation and catharsis. But he agrees on the fundamental premise: music does not merely represent emotion, it *produces* it physiologically.

## The Technical Architecture

The Greek system was built on **tetrachords** — four-note units spanning a perfect fourth (4:3 ratio). Two tetrachords joined make an octave (the *octave species*). Each of the seven modes was defined by which octave species you used: which notes were your half-steps versus whole-steps.

In modern terms:
- **Dorian**: D–E–F–G–A–B–C–D (whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half, whole)
- **Phrygian**: E–F–G–A–B–C–D–E (half, whole, whole, whole, half, whole, whole)
- **Lydian**: F–G–A–B–C–D–E–F (whole, whole, whole, half, whole, whole, half)

The most important structural principle: each mode has a characteristic **final** (resting tone) and **dominant** (reciting tone). These determine the "gravity" of the mode — which notes feel like home. This is why the same seven notes can produce seven psychologically distinct experiences.

## Plato's Republic: The First Music Censorship Argument

Plato's case in *Republic* III is the first systematic argument for state music censorship in Western history, and its logic has echoed through every subsequent period of moral panic about music:

1. Music imitates character (*mimesis*).
2. What we imitate, we become (habit forms character).
3. Therefore: ban the modes that imitate bad character.
4. Preserve only Dorian (martial virtue) and Phrygian (religious courage).

The key finding here: **this is the same logic later used by the Church Fathers to regulate Christian liturgical music.** Augustine (*Confessions* X.33) is tormented by whether beautiful music corrupts piety. Pope Gregory I's codification of modes for liturgical use is Plato's argument applied to Christian practice. The Western church inherited not just the modes themselves but the *ethical framework* for policing them.

## Transmission to Byzantine Chant and European Church Modes

The Greek modal system did not travel directly from Plato to medieval Europe. The transmission path is:

**Greek modal theory** (Aristoxenus, c. -320) → **Neo-Pythagorean synthesis** (Nicomachus, Ptolemy, c. 100–150 CE) → **Boethius** (*De Institutione Musica*, c. 510 CE) → **Carolingian music theorists** (Aurelian of Réôme, Hucbald, c. 850–900) → **Medieval church modes** (the 8-mode system, Modes I–VIII)

There is an important complication: when medieval theorists named their church modes "Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian," they *misidentified* the Greek modes. Boethius got the system partly wrong, and the medieval Dorian is not the Greek Dorian. This is documented by M.L. West and Harold Powers. The *names* survived; the *assignments* shifted.

The parallel route is through Byzantine Christianity: the **Octoechos** (eight-mode system) of Byzantine chant is a direct continuation of Greek modal practice, never broken by the Latin transmission confusion. Byzantine ison (sustained drone) connects to the [[music-sacred-drone]] tradition.
