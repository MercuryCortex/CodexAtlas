## The Four Sprouts (Sì duān 四端) — Mencius's Innate Moral Psychology

Mencius (孟子, c. 372-289 BCE), the most influential Confucian thinker after Confucius himself, articulated the doctrine of the **four sprouts** (sì duān 四端) in *Mencius* 2A.6 — a foundational text of Chinese moral psychology and the locus classicus for his thesis that **human nature is good** (xìng shàn 性善). The four sprouts are the inborn affective-cognitive dispositions that, when cultivated, develop into the four cardinal Confucian virtues. Mencius's claim is that these sprouts are universal human equipment — every human being possesses them by nature, whether or not they cultivate them.

### The Famous Thought Experiment: The Child at the Well

Mencius 2A.6 opens with a thought experiment that has been canonical in Chinese moral philosophy for 2300 years and is now standard in Western introductory ethics textbooks:

> "Suppose someone suddenly sees a child about to fall into a well — anyone in such a situation would have a feeling of alarm and compassion (cèyǐn zhī xīn 惻隱之心). They would feel this not because they wished to gain the favor of the child's parents, not because they sought praise from their neighbors or friends, not because they disliked the child's cries — but spontaneously, from their own constitution."

The argument is empirical: introspect what arises in such a moment. The answer is *cèyǐn* (compassion-alarm), and it arises *prior* to any calculation of personal advantage. Mencius infers from this universal pre-reflective response that humans have an innate moral disposition. The argument is widely regarded as the first rigorous defense of innate moral psychology in any tradition — predating Rousseau's *pitié* by 2000 years and Frans de Waal's primatological work on innate empathy by 2300.

### The Four Sprouts and Their Cultivation

The four sprouts and their full-grown virtues are:

| Sprout (端) | Translation | Develops into Virtue (德) |
|---|---|---|
| Cèyǐn 惻隱 | Compassion-alarm | Ren 仁 (humaneness) |
| Xiūwù 羞惡 | Shame-and-aversion | Yì 義 (righteousness) |
| Círàng 辭讓 | Deference-and-yielding | Lǐ 禮 (ritual propriety) |
| Shìfēi 是非 | Right-and-wrong judgment | Zhì 智 (wisdom) |

Mencius 2A.6 concludes: "Those who lack a sense of compassion are not human; those who lack a sense of shame are not human; those who lack a sense of deference are not human; those who lack a sense of right and wrong are not human. The sense of compassion is the sprout of humaneness; the sense of shame, the sprout of righteousness; the sense of deference, the sprout of ritual propriety; the sense of right and wrong, the sprout of wisdom. Humans have these four sprouts as they have their four limbs."

The biological metaphor is precise and deliberate. *Duān* (端) literally means "tip, sprout, beginning of growth." The sprouts are real moral capacities, but they are not yet developed virtues — they require **cultivation** (yǎng 養) through sustained practice, moral exemplars, and right environment to mature into full virtues. This is the central program of Mencian moral self-cultivation (the topic of Ivanhoe 2000).

### The Xunzi Debate: Innately Good vs. Innately Bad

Mencius's *xìng shàn* doctrine generated the most consequential debate in classical Chinese moral philosophy. **Xunzi** (荀子, c. 310-235 BCE), the third great Warring States Confucian, argued the opposite thesis: *xìng è* — human nature is bad (literally "evil," better "ugly/disagreeable"). For Xunzi, humans are naturally selfish and aggressive; the social-ritual order (lǐ) is a cultural achievement that constrains and reshapes raw human nature.

The Mencius-Xunzi debate prefigures the Rousseau-Hobbes debate in early modern European thought by 1900 years — and frames the entire question with comparable analytic precision. Both Chinese thinkers agree that **cultivation is necessary**; they disagree on **what is being cultivated**. For Mencius, cultivation actualizes innate moral potential; for Xunzi, cultivation reshapes recalcitrant moral matter.

The Neo-Confucian synthesis (Zhu Xi 朱熹, 1130-1200 CE) effectively sided with Mencius — the Mencian *xìng shàn* doctrine became Song-Ming Neoconfucian orthodoxy. Modern Confucian thinkers (Mou Zongsan, Tu Weiming) have continued the Mencian tradition while incorporating Xunzian recognition of the role of culture and ritual.

### The MASSIVE WIN: Mencius and Aristotle on Natural Foundations of Virtue

Jiyuan Yu (2007, *Mirrors of Virtue*) and Bryan Van Norden (2007, *Virtue Ethics*) have documented the structural parallel between Mencian and Aristotelian moral psychology. Both: (a) posit a natural foundation for virtue — sprouts (Mencius) and first-nature potentials (Aristotle); (b) require cultivation for actualization — without practice, the sprouts wither and the potentials are not realized; (c) reject pure-rationalist accounts of moral knowledge — moral truth is discovered through trained perception and habituated response, not pure deduction; (d) ground human flourishing in distinctively human nature — eudaimonia and *liángzhī* (moral knowing) as the actualization of innately-human capacities.

The convergence is independent — Mencius (4th-c. BCE Lu) and Aristotle (4th-c. BCE Athens) were near-contemporaries with no possibility of direct contact. They independently formulated the structural commitments of virtue ethics. This is among the strongest evidence for a cross-cultural philosophical convergence on the **virtue-ethics framework** in the Axial Age.

### Modern Cognitive Science: Mencius Vindicated?

Contemporary primatological and developmental-psychological research has substantially vindicated the Mencian empirical claim. Frans de Waal's work on chimpanzee empathy (*The Age of Empathy*, 2009); Felix Warneken and Michael Tomasello's experiments on 14-month-old infants showing spontaneous helping behavior (*Science* 2006); Paul Bloom's *Just Babies* (2013) — all document pre-reflective moral responses in human infants and great apes that closely parallel Mencius's *cèyǐn*. The Mencian thesis that humans have innate moral equipment is, on contemporary evidence, more defensible than the Xunzian *tabula rasa* alternative.
