## The Six Pāramitās — Bodhisattva Perfections of Mahāyāna Buddhist Ethics

The **six pāramitās** (Sanskrit: ṣaṭ pāramitā; Pali: chappāramī, but the doctrine is primarily Mahāyāna) are the foundational moral-spiritual framework of Mahāyāna Buddhist ethics — the six "perfections" (pāramitā) that constitute the bodhisattva path from initial vow to full Buddhahood. The doctrine appears in mature form in the Prajñāpāramitā literature of the late centuries BCE through 3rd century CE (the *Aṣṭasāhasrikā* and the larger Prajñāpāramitā sutras), is systematized by Nāgārjuna (c. 150-250 CE) and Asaṅga (c. 4th c.), and reaches its definitive articulation in Śāntideva's *Bodhicaryāvatāra* (8th century CE) — the most influential single text of bodhisattva moral psychology.

The six pāramitās are:

1. **Dāna pāramitā** (दान) — Perfection of generosity
2. **Śīla pāramitā** (शील) — Perfection of ethical discipline
3. **Kṣānti pāramitā** (क्षान्ति) — Perfection of patience/forbearance
4. **Vīrya pāramitā** (वीर्य) — Perfection of energy/diligence
5. **Dhyāna pāramitā** (ध्यान) — Perfection of meditative concentration
6. **Prajñā pāramitā** (प्रज्ञा) — Perfection of wisdom

(A ten-pāramitā list, adding *upāya* skillful-means, *praṇidhāna* vows, *bala* spiritual-powers, and *jñāna* gnosis, appears in some later Mahāyāna sources — most prominently the *Daśabhūmika Sūtra*, where each pāramitā is correlated with one of the ten bodhisattva stages.)

### The Bodhisattva Commitment as Ethical Framework

The pāramitā scheme is structurally distinct from the earlier Buddhist [[moral-noble-eightfold-path-as-ethics]] in its **soteriological orientation**. The eightfold path articulates conduct for the individual seeking nibbāna/nirvāṇa (the goal of the arhat). The pāramitās articulate the cumulative cultivation required of the **bodhisattva** — the being who, having generated *bodhicitta* (the awakening-mind, the resolution to attain Buddhahood for the sake of all sentient beings), undertakes the multi-eon (kalpa) path to full Buddhahood with the explicit purpose of liberating other sentient beings.

The framework's distinctive moral commitment is **other-orientation**. Each pāramitā is cultivated *for the sake of* sentient beings, not for personal liberation. The bodhisattva's *dāna* is universal generosity, including the readiness to give one's life (the famous *jātaka* tales of the Buddha-to-be's past lives include giving his body to a starving tigress); *kṣānti* includes patience under unprovoked persecution by other beings (Śāntideva *BCA* VI); *prajñā* is the wisdom that perceives the empty (*śūnya*) nature of all phenomena and thereby liberates from the dualistic self-other framing that obstructs universal compassion.

### Prajñā Pāramitā as Master Virtue

The sixth pāramitā — *prajñā*, wisdom — has a special status. In the Prajñāpāramitā sutra tradition (the foundational scriptural basis of Mahāyāna), prajñā is what makes the other five into pāramitās rather than mere worldly virtues. *Dāna* without *prajñā* is mere generosity; *dāna* informed by prajñā is the perfection of generosity, performed without attachment to giver, gift, or recipient (the famous "three-fold purity" of bodhisattva giving). The *Aṣṭasāhasrikā Prajñāpāramitā Sūtra* repeatedly emphasizes that the first five pāramitās are "blind" without the eye of prajñā guiding them.

Structurally this is the **master-virtue** thesis familiar from [[moral-aristotelian-virtues-golden-mean]] (phronēsis perfects the moral virtues) and from [[moral-confucian-ren]] (zhì / practical wisdom completes the other virtues). The pāramitā tradition's distinctive theoretical move is to ground prajñā specifically in the doctrine of **emptiness** (śūnyatā): the wisdom that perceives the empty/relational nature of all phenomena is what allows the other pāramitās to be performed without the subtle attachment that would otherwise make them merely worldly virtues.

### The MASSIVE WIN: Mahāyāna Virtue Ethics and Cross-Tradition Convergence

The pāramitā framework is, in structure, recognizably a virtue ethics — the moral life is cultivation of stable virtuous dispositions through sustained practice. Damien Keown (1992, *The Nature of Buddhist Ethics*) argued for this reading; the consensus in 21st-century Buddhist-ethics scholarship is that the pāramitā framework is virtue-ethical at its core. This places Mahāyāna Buddhism in a tri-traditional convergence with Aristotelian-Catholic virtue ethics and Confucian virtue ethics on the structural commitments of:

- (a) **stable disposition** rather than rule-compliance as the primary moral category
- (b) **habituation-based cultivation** as the moral life's central activity
- (c) **practical-wisdom master-virtue** that perfects the other virtues
- (d) **integrated character** as the moral telos

The distinctive Mahāyāna contribution to this cross-tradition virtue ethics is the **bodhicitta-soteriological orientation**: the cultivation of virtue not for personal eudaimonia/liberation but for universal liberation of sentient beings. This is the deepest moral-theological innovation of Mahāyāna and has had reception even outside Buddhism — Schopenhauer's late ethics, Tolstoy's universal-love writings, the Albert Schweitzer reverence-for-life tradition.

### Śāntideva's *Bodhicaryāvatāra* and the Practical Ethics of the Bodhisattva

The definitive text of pāramitā-tradition practical ethics is **Śāntideva's *Bodhicaryāvatāra*** ("Way of the Bodhisattva," 8th century CE, Nālandā). The ten chapters systematically work through the bodhisattva path: generating bodhicitta (I-III), confessing transgressions (II), the bodhicitta vow (III), then chapters on each major pāramitā (IV-IX with dhyāna and prajñā as IX). Chapter VI on kṣānti contains the canonical Mahāyāna analysis of anger and its overcoming; chapter IX on prajñā provides the most influential Mahāyāna articulation of the emptiness doctrine in liturgical-practical form.

The *Bodhicaryāvatāra* has been the central practical-ethics text of Tibetan Buddhism for a millennium. The Dalai Lama's *A Flash of Lightning in the Dark of Night* (1994) is a 20th-century commentary; Pema Chödrön's *No Time to Lose* (2005) is the most influential contemporary Western reception.
