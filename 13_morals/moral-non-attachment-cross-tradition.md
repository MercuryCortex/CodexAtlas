## Identity

**Non-attachment** — freedom from craving, the loosening of the heart's grip on objects, outcomes, and the self's own possessions — is one of the most widely attested ethical-soteriological principles in the record of human thought. Across traditions that shared little or no formative contact, a single diagnosis recurs: **desire/attachment is the root of bondage and suffering, and freedom is its release.** Each tradition coined its own technical vocabulary for the same counsel — Pali *taṇhā* / *upādāna* and *virāga*, Sanskrit *tṛṣṇā*, *rāga*, *saṅga*, and *vairāgya*, Arabic *zuhd* and *faqr*, Greek *apatheia* and *adiaphora*, Middle High German *Abgeschiedenheit*, Spanish *indiferencia* and *nada* — and reached it by markedly different routes and with markedly different metaphysical justifications.

The concept spans a span of roughly two millennia in its major articulations, from the Buddha's diagnosis of craving (~-450) and the near-contemporary Upaniṣadic/śramaṇa ideal of *vairāgya*, through Stoic *apatheia* (Zeno, ~-300) and the Bhagavad Gītā's *niṣkāma karma* (~-200 to +200), to Sufi *zuhd* (8th–11th c.), Eckhart's *Abgeschiedenheit* (~1300), and the Ignatian *indiferencia* codified in the *Spiritual Exercises* (approved 1548). In the Atlas this node is a **cross-tradition convergence hub** — like [[moral-golden-rule-cross-tradition]] it is given a single home tradition (Buddhism, where non-attachment is the structural core of the path rather than one virtue among many) while its reach across traditions is carried entirely by the wires, not by its `tradition` field.

## The doctrine across five traditions

**Buddhism — *taṇhā*, *upādāna*, *virāga*.** The whole Buddhist path is built on non-attachment. The Second Noble Truth ([[four-noble-truths]], *Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta*, SN 56.11) names *taṇhā* (craving — for sense-pleasure, for existence, for non-existence) as the origin of *dukkha*; the Third names the cessation of that craving as *nibbāna*, literally the "blowing out" of the three fires of greed, hatred, and delusion (*Ādittapariyāya Sutta*, SN 35.28). *Upādāna* (clinging/grasping) is the ninth link of dependent origination; its reversal is *virāga* (dispassion) leading to release (*vimutti*). The *Alagaddūpama Sutta* (MN 22) presses the point to its limit in the raft simile: even the Dhamma is to be used and let go, not grasped. See also [[doctrine-nirvana-buddhist-cessation]] and the metaphysical ground in [[anatman-no-self]].

**Hinduism — *vairāgya* and *niṣkāma karma*.** Classical Hindu thought works both a renunciant pole and an engaged pole. *Vairāgya* (dispassion, non-attachment to seen and heard objects) is a technical prerequisite of liberation: *Yoga Sūtra* 1.12–16 pairs *abhyāsa* (sustained practice) with *vairāgya* as the twin means of stilling the mind, culminating in *para-vairāgya* born of knowledge of the *puruṣa*; and the six treasures (*ṣaṭ-sampatti*) of Advaita make *vairāgya* a gate to *mokṣa* ([[doctrine-moksha-hindu-liberation]]). The engaged pole is the [[phase-2-027-bhagavad-gita|Bhagavad Gītā's]] *niṣkāma karma* / *karma-yoga*: "your right is to action alone, never to its fruits" (2:47); "yoga is equanimity" (*samatvaṃ yoga ucyate*, 2:48); one who acts "abandoning attachment" is untouched by evil "as a lotus-leaf by water" (5:10). Crucially the Gītā argues *against* mere renunciation of action in favour of acting without clinging to results — non-attachment *in* the world.

**Islam (Sufism) — *zuhd* and *faqr*.** In the Sufi map of the soul's stations ([[moral-sufi-spiritual-stations-maqamat]], systematized by al-Sarrāj, d. 988, and al-Qushayrī, d. 1074), *zuhd* (renunciation) is interior detachment from the world's claim on the heart — Junayd of Baghdad: "*zuhd* is to have one's hand empty of property and one's heart empty of pursuit" — and *faqr* (spiritual poverty) is the recognition that the seeker owns nothing of their own before God. Rābiʿa al-ʿAdawiyya (d. 801) radicalized the ideal into disinterested love (worshipping God neither from fear of Hell nor desire for Paradise), and the tradition's culmination is *fanāʾ*, the annihilation of the ego-self. The counsel is theocentric: empty the heart of all pursuit *but* God.

**Stoicism — *apatheia* and the preferred indifferents.** Stoic ethics ([[moral-stoic-virtue-ethics]]) holds that virtue alone is good and the passions (*pathē*) are false judgments about the value of externals. Health, wealth, and reputation are "preferred indifferents" (*proēgmena adiaphora*) — to be selected when available but never genuinely good, so their loss cannot harm the sage. Epictetus' *Enchiridion* opens with the "dichotomy of control" (some things are up to us — our judgments and desires — and some are not) and counsels: "Never say of anything, 'I have lost it,' but 'I have given it back'" (Ench. 11). The result is *apatheia*, freedom from the passions — the theme carried, via the patristic reception, into [[apatheia-and-virtue]].

**Christianity — *Abgeschiedenheit*, *indiferencia*, *nada*.** Christian detachment runs from the desert ascetics' appropriation of Stoic *apatheia* (Evagrius, Cassian) to its mystical summit in [[meister-eckhart|Meister Eckhart]], whose *Abgeschiedenheit* (detachment, releasement) strips the soul of all created images, desires, and even representations of God, making it "empty" and thus receptive to the divine birth — the treatise *On Detachment* ranks it above love and humility, and Sermon 52 (*Beati pauperes spiritu*) reads the Beatitudes' "poor in spirit" as one who wills nothing, knows nothing, and has nothing. The same counsel is codified as Ignatian *indiferencia* (*Spiritual Exercises* §23: we should hold ourselves "indifferent to all created things" — health or sickness, wealth or poverty, honour or dishonour — desiring only what serves the end for which we are made) and as John of the Cross's *nada* (the *todo y nada* of the Ascent of Mount Carmel: to come to possess all, desire to possess nothing).

## Cross-tradition significance (transmission vs convergence)

The honest picture is not one convergence but **three relationships of different kinds**, and the node's wires are typed to keep them distinct.

**(1) Within the Indic bloc — shared heritage, not independent convergence.** Buddhist *virāga* and Hindu *vairāgya* are the same word-root, and Buddhist *taṇhā* answers Hindu *tṛṣṇā*; both non-attachment ideals emerge from the same eastern-Gangetic renunciate (*śramaṇa*) matrix of roughly -500 to -200 (Bronkhorst 2007). This is a **secure common inheritance**, comparable to the *tĕhôm*/*Tiāmat* cognate in the Atlas's flagship "deep" cluster — a linguistic and cultural link, not a case of two cultures arriving at the same idea in isolation. The genuine disagreement inside the bloc is metaphysical: *mokṣa* typically realizes a true Self (*ātman*), whereas *nibbāna* explicitly denies one (*anattā*) — the same counsel of non-clinging on opposite ontologies.

**(2) Within the Greco-Christian bloc — documented transmission.** Stoic *apatheia* passes into Christian ascetic theology by traceable channels: Clement of Alexandria makes the true Christian *apathēs*, Evagrius and Cassian build a monastic psychology of the passions on it, and the discipline flows west to Eckhart's *Abgeschiedenheit* (Nussbaum 1994; McGinn 2001). This is **borrowing, not independent parallel**.

**(3) Across the Indic and Mediterranean blocs — the contested case.** Whether Hellenistic and Indian non-attachment ideals influenced one another (via the Gandhāran contact zone, or Pyrrho's exposure to Indian ascetics during Alexander's campaign, with Pyrrhonist *ataraxia* set beside Buddhist calm) is the live diffusion question. The Bhagavad Gītā node itself flags the *karma-yoga* ↔ Stoic *amor fati* parallel as "contested-but-suggestive." The maximalist diffusion case (McEvilley 2002) is widely judged intriguing but unproven; the standard verdict is **structural/typological parallel, not demonstrable transmission**.

So the convergence is real but layered: secure cognate links inside India, documented transmission inside the Mediterranean-Christian world, and a genuinely open question between them — a genuine cross-cultural discovery in the third case, not a single stream.

## Disputes

**One principle or many?** The strong reading — the "perennial philosophy" (Huxley 1945) and Hadot's account of ancient philosophy as convergent "spiritual exercises" — treats non-attachment as a universal contemplative discovery. The constructivist critique (Katz 1978 and successors) warns that this flattens real differences: the *objects*, *justifications*, and *ends* diverge sharply. Buddhist non-clinging aims at the extinction of rebirth on a no-self metaphysics; Vedāntic *vairāgya* aims at realizing the true Self; Sufi *zuhd* and Ignatian *indiferencia* empty the heart *for* union with a personal God; Stoic detachment rests on a rational cosmos in which externals are simply indifferent. Same behavioural counsel — loosen the grip of craving — resting on incompatible worldviews and pointing at different *telē*.

**Renunciation vs engagement.** The node deliberately gathers two structures that are not identical: a **world-renouncing** pole (monastic *zuhd*, *saṃnyāsa*, the anchorite — reduce the objects of attachment) and an **act-in-the-world** pole (the Gītā's *niṣkāma karma*, the Stoic selection of preferred indifferents, Ignatian indifference exercised *in* action — inner freedom while fully engaged). The Gītā is explicit that these can conflict, and argues for the second against the first. Reading them as one ideal risks obscuring a real intra-traditional debate about whether freedom from craving requires withdrawal from the world or perfection within it.

**Home-tradition caveat.** Assigning Buddhism as the singular home tradition is a judgment about centrality and datable articulation, not a claim of priority over the roughly contemporary Hindu *vairāgya*; the two are better read as siblings of a shared Gangetic ascetic culture than as origin and derivative.
