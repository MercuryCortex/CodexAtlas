## Islamic Sharia — The Most Comprehensive Living Divine-Law Moral System

*Sharīʿa* (Arabic: شَرِيعَة, literally "the way to the watering hole," semantically "the path to be followed") is the comprehensive moral-legal framework of Islam, derived from divine sources and governing every dimension of Muslim life — from ritual worship to commercial transactions, family law to criminal justice, personal virtue to political order. It is simultaneously moral theology, jurisprudence, and practical ethics, making it the most ambitious attempt at a total divine-command moral system in human history by scope and current adherent population.

### The Four Sources (*Usul al-Fiqh*)

Classical Islamic jurisprudence (*fiqh*) derives sharia from four authoritative sources in hierarchical order:

**1. Quran** (*al-Qur'ān*): The direct, unmediated speech of God (*kalām Allāh*) as revealed to the Prophet Muhammad over 23 years (610-632 CE). The Quran's moral directives are the highest authority — they represent divine will expressed in divine language. Moral obligations derived from explicit Quranic commands (*nass*) are binding without qualification.

**2. Sunna** (*al-Sunnah*): The Prophet's practice — his deeds, utterances, and tacit approvals (*iqrar*) as preserved in the Hadith literature. The Sunna is the second source because Muhammad's life is the human embodiment of Quranic principles. The Hadith collections (Bukhari, Muslim, Abu Dawud, Tirmidhi, etc.) form the corpus through which Sunna is accessed. The [[moral-golden-rule-cross-tradition]] is attested in the Sunna: "None of you truly believes until he wishes for his brother what he wishes for himself" (Nawawi Hadith 13).

**3. Ijma** (*al-Ijmāʿ*): Scholarly consensus — the unanimous agreement of qualified Islamic scholars (*mujtahidun*) on a legal-moral question. Where Quran and Sunna are silent, scholarly consensus provides authoritative guidance. The Prophet's statement "My community will never agree on an error" provides the theological warrant.

**4. Qiyas** (*al-Qiyās*): Analogical reasoning — deriving new rulings by analogy from established principles. If wine is prohibited because of its intoxicating effect, then other intoxicating substances are also prohibited by analogy. Qiyas introduced rational method into divine-command jurisprudence, creating internal pressure toward the [[moral-natural-law]] position.

### The Five Legal Categories: Islam's Moral Taxonomy

The most philosophically distinctive feature of sharia is its five-tier moral classification of all human acts (*al-ahkam al-khamsa*):

1. **Wājib / Farḍ** (Obligatory): Acts God has commanded; omission is sinful. (e.g., the five daily prayers, fasting Ramadan, pilgrimage once in a lifetime if able)
2. **Mandūb / Mustaḥabb** (Recommended): Acts God approves but does not require; performing generates merit; omitting is not sinful. (e.g., additional voluntary prayers, visiting the sick)
3. **Mubāḥ** (Permitted/Neutral): Acts on which sharia is silent; morally indifferent. (Most everyday activities)
4. **Makrūh** (Discouraged/Reprehensible): Acts God disapproves but does not prohibit; performing is not sinful but generates demerit; omitting is rewarded. (e.g., divorce without just cause, consuming garlic before entering a mosque)
5. **Ḥarām** (Forbidden): Acts God has prohibited; commission is sinful. (e.g., consuming alcohol, pork, usury, murder, adultery)

This five-tier taxonomy is the most nuanced moral classification system in any divine-command tradition. It recognizes a gradient of moral obligation rather than the binary permitted/forbidden of simpler systems. Compare:

- The Mosaic tradition's ([[moral-ten-commandments]]) basic structure: commanded, forbidden, and the vast space of permitted (with rabbinic elaboration into 613 commandments that fill in the permitted space with further obligations and prohibitions).
- Buddhist precepts' structure ([[moral-buddhist-five-precepts]]): abstain-from vs. not specified.
- The five-category sharia taxonomy uniquely captures the moral significance of the space between obligation and prohibition — the "recommended but not required" and "discouraged but not forbidden" zones that constitute most of moral life.

### The MASSIVE WIN: Sharia as the Living Heir of the Sinai Covenant

Sharia is not presented in Islam as a replacement for earlier divine law but as its restoration and completion. The Quran addresses this directly: Moses received the Torah (*Tawrat*) and Jesus the Gospel (*Injil*), and Muhammad received the Quran — each as divine guidance for their communities, with the Quran as the final, preserved, and universal revelation.

This makes sharia the conscious successor to the Mosaic covenant and the Decalogue tradition. The structural parallel between sharia's four sources and Jewish halakha's sources (Torah, Talmud/Oral Torah, rabbinic responsa, custom) is not coincidental — both are systems for deriving comprehensive moral-legal guidance from a primary divine text, with accumulated scholarly interpretation forming the secondary source. Both traditions distinguish the eternal divine law from its temporal application; both have ongoing debates about reform vs. traditionalism; both struggle with the relationship between divine command and rational ethics.

### The Ash'arite Theology: Sharia as Pure Divine Command

The dominant theological underpinning of Sunni sharia is **Ash'arism** — the school founded by Abu al-Hasan al-Ash'ari (874-936 CE). The Ash'arite position on moral epistemology is that good (*hasan*) and evil (*qabih*) are determined purely by divine declaration (*shar'*). Reason alone cannot establish moral norms — reason can be a tool for applying revealed law, but it cannot originate moral obligations. This is Horn 1 of the [[moral-euthyphro-dilemma]]: acts are good because God declares them good.

The Mu'tazilite school had argued the opposite: reason can establish moral norms because good and evil are objective properties of acts, and God commands what is independently good (Horn 2 — natural law within Islam, see [[moral-natural-law]]). The Ash'arites won the Sunni theological debate; the Mu'tazilites retained influence in Shia Islam and in medieval Islamic philosophy (Avicenna, Averroes).

This internal Islamic debate — Ash'arite vs. Mu'tazilite, Horn 1 vs. Horn 2 of Euthyphro — is structurally identical to the Ockham-vs-Aquinas debate in Christian scholasticism. Three traditions, the same logical problem, the same two positions, arrived at through internal theological debate rather than cross-traditional contact. The [[moral-euthyphro-dilemma]] thus appears independently in all three Abrahamic traditions — confirming that it is not merely a Greek philosophical puzzle but a structural feature of any moral theology that combines monotheism with rational ethical discourse.

### Sharia and the 1.8 Billion: Scale of Living Divine-Command Morality

By population, sharia is the largest active expression of divine-command moral theory on earth. Some form of sharia governs family law in over 50 Muslim-majority countries, criminal law in several (Saudi Arabia, Iran, parts of Nigeria), and personal piety for Muslims worldwide. The ongoing contemporary debates about sharia reform — An-Na'im's argument that classical sharia cannot be reconciled with modern human rights without reform grounded in Quranic re-reading; traditionalist arguments that sharia is inherently adaptive through ijtihad (independent reasoning) — are themselves expressions of the ancient Ash'arite/Mu'tazilite tension: is the moral content of sharia fixed by divine command, or is human reason co-constitutive of its ongoing application?
