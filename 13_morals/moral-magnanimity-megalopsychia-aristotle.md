## Megalopsychia (Μεγαλοψυχία) — Aristotle's Great-Souled Person and Its Critics

**Megalopsychia** (Greek: μεγαλοψυχία, "greatness of soul"; standardly translated "magnanimity") is the virtue Aristotle treats in *Nicomachean Ethics* IV.3 (1123a34-1125a35) as the "crown of the virtues" — the virtue that adorns and presupposes all the others. It is also the single most controversial virtue in Aristotle's enumeration, because its content — the disposition to claim and accept the great honors one knows oneself to deserve — appears to directly invert the central moral commitments of Christian, Buddhist, and modern egalitarian moral traditions. The text of *NE* IV.3 has been a privileged site of cross-tradition moral-philosophical critique for two millennia.

### The Aristotelian Definition

Aristotle defines the *megalopsychos* (the great-souled person) as one who "thinks themselves worthy of great things and is in fact worthy of them" (*NE* 1123b1-2). The mean here is between **vanity** (*chaunotēs* — thinking oneself worthy of great things but in fact unworthy) and **pusillanimity** (*mikropsychia* — thinking oneself worthy of small things while being in fact worthy of great). The great-souled person thus has the virtue of *accurate self-assessment in the direction of greatness*.

The content of the virtue is elaborated through a series of behavioral specifications. The megalopsychos: (a) is concerned with honor and dishonor, but takes only moderate pleasure even in great honor because he knows it is his due; (b) is not concerned to please others by flattering them or doing small services; (c) speaks frankly because frankness is appropriate to one who fears nobody; (d) holds himself reservedly toward people of moderate worth; (e) is slow to act but acts on a great scale when he does; (f) walks slowly, has a deep voice, speaks with measured speech; (g) does not engage in petty hatreds or fears; (h) does not bear grudges because he is too magnanimous to remember slights; (i) is a man of action who would rather give benefits than receive them.

### The Christian Inversion: Beatitudes vs. Megalopsychia

The Sermon on the Mount's [[moral-beatitudes-sermon-on-mount]] ("blessed are the meek… blessed are the poor in spirit… blessed are those who mourn") explicitly invert the Aristotelian magnanimity ideal point-by-point. Where the megalopsychos walks slowly with deep voice claiming his honor, the Beatitudes bless the meek who inherit the earth precisely *because* they do not claim it. Where the megalopsychos disdains small services to others, the Sermon commands love-of-enemy and going-the-second-mile. Where the megalopsychos does not bear grudges because he is above them, the Sermon's mercy-Beatitude commands active forgiveness from a position of vulnerability, not transcendence.

This is one of the most striking direct philosophical-religious confrontations between two complete ethical systems in the Western canon. **Augustine** in *Civitas Dei* XIV.13 reads the Aristotelian megalopsychia as a form of *superbia* (pride) — the foundational sin from which all other sins derive. **Aquinas** in *Summa Theologiae* II-II.129 attempts to rehabilitate magnanimity by recasting it as the proper proportionality-claim of one who knows their gifts come from God — but the result has not satisfied non-Catholic Christian readers, who have generally treated megalopsychia as the paradigm pre-Christian pagan ideal that the gospel overturns.

### The MASSIVE WIN: Nietzsche's Reading of the Inversion

Friedrich Nietzsche's *Zur Genealogie der Moral* (1887) makes the Aristotelian-Christian confrontation the foundational moment of his "slave-morality" hypothesis. Nietzsche reads the Beatitudes as a deliberate slave-revolt against the classical aristocratic ideal — the ressentiment of those structurally incapable of achieving megalopsychia turning their incapacity into a moral virtue by claiming that meekness is *better than* greatness of soul. The Aristotelian magnanimous person, Nietzsche argues, is the *master* (the noble, the one who creates values from a position of strength); the Beatitude-blessed meek is the *slave* (the one who denies the master's values from a position of weakness).

Nietzsche's reading is contested by Christian readers — Karl Barth, Stanley Hauerwas, John Milbank all argue that the Sermon on the Mount's interiorization-of-virtue is not a slave-morality but a distinct moral-anthropological commitment grounded in a different metaphysical-theological frame. The debate continues to be the central site of philosophical engagement with the Aristotle-vs-Christianity question.

### MacIntyre's *After Virtue* Recovery

Alasdair MacIntyre's *After Virtue* (1981) made the Aristotelian virtues — including, controversially, megalopsychia — the foundation of contemporary virtue-ethics recovery. MacIntyre argues that the Aristotelian virtue tradition was effectively abandoned after the Enlightenment's failure to ground morality in pure reason, and that the recovery of virtue ethics requires re-engaging the full Aristotelian catalog including its more difficult elements like megalopsychia. The 21st-century virtue-ethics revival (Hursthouse, Foot, Annas, Nussbaum) has been substantially shaped by this MacIntyrean program.

The contemporary reception is mixed. Some virtue ethicists (Crisp 2006, Howland 2002) defend a recognizable Aristotelian magnanimity as an authentic and valuable moral ideal — the disposition to claim what one is genuinely owed, neither understating one's worth nor overstating it. Others (Curzer 1990, Brewer 2009) treat the specifically Aristotelian content as historically contingent on the honor-culture of 4th-century Athens and not transferable to contemporary moral life.

### Cross-Tradition Reception

The structural parallel between Aristotelian megalopsychia and the **Confucian junzi** (君子, gentleman-sage) is suggestive. Both are moral-aesthetic ideals of complete-virtuous-character; both involve appropriate self-assessment; both are the "crown" of the broader virtue scheme. But the Confucian junzi is constituted by **relational humaneness** ([[moral-confucian-ren]]) — the right-feeling and right-acting in the wǔlún relational matrix — while the Greek megalopsychos is constituted by **self-aware proportion-claiming** — the accurate knowledge of one's own worth and the readiness to claim it. The convergence is at the level of "crowning-virtue function" but diverges sharply at the level of substantive virtue-content.
