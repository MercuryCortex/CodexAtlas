## Sin and Moral Transgression — Offense Against a Lawgiver vs. Karmically-Harmful Error Across Five Traditions

Every developed religious tradition marks off a class of acts that must not be done, and names the condition incurred by doing them. But the traditions differ profoundly on *what kind of thing* that condition is. Is a transgression an **offense against a personal divine lawgiver** — a rupture of relationship that leaves the agent **guilty** and in need of forgiveness that only the offended party can grant? Or is it a **causally-harmful error**, rooted in ignorance, that automatically **pollutes** the agent or deforms their future, with consequences as impersonal and reliable as physics? This is one of the deepest fault lines in the comparative study of moral wrongdoing, and it runs between the Abrahamic (Semitic-monotheist) traditions on one side and the Indic (karmic) traditions on the other. This node maps the five principal vocabularies of sin — Christian *hamartia*, Jewish *ḥeṭ / ʿavon / pesha*, Islamic *dhanb / ithm* with the *kabāʾir / ṣaghāʾir* grading, Hindu *pāpa* (against *puṇya*), and Buddhist *akusala* — and frames the guilt-versus-ignorance axis that separates them.

### The Two Poles: Guilt Before a Lawgiver vs. Karmic or Impure Error

Paul Ricoeur's *The Symbolism of Evil* (1960) supplies the master framework. Ricoeur distinguishes three symbolic strata in the human experience of wrongdoing: **defilement** (*souillure* — the archaic sense of moral wrong as a *stain* that contaminates), **sin** (*péché* — wrong as the *rupture of a personal bond* with the divine), and **guilt** (*culpabilité* — the interiorized, individual sense of *being at fault*). Every tradition contains all three, but each foregrounds a different one, and that choice of emphasis is what the guilt-ignorance axis measures.

The **Abrahamic pole** foregrounds sin-as-relational-rupture and guilt: because there is a personal God who commands, transgression is a *personal offense* against a *someone*, and the debt it creates can only be cancelled by that someone (through atonement, sacrifice, repentance, or grace). The **Indic pole** foregrounds defilement and impersonal causation: *pāpa* is a demerit that ripens into suffering by the law of karma, and *akusala* is unskillful action that harms the agent whether or not any god notices — so the remedy is *purification* or *the dissolving of ignorance*, and no forgiver is required. The axis is real, but — as the sections below insist — it is a matter of *primary emphasis*, not of exclusive types.

### Christianity: *hamartia* and the grading of sin

The New Testament word for sin is Greek **ἁμαρτία** (*hamartia*), from the verb *hamartanō*, whose standard lexical sense is "to **miss the mark**, to fail of one's aim" (the archery/path metaphor). The Septuagint had already chosen *hamartia* to render the Hebrew *ḥaṭṭāʾt* — so the Greek word carried a Hebrew freight into Christian usage (see *Cross-tradition significance*). Paul universalizes it: "all have sinned (*hēmarton*) and fall short of the glory of God" (Romans 3:23), and in Romans 5–7 sin becomes not merely an act but a **power** (*hē hamartia*) that entered the world through Adam and enslaves the will.

Christianity develops two great gradings of sin. The first is **mortal vs. venial** (*peccatum mortale / veniale*), rooted in 1 John 5:16–17 ("there is a sin unto death... and there is a sin not unto death") and systematized by Thomas Aquinas (*Summa Theologiae* Ia–IIae, esp. q. 88): mortal sin destroys charity and the soul's ordering to God and merits damnation absent repentance; venial sin wounds but does not sever the relationship. The second is the scheme of the **seven capital (or "deadly") sins** — *superbia, avaritia, luxuria, invidia, gula, ira, acedia* — which Morton Bloomfield (1952) traced from the *eight logismoi* ("evil thoughts") of Evagrius Ponticus (4th c.) through John Cassian to the canonical list of seven fixed by Gregory the Great (*Moralia in Job*, c. 590). Distinct again is the Latin doctrine of **original sin** (see [[original-sin]] and [[fall-of-humanity]]): Augustine's teaching that Adam's guilt, not merely his mortality, is inherited by every descendant — a doctrine with no exact counterpart outside Latin Christianity.

### Judaism: *ḥeṭ*, *ʿavon*, *pesha* — the three grades

Biblical Hebrew grades transgression by intent using three principal roots, which appear together in the great confessional formulae. **ḥeṭ / ḥaṭṭāʾt** (√ḥ-ṭ-ʾ, "to miss, to err") is the least culpable — *inadvertent* sin, error, missing the way; the *ḥaṭṭāʾt* ("sin-offering," Leviticus 4) atones specifically for sin committed *bishgagah*, "in inadvertence." **ʿavon** (√ʿ-w-h, "to bend, to twist") is *iniquity* — the deliberate, crooked act together with the guilt and liability it fixes on the agent. **pesha** (√p-š-ʿ, "to rebel, to breach") is the gravest — *transgression as willful rebellion*, the same word used politically for a vassal's revolt (2 Kings 3:7). The three stand side by side in God's self-revelation as "forgiving *ʿavon wā-pesha wə-ḥaṭṭāʾā*" (Exodus 34:7) and in the high priest's confession over the scapegoat, who bears "all their iniquities (*ʿavonot*)... and all their transgressions (*pishʿehem*), all their sins (*ḥaṭṭoʾtām*)" (Leviticus 16:21). The Talmud (Yoma 36b) debates the correct order of their confession precisely because they form an ascending scale of culpability, from inadvertent error to defiant rebellion.

Two scholarly correctives complicate the "guilt" reading. Gary Anderson (*Sin: A History*, 2009) shows that the dominant *metaphor* shifts over time: the older Hebrew image is sin as a **weight or burden** one carries (one "bears" iniquity), which in the Second Temple period gives way to sin as a **debt** (Aramaic *ḥôbā*) — the metaphor behind the Lord's Prayer's "forgive us our debts." And Jonathan Klawans (*Impurity and Sin in Ancient Judaism*, 2000) demonstrates that Israelite religion also treats grave moral sin as a **defiling impurity** that pollutes the land and the sanctuary — so the "stain" pole that the axis assigns to India is strongly present inside the Hebrew system too.

### Islam: *dhanb*, *ithm*, and the *kabāʾir / ṣaghāʾir*

Qurʾanic Arabic has a rich sin-vocabulary, analyzed classically by Toshihiko Izutsu (*Ethico-Religious Concepts in the Qurʾān*, 1966): **dhanb** (fault, offense), **ithm** (sinful wrongdoing and its guilt), **khaṭīʾa** (error/sin — the Semitic **cognate** of Hebrew *ḥeṭ*), **maʿṣiya** (disobedience — the relational core, since a sin is literally a *disobeying* of God), and **sayyiʾa** / **fāḥisha** (an evil deed / a grave indecency). The signature Islamic grading is **kabāʾir vs. ṣaghāʾir** — major vs. minor sins. Qurʾan 4:31 promises that "if you avoid the major sins (*kabāʾir*) you are forbidden, We will remit your lesser misdeeds," and 53:32 speaks of those who shun the *kabāʾir* and *fawāḥish* "save small faults (*lamam*)." Later jurists enumerated the majors: al-Dhahabī (d. 748/1348) lists some seventy in his *Kitāb al-Kabāʾir*. Above the entire scale stands **shirk** — associating partners with God — the one sin declared **unforgivable** if unrepented (Qurʾan 4:48, 4:116). Islam is thus the paradigmatic instance of the lawgiver pole: sin *is* disobedience to a commanding God, graded by the gravity of the command breached.

### Hinduism: *pāpa* against *puṇya*

In the Indic frame the governing pair is **pāpa** (demerit, sin, evil) against **puṇya** (merit, virtue) — the two sides of a karmic ledger. *Pāpa* is not primarily an offense against a lawgiver; it is action contrary to *dharma* that generates bad karma and ripens, automatically, into suffering and lower rebirth (see [[moral-karma-as-moral-law]] and [[moral-rta-cosmic-order]]). Crucially, *pāpa* also behaves as an **impurity** — a stain that can be *removed* by *prāyaścitta* (expiation/penance), by bathing at a *tīrtha* (sacred ford), by *dāna* (giving), *tapas* (austerity), or *japa* (recitation). The *Mānava-Dharmaśāstra* (Manu), in Patrick Olivelle's critical edition (2005), devotes its eleventh chapter to expiations and enumerates the **mahāpātakas**, the "great sins" (Manu 11.55): killing a Brahmin (*brahmahatyā*), drinking liquor (*surāpāna*), stealing gold (*steya*), violating the guru's wife (*gurutalpa*), and associating with those who commit these. Yet the theistic *bhakti* traditions qualify the impersonal picture sharply: there sin can be a genuine offense against a personal Lord (*Īśvara*), and divine grace (*prasāda*) can forgive *pāpa* directly — the point at which the Indic pole reaches back toward the Abrahamic.

### Buddhism: *akusala*, the unskillful

Buddhism supplies the cleanest illustration of the ignorance pole because it has, strictly, **no concept of sin at all**. Its category is **akusala** — "unwholesome" or "**unskillful**" — the opposite of *kusala*, "wholesome/skillful." An *akusala* act is not an affront to a deity (there is none to affront) but action springing from the **three unwholesome roots** — *lobha* (greed), *dosa* (hatred), and *moha* (delusion) — of which *moha*/*avidyā* (ignorance) is the deepest source, so that transgression is, at bottom, a *mistake* born of not-seeing. What makes an act *akusala* is above all its **intention** (*cetanā*): "it is intention that I call karma." The tradition specifies ten *akusala-kammapatha* (unwholesome courses of action) and, at the extreme, the five *ānantarika-kamma* — heinous deeds of immediate retribution (matricide, patricide, killing an arhat, wounding a Buddha, splitting the Sangha). Damien Keown (*The Nature of Buddhist Ethics*, 1992) argues that translating *akusala* as "sin" is a category-mistake: it is a **soteriological-psychological** notion, not a juridical-theological one. Even so, Buddhism developed **confession** — the fortnightly *uposatha* recitation of the *Pātimokkha* and Mahāyāna repentance liturgies — so the impulse to disclose and clear transgression is not confined to the theistic traditions.

### The MASSIVE WIN: The Guilt–Ignorance Axis

The comparative payoff is a single, sharp contrast that reorganizes the whole field. On the **Abrahamic** model, transgression is *relational and juridical*: because a personal God commands, sin is an **offense that incurs guilt**, a debt or a rupture that must be *cancelled* — hence the centrality of atonement, sacrifice, repentance, and grace, all of which presuppose a **forgiver**. On the **Indic** model, transgression is *causal and impersonal*: *pāpa* and *akusala* are **error, demerit, or impurity** that ripen or adhere by the automatic law of karma — hence the centrality of *purification* (washing off a stain) and *enlightenment* (dissolving the ignorance at the root), neither of which needs a forgiver at all. Guilt is a *state before a person*; impurity is a *condition of the self*; ignorance is a *defect of vision*. The traditions solve the same problem — how to be rid of the wrong one has done — with two structurally different instruments: a **transaction that cancels guilt**, or a **process that removes a stain or lifts a blindness**.

The honest refinement, following Ricoeur, is that both poles run *through every tradition as strata*. Hebrew religion has a powerful defilement layer (Klawans) and moved toward a debt-metaphor (Anderson); Hindu *bhakti* and theistic Buddhism have genuine forgiveness layers. What differs is which stratum each tradition makes *load-bearing* — and on that, the divide between the covenant religions and the karmic religions is as clear as any structural contrast in comparative ethics.

### Cross-tradition significance: convergence, not transmission

Secure **linguistic continuity** exists *only within* the Semitic/Abrahamic pole. Hebrew *ḥeṭ* (√ḥ-ṭ-ʾ) and Arabic *khaṭīʾa* (√ḫ-ṭ-ʾ) are genuine **cognates**, both descended from a common Semitic root for "erring." And the Septuagint's decision to render *ḥaṭṭāʾt* by *hamartia* is a **translation-bridge** that carried the Hebrew "missing-the-mark" image into Greek and thence into the entire Christian tradition — a documented channel of transmission, not a coincidence. Across the **Abrahamic–Indic divide, by contrast, there is no cognation and no documented borrowing**: Sanskrit *pāpa* and Pali *akusala* are etymologically unrelated to the Semitic roots, and no historical contact accounts for the parallel. The correspondence is therefore **structural / typological convergence** — the predictable result that every society positing post-mortem or karmic accountability evolves a graded vocabulary of the not-to-be-done — and must not be presented as a genealogical tree. This is the same discipline the Atlas applies to the primordial-deep cluster: distinguish the secure cognate (there, *tĕhôm* ~ *Tiāmat*) from the merely structural parallel.

One earlier layer does plausibly feed the Abrahamic pole: the Egyptian **Negative Confession** ([[moral-egyptian-negative-confession-42]], c. 1550 BCE) is one of the oldest surviving *structured checklists* of transgressions, and Jan Assmann (1990) reads the shared moral categories as **structural convergence** with — not proven descent into — the Mosaic Decalogue. It is an ancestor (if any) of the Abrahamic *enumerated-sin* genre, and of nothing in the Indic vocabulary.

### Disputes

- **Is "sin" even the right word?** Keown argues that "sin" mistranslates *akusala*, smuggling in a divine lawgiver Buddhism explicitly denies; Indologists raise the same objection to glossing *pāpa* as "sin." This node uses "sin / transgression" as an **etic** comparative label for the shared slot — the not-to-be-done and its consequence — not as a claim that the Indic categories are theological. A reviewer may reasonably prefer a more neutral title (e.g. "moral transgression") to avoid importing the Abrahamic frame.
- **The guilt/impurity binary is too clean.** Ricoeur and Klawans show that defilement/impurity is central to the Hebrew system, and Anderson shows the dominant Second-Temple metaphor was *debt*, not "missing the mark." The axis is a matter of *primary emphasis*, not exclusive types; overdrawn, it caricatures both sides.
- **Original sin is not "the" Christian doctrine of sin.** The Augustinian doctrine of *inherited guilt* ([[original-sin]]) is a specifically **Latin** intensification, rejected by Eastern Christianity (which inherits mortality, not guilt), absent from Judaism, and contradicted by the Islamic doctrine of *fiṭra* (an innately sound human nature). Treating it as the paradigm of sin misrepresents both the intra-Christian split and the wider comparison.
