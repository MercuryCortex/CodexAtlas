## Humata Hūxta Huvaršta — Zoroastrian Triple Ethic of Good Thoughts, Good Words, Good Deeds

**Humata hūxta huvaršta** (Avestan: humata-hūxta-huvaršta; Pahlavi: humat-hūxt-huvašt; "good thoughts, good words, good deeds") is the foundational moral formula of Zoroastrian ethics — the triple-aspect ethic that operationalizes the cosmic struggle between asha (truth/order) and druj (falsehood/chaos) at every level of human moral agency. The formula is among the oldest continuously-used liturgical-ethical formulas in any religious tradition, attested in the Younger Avestan corpus from at least the early 1st millennium BCE and likely going back to Zarathustra's own teaching c. 1200-1000 BCE.

The locus classicus is **Yasna 19.18** and **Yasna 35.2-8**, where the triple ethic is integrated into the daily liturgical recitation. Yasna 35.2 in Skjærvø's 2011 translation: *"We praise the good thought, the good word, the good deed, both those (already) thought, spoken, done — and those yet to be thought, spoken, done."* The formula is recited daily by observant Zoroastrians during the *Hōšbām* morning devotion and at the renewal of the *kustī* sacred girdle.

### The Triple Structure and Its Theological Implications

The triple ethic is not three separate moral commands but a **single integrated moral demand** articulated at three levels of agency. The Zoroastrian moral subject is responsible:

1. **At the level of thought** (humata, *humata*) — the intentions, attitudes, dispositions one entertains in one's mind; the inner consent or dissent to *asha* or *druj* prior to any external manifestation.

2. **At the level of speech** (hūxta, *hūxta*) — the words spoken, including not only the prohibition of falsehood but the positive obligation to truthful speech, to honest contracts, to faithful witness, and to liturgical-ritual speech rightly performed.

3. **At the level of action** (huvaršta, *huvaršta*) — the deeds done, including the *yašt* sacrificial obligations, the obligations of agricultural labor (Zoroastrian moral theology specifically valorizes agricultural production as participation in the divine project), and the ritual-ethical obligations of the community.

The theological insight is that moral integrity requires **alignment across all three levels**. Good action without good thought is hypocrisy; good thought without good action is impotence; good speech without good action is empty oath. The triple ethic articulates a holistic moral psychology that integrates inner disposition, social communication, and external conduct into a single normative demand.

### The Choice of Religion: Yasna 30.3-4

The triple ethic is grounded in the foundational Zoroastrian doctrine of **moral choice**. Yasna 30.3-4 (one of the Gāthās attributed to Zarathustra himself) presents the cosmic struggle between Spənta Mainyu (Beneficent Spirit) and Aŋra Mainyu (Hostile Spirit) and frames human history as the arena of human moral choice:

> "The two primal Spirits, who are twins, appeared as the better and the bad, in thought, in word, and in deed. Between these two, the wise choose well, the unwise not so."

The triple formula here — "in thought, in word, and in deed" — names the three levels at which the moral choice is enacted. The Zoroastrian moral agent is constitutively a *chooser*: at every level of agency, at every moment, one is choosing alignment with asha or with druj.

### The MASSIVE WIN: Cross-Tradition Triadic Moral-Psychology Convergence

The Zoroastrian triple ethic stands in a striking cross-tradition convergence with multiple Eurasian moral traditions on the structural commitment to **integrating thought-speech-action**. The Buddhist [[moral-noble-eightfold-path-as-ethics]] organizes its eight factors into three Sīla (ethical conduct: speech-action-livelihood), three Samādhi (concentration: effort-mindfulness-concentration), and two Paññā (wisdom: view-intention) — making the right-thought / right-speech / right-action triad explicit and central.

Other parallels: the Christian Catholic moral-theology distinction between sins of thought, word, and deed (preserved in the *Confiteor* — "in thought and in word, in what I have done and in what I have failed to do"); the rabbinic distinction between *kavanah* (intention), *dibbur* (speech), and *ma'aseh* (action) in legal-moral analysis; the Vedic-Hindu triple of *manas* (mind), *vāc* (speech), and *karma* (action) as the three modes of human agency. The cross-tradition convergence on this triple structure suggests it captures something genuinely fundamental about the structure of human moral agency.

The Zoroastrian formulation may be the earliest articulated form of the cross-tradition pattern. If Boyce's dating of Zarathustra to c. 1200-1000 BCE is correct (a position not universally accepted; Skjærvø and others date him later), the humata-hūxta-huvaršta formula precedes the Buddhist eightfold path by 700-500 years, the Christian Confiteor by 1800-2000 years, and the rabbinic intention-speech-action distinction by 1000-1300 years.

### The Practical Ethics: Agriculture, Truth-Telling, Ritual Purity

The Zoroastrian triple ethic generates a distinctive practical-ethical program. In contrast to the Hindu *renunciation* tradition (the ideal of *saṃnyāsa*, leaving the world) and the Buddhist *monastic* tradition (the ideal of going-forth, *pabbajjā*), Zoroastrianism specifically valorizes engaged worldly action — agricultural production, family life, social-economic engagement — as the locus of moral practice. The *good deeds* of *huvaršta* include the *yašt* sacrifices but also include planting trees, irrigation work, family responsibility, and economic productivity (the Sasanian-period Pahlavi text *Pus i Dānāgān* articulates this systematically).

The *good words* of *hūxta* center on **truth-telling** — not merely the prohibition of falsehood but the positive cultivation of honest speech as a religious-ethical obligation. The Zoroastrian valorization of truth-telling has been historically remarked by Greek and Roman observers (Herodotus *Histories* 1.136-138) and is preserved in the modern Parsi community's traditional moral self-identification.

The *good thoughts* of *humata* center on the cultivation of attitudinal dispositions aligned with *asha* — kindness, generosity, integrity, devotion — and the avoidance of attitudinal dispositions aligned with *druj* — anger, envy, deceit, malice. The integration of inner-disposition cultivation with external-conduct discipline anticipates the interiorization moves of [[moral-beatitudes-sermon-on-mount]] and [[moral-aristotelian-virtues-golden-mean]] by 1000-1500 years.
