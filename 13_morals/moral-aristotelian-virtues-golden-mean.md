## Aristotelian Virtue Ethics — The Golden Mean and Eudaimonia

Aristotle's **Nicomachean Ethics** (c. 350 BCE) is the foundational text of Western virtue ethics. Its central claim is that human flourishing — **eudaimonia** ("happiness," better translated as "flourishing" or "living well") — is the supreme human good, and that eudaimonia is achieved through the lifelong cultivation of **aretē** (excellence/virtue) by habituation. Virtues are stable dispositions of character, not isolated acts; they are acquired by practice as a craft is acquired, not bestowed by divine command or discovered by pure reason.

The Ten-Book treatise was lectures delivered at the Lyceum, edited posthumously and named after Aristotle's son Nicomachus. Together with the parallel *Eudemian Ethics* (probably an earlier draft) and the *Magna Moralia* (probably a Peripatetic epitome), it constitutes the most influential systematic moral treatise in Western intellectual history.

### The Doctrine of the Mean (*Mesotēs*)

Aristotle's most famous and most misunderstood thesis is the **doctrine of the mean**: every moral virtue is a "mean between two extremes" (*Nic. Eth.* II.6, 1106b36). Courage (*andreia*) is the mean between cowardice (deficiency) and rashness (excess); generosity (*eleutheriotēs*) is the mean between stinginess and wastefulness; truthfulness (*alētheia*) is the mean between self-deprecation and boastfulness; magnanimity (*megalopsychia*) is the mean between pusillanimity and vanity; even-temperedness (*praotēs*) is the mean between irascibility and spiritlessness.

The doctrine is *not* a counsel of moderation in all things (the popular misreading) — Aristotle is explicit that some actions and emotions (murder, adultery, envy, spite) admit of no mean: they are wrong in any quantity. The mean is also *not* a numerical midpoint; it is the contextually-correct response, "according to right reason" (*kata ton orthon logon*), determined by the person of practical wisdom (*phronimos*). The mean is "relative to us" (*pros hēmas*) — what counts as courage for a small child differs from courage for a soldier.

The structural insight is that **moral excellence is a craft of calibration**, not a rule-following exercise. A person with virtue has trained themselves to feel the right emotion, toward the right object, in the right degree, at the right time, for the right reason, in the right way. This is the central character-ethics insight that has been recovered in twentieth-century virtue theory (Anscombe 1958, MacIntyre 1981, Foot 1978, Hursthouse 1999).

### The Four Cardinal Virtues (with Plato as Hinge)

The **four cardinal virtues** — *phronēsis* (practical wisdom), *andreia* (courage), *sōphrosynē* (temperance), and *dikaiosynē* (justice) — are not formally enumerated as a tetrad by Aristotle himself; the tetrad is Platonic (*Republic* IV, 427e-444e). Aristotle treats each virtue independently in *Nic. Eth.* III-V. The systematic four-fold scheme entered the Western canon through Plato → Stoa → Cicero (*De Officiis* I, 15) → Ambrose → Augustine → Aquinas, where it became fused with the three theological virtues (faith, hope, love — 1 Cor 13:13) into the seven-virtues schema of Catholic moral theology.

The Aristotelian innovation over Plato is the integration of *phronēsis* as the practical-reason "master virtue" that perceives the contextual mean in any given situation. Plato treated wisdom as primarily contemplative; Aristotle distinguishes contemplative wisdom (*sophia*) from practical wisdom (*phronēsis*) and locates moral excellence in the latter. This is the structural ancestor of the Aquinas-Catholic moral-theology architecture and of Kantian-Hegelian distinctions between theoretical and practical reason.

### Eudaimonia as Telos: The Function (Ergon) Argument

The *Nicomachean Ethics* opens with the question: what is the human good? Aristotle answers by an inferential structure called the **function (ergon) argument** (*Nic. Eth.* I.7, 1097b22-1098a20). Every craft has a function; the good of the craft is excellence at that function. A flute-player is good if they flute well; a sculptor is good if they sculpt well. The human is good if they perform the distinctively human function well. The distinctively human function is *rational activity of the soul in accordance with virtue*, conducted over a complete life. Eudaimonia is therefore the rational-virtuous activity of the soul in a complete life-span.

This is a **teleological** ethics: human flourishing is grounded in the kind of being humans are. It rejects both divine-command theory (the good is not what God commands but what fulfills human nature; see [[moral-euthyphro-dilemma]]) and consequentialism (the good is not pleasure or preference-satisfaction but excellent functioning). It is structurally parallel to Buddhist liberation-as-flourishing (the *summum bonum* is *nirvana*, achieved by the eight-fold cultivation; see [[moral-noble-eightfold-path-as-ethics]]) and to Confucian *ren* as the fulfillment of distinctively human nature.

### The MASSIVE WIN: Convergence with Confucian and Buddhist Character Ethics

Jiyuan Yu (2007, *The Ethics of Confucius and Aristotle*) has documented in detail the structural parallel between Aristotelian *aretē* and Confucian *ren* ([[moral-confucian-ren]]). Both traditions ground moral theory in habituation-based character cultivation; both reject divine command and rule-following as the primary moral framework; both identify a master virtue (Aristotelian *phronēsis* / Confucian *zhi* practical wisdom paired with *ren* humaneness); both locate the moral life in concrete relational contexts rather than abstract principles. The convergence is independent — no documented contact between Athens of the 4th century BCE and Lu of the 5th century BCE — and constitutes one of the clearest cases of independent cross-cultural derivation of virtue ethics.

Damien Keown (1992, *The Nature of Buddhist Ethics*) has argued that Buddhist ethics is best understood as analogous to Aristotelian virtue ethics: the [[moral-noble-eightfold-path-as-ethics]] is a regimen of character cultivation aimed at flourishing (*nirvana*), not a rule-system aimed at compliance. The eight path-factors (right view, intention, speech, action, livelihood, effort, mindfulness, concentration) parallel the Aristotelian unity-of-the-virtues thesis: full possession of one virtue requires the others.

This three-way convergence (Greek, Indian, Chinese) on a habituation-based, telos-directed, character-cultivation ethics is among the strongest evidence for the **Axial Age thesis** (Jaspers 1949): in the period c. 800-200 BCE, Eurasian civilizations independently moved from external rule-following religious morality to interiorized character ethics.
