## Natural Law — Moral Norms Discoverable by Reason as Built into Nature

Natural law (*lex naturalis*; Greek: *physikos nomos*) is the doctrine that moral norms exist independently of human convention and divine positive command, and are in principle discoverable through the exercise of human reason applied to the nature of human beings and the world. Moral knowledge does not require revelation or the authority of a commanding God — it requires reason rightly applied to what human nature is and what it is for.

Natural law is the primary philosophical alternative to [[moral-divine-command-theory]] within Western moral philosophy, and the key bridge between Greek ethics and Christian, Jewish, and Islamic moral theology.

### The Stoic Foundation: Logos as Cosmic Moral Order

Natural law theory originates with the **Stoics** (Zeno of Citium, c. 334-262 BCE; Chrysippus, c. 280-207 BCE). For the Stoics, the universe is pervaded by *Logos* — a rational principle that is simultaneously the causal order of nature and the moral order of rational beings. Humans are uniquely capable of participating in the Logos through reason; acting in accordance with reason is acting in accordance with nature; acting in accordance with nature is virtue; virtue is the only genuine good.

This generates natural law in its purest form: the moral law is the rational law of nature, accessible to human reason, binding on all rational beings regardless of their citizenship, culture, or religious tradition. This universalism was revolutionary — Stoic natural law provided the philosophical basis for the Roman legal concept of *ius gentium* (law of peoples), a law binding on all nations that transcended the positive law of any particular city.

**Cicero** (106-43 BCE) provides the most influential ancient statement (*De Re Publica* 3.22): "True law is right reason in agreement with nature; it is of universal application, unchanging and everlasting; it summons to duty by its commands, and averts from wrongdoing by its prohibitions... It is a sin to try to alter this law, nor is it allowable to repeal any part of it, and it is impossible to abolish it entirely... One eternal and unchangeable law will be valid for all nations and all times, and there will be one master and ruler, that is, God."

Note the crucial move: Cicero identifies natural law with divine reason. The Logos/God distinction from Stoic theology serves as the bridge between Greek natural law and theological natural law.

### Aquinas: Natural Law as Participation in God's Eternal Law

Thomas Aquinas (1225-1274) provides the synthesis that dominated Catholic and much of Protestant moral philosophy for seven centuries. His classification in *Summa Theologiae* I-II, qq. 90-94:

1. **Eternal Law** (*lex aeterna*): God's reason as it governs all creation — the ultimate source of all order.
2. **Natural Law** (*lex naturalis*): Human reason's participation in the eternal law — the portion of God's rational order that human beings can access through their natural rational faculties.
3. **Human Law** (*lex humana*): Positive law derived from natural law by human reason and applicable to particular societies.
4. **Divine Law** (*lex divina*): Revealed law (Scripture, Tradition) that supplements natural law, especially regarding supernatural ends.

The brilliant Thomistic move: natural law is not *independent* of God (which would create the Euthyphro Horn 2 problem) but is *participation* in God's eternal law. Moral norms are grounded in God, but accessible to human reason without revelation. This allows Aquinas to affirm both divine sovereignty (ethics is ultimately theological) and human rational autonomy (ethics is accessible by reason). The [[moral-euthyphro-dilemma]] is resolved by distinguishing: God doesn't command what is arbitrarily good (avoiding Horn 1's arbitrariness), nor does God command what is independently good (avoiding Horn 2's implication of God's constraint) — instead, what God's eternal law requires *just is* rational natural law, and humans access both through reason.

### The MASSIVE WIN: Natural Law as the Bridge to Modernity

The transmission of natural law from Stoics through Cicero through Aquinas to early modern political philosophy is one of the most consequential chains in intellectual history:

- **Hugo Grotius** (1583-1645): secularized natural law — he famously argued that natural law would be valid "even if God did not exist" (*etiamsi daremus non esse Deum*). This move makes natural law available to the emerging secular state.
- **John Locke** (1632-1704): natural law grounds natural rights — humans have rights to life, liberty, and property by nature; government exists to protect these natural rights; if it fails, the people may dissolve it. The political philosophy of the American and French revolutions is built on Lockean natural-rights theory, which is Ciceronian/Thomistic natural law transmitted through Protestant natural-rights thinking.
- **The Declaration of Independence**: "We hold these truths to be self-evident" — Jefferson's "self-evident" moral truths are natural law truths, in principle accessible to all rational beings. The document's moral confidence depends entirely on the natural law tradition.

The chain: Stoic Logos → Cicero → Aquinas → Grotius → Locke → Declaration of Independence — natural law as the intellectual spine of Western liberal political philosophy.

### Cross-Tradition Parallels: Three Independent Natural-Law Frameworks

Natural law is not exclusively a Western Greek-Christian invention. Three independent frameworks arrive at analogous conclusions:

**Confucian Tianming**: The Mandate of Heaven ([[moral-confucian-ren]]) is a cosmic moral order accessible through the virtue of the sage-king — not a personal commanding God but a natural moral order that the universe enforces through history. Rulers who violate it lose the Mandate; those who embody it bring order.

**Zoroastrian Asha**: The cosmic principle of truth-and-order ([[moral-zoroastrian-asha]]) is built into the structure of reality as Ahura Mazda's nature — humans access it through right thought, speech, and action. This is natural law with a more theistic flavor than Stoicism but the same structure: cosmic moral order as the standard for human conduct.

**Islamic Mu'tazilite theology**: The Mu'tazilite school argued that good and evil (*hasan wa-qabih*) are rationally knowable independently of divine revelation — God commands what is good because it IS good, and human reason can discern this. This is natural law within Islamic theology, the same structural position as Aquinas (Horn 2 of the [[moral-euthyphro-dilemma]]). The Mu'tazilites lost the Sunni theological debate to the Ash'arites (who held Horn 1), but Mu'tazilite rationalist ethics influenced medieval Islamic philosophy (Avicenna, Averroes) and remains the dominant position in Shia theology.

Three traditions — Greek-Christian, Confucian, Islamic rationalist — independently developing the same structural claim: moral truth is rational, natural, and in principle universally accessible. This convergence is the strongest cross-traditional evidence for natural law as a description of something real about human moral cognition, not merely a historical artifact of Greek philosophy.
