# The Prohibition of Usury — One Abrahamic Ban, Three Fates

## Identity

"Usury" (Latin *usura*, "a charge for the use" of a loan) originally meant **any** interest on a loan, not merely excessive interest. The categorical religious prohibition of lending at interest is one of the most consequential moral rules in Western economic history — a single ban, first legislated in the Torah, inherited by Christianity and Islam, and carried by the three Abrahamic traditions to three radically different destinations. In the Hebrew Bible it is *neshekh* ("a bite") and *tarbit / marbit* ("increase"); in rabbinic law *ribbit*; in Latin Christendom *usura*; in the Quran *ribā*. What began as a rule protecting the vulnerable Israelite debtor became, in Christian hands, the doctrine that condemned all interest as sin and then quietly dissolved into the foundations of modern banking — while in Islam it survived intact and, in the twentieth century, generated an entire parallel financial system. Interest itself is far older than the ban: Sumerian and Babylonian temples and palaces lent at interest, and the Code of Hammurabi (§§88-96) already **caps** it (20% on silver, 33⅓% on grain). The Israelite prohibition is not a description of ancient practice but a religious **reform against** it — and it is that reform, not interest, that the daughter traditions inherited.

## The Jewish prohibition (neshekh / tarbit)

The ban is stated three times in the Torah, across its major legal strata:

- **Covenant Code** — Exodus 22:25 (Heb. 22:24): "If you lend money to any of my people, to the poor among you, you shall not be to him as a creditor, and you shall not exact interest (*neshekh*)."
- **Holiness Code** — Leviticus 25:35-37: you shall not take *neshekh* or *tarbit* from your impoverished brother; "you shall not lend him your money at interest, nor give him your food at a profit."
- **Deuteronomic Law** — Deuteronomy 23:19-20 (Heb. 20-21): "You shall not charge interest on loans to your brother... To a foreigner (*nokhri*) you may charge interest, but to your brother you may not."

Not lending at interest is made a mark of the righteous in the Prophets and Psalms (Ezekiel 18:8,13,17; Psalm 15:5; Proverbs 28:8). The famous **brother / foreigner** distinction of Deuteronomy 23:20 — interest forbidden to a fellow Israelite, permitted to the *nokhri* — is the hinge on which the whole cross-tradition story turns (see Disputes).

Rabbinic law systematized *neshekh* and *tarbit* into a single, strict category of *ribbit*, elaborated in Mishnah **Bava Metzia** chapter 5 and the Talmud, and codified by **Maimonides** in the *Mishneh Torah* (*Hilkhot Malveh ve-Loveh* 4-8) — see [[moral-mishneh-torah-ethical-code]]. Because the ban applied only *within* the community, medieval Jewish commerce, which needed credit like any other, developed the **heter iska** ("business dispensation") — a contract that re-characterizes an interest loan as a profit-and-loss partnership, making the lender a co-investor rather than a creditor. The device, formalized especially in sixteenth-century Poland, is structurally identical to the workarounds Islam would later institutionalize.

## The Christian ban and its erosion

Christianity absorbed the Torah prohibition and universalized it. Its proof-text is **Luke 6:35** — "lend, expecting nothing in return" (*mutuum date nihil inde sperantes*) — read together with the Deuteronomic ban, but now with the "brother" extended to **all** people. The Church legislated against usury for a millennium:

- **Council of Nicaea** (325), canon 17: forbade clergy to take interest.
- **Second Lateran Council** (1139), canon 13: condemned usury as "detestable and disgraceful," reprobated by both divine and human law.
- **Third Lateran Council** (1179), canon 25: manifest usurers excommunicated, denied communion and Christian burial.
- **Council of Vienne** (1311-12): declared that anyone who **maintains usury is not a sin** is to be punished as a heretic.

The theological rationale reached its most rigorous form in **Thomas Aquinas**, *Summa Theologiae* II-II q. 78. Aquinas argues that money is a *fungible* consumed in its very use, so to charge separately for the "use" of a loaned sum is to **sell the same thing twice** — to sell what does not exist. And because interest accrues with time, the usurer in effect **sells time**, which belongs to no one but God. Beneath this lies **Aristotle** (*Politics* I.10, 1258b): money is barren, and money breeding money (*tokos*, "offspring") is "the most unnatural" form of gain — see [[moral-natural-law]]. The Christian ban is thus **doubly-sourced**: the Torah/Gospel command *and* the Aristotelian argument from nature.

Yet the same scholasticism that perfected the ban also dissolved it. Canonists distinguished forbidden *usura* from licit *interesse* — compensation for a lender's **loss** (*damnum emergens*) or forgone gain (*lucrum cessans*). The Franciscan **montes pietatis** (charitable pawnshops, from 1462) charged modest fees and were approved by the **Fifth Lateran Council** (1515). **John Calvin** (c. 1545) rejected a blanket prohibition, distinguishing exploitative loans to the poor from productive commercial credit — a Reformation turning point that Nelson made the center of his history. By the nineteenth century the Holy Office was quietly instructing confessors not to disturb those taking legal interest, and the 1917 Code of Canon Law permitted it. The maximal medieval ban had become, in practice, the interest-bearing economy of modern capitalism.

## The Islamic prohibition (ribā)

The Quran forbids **ribā** ("increase") in the strongest terms, and — unlike some rulings — the prohibition is held to be *muḥkam* (definitive) and **unabrogated**. The key passage, **al-Baqara 2:275-279**, declares "God has permitted trade (*al-bayʿ*) and forbidden ribā (*ḥarrama al-ribā*)" (2:275), warns that "God blights ribā and nourishes charity" (2:276), and issues an ultimatum: give up outstanding ribā or "be warned of war from God and His Messenger"; on repentance "you shall have your principal — wrong not, and be not wronged" (2:278-279). Further prohibitions appear at **3:130** ("do not consume ribā doubled and multiplied") and **30:39**. Jurists distinguish *ribā al-nasīʾa* (interest on deferred loans) from *ribā al-faḍl* (unequal exchange of like commodities, derived from hadith). Ribā is one of the named *ḥarām* acts of the [[moral-islamic-sharia]].

The distinctive twentieth-century development is **Islamic finance**: rather than erode the ban, Muslim jurists and economists rebuilt banking around it. Profit-and-loss-sharing and asset-backed contracts — *mudāraba* (silent partnership), *mushāraka* (joint venture), *murābaḥa* (cost-plus sale), *ijāra* (leasing), and *sukūk* ("Islamic bonds") — replace interest with shared risk and real transactions, on the same logic as the Jewish *heter iska*. From the Mit Ghamr Savings Bank in Egypt (1963) and Dubai Islamic Bank (1975), the sector has grown to a multi-trillion-dollar industry, defended as fulfilling the *maqṣid* of *ḥifẓ al-māl* (protection of wealth) — see [[moral-sharia-five-objectives-maqasid]].

## Cross-tradition significance — one prohibition, three fates

This is the rare cross-tradition case in which the connection is **transmission, not convergence**, and securely so. The Christian and Islamic bans are not independent inventions that happen to resemble the Jewish one; on the Jewish→Christian line they are the **same prohibition inherited** through a shared scriptural lineage (the Islamic ribā ban is a parallel Abrahamic development, not a direct inheritance) — Christianity through the Torah and Luke 6:35, Islam through the Quran's explicit continuity with the "People of the Book." What differs is not the origin but the **fate**:

- **Judaism** kept the ban *intra-communally*, softened its commercial bite with the *heter iska*, and — through the Deuteronomic foreigner-clause and Christian exclusion from other trades — was pushed into the medieval European **moneylender** niche the ban had ostensibly restricted.
- **Christianity** universalized the ban to its strictest form, then dismantled it from within (the *interesse* exceptions, Calvin, the *montes pietatis*), so that the tradition which most fiercely condemned interest became the cradle of modern interest-based finance.
- **Islam** preserved the ban as unabrogated and, in the twentieth century, **re-activated** it — building an entire parallel financial architecture rather than legislating exceptions.

The classic interpretive frame is **Benjamin Nelson's** *The Idea of Usury* (1949): the Deuteronomic "brother / other" distinction was universalized by Christianity into "all men are brothers — usury to none," and then inverted by modernity into "all men are others — usury to all." One verse (Deut 23:20), three trajectories. A secondary but genuine complication is that the Christian rationale carries an **Aristotelian tributary** (money as sterile) alongside the scriptural command — so the Christian ban is not a pure inheritance from Israel but a fusion of Hebrew law and Greek natural philosophy. The honest summary: the **transmission is secure** (this is a shared Abrahamic inheritance, not parallel evolution), and the interest of the case lies precisely in how a single inherited rule produced three incompatible economic worlds.

## Disputes

**The foreigner-clause.** Whether Deuteronomy 23:20's permission to charge the *nokhri* interest is ethnic-exclusivist or context-bound is contested. Many read it as a concession to the realities of international trade (the *nokhri* being a resident foreign merchant, himself a lender), not a license to exploit outsiders; the surrounding Holiness legislation is markedly protective of the resident alien (*ger*). The verse's later career — cited on all sides of the medieval "Jewish usury" debates — outran anything the text plainly says.

**The medieval moneylender.** The popular idea that Jews became Europe's moneylenders *because* the Torah permitted lending to foreigners is now heavily qualified. Historians (e.g. Shatzmiller, *Shylock Reconsidered*) emphasize instead that Christian usury law barred Christians from the trade while guild and land restrictions barred Jews from most others — so the niche was created as much by **Christian exclusion** as by the biblical clause.

**What ribā means.** Modernist Muslim scholars have argued that the Quranic ribā condemned in 3:130 ("doubled and multiplied") was specifically the pre-Islamic practice of doubling a debt on default, not modern bank interest. **Fazlur Rahman** ("Riba and Interest," 1964) made the influential case that the moral objective — preventing exploitation and injustice — matters more than the contractual form, and the Egyptian jurist Muhammad Sayyid Tantawi later issued fatwas permitting fixed bank interest. (An early permissive view is often attributed to Muhammad Abduh, though the attribution is contested.) The mainstream position, and the entire Islamic-finance movement, rejects this: **all** bank interest is ribā, and the answer is not to redefine the ban but to bank without it.
