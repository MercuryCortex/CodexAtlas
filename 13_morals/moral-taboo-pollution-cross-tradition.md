## Taboo, Purity, and Pollution — The Forbidden-Sacred Across Traditions

Across cultures with no contact, a single structure recurs: certain persons, places, foods, acts, and bodily states are **set apart** from ordinary life as simultaneously **dangerous and holy**, and elaborate systems of prohibition and purification manage the boundary between them and the everyday. This is the domain of **taboo** (Polynesian *tapu*), of ritual **pollution** and **purity**, and of the **forbidden-sacred**. Its distinctive feature — the one that separates it from ordinary morality — is that the prohibited thing is not, in the first instance, *evil*: a corpse, a menstruating woman, a chief's food-bowl, or an anomalous animal is not wicked. The prohibition tracks a charge, a set-apartness, a location of the thing relative to a system of order — and only later, and unevenly, does it fuse with the moral category of sin.

### The word and the double sense

The English word **taboo** is a direct 18th-century loan from Polynesian ***tapu***, recorded by Captain James Cook at Tonga in 1777 and published in the account of his third voyage (1784). In Polynesian usage *tapu* means at once "sacred" and "forbidden"; its opposite, ***noa***, means "ordinary, unrestricted, free." A thing is *tapu* because it carries a dangerous concentration of *mana* (sacred power), and contact across the charge-differential is harmful independent of any moral quality of the person — see [[ritual-polynesian-tapu]].

This fusion of *holy* and *forbidden* in one word is not a Polynesian peculiarity but a recurring linguistic fact:

- **Latin *sacer*** means both "consecrated / holy" and "accursed": the *homo sacer* of archaic Roman law is the man so set apart that he may be killed with impunity yet not sacrificed. Benveniste (1969) traces the way *sacer* (set apart, charged) and *sanctus* (bounded, protected) divide the semantic field of the sacred.
- **Semitic *ḥ-r-m***: Arabic ***ḥarām*** ("forbidden") shares its root with ***ḥaram*** — the sacred precinct (the Meccan *al-Masjid al-Ḥarām*), *iḥrām* (the pilgrim's consecrated state), and *Muḥarram* (the sacred month). The one root yields both "forbidden" and "sacred." Its Hebrew cognate ***ḥerem*** is "the ban" — a thing devoted to God and therefore untouchable, destroyed, or excommunicated — while the ordinary Hebrew word for "holy," ***qādôš***, means literally "set apart."

The double sense is not accidental. The holy and the forbidden coincide because both name a zone **cut off from the ordinary** and therefore requiring insulation: what is too charged to touch is described, from one side, as sacred and, from the other, as prohibited.

### The pollution/purity system

Where the sacred is a charge, **pollution** is what happens when the boundary is crossed, and **purity** is the ordinary condition to which ritual returns a person. Mary Douglas's *Purity and Danger* (1966) supplied the governing analysis: **dirt is "matter out of place,"** and pollution beliefs are a society's way of policing the boundaries of its own system of classification. The "abominations" of Leviticus 11 are, on her reading, precisely the creatures that fall between the taxonomic categories of a cosmology (the anomalous, the boundary-crossing). Pollution is a **symbolic**, not a hygienic, phenomenon — see [[theme-purity-and-pollution]].

The same grammar — *contact transmits impurity; ritual (usually water, or the passage of time) removes it* — recurs across unrelated traditions:

- **Hebrew *ṭumah / ṭaharah***: the impurity code of Leviticus 11-15 (corpse-contact, genital discharge, *ṣāraʿat* / "leprosy," childbirth, forbidden foods), removed by immersion in the *miqveh* and the passage of time. Milgrom (1991) reads the whole system as a coherent symbolic opposition of **life and death** rather than a list of hygienic rules — see [[priestly-purity]] and [[ritual-mikveh-immersion]].
- **Islamic *ṭahāra***: the *ḥalāl / ḥarām* classification of acts and foods, plus the states of *ḥadath* (minor/major impurity) removed by *wuḍūʾ* and *ghusl* before prayer — see [[moral-islamic-sharia]] and [[ritual-wudu-ritual-purity]].
- **Hindu *śauca / aśauca***: birth- and death-pollution transmitted through kin and contact, removed by water and by fixed periods of time; on Dumont's analysis the pure/impure axis structures the entire varṇa-jāti hierarchy — see [[moral-dharma-svadharma-hindu-station-duty]].
- **Shinto *kegare / harae***: impurity accruing from death, blood, and illness, cleansed by *harae* and *misogi* — see [[ritual-misogi-purification]].
- **Polynesian *tapu / noa***: the *tohunga* (ritual specialist) lifts *tapu* by *noa* rites, often with water, returning persons and objects to ordinary use.

In each system the polluting contact is, in origin, **not a sin**: the mourner, the new mother, and the warrior returning from battle have done nothing wrong. They have entered a charged state and must be returned, by ritual, to the ordinary.

### Danger and holiness: the phenomenology

Why should holiness present itself as dangerous? The phenomenology of religion gives a consistent answer. Rudolf Otto (*The Idea of the Holy*, 1917) named the core experience the **numinous** — a *mysterium tremendum et fascinans*, an object at once overwhelming and terrifying (*tremendum*) and irresistibly attractive (*fascinans*) — see [[numinous]]. Émile Durkheim (1912) built his very definition of religion on the same intuition: the sacred is the class of "things set apart and **forbidden**," and religion is the system of beliefs and practices relative to them — see [[sacred-and-profane]], [[emile-durkheim]], and the derived notion of [[hierophany]] developed by [[mircea-eliade]]. William Robertson Smith (*Religion of the Semites*, 1889) had already argued that in early Semitic religion the categories of the "holy" and the "unclean" are not opposites but two aspects of a single quality of set-apart-ness — the same insight the Polynesian material makes transparent.

### Cross-tradition significance: convergence, not diffusion

The honest cross-tradition claim here is deliberately narrow. **Two** connections are matters of genuine transmission; the rest are **independent convergence**.

1. **The word *taboo* is a real loan** — an 18th-century borrowing of Polynesian *tapu* into English (and thence into the vocabulary of anthropology). This is transmission, but only of a *term*, not of the underlying institution.
2. **The Semitic *ḥ-r-m* parallel is a secure linguistic cognate**: Arabic *ḥarām* and Hebrew *ḥerem* descend from a common Proto-Semitic root — a shared inheritance in the same way *tĕhôm* and *Tiāmat* are cognate, not a case of one borrowing from the other.

Everything else — the structural identity between Polynesian *tapu*, Hebrew *ṭumah*, Hindu *aśauca*, and Shinto *kegare* — is **typological convergence, not diffusion**. These purity systems arose in populations with no contact, and Douglas's thesis is explicitly *structuralist-universalist*: purity and pollution are a general property of any system that classifies the world and must therefore manage its anomalies and boundaries. The parallel is real and deep, but its cause is a shared feature of human symbolic cognition, not a shared source. The distinction the Atlas insists on: the *ḥerem ~ ḥarām* cognate is **linguistic** and secure; the *tapu ≈ ṭumah ≈ aśauca* likeness is **structural** and convergent.

### Disputes

- **The category itself.** Franz Steiner (*Taboo*, 1956) and Roger Keesing ("Rethinking Mana," 1984) warned that "taboo" and "mana" are Victorian scholarly reifications — a Polynesian word over-generalised by Frazer and Durkheim into a supposed universal essence that flattens distinct emic (insider) systems. On this view the very comparison risks imposing a European frame; the counter-view (Douglas) is that the structural regularity is real and the shared term merely convenient.
- **Douglas on Leviticus.** Milgrom (1991) reads the biblical impurity system as an integrated symbolism of *life versus death* rather than of taxonomic anomaly, and Douglas herself substantially **revised** her 1966 reading in *Leviticus as Literature* (1999) — a rare public reversal by a founder of the modern symbolic anthropology of pollution.
- **Dumont on caste.** The claim that pure/impure is *the* organising principle of the caste system (*Homo Hierarchicus*, 1966) is contested by Nicholas Dirks (*Castes of Mind*, 2001), who argues that caste as a rigid hierarchy was substantially hardened under colonial administration, and by critics who hold that Dumont over-privileged the Brahminical ideological view.
- **Pollution versus sin.** European Christianity largely **collapsed** ritual impurity into moral "uncleanness," translating Hebrew *ṭāmēʾ* as "unclean" and reading it ethically. This is why the present node sits among the *morals* while insisting that the systems it describes are, at root, ritual-ontological rather than ethical: whether ritual pollution should be counted as a *moral* category at all is exactly the disputed question, and the traditions themselves answer it differently.
