## The Ten Commandments — Divine Law Written in Stone, the Prototype of Moral Code

The Decalogue (*deka logoi*, "ten words/utterances"; Hebrew: *aseret ha-dibrot*) is the foundational moral-legal code of the Mosaic covenant, given at Sinai (Exodus 20:1-17; Deuteronomy 5:6-21). It is the paradigm instance of [[moral-divine-command-theory]] in the Abrahamic traditions: God speaks, and the spoken words are simultaneously law and morality. The Decalogue has no parallel in ancient Near Eastern religion for this structural feature: God speaks in the first person, directly to the assembled people of Israel, without a royal mediator — not to a king who then promulgates law, but to the covenant community as a whole.

### The Two Tables

The Decalogue divides into two well-recognized sections:

**First Table (Commandments 1-4): Duties to God**
1. "I am the LORD your God... you shall have no other gods before me." — Exclusive monotheism / monolatry (the theological foundation)
2. "You shall not make for yourself an idol..." — Prohibition of image worship
3. "You shall not take the name of the LORD your God in vain." — Prohibition of false oaths and divine-name abuse
4. "Remember the Sabbath day, to keep it holy." — Sacred time; the weekly cessation of labor as imitatio Dei (God rested on the seventh day)

**Second Table (Commandments 5-10): Duties to Human Beings**
5. "Honor your father and your mother." — Filial piety; the social order rooted in the family
6. "You shall not murder." — Prohibition of unlawful killing (*ratsakh* = murder, not all killing)
7. "You shall not commit adultery." — Sexual fidelity; marriage as covenant
8. "You shall not steal." — Property as protected sphere
9. "You shall not bear false witness against your neighbor." — Truth-telling in legal-social contexts
10. "You shall not covet your neighbor's house... wife... or anything that belongs to your neighbor." — Prohibition of covetous desire — the inner disposition, not only external action

The Decalogue is uniquely structured in reaching to the inner life (commandment 10) — what one *desires* is within the scope of divine law, not just what one *does*. This interiorization of moral law anticipates Jesus' Sermon on the Mount ("You have heard it said, 'You shall not murder' — but I say to you, whoever is angry with his brother is already liable") and the Buddhist precepts' concern with intentionality.

### The MASSIVE WIN: Divine Law Inscribed in Stone — A Cross-Traditional Pattern

The Decalogue is not the only instance of divine or cosmic moral authority inscribed on stone in ancient history. Four traditions create the pattern of moral law physically inscribed for permanence:

**Hammurabi's Code** (c. 1754 BCE) — Babylonian divine-king law inscribed on a basalt stele nearly 2.25 meters tall, topped by an image of Hammurabi receiving the laws from the sun god Shamash. 282 laws covering property, family, criminal, and commercial matters. The stele was displayed in public; its authority is simultaneously divine (given by Shamash) and royal (proclaimed by Hammurabi). The parallel with the Sinai narrative is structural: divine authority → law → physical inscription → public display.

**The Mosaic Decalogue** (c. 1200-600 BCE) — Inscribed by God on stone tablets (*luchot ha-even*) — twice, as the first tablets are broken by Moses and replaced. The physical inscription is central to the narrative: these are not oral traditions but divine writing (*כְּתַב אֱלֹהִים*, "God's script") on durable stone.

**Ashoka's Rock Edicts** (c. 250 BCE) — The Buddhist emperor Ashoka inscribed his moral code on massive rock faces and polished stone pillars across his empire. The edicts promote *dhamma* (righteousness, moral conduct based on the [[noble-eightfold-path]]) and explicitly prohibit animal sacrifice, cruelty to animals, and violence. Not divine command but imperial moral authority deployed through physical inscription for permanence and reach.

**The Twelve Tables** (c. 450 BCE, Rome) — The foundational Roman legal code, inscribed on bronze (or wood) tablets and displayed in the Forum. Not divine command but ancestral *mos maiorum* (custom of the ancestors) given permanent form in writing, representing Roman civic moral order.

Four traditions. Four stone (or metal) inscriptions of moral authority. Each claims a different source of authority: divine revelation (Decalogue), divine-royal transmission (Hammurabi), imperial dharmic authority (Ashoka), ancestral civic tradition (Rome). But all share the same conviction: moral norms must be fixed, public, and durable — inscribed in matter so they cannot be altered by individual whim.

The Decalogue's distinctive claim is that God — not a king, not an emperor — speaks the law directly to the entire people. This direct divine-to-community address, unmediated by a royal intermediary, remains unique in ancient law.

### Cross-Traditional Reach: The Decalogue in Islam

The Quran 17:23-39 contains what scholars call a "Quranic Decalogue" — a sequence of seventeen moral commands that closely parallels the Mosaic second table:
- "Your Lord has decreed that you worship none but Him, and that you be kind to parents..." (17:23) — commandments 1 and 5
- "Do not take a life that God has made sacred, except by just right." (17:33) — commandment 6
- "Do not go near adultery..." (17:32) — commandment 7
- "Do not consume the wealth of orphans..." (17:34) — commandment 8
- "And fulfill the covenant..." (17:34) — commandment 9's spirit

The Quranic passage is not presented as a copy of the Decalogue but as a direct divine revelation establishing the same moral categories — confirming the Mosaic tradition while recontextualizing it in the Islamic revelatory framework. [[tradition-islam]] thus inherits the Decalogue's moral structure while rejecting its Sinai-covenant specificity in favor of universal divine command.

### From the Decalogue to the Golden Rule

Hillel's famous summary (Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 31a) — "What is hateful to you, do not do to your neighbor; that is the whole Torah; the rest is commentary" — performs the same move as Jesus in Matthew 7:12 ("This is the Law and the Prophets"): the entire Decalogue (and beyond) can be compressed into the Golden Rule of moral reciprocity. This shows that the [[moral-golden-rule-cross-tradition]] is not merely parallel to the Decalogue but is understood within the Jewish and Christian traditions as its moral kernel — the principle from which the specific commandments can be derived.
