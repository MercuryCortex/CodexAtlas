# Just War

## Identity

**Just-war ethics** is the body of normative reasoning that specifies when a recourse to organised, religiously-sanctioned violence is *licit* (**jus ad bellum** — the right to make war) and how such violence must be *restrained* once begun (**jus in bello** — right conduct within war). The Latin phrase *bellum iustum* gives the comparative slot its name; the fully-articulated two-part doctrine — with its criteria of **just cause, legitimate authority, right intention, last resort, proportionality**, and **non-combatant immunity** — is the achievement of the Western Christian tradition running from Augustine through Aquinas to the School of Salamanca and Grotius, from which the modern secular law of armed conflict descends. In the Atlas this node sits at the intersection of the sacred and the sword: it is the point where four major traditions each try to answer the same question — *under what conditions may a community kill in the name of what it holds holy, and where must it stop?* — and it is set deliberately in tension with the **[[moral-ahimsa-nonviolence|non-violence]]** pole on one side and the **[[crusade-theology-of-malecide|holy-war]]** strand on the other.

The striking fact is that four traditions with little or no direct doctrinal borrowing — Christian *bellum justum*, Islamic *jihad* and *siyar*, Hindu *dharma-yuddha*, and Jewish *milhemet mitzvah* / *milhemet reshut* — converge on a nearly identical grid of restraints: war must be declared by legitimate authority, for a just cause, and waged without deliberately targeting those who do not fight.

## The four traditions

### Christian bellum justum

The Latin tradition inherits its vocabulary from Roman law — Cicero's *iustum bellum* (*De officiis* I.11) and the priestly *ius fetiale* governing the formal declaration of war — and Christianises it. **Augustine** (*Contra Faustum* XXII.74-79; *De civitate Dei* XIX; *Ep.* 189 to Boniface, c. 400) supplies the ad-bellum core: a war is just when waged by **legitimate authority**, for a **just cause** (the redress of a wrong), and with **right intention** (the restoration of peace, not the lust to dominate — *libido dominandi*). Crucially, Augustine treats killing-in-just-war as *regrettable necessity*, not merit. **Aquinas** systematises this in three conditions (*Summa Theologiae* II-II q.40 a.1, c. 1271): *auctoritas principis*, *causa iusta*, *intentio recta*. The School of Salamanca — **Francisco de Vitoria** (*De iure belli*, 1539) and **Francisco Suárez** — extend the doctrine to the Spanish conquest of the Americas, denying that unbelief alone is a just cause, and develop the *in bello* limits. **Hugo Grotius** (*De iure belli ac pacis*, 1625) then recasts the whole framework as natural law and the *ius gentium* — see **[[moral-natural-law]]** — founding the modern law of nations.

### Islamic jihad and siyar

*Jihad* ("striving") in classical juristic usage denotes the collective obligation (*fard kifaya*) of armed struggle in the path of God; the discipline that regulates it is the **siyar**, the Islamic law of war and interstate relations, whose foundational text is the *Kitab al-Siyar* of **Muhammad al-Shaybani** (d. 805). The ad-bellum conditions require **right authority** (the imam) and a recognised **just cause**; the in-bello conditions impose **non-combatant immunity**, canonically formulated in **Abu Bakr's ten instructions** to Usama's army (do not kill women, children, or the aged; do not slay monks in their cells; do not cut down fruit trees or slaughter livestock save for food) and grounded in **Qur'an 2:190** — "do not transgress limits." A famous tradition distinguishes the *greater* jihad (*al-jihad al-akbar*, the struggle against the lower self) from the *lesser* jihad (*al-jihad al-asghar*, armed struggle); its authenticity is disputed (see **Disputes**). This body of law is a province of the wider **[[moral-islamic-sharia|sharia]]**.

### Hindu dharma-yuddha

The Sanskrit epic tradition frames righteous war as **dharma-yuddha**, a war fought according to *dharma*. The **[[phase-2-027-bhagavad-gita|Bhagavad Gita]]** — embedded in the **[[phase-3-095-mahabharata|Mahabharata]]** — opens with the warrior Arjuna laying down his bow rather than kill his kinsmen; Krishna's answer is that for a *kshatriya* "there is no greater good than a righteous war" (*dharmya samgrama*, Gita 2.31), because fighting justly is the warrior's **[[moral-dharma-svadharma-hindu-station-duty|station-duty]]** (*svadharma*). The in-bello code — scattered through the Mahabharata's *parvans* and codified at **Manusmriti 7.90-93** — forbids striking one who is fleeing, disarmed, asleep, surrendered, or a non-combatant, and prohibits barbed, poisoned, or fire-tipped weapons; combat is to be between equals, ceasing at nightfall. Kautilya's *Arthashastra*, by contrast, sets *dharmayuddha* against *kutayuddha* (concealed, treacherous war) and pragmatically prefers the latter when advantageous — the realist counter-current inside the tradition.

### Jewish milhemet mitzvah and milhemet reshut

Rabbinic law divides war into **milhemet mitzvah** (commanded war) and **milhemet reshut** (discretionary/optional war). **Mishnah Sotah 8:7** and **Maimonides' Mishneh Torah** (*Hilkhot Melakhim* 5-6, c. 1180 — the code catalogued at **[[moral-mishneh-torah-ethical-code]]**) define the commanded wars as the conquest of the seven Canaanite nations, the war against Amalek, and defence against an attacker; discretionary wars (a king's wars of expansion) require the authorisation of the **Sanhedrin of 71** and the Urim ve-Tummim — a striking *ad bellum* authority-condition. The conduct-code of **Deuteronomy 20** (7th c. BCE, arguably the earliest codified war-restraint feeding this comparative slot) requires an offer of **terms of peace before a siege** (20:10), exempts four classes from the draft (20:5-8), and forbids destroying the besieged city's **fruit trees** (*bal tashhit*, 20:19-20). Against these restraints stands the **herem**, the total ban on the Canaanite nations (20:16-18) — the holy-war exception.

## Cross-tradition significance (transmission vs convergence)

The honest question is whether these four just-war traditions are *related* or merely *parallel*. The answer differs sharply by pair, and the distinction between a secure transmission-chain and a structural convergence must be kept clean.

**Secure transmission runs only inside the Western strand.** Cicero's *iustum bellum* and the *ius fetiale* → Augustine → Gratian's *Decretum* (C.23) and Aquinas → Vitoria and Suárez → Grotius → the Hague and Geneva law of armed conflict is a genuinely documented chain of textual dependence (Russell 1975; Tuck 1999). This is the one axis where "just war" is a single, transmitted tradition rather than a family resemblance.

**Between the Western and Islamic traditions the relation is a shared late-antique environment, not demonstrable borrowing.** Whether early Islamic *siyar* drew on Roman-Byzantine or Sasanian law of war is debated, but the dominant scholarly view (Khadduri 1955; Kelsay 2007) is that the jurists systematised the *siyar* largely from the Qur'an, the *sunna*, and Arab custom, arriving independently at authority-, cause-, and non-combatant-conditions that *resemble* the Christian ones. James Turner Johnson (1997), the standard comparative treatment, reads the Christian and Islamic materials as two parallel "just-war traditions," each with its own restraint-strand and its own holy-war strand.

**The Hindu and Jewish materials are convergent, not transmitted.** The Mahabharata's *dharma-yuddha* rules and the Deuteronomic-rabbinic *milhamah* taxonomy developed with no contact with the Latin tradition; their overlap with it — legitimate authority, just cause, restraint on weapons and on killing non-combatants — is best explained as **independent convergence** on the universal problem of limiting sacralised violence within a community's law (Roy 2012; Brekke 2006). The parallel is therefore *structural/typological*, of the same evidentiary kind as a shared cosmogonic motif rather than a loanword: powerful as a comparative pattern, but carrying no claim of borrowing.

What the convergence *does* show is that a recognisable set of restraint-conditions recurs wherever a literate tradition tries to make war answerable to the sacred — and, equally, that each tradition also generates a **holy-war strand** in which those conditions are suspended by direct divine command.

## The holy-war strand in tension

Every one of these traditions contains, beside its restraint-doctrine, a countervailing strand in which God's command overrides the ordinary limits — the point James Turner Johnson marks by distinguishing **just war** (limited, restraint-based, this-worldly cause) from **holy war** (religiously commanded, its violence meritorious and its limits dissolved). In the Hebrew Bible this is the **herem**; in Latin Christianity it is the crusade — Urban II's plenary indulgence (1095) and, decisively, Bernard of Clairvaux's reclassification of crusader killing as **[[crusade-theology-of-malecide|malecide]]** rather than homicide, meritorious rather than merely permitted; in Islam it is the classical doctrine of an offensive *jihad* to expand the *dar al-Islam* against the *dar al-harb* (Khadduri's reading of the jurists). The just-war conditions are the tradition's own internal brake on this strand; the history of each tradition is in part the contest between the two. This is why just-war ethics stands in permanent tension with the **[[moral-ahimsa-nonviolence|non-violence]]** pole on one side (which denies that any killing can be righteous) and the holy-war pole on the other (which denies that righteous killing needs restraint).

## Disputes

- **The greater/lesser jihad hadith.** The report that the Prophet, returning from battle, said "we have returned from the lesser jihad to the greater jihad" is *not* found in the six canonical Sunni collections; it is transmitted by al-Bayhaqi and al-Khatib al-Baghdadi and graded weak or spurious by many hadith critics. **David Cook** (*Understanding Jihad*, 2005) argues that the "greater jihad" motif is a later, largely Sufi development and that the classical juristic *jihad* is primarily military — a corrective to the popular apologetic emphasis on the inner struggle. The interior-struggle sense is nonetheless deeply rooted in the Sufi and ethical literature; both readings are real strands of the tradition.
- **Was classical Islamic jihad offensive or defensive?** Majid Khadduri (1955) read the classical jurists as positing a standing collective obligation to expand the *dar al-Islam*; modernist and revisionist scholars read the Qur'anic warrant (2:190; 22:39) as essentially defensive. The medieval juristic mainstream and the modern reformist reading genuinely diverge here.
- **Were the dharma-yuddha rules ever operative?** Scholars caution that the Mahabharata's chivalric war-code may be an idealised epic norm rather than a description of practice, and the epic itself repeatedly narrates its own violation (the killing of Bhishma, Drona, and Karna by stratagem); Kautilya's endorsement of *kutayuddha* shows the pragmatic counter-tradition (Roy 2012).
- **Is "just war" one tradition or a Western construction?** Comparativists (Johnson 1997; Brekke 2006) warn that assembling Hindu, Jewish, and Islamic materials under the Latin heading *bellum justum* risks importing a Western grid; the parallels are real but the unifying label is retrospective.
