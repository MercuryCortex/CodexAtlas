# Human Rights — the Secular Universalization of Natural Law

**Human rights** are the modern doctrine that every person, simply by being human, holds
certain inalienable moral claims — to life, liberty, bodily integrity, conscience, and more —
binding on all authorities everywhere. It is the contemporary terminus of the natural-law
tradition: the idea, running from the Stoics through Aquinas's eternal law (grounded in the
divine Reason, the [[theme-logos-theology|Logos]]) to [[moral-natural-law|natural law]], that
moral norms are built into the rational structure of reality and discoverable by reason.

## The transmission to today

- **Scholastic natural law → natural rights.** Late-medieval canonists and Aquinas frame an
  objective moral order; the language shifts from *what is right* (natural law) toward *rights
  the individual holds* (Tierney traces this to 12th-13th c. canon law).
- **The Enlightenment secularization.** [[john-locke|Locke]] (1689) and Grotius re-ground rights
  in the individual rather than in the divine lawgiver — life, liberty, property as pre-political
  claims. The American (1776) and French (1789) declarations enact them.
- **The universal declaration.** After the Second World War, the UN Universal Declaration of
  Human Rights (1948) proclaims them universal and inalienable, birthing the modern
  international human-rights regime — the moral *lingua franca* of the contemporary world.

That arc is why the ancient cosmic-order-as-law family — Stoic *logos*, Christian Logos-theology,
and its cognates in Vedic *ṛta* and Zoroastrian *aša* — is not a museum piece: its direct
descendant is the vocabulary in which the present argues about justice.
