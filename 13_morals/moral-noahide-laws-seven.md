## The Seven Noahide Laws — Talmudic Universal Ethics for Non-Jews

The **Seven Noahide Laws** (Hebrew: שֶׁבַע מִצְוֹת בְּנֵי נֹחַ, *Sheva Mitzvot Bnei Noach* — "Seven Commandments of the Sons of Noah") are the rabbinic Jewish framework for **universal ethics** — the seven principles understood as divinely commanded to all humanity through Noah after the flood (Genesis 9), prior to and independent of the Sinai covenant that binds Israel. They constitute one of the most theologically significant moral systems in any tradition: a divinely-revealed universal ethics that does not require conversion to or acceptance of Judaism.

The seven laws are enumerated in **Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 56a-b**, with a parallel formulation in *Tosefta Avodah Zarah* 8:4. The standard rabbinic list:

1. **Prohibition of idolatry** (אֲבוֹדָה זָרָה, *avodah zarah*) — no worship of foreign gods
2. **Prohibition of blasphemy** (בִּרְכַּת הַשֵּׁם, *birkat haShem*) — no cursing of God's name
3. **Prohibition of murder** (שְׁפִיכוּת דָּמִים, *shfichat damim*) — no shedding of human blood
4. **Prohibition of sexual immorality** (גִּלּוּי עֲרָיוֹת, *gilui arayot*) — no incest, adultery, bestiality
5. **Prohibition of theft** (גְּזֵל, *gezel*) — no taking of property without consent
6. **Prohibition of eating flesh from a living animal** (אֵבֶר מִן הַחַי, *ever min hachai*) — fundamental respect for animal life
7. **Obligation to establish courts of justice** (דִּינִים, *dinim*) — civic-legal order, the positive duty to constitute a juridical system

### The Theological Framework: A Universal Covenant

The Noahide laws articulate the rabbinic theological response to a structural question: **what does God demand of non-Jews?** The dominant ancient Mediterranean answer was either (a) tribal-particularist (each people has its own ancestral religious-ethical system) or (b) universalist via conversion (Christianity, Islam). The rabbinic answer is distinct: **universal ethics without conversion**. A non-Jew who observes the seven Noahide laws is righteous-among-the-nations (*chasid umot ha'olam*) and has a share in the world-to-come (Sanhedrin 105a; Maimonides, *Mishneh Torah, Laws of Kings* 8:11).

This is theologically remarkable: rabbinic Judaism articulates a soteriology in which the non-Jew does not need to become a Jew to be saved. The non-Jew has access to a different and equally divinely-ordained covenantal framework. The salvific universalism of Christianity ("there is one mediator… Christ Jesus" — 1 Tim 2:5) and Islam ("the religion before God is Islam" — Qur'an 3:19) is rejected; replaced by a two-covenant structure (Sinai for Israel; Noah for humanity) that grounds ethical universality without requiring religious uniformity.

### The Source-Text: Genesis 9 and the Rabbinic Reading

The Noahide laws are derived rabbinically from Genesis 9:1-17, the post-flood divine speech to Noah and his sons. The Talmudic argument (Sanhedrin 56b-57a) reads the seven prohibitions out of seven exegetical features of the passage and adjacent texts. Genesis 9:4-6 provides the explicit prohibitions on consuming blood (and by extension, flesh from a living animal) and on murder ("whoever sheds the blood of man, by man shall his blood be shed; for in his own image God made mankind"). The other five are derived by rabbinic *gezerah shavah* (analogical inference) and *kal vechomer* (a fortiori) reasoning from the broader Genesis 1-11 narrative.

The exegetical move is significant: the rabbis are *not* claiming the seven laws are explicitly enumerated in Torah; they are claiming that the post-flood divine covenant *implies* a foundational ethics binding on all humans, and that this ethics is structured around these seven principles. The seven are rabbinic systematization, not biblical enumeration.

### The MASSIVE WIN: Universal Monotheist Ethics Without Conversion

The Noahide framework constitutes a distinctive rabbinic-Jewish solution to the universal-ethics problem that Christianity, Islam, and modern Enlightenment thought all confront. **Christianity** universalizes by extending the salvific covenant to all who confess Christ (Romans 10:9-13); **Islam** universalizes through *fitra* (innate human disposition toward monotheism) and the call to *islām* as universal religion; **Kantian ethics** universalizes through the categorical imperative's appeal to rational agency. The rabbinic Noahide answer is structurally distinct: maintain religious particularism (Jews keep the Sinai covenant) while affirming ethical universalism (humanity keeps the Noah covenant).

The structural parallel with **Islamic** universal ethics is particularly striking. Sharia's distinction between *huquq Allah* (rights/obligations to God) and *huquq al-'ibad* (rights between humans) parallels the Noahide structure: certain ethical obligations are universally binding (the *huquq al-'ibad* and the Noahide ethics); others are tradition-specific religious obligations. Both rabbinic and Islamic legal traditions arrive at a two-tier structure of universal-ethical and religious-particular law.

### Modern Reception and Reformation Echo

The Noahide doctrine had a remarkable Christian reception. **Hugo Grotius** (*De Veritate Religionis Christianae*, 1627) used the Noahide laws as evidence of natural law accessible to all humans; **John Selden** (*De Iure Naturali et Gentium iuxta Disciplinam Ebraeorum*, 1640) developed a comprehensive Christian engagement with rabbinic Noahide jurisprudence as the basis for international law. The Noahide framework was thus a critical intermediary in the development of Grotian-Seldenian international law, the foundation of the modern law-of-nations tradition.

In the 20th century, the Chabad movement under the Lubavitcher Rebbe Menachem Mendel Schneerson (1902-1994) made the promulgation of the Noahide laws to non-Jews a central component of Chabad outreach — interpreting Maimonides's instruction that Jews should encourage non-Jews to keep the seven laws (*Mishneh Torah, Kings* 8:10) as a contemporary missiological mandate. This has generated a small but theologically distinctive contemporary Noahide movement of non-Jews who explicitly adopt the seven-law framework.
