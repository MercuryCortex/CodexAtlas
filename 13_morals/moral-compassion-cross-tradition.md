# Compassion — Mercy at the Moral Core of the World's Traditions

## Identity

Ask what sits at the centre of a tradition's ethics and, again and again, the answer is compassion — the capacity to be moved by another's suffering and to act to relieve it. Buddhism makes *karuṇā* one of the four "divine abidings" and, in the Mahāyāna, the very motive of buddhahood; Islam names God *al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm* — "the Compassionate, the Merciful" — at the head of all but one of the Qurʾān's chapters; the Hebrew Bible calls God "compassionate and gracious" (*raḥum weḥannun*, Exod 34:6); Jesus tells the parable of the Good Samaritan and blesses "the merciful"; Confucius makes *rén* — humaneness — the supreme virtue, and Mencius roots it in the "heart of compassion"; Hindu texts enjoin *dayā*. Compassion is, together with the [[moral-golden-rule-cross-tradition|Golden Rule]], among the most widely shared moral universals on record. This node maps the parallel and — the harder task — states exactly where the parallel is a shared inheritance and where it is independent convergence.

## The attestations

**Buddhism — *karuṇā*.** *Karuṇā* (Pali/Sanskrit, "compassion") is the second of the four *brahmavihāras* or "divine abidings" — loving-kindness (*mettā*), compassion (*karuṇā*), sympathetic joy (*muditā*), equanimity (*upekkhā*) — cultivated as boundless states radiated toward all beings (see [[metta-lovingkindness]]). In the Mahāyāna, *mahākaruṇā* ("great compassion"), paired with wisdom (*prajñā*), becomes the defining motive of the [[bodhisattva-vow|bodhisattva]], who postpones final liberation for the sake of all sentient beings; the bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara is compassion personified. Compassion here is not sentiment but the engine of the entire path (see [[moral-paramitas-six-bodhisattva]]).

**Hinduism — *dayā*.** Sanskrit *dayā* ("compassion, mercy") appears among the ethical restraints of yoga and throughout the wisdom literature. Its most famous locus is Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 5.2, where the single syllable "*da*" is heard three ways by gods, humans and demons — *damyata* (restrain yourselves), *datta* (give), *dayadhvam* (be compassionate). Compassion for all that lives is also the root of [[moral-ahimsa-nonviolence|ahiṃsā]], non-harm.

**Confucianism — *rén* and the heart of compassion.** *Rén* (仁), "humaneness" or "co-humanity" (the graph joins "person" and "two"), is the cardinal Confucian virtue (see [[moral-confucian-ren]]). Mencius grounds it in feeling: anyone who suddenly saw a child about to fall into a well would feel *cèyǐn zhī xīn*, "the heart of compassion/alarm" — not from calculation but spontaneously — and this involuntary compassion is the "sprout" (*duān*) of *rén* (Mengzi 2A6; see [[moral-mencius-four-sprouts-siduan]]).

**Judaism — *raḥamim* and *ḥesed*.** The Hebrew Bible carries two great mercy-words. *Raḥamim* (רַחֲמִים, "compassion") is grammatically the plural of *reḥem* (רֶחֶם, "womb"); Phyllis Trible reads divine *raḥamim* as "womb-love," the visceral mercy a mother feels (Jer 31:20). Distinct from it — a different root entirely — is *ḥesed* (חֶסֶד), "steadfast love" or covenant-loyalty. God's self-revelation at Sinai fuses the two: "*YHWH, YHWH, a God compassionate (raḥum) and gracious … abounding in ḥesed*" (Exod 34:6, the "Thirteen Attributes of Mercy").

**Christianity — *eleos*, *agapē*, *caritas*.** Jesus sets mercy at the centre of the Sermon on the Mount — "Blessed are the merciful, for they shall obtain mercy" (Matt 5:7; see [[moral-beatitudes-sermon-on-mount]]) — and dramatises it in the Good Samaritan, who alone "showed mercy" and who, seeing the wounded man, "had compassion" (Luke 10:33–37; the Greek *esplanchnisthē*, from *splanchna*, "the bowels," the gut as the seat of pity). The distinctive Christian love-word is *agapē* (ἀγάπη), rendered *caritas* in the Latin Vulgate and "charity" in older English (1 Cor 13); Anders Nygren's *Agape and Eros* made its self-giving, unmerited character the classic contrast to acquisitive *erōs* (see [[ritual-agape-love-feast]]).

**Islam — *raḥma*.** Mercy is the most-invoked attribute of God in Islam. Every chapter of the Qurʾān but one opens with the *basmala*, "In the name of God, *al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm*" — both names built on the root *r-ḥ-m*. God declares, "My mercy (*raḥma*) encompasses all things" (Q 7:156); a *ḥadīth qudsī* has God say His mercy outstrips His wrath. Crucially, the tradition itself makes the womb-connection explicit: in a widely transmitted ḥadīth God says, "I am *al-Raḥmān*; I created the *raḥim* [the womb, and so the kinship-tie] and derived its name from My own" (al-Bukhārī; al-Tirmidhī).

## Cross-tradition significance — one shared root, many independent arrivals

The temptation is to call all of this a single cross-cultural intuition and stop. The Atlas resists that, because the cases are not all of one kind. Three levels must be kept apart.

**1. A secure linguistic cognate (shared inheritance).** Hebrew *raḥamim* and Arabic *raḥma* are not a coincidence: both descend from the common Semitic root *r-ḥ-m*, whose concrete noun is "womb" (Hebrew *reḥem*, Arabic *raḥim*). Compassion, in the Semitic imagination, is literally *womb-feeling* — the mercy owed to those who share a womb, extended outward and upward to God. Because Hebrew and Arabic are sibling Semitic languages, and because Islam arose within the Abrahamic world, this is genuine genealogical continuity, not independent invention. Trible documents it on the Hebrew side, Izutsu on the Arabic; the ḥadīth of the *raḥim* shows the tradition drawing the etymology itself.

**2. A typological convergence (compassion as a bodily organ).** Independently of that shared root, several unrelated languages seat compassion in a visceral organ. Greek *splanchnizomai* — the verb used of Jesus' compassion and the Good Samaritan's — derives from *splanchna*, "the bowels/inward parts" (the KJV's "bowels of mercies"). Chinese *cèyǐn zhī xīn* places compassion in the *xīn* (心), the heart-mind. Hebrew itself pairs *raḥamim* with the *meʿîm*, the "inward parts" that "yearn." This recurrence of a gut/womb/heart image is a convergence of *metaphor*, not a shared *word* — human beings across unconnected cultures feel compassion as something that moves in the body.

**3. Independent doctrinal convergence (no linguistic or transmission link).** Buddhist *karuṇā*, Confucian *rén*/*cèyǐn*, and Hindu *dayā* share no vocabulary with the Semitic cluster and — in their core formulations — no documented channel of transmission. The Pali *brahmavihāras* (5th c. BCE), Mencius's heart of compassion (4th c. BCE), and the Upaniṣadic *dayadhvam* predate any significant contact with the Abrahamic traditions. That these traditions nevertheless place compassion at the ethical centre is best read, exactly like the [[moral-golden-rule-cross-tradition|Golden Rule]], as convergence on a human moral universal rather than diffusion from a single source.

## Disputes

The honest picture is therefore layered, and each layer can be overstated. The Hebrew–Arabic *r-ḥ-m* cognate is secure and does entail real shared inheritance; but it should not be inflated into a claim that *all* the traditions' mercy-words are related — they are not. Conversely, the Buddhist, Confucian and Hindu parallels are strong and independent, and should not be collapsed into the Semitic etymology: *karuṇā* has its own (contested) derivation, and the maternal image in the Buddhist *Karaṇīya Mettā Sutta* ("as a mother would protect her only child") is a rhetorical simile, not a shared womb-etymology. Later contact does complicate the tidy story — Buddhist and Christian ethics met along the Silk Road, and modern movements such as Karen Armstrong's Charter for Compassion (2009) deliberately synthesise the traditions — but the *foundational* formulations are pre-contact. Where the traditions genuinely differ is in scope and ground: Buddhist and Jain compassion reaches explicitly to *all sentient beings* and is grounded in the denial of a separate self; Confucian compassion radiates outward from graded family relations; Abrahamic mercy is grounded in the character of a personal God who is Himself *raḥum*. The shared centre is real; the metaphysics beneath it are not the same.
