## Karma as Cosmic Moral Law — The Non-Theistic Alternative to Divine Judgment

*Karma* (Sanskrit: *karman*, "action"; Pali: *kamma*) is the principle that intentional actions generate consequences proportional to their moral quality — consequences that inevitably return to the actor, across this life and potentially across multiple rebirths. It is the most important non-theistic moral framework in human history: a complete moral accounting system that requires no divine lawgiver, no personal God, no external judge. Morality is built into the causal structure of reality.

### The Three Versions: Hindu, Buddhist, Jain

The concept appears across the three main South Asian traditions in significantly different forms, and the differences are philosophically crucial.

**Vedic/Hindu karma** has the oldest textual roots. The earliest Vedic use of *karman* is purely ritual: the correct performance of sacrificial acts (*yajña*) generates merit (*punya*) and the incorrect performance generates demerit. The Upanishads (c. 800-400 BCE) expand this from ritual action to moral action generally. The *Brhadaranyaka Upanishad* (3.2.13) states the principle with striking directness: "A man becomes good through good action, bad through bad action." By the time of the *Bhagavad Gita* (c. 2nd c. BCE-2nd c. CE), karma is a cosmic law governing rebirth: actions in this life determine the conditions of the next birth. The Bhagavad Gita introduces the famous *nishkama karma* doctrine — action without attachment to results — as the solution to the karmic bind.

**Buddhist karma** radicalizes the concept through a crucial revision: the moral quality of karma is determined by **intention** (*cetanā*), not by the external form of the act. The Buddha (as reconstructed in the Pali Canon) explicitly states: "It is intention that I call karma." This shifts karma from a mechanical action-consequence law to a psychological moral law — what matters is the mental state motivating the act. Intentional harmful action generates negative karma; intentional beneficial action generates positive karma; non-intentional action generates no karma. This revision makes Buddhist moral psychology incompatible with the Jain version. See [[tradition-buddhism]] and [[noble-eightfold-path]].

**Jain karma** is the most literalized version and philosophically the most distinctive. For Jain philosophy, karma is not metaphorical causation — it is actual subtle matter (*karma-pudgala*) that physically attaches to the soul-substance (*jiva*). Negative emotions (anger, pride, deception, greed) cause an influx (*āsrava*) of karmic particles that literally coat the soul, obscuring its inherent luminosity and consciousness. Liberation (*moksha*) requires the complete exhaustion (*nirjarā*) of accumulated karmic matter, achieved through austerities (*tapas*) that burn off karma, and the cessation of new karmic influx through the [[jain-karma]] system. The Jain position — karma as actual material particles accumulating on a real soul-substance — is unique in the history of moral philosophy. See [[tradition-jainism]].

### The MASSIVE WIN: A Complete Moral Universe Without God

The significance of karma as a moral framework can only be grasped by comparing it to the Abrahamic alternatives. In [[tradition-judaism]], [[tradition-christianity-canonical]], and [[tradition-islam]], moral accounting is managed by a personal God: God witnesses, records, judges, and punishes/rewards. The Day of Judgment (Yom HaDin, Last Judgment, Qiyamah) is the moment of divine moral reckoning. Without God, on the Abrahamic model, there is no moral reckoning — atheism is often taken to imply moral nihilism.

Karma offers the opposite picture: a complete, impersonal, automatic moral accounting system that requires no divine witness or judge. Every intentional act generates an exact consequence that will inevitably manifest — in this life or future ones. The universe itself is morally structured; transgression automatically generates suffering; virtue automatically generates merit. This is not moral nihilism but moral realism of a thoroughgoing kind: moral consequences are as reliable as physical causation.

This enables Buddhism and Jainism to be simultaneously **atheistic** (rejecting a creator God) and **strongly moralistic** — a combination that the Abrahamic traditions typically treat as impossible. The Jain tradition is explicitly atheistic (*nastika* with respect to creator-gods) and yet has one of the most demanding moral codes in human history. Karma is the bridge.

The karmic framework also generates a distinct moral anthropology: the individual is entirely responsible for their own moral condition — no original sin, no vicarious atonement, no divine forgiveness. What one has sown, one reaps. This makes karma a more austere moral vision than most Abrahamic accounts, which provide for divine mercy interrupting the causal chain.

### Karma and Cosmic Accounting

For the Jain tradition especially, the moral accounting of karma extends to all sentient beings (*jivas*) — not just humans but animals, insects, and microscopic organisms all possess souls and generate karma. This generates the radical Jain principle of *ahimsa* (non-violence) toward all living beings, which the [[jain-karma]] theme node covers. The [[moral-buddhist-five-precepts]] extend ahimsa to the lay Buddhist moral framework as its first and most fundamental precept.

The contrast with Zoroastrian [[moral-zoroastrian-asha]] is illuminating: Asha is a cosmological moral framework structuring reality as the battle between truth/order and the Lie/chaos. Karma is a cosmological moral framework structuring reality as an automatic consequence-engine. Both embed morality in cosmic structure rather than divine decree — but Zoroastrianism does so theistically (Ahura Mazda as the source of Asha), while karma is non-theistic. Together, these two frameworks show that cosmological moral realism — morality as built into the structure of reality itself — is not a Western Enlightenment invention but a cross-traditional convergence across the Indian and Iranian axial traditions.
