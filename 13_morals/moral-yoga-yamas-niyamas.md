## Patañjali's Yamas and Niyamas — The Ten Ethical Foundations of Classical Yoga

Patañjali's *Yoga Sūtras* (c. 2nd century BCE - 4th century CE) codify Yoga as one of the six orthodox darshanas (philosophical schools) of classical Hindu thought, organizing the entire path of yogic practice into eight limbs (*aṣṭāṅga*). The first two limbs — *yamas* (five ethical restraints) and *niyamas* (five ethical observances) — constitute the moral foundation without which the remaining six limbs (posture, breath control, sense-withdrawal, concentration, meditation, absorption) are explicitly held to be impossible.

### The Five Yamas (Yoga Sūtra 2.30)

1. **Ahiṃsā** — Non-violence in thought, word, and deed
2. **Satya** — Truthfulness
3. **Asteya** — Non-stealing
4. **Brahmacarya** — Sexual restraint / chastity
5. **Aparigraha** — Non-possessiveness / non-grasping

### The Five Niyamas (Yoga Sūtra 2.32)

1. **Śauca** — Purity (of body and mind)
2. **Santoṣa** — Contentment
3. **Tapas** — Ascetic discipline / heat-generating practice
4. **Svādhyāya** — Self-study and study of scripture
5. **Īśvara-praṇidhāna** — Surrender to / contemplation of the Lord

The yamas are *restraints* — abstentions whose absence purifies the practitioner. The niyamas are *observances* — positive disciplines whose presence cultivates the practitioner. Together they constitute the *ethics-prerequisite* (*sīla-equivalent*) of Yoga, parallel in structural function to the sīla-samādhi-paññā architecture of the Buddhist [[moral-noble-eightfold-path-as-ethics]].

### The MASSIVE WIN: Direct Śramaṇa Inheritance Inside Orthodox Hinduism

The yamas are identical to the Jain Five Great Vows ([[moral-jain-five-mahavratas]]) in name, order, and substance: *ahiṃsā, satya, asteya, brahmacarya, aparigraha*. This identity is not coincidence and not parallel evolution — it is direct doctrinal inheritance. Yoga as Patañjali codifies it absorbs the renunciate (*śramaṇa*) ethical tradition's developed code of five great vows into the framework of an orthodox Hindu darshana that affirms the Vedic revelation. The same five-item ethical structure that the Jain tradition ([[mahavira]]) inherited from its śramaṇa background and the Buddhist tradition ([[buddha-shakyamuni]]) reorganized for its Five Precepts ([[moral-buddhist-five-precepts]]) appears intact in Patañjali's text as the ethical foundation of orthodox Yoga.

This is one of the clearest cases of cross-school doctrinal sharing in Indian intellectual history. The Yoga school officially affirms the *Vedas* as revealed scripture and is counted as one of the six āstika (orthodox) schools; the Jain and Buddhist traditions are *nāstika* (unorthodox, Veda-rejecting). Yet the foundational ethical code of orthodox Yoga is the ethical code of unorthodox Jainism, transmitted through the shared śramaṇa milieu in which all three traditions developed before the orthodox/unorthodox classification hardened. The yamas are evidence that the orthodox/unorthodox boundary, while real in cosmological and scriptural doctrine, was permeable in ethical practice — a permeability with deep consequences for how Indian ethics evolved.

### Ahiṃsā as the Master Yama

Patañjali (YS 2.35) gives ahiṃsā [[moral-ahimsa-nonviolence]] a unique structural status: when ahiṃsā is established in the yogin, enmity ceases in the yogin's presence. This is not metaphor. The classical commentaries (Vyāsa, Vācaspati Miśra) treat ahiṃsā as the *foundational* yama from which the others derive: truthfulness is required because falsehood harms; non-stealing is required because theft harms; brahmacarya is required because uncontrolled sexuality harms; aparigraha is required because possessiveness generates the desire that issues in harm. The other four yamas are species of the genus ahiṃsā.

This treatment makes Patañjali's ethical framework structurally analogous to the Buddhist Five Precepts' grounding in non-harm and the Jain mahāvratas' grounding in *ahiṃsā paramo dharmaḥ* ("non-harm is the supreme dharma"). The convergence of three traditions on ahiṃsā as the foundational moral principle — independently formulated in Jain, Buddhist, and Patañjala traditions, all in the same north Indian renunciate environment c. 6th-3rd century BCE — is one of the most striking moral-philosophical convergences in world history.

### The Eight Limbs (Aṣṭāṅga Yoga)

The yamas and niyamas are the first two of eight limbs systematized in *Yoga Sūtra* 2.29:

1. *Yama* — ethical restraints
2. *Niyama* — ethical observances
3. *Āsana* — posture (a single steady seat for meditation, NOT the modern postural-yoga sense)
4. *Prāṇāyāma* — breath regulation (see [[pranayama-yogic-breath]])
5. *Pratyāhāra* — withdrawal of the senses
6. *Dhāraṇā* — concentration
7. *Dhyāna* — meditation
8. *Samādhi* — meditative absorption

The Aṣṭāṅga structure is sequential in a specific sense: each later limb depends on the stability of all preceding limbs. Without the moral foundation of yamas and niyamas, posture and breath regulation produce only physical effects; concentration without ethical foundation may produce powers (*siddhis*) but not liberation. This makes Yoga, like the Buddhist Path, a system in which ethics is *not* separable from meditative practice — the integration of moral and meditative training is shared structural inheritance from the śramaṇa traditions.

A note on translation: the wildly popular modern postural-yoga tradition (Iyengar, Ashtanga Vinyasa, Vinyasa Flow, etc.) takes its name from this classical text but represents a distinct development. Modern postural yoga is largely a 19th-20th-century synthesis (David Gordon White and Mark Singleton have documented the colonial-era construction) drawing on haṭha yoga texts of the 11th-15th centuries CE rather than directly on Patañjali. The eight limbs of Patañjali devote a single sūtra to āsana and treat it as a steady seat for meditation; the modern postural tradition expands āsana into hundreds of poses. The yamas and niyamas, by contrast, are preserved more faithfully across the modern teaching lineages as the ethical foundation of practice.

### Reception in Hindu and Modern Ethics

The yamas and niyamas are quoted, taught, and observed across the Hindu tradition far beyond the formal Yoga school. They appear (with minor variation) in the *Bhagavata Purāṇa*, in the *Bhagavad Gītā*'s ethical sections, and in the entire later bhakti and Vedānta literatures as the foundation of any spiritually serious life. They became the ethical vocabulary of Gandhi's [[mahatma-gandhi]] political program (which explicitly extracted ahiṃsā and satya — recoined as *satyāgraha*, "truth-force" — as instruments of mass civil resistance), and through Gandhi entered global non-violent political theory.

Modern transnational yoga, in both its physical-fitness and meditative forms, propagates the yamas and niyamas as a global ethical vocabulary. The five yamas in particular — non-violence, truthfulness, non-stealing, sexual restraint, non-possessiveness — function in 21st-century global culture as a portable ethical floor accepted across religious and secular contexts. The ethical code Patañjali absorbed from the śramaṇa traditions into orthodox Yoga is now a globally circulated moral vocabulary — an extraordinary 2,000-year transmission from north Indian renunciate ascetics to contemporary wellness culture.
