## Cardinal and Theological Virtues — The Seven-Virtue Synthesis

### Identity

The seven-virtue scheme of Latin Christianity binds together two sets of a different order. The four **cardinal** virtues — **prudence** (*prudentia*), **justice** (*iustitia*), **fortitude** (*fortitudo*), and **temperance** (*temperantia*) — are the classical Greco-Roman tetrad, called "cardinal" from Latin *cardo* ("hinge"): the hinges on which the moral life turns. The three **theological** virtues — **faith** (*fides*), **hope** (*spes*), and **love / charity** (*caritas*) — are the Pauline triad of 1 Corinthians 13:13. Together they make seven: four acquired by human habituation and ordered to natural human flourishing, three infused by divine grace and ordered directly to God. The scheme is the backbone of Catholic moral theology, definitively systematized by Thomas Aquinas, and it stands in the medieval imagination as the luminous counterpart to the seven capital vices (the "deadly sins"). This node is the **enumerated seven-virtue synthesis**; it is distinct from [[moral-aristotelian-virtues-golden-mean]], which treats the doctrine of the mean and the general theory of moral virtue.

### The four cardinal virtues (Plato → Cicero → Ambrose)

The tetrad is first enumerated in Plato's *Republic* IV (427e-434c), where wisdom (*sophia*), courage (*andreia*), temperance (*sōphrosynē*), and justice (*dikaiosynē*) are the virtues of the three parts of the soul and of the just city. The Stoics adopted the same four as the *aretai* that jointly constitute the single virtue of the sage (see [[moral-stoic-virtue-ethics]]). **Cicero** rendered them into Latin in *De Officiis* I.5-15 (44 BCE) as the four sources of the *honestum* — wisdom, justice, greatness of spirit (*magnitudo animi* = fortitude), and seemliness/moderation (temperance) — the text through which the tetrad passed to the Latin West. **Ambrose of Milan**, in his *De Officiis Ministrorum* (c. 391), a deliberate Christian rewriting of Cicero's treatise, Christianized them; and it is generally to Ambrose (*Expositio Evangelii secundum Lucam* V.62) that the term *virtutes cardinales* is credited. Aquinas gives the definitive analysis in *Summa Theologiae* I-II q.61: the four are "cardinal" (principal) because every other moral virtue reduces to one of them, and each governs a distinct domain — reason itself (prudence), dealings with others (justice), the resolve to face danger (fortitude), and the restraint of desire (temperance).

### The three theological virtues (Paul → Aquinas)

Faith, hope, and love appear as a fixed triad in Paul — most famously 1 Cor 13:13 ("And now abideth faith, hope, charity, these three; but the greatest of these is charity"), and again at 1 Thess 1:3 and Rom 5:1-5. They are called "theological" because, in Aquinas's account (*Summa Theologiae* I-II q.62), their object is God himself, they are known only through divine revelation, and — unlike the cardinal virtues — they cannot be acquired by habituation but are infused by grace. Their ordering is deliberate: faith apprehends God as truth, hope reaches toward God as an attainable good, and charity (*caritas* / *agapē*) unites the soul to God as friend, and so is called the "form of the virtues" (*forma virtutum*) that vivifies all the rest. This is the decisive Christian innovation on the classical scheme: the tetrad perfects human nature, while the triad orders the human being to a supernatural end beyond nature's reach.

### The seven-virtue synthesis

Welding four acquired virtues to three infused ones into a single sevenfold scheme is a specifically Latin-Christian construction, and a gradual one. **Augustine** had already absorbed the cardinal four by redefining them as forms of the love of God (*De Moribus Ecclesiae Catholicae* I.15.25, c. 388: temperance is "love keeping itself whole and incorrupt for God," fortitude "love bearing all things readily for God's sake," and so on), subordinating the pagan virtues to charity. The fully articulated seven — and the systematic treatise on the cardinal virtues as such — belongs to the twelfth- and thirteenth-century schools: Philip the Chancellor, Albert the Great, and above all Aquinas, whose *Prima Secundae* (qq. 55-67) is the classic synthesis (documented in Houser 2004 and Bejczy 2011). Aquinas keeps the two orders carefully distinct even as he unites them: the cardinal virtues are connected among themselves through prudence, the theological virtues among themselves through charity, and the whole is crowned by the seven gifts of the Holy Spirit.

### Cross-tradition significance

The cross-tradition story of this node is unusually clean, because it is largely a story of **transmission** rather than convergence — and the two halves transmit differently. The four cardinal virtues are a documented, textual lineage: Plato (*Republic* IV) → the Stoa → Cicero (*De Officiis*) → Ambrose → Augustine → Aquinas. The identity of the Christian cardinal four with the Stoic four (see [[moral-stoic-virtue-ethics]]) is therefore **not** a coincidence of independent moral insight but direct borrowing, mediated by Cicero and naturalized by Ambrose's Christian rewrite of Cicero's own *De Officiis*. Bejczy (2011) traces this descent in detail across the Latin Middle Ages, stressing that the scheme was received and prized despite its frankly pagan origin. The relation to [[moral-natural-law]] is the same lineage seen from another angle: the cardinal virtues and Aquinas's natural law are twin renderings of Greek ethical rationalism into Christian theology, set out in adjacent questions of the same *Prima Secundae*.

The three theological virtues have no such classical pedigree. Faith, hope, and love as a normative triad are Pauline, and the move to treat them as **virtues** — habits perfecting the soul, on the same analytic footing as prudence or courage — is the Christian contribution with no Greek precedent. Where the tetrad answers "what is human excellence?", the triad answers a question the Greek schemes did not pose: how the human being is oriented to a transcendent God. Ambrose's coinage of "cardinal" occurs, tellingly, in his commentary on the Beatitudes (see [[moral-beatitudes-sermon-on-mount]]), and the later tradition multiplied such sevenfold correspondences.

Set beside enumerated virtue-schemes elsewhere, the resemblance is structural rather than genetic. The Han Confucian *wǔcháng* — the "five constants" *rén, yì, lǐ, zhì, xìn* (see [[moral-confucian-five-constants]]) — is likewise a closed, numbered set of cardinal virtues serving as the canonical moral scaffold of a civilization; but there is no route of contact between Han China and patristic Rome, and the parallel is independent convergence on the usefulness of a fixed, memorizable moral list, not shared descent.

### Disputes

Three points deserve honest statement.

**First, the popular pairing of "seven virtues against seven deadly sins" conflates distinct schemes.** The seven capital vices in their standard form (pride, envy, wrath, sloth, avarice, gluttony, lust — the list fixed by Gregory the Great, *Moralia in Job*, c. 590) are *not* the systematic opposites of the cardinal-plus-theological seven. The one-to-one "contrary virtues" of medieval catechesis and of the *Psychomachia* tradition (humility against pride, chastity against lust, and so on) form a third list again. The seven capital vices have a separate genealogy from the Egyptian desert (Evagrius's eight "thoughts," reduced by Cassian and Gregory). The neat "seven vs seven" symmetry is a later devotional harmonization, not an original structural correspondence.

**Second, the tetrad is Platonic, not Aristotelian.** Aristotle never enumerates the four as a set (see [[moral-aristotelian-virtues-golden-mean]]); what Aquinas takes from Aristotle is the *analysis* of moral virtue — acquisition by habituation, the mean according to right reason — which he then applies to the Platonic-Stoic-Ciceronian four. Attributing the cardinal virtues themselves to Aristotle is a common but real error.

**Third, whether the faith-hope-love triad is Paul's own coinage** or inherited from earlier Christian (or Hellenistic-Jewish) liturgical language is debated; the triad's tight, formulaic recurrence across several letters has led some scholars to suspect a pre-Pauline traditional formula rather than a Pauline invention. Either way, its elevation into a triad of theological *virtues* is the work of later, especially scholastic, theology.
