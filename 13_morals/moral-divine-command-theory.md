## Divine Command Theory — Morality Derived from God's Will

Divine Command Theory (DCT) is the metaethical position that moral obligations derive entirely from God's commands: an act is morally right *because* God commands it; morally wrong *because* God prohibits it. Moral goodness is not an independent property of actions that God perceives and endorses — it is constituted by divine will. Where God commands, there obligation follows; where God prohibits, there wrongness follows; what God neither commands nor prohibits is morally neutral.

### Historical Origins and Formulations

The philosophical articulation of DCT emerged from medieval Christian scholasticism, but the underlying conviction — that God's authoritative will is the source of human moral obligation — pervades all three Abrahamic traditions from their origins.

**William of Ockham** (c. 1287-1347) provides the strongest voluntarist formulation: even if God commanded what appears cruel — including, hypothetically, hatred of God — such an act would become morally good by virtue of the command. For Ockham, nothing in the nature of acts constrains divine freedom; God's will is absolutely sovereign. This "radical voluntarism" generated immediate controversy but shaped Protestant thought.

**John Duns Scotus** (c. 1266-1308) offered a moderate version: the first table of the Decalogue (duties to God) follows necessarily from God's nature, but the second table (duties to humans) is contingent on divine will and could have been otherwise. This distinction between necessary and contingent divine commands remains influential.

**Calvinist covenant theology** embeds DCT in a contractual framework: God freely establishes covenants defining human moral obligations. The moral law expressed in the Decalogue is binding not because of its intrinsic rationality but because God has freely chosen to enter covenant with humanity.

**Islamic Ash'arism** represents the dominant DCT position within Islamic theology. The Ash'arite school, following Abu al-Hasan al-Ash'ari (874-936 CE), held that *hasan* (moral good) and *qabih* (moral evil) are determined purely by divine decree (*shar'*). An act has no moral quality independent of what God reveals about it. This position was adopted by the majority of Sunni theologians. See [[tradition-sunni-islam]].

### The Abrahamic Convergence

DCT is not merely a philosophical theory — it is the operative moral theology of the three largest monotheistic traditions:

**Judaism**: The 613 *mitzvot* (commandments) of the Torah constitute divine commands binding upon Israel. Halakhic reasoning interprets and applies these commands, but their ultimate authority is the divine lawgiver. The Sinai revelation in [[tradition-judaism]] is the paradigm event: God speaks, Israel is obligated. The question of *why* God commands as He does — whether divine commands track independent moral truths or constitute them — is debated across rabbinic literature. Maimonides (1138-1204) argues that divine commands generally track rational goods (*reasons* for the commandments), while the voluntarist wing (following Duns Scotus' Islamic parallel, the Mu'tazilite-vs-Ash'arite split) holds that the command itself is sufficient.

**Christianity**: The Ten Commandments ([[moral-ten-commandments]]) are received as God's direct moral legislation. Medieval Christian theology largely embraced natural law as complementary to divine command (following Aquinas), but Protestant Reformation traditions — especially in the Calvinist and Lutheran strands — re-emphasized the primacy of divine command. Karl Barth's 20th-century "command ethics" represents a sophisticated neo-orthodox DCT: ethics flows entirely from God's concrete command in each moment, not from general principles.

**Islam**: [[tradition-islam]]'s foundational moral framework is explicitly divine-command: the Quran is God's direct speech (*kalām Allāh*), and its moral requirements are obligatory precisely because God has spoken them. The five pillars of Islam, the prohibitions of the Quran, and the elaborated sharia ([[moral-islamic-sharia]]) are all expressions of DCT in lived practice for 1.8 billion people.

### The Central Problem: The Euthyphro Dilemma

The most searching philosophical challenge to DCT was posed long before the Abrahamic traditions developed their DCT positions. In Plato's *Euthyphro* (c. 399 BCE), Socrates asks: "Is something pious because the gods love it, or do the gods love it because it is pious?" — the [[moral-euthyphro-dilemma]]. Applied to monotheistic DCT: Is murder wrong because God forbids it (Horn 1), or does God forbid it because murder is independently wrong (Horn 2)?

Horn 1 (DCT proper) faces the arbitrariness objection: if God had commanded murder, murder would be morally obligatory. This seems to make morality entirely contingent on divine will with no constraints — a troubling conclusion.

Horn 2 abandons DCT: if God commands what is independently good, then morality is independent of God, and God's role becomes epistemic (God tells us what is good) rather than constitutive (God makes things good).

Modern DCT defenders — Robert Adams, Philip Quinn, William Alston — respond through "modified divine command theory": God necessarily commands from God's essentially good nature, so the arbitrariness of Horn 1 is avoided because God cannot coherently command evil (given God's nature). This move, however, locates the moral bedrock in God's nature rather than in God's will, creating pressure back toward natural law territory.

### Cross-Tradition Significance

DCT is the single most historically influential moral framework by population: over half of humanity today belongs to traditions that ground morality in divine command (Islam, Judaism, Christianity, and branches of Hinduism and Sikhism). The debate between DCT and [[moral-natural-law]] (reason can discover moral truths independently) has structured Western moral philosophy since at least Cicero and continues in contemporary analytic philosophy of religion. The [[ethical-monotheism]] theme captures how the monotheistic traditions deploy this framework; DCT is the specific metaethical mechanism.
