## The Euthyphro Dilemma — Is Something Good Because God Commands It?

One of philosophy's most durable and consequential problems was stated in a single question by Socrates in Athens around 399 BCE, in Plato's dialogue *Euthyphro*: "Is something pious because the gods love it, or do the gods love it because it is pious?"

In its modern monotheistic form, the dilemma runs: Is an act morally good **because** God commands it — or does God command it **because** it is morally good?

Both answers create profound difficulties.

### The Two Horns

**Horn 1 — Divine Command (Voluntarism):** Moral goodness is whatever God commands. Murder is wrong because God forbids it. If God had commanded murder, murder would be morally obligatory.

*Problem:* This makes morality entirely arbitrary — contingent on divine will with no independent constraints. If God commanded gratuitous cruelty, cruelty would be good. Most people find this conclusion unacceptable. William of Ockham, the most rigorous medieval defender of Horn 1, accepted this implication: God could have commanded us to hate God, and such hatred would then be our obligation. This "radical voluntarism" made many theologians deeply uncomfortable.

**Horn 2 — Moral Realism / Natural Law:** God commands what is morally good because it is independently good. Murder is wrong because of something about murder itself — and God, perceiving this, prohibits it.

*Problem:* If moral goodness is independent of God's will, then morality doesn't ultimately rest on God at all. God's role becomes epistemic (a reliable revealer of moral truth) rather than constitutive (the creator of moral truth). This seems to place a constraint on divine omnipotence — God cannot simply decree that cruelty is good — and suggests that even without God, there would still be moral facts.

Neither horn is painless. The dilemma has no clean escape.

### The MASSIVE WIN: One Greek Question, Three Traditions, 2,400 Years

This is among the most powerful cross-tradition convergences in the vault. Socrates posed the dilemma in 399 BCE, a century before Alexander brought Greek thought to the Near East. Yet the *identical* structural problem — stated independently — became the central controversy in **all three Abrahamic moral theologies**:

**Islam:** The defining schism of early Islamic moral theology runs precisely along the Euthyphro fault line. The **Mu'tazilites** (8th-10th c.) argued that good and evil (*hasan wa-qabih*) are rationally knowable — God commands what is good because it IS good by nature (Horn 2). God's justice requires this; an all-good God cannot command evil. The **Ash'arites**, following al-Ash'ari (874-936 CE), counter-argued that nothing is intrinsically good or evil — divine command constitutes goodness (Horn 1). The Ash'arite position became the dominant Sunni position. [[tradition-sunni-islam]] embeds the Euthyphro Dilemma's Horn 1 as its operative moral theology, while Mu'tazilite and later Mu'tazilite-influenced Shi'ite theology embeds Horn 2.

**Judaism:** The debate appears in medieval Jewish philosophy with comparable force. **Maimonides** (1138-1204) argued that divine commands generally have reasons rooted in human benefit and rational moral structure (closer to Horn 2 — God commands what is good by its nature). This rationalist tradition was challenged by **voluntarist** positions influenced by Ockhamism and by mystical traditions that ground obligation in pure divine will. The 613 *mitzvot* of [[tradition-judaism]] become the test case: are they good because God commanded them, or did God command them because they are good?

**Christianity:** Aquinas (1225-1274) resolved toward Horn 2 via his natural law theory: God commands according to God's essentially rational nature, and human reason can partially access the moral order independently. This is "Horn 2 with theological grounding." But Protestant Reformers — especially in the Calvinist tradition — pushed back toward Horn 1: God's will is sovereign, and the attempt to subordinate it to rational moral standards smacks of Greek philosophy's pollution of biblical theology.

### The Modified Divine Command Theory Response

The dominant contemporary response to the dilemma is Robert Adams' "modified divine command theory" (1973): God necessarily commands from God's essentially good and loving nature. On this view, Horn 1's arbitrariness is avoided — God cannot coherently command cruelty because cruelty contradicts God's nature. But Horn 2's worry is also avoided — moral standards are not independent of God because they are grounded in God's nature.

Critics note that this move relocates the moral bedrock from God's will to God's nature — and then we face a third-order Euthyphro question: Is God's nature good because God has it, or does God have it because it's good? The dilemma recurses.

### Why This Matters for the Vault

The Euthyphro Dilemma is not merely a philosophical puzzle — it is the structural spine of moral theology in [[tradition-judaism]], [[tradition-christianity-canonical]], and [[tradition-islam]], all three running through the same logical grid that Plato's Socrates constructed 2,400 years ago. The Greek philosophical tradition, usually treated as the precursor to or competitor of monotheism, turns out to have generated the single most durable problem WITHIN monotheistic moral theology. This is a rare case where the "pagan" Greek tradition becomes permanently lodged inside the doctrinal machinery of all three successor traditions.
