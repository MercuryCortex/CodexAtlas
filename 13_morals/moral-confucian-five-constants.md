## The Five Constants — Wǔcháng as the Systematic Architecture of Confucian Virtue

The Five Constants (*wǔcháng* 五常) are the canonical set of Confucian virtues: *rén* 仁 (benevolence), *yì* 義 (righteousness), *lǐ* 禮 (ritual propriety), *zhì* 智 (wisdom), and *xìn* 信 (trustworthiness). Where the [[moral-confucian-ren]] node treats *rén* as the master virtue of Confucius' [[confucius]] *Analects*, the Five Constants represent the Han-dynasty systematization (2nd century BCE - 1st century CE) of Confucian ethics into a structured five-virtue architecture, codified by Dong Zhongshu (179-104 BCE) and the *Bohu Tong* (Comprehensive Discussions in the White Tiger Hall, 79 CE).

### The Five Virtues

1. **Rén** 仁 — Benevolence / humaneness / fellow-feeling. The cardinal virtue from which the others flow; treated in detail in [[moral-confucian-ren]].
2. **Yì** 義 — Righteousness / appropriate-action / moral rightness. The virtue of doing what is fitting in the situation, often against self-interest.
3. **Lǐ** 禮 — Ritual propriety / decorum / ceremonial appropriateness. Not mere etiquette — *lǐ* is the structured form through which moral content acquires social efficacy.
4. **Zhì** 智 — Wisdom / practical insight / discernment. The capacity to recognize moral situations and judge appropriate action.
5. **Xìn** 信 — Trustworthiness / sincerity / reliability. The virtue of being someone whose word is reliable and whose conduct is consistent.

The five are unified — each presupposes and reinforces the others. *Rén* without *lǐ* is sentimentality without form; *lǐ* without *rén* is hollow ceremony; *yì* without *zhì* is mistaken righteousness; *zhì* without *xìn* is cleverness without integrity. The integrated cultivation of all five is the moral life of the Confucian *junzi* (exemplary person).

### The MASSIVE WIN: Han Systematization and the Correlative Cosmology

The codification of exactly five virtues is not an accident of accumulation. Dong Zhongshu's *Chunqiu Fanlu* embedded the Confucian virtues into the correlative cosmology of the Han dynasty: the Five Phases (*wǔxíng*: wood, fire, earth, metal, water), the Five Directions (east, south, center, west, north), the Five Seasons, the Five Organs, the Five Tones. The Five Constants take their place in this cosmological matrix:

- *Rén* (benevolence) ↔ wood ↔ east ↔ spring
- *Yì* (righteousness) ↔ metal ↔ west ↔ autumn
- *Lǐ* (ritual propriety) ↔ fire ↔ south ↔ summer
- *Zhì* (wisdom) ↔ water ↔ north ↔ winter
- *Xìn* (trustworthiness) ↔ earth ↔ center ↔ late summer

This correlative framework grounds the virtues in cosmic structure: to cultivate *rén* is to participate in the generative life-force of spring; to embody *yì* is to channel the sharpness of autumn judgment. The Confucian virtues are *natural* in the strong sense — not merely human conventions but reflections of cosmic patterns of which humans are participants. This makes Han Confucianism a natural-law ethics ([[moral-natural-law]]) without a commanding deity: the virtues are grounded in cosmic order (*tiān*, Heaven; the same word that grounds Mandate-of-Heaven political theory).

The structural similarity to Stoic ethics ([[moral-stoic-virtue-ethics]]) is striking. The Stoics grounded their four cardinal virtues in the cosmic *logos* without a personal commanding God. Han Confucianism grounded its five virtues in cosmic *tiān* and the Five-Phase correlations without a personal commanding God. Both traditions arrived at structurally similar virtue-ethical systems with cosmic grounding in the same window (3rd century BCE - 2nd century CE), independently, without documented contact. This is one of the cleanest cases of cross-cultural convergence on a non-theistic virtue-cosmology in the history of ethics.

### Genealogy from Confucius and Mencius

The Five Constants are not invented by Han systematizers — they are extracted from a tradition that runs from Confucius through Mencius (*Mengzi*, 4th century BCE) into the Han. Confucius treats *rén* and *lǐ* extensively; Mencius adds *yì* and *zhì* as parallel virtues anchored in the *four sprouts* (*sì duān*, *Mengzi* 2A.6) — innate moral feelings present in all humans:

- The heart of compassion → *rén*
- The heart of shame → *yì*
- The heart of deference → *lǐ*
- The heart of right-and-wrong → *zhì*

Mencius' four-sprouts theory is one of the most important contributions to world moral psychology: humans are not blank slates needing external moral instruction, but bearers of innate moral capacities requiring only proper cultivation. This is a strong nativist moral psychology that Western ethics did not arrive at until Bishop Butler (1726) and modern moral nativism. *Xìn* (trustworthiness) is added in the Han systematization to complete the cosmologically-required set of five — moving the tradition from a four-sprouts ethics into the formalized Five Constants architecture.

### Cross-Tradition Reach

The Five Constants became the operational virtue-ethics of imperial Chinese governance for 2,000 years. They structured the civil service examinations (the *kējǔ* system, c. 605-1905 CE), which required candidates to demonstrate mastery of the Confucian canon and the virtues it codifies. They shaped the moral self-cultivation of literati across the Tang, Song, Ming, and Qing dynasties; they were reformulated in the Neo-Confucian synthesis of Zhu Xi ([[zhu-xi]]) and Wang Yangming; they entered Korea through Joseon-dynasty state Confucianism ([[tradition-joseon-confucianism]]), Vietnam through Lý-Trần-and-later neo-Confucian adoption, and Japan through the Tokugawa-era Confucian school and the bushidō tradition ([[moral-bushido-samurai-code]]).

The transmission to Japanese bushidō is especially clean: four of the seven cardinal samurai virtues — *jin* 仁, *gi* 義, *rei* 礼, *chū* 忠 (loyalty, which Japanese tradition emphasized more than Chinese) — are Japanese readings of the Chinese Five Constants. The Confucian virtue architecture, originally formulated for a literati-administrative class, was adapted by Tokugawa scholars (Yamaga Sokō especially) to define warrior ethics. This is one of the most consequential moral-philosophical transmissions in East Asian history: the same virtue-set that structured Chinese civil-service ethics for a millennium became the ethical framework of the Japanese samurai class.

### Comparison with the Decalogue and the Five Precepts

The Five Constants are structurally distinct from the rule-based codes of the [[moral-ten-commandments]] and the [[moral-buddhist-five-precepts]]. Where the Decalogue prohibits specific acts and the Precepts enumerate specific abstentions, the Constants name character-states to be cultivated. The Confucian ethical question is not "what should I refrain from doing?" but "what kind of person should I become?" This makes Confucianism, alongside Stoicism and Aristotelian ethics, one of the world's great *virtue ethics* traditions — and the only one to have served continuously as the official state ethics of a major civilization for over two millennia.
