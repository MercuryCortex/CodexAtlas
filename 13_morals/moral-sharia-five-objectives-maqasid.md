## Maqāṣid al-Sharīʿah — The Five Objectives of Islamic Law (al-Ghazālī and al-Shāṭibī)

**Maqāṣid al-Sharīʿah** (مقاصد الشريعة, "Objectives of Islamic Law") is the Islamic juridical doctrine that the Sharia, considered as a whole, exists to protect and promote five foundational human goods. The doctrine was articulated programmatically by **Abū Ḥāmid al-Ghazālī** (d. 1111) in *al-Mustaṣfā* and systematized definitively by **Abū Isḥāq al-Shāṭibī** (d. 1388, Granada) in *al-Muwāfaqāt fī uṣūl al-aḥkām*. The five objectives provide the **teleological framework** that integrates the otherwise disparate ruling-corpus of Islamic jurisprudence (*fiqh*) into a unified ethical-legal system.

The five necessities (*al-ḍarūriyyāt al-khams*) are:

1. **Protection of religion** (ḥifẓ al-dīn — حفظ الدين)
2. **Protection of life** (ḥifẓ al-nafs — حفظ النفس)
3. **Protection of intellect** (ḥifẓ al-ʿaql — حفظ العقل)
4. **Protection of lineage/family** (ḥifẓ al-nasl — حفظ النسل)
5. **Protection of property** (ḥifẓ al-māl — حفظ المال)

Every Sharia ruling, on the Maqāṣid view, ultimately serves one or more of these five necessities. Prohibitions on murder and battery protect life; prohibitions on intoxicants protect intellect; marriage and inheritance law protect lineage; commercial law and theft prohibitions protect property; idolatry prohibitions and prayer obligations protect religion. The legal-moral architecture is teleological: rulings are intelligible by reference to the purposes they serve.

### Three-Tier Necessity Hierarchy

Al-Shāṭibī (developing al-Ghazālī's framework) articulates a three-tier hierarchy of legal interests:

- **Ḍarūriyyāt** (necessities): the five above — without these, individual and social life collapses.
- **Ḥājiyyāt** (needs): interests whose protection prevents hardship but whose absence does not destroy life. Examples include trade-contract conveniences, the *rukhsa* (legal dispensation) for travelers' prayer-shortening, hire-purchase arrangements.
- **Taḥsīniyyāt** (improvements/embellishments): interests that perfect the social-moral order without being necessary. Examples include cleanliness obligations beyond minimum, charitable-giving beyond *zakāt*, dress-norms beyond bare modesty.

The hierarchy provides a principled resolution to conflict-of-interests cases in *fiqh*: when a necessity conflicts with a need, the necessity prevails; when two necessities conflict (e.g., a forced choice between life and lineage), the higher-ranked one prevails. This makes Maqāṣid theory a genuine **deliberative ethics** as well as a *post-hoc* legal-theoretical structure.

### The MASSIVE WIN: Convergence with Aquinas's Natural Law

The structural parallel between al-Shāṭibī's Maqāṣid and Aquinas's natural-law theory ([[moral-natural-law]]) is among the most striking convergences in medieval philosophical-theological ethics. Aquinas's *Summa Theologiae* I-II.94.2 (composed c. 1270) articulates the basic goods of natural law as: (a) self-preservation, (b) procreation and care for offspring, (c) knowledge of God and life in society. The list maps with remarkable precision onto al-Shāṭibī's five necessities: self-preservation ≈ ḥifẓ al-nafs; procreation+offspring ≈ ḥifẓ al-nasl; knowledge of God ≈ ḥifẓ al-dīn; rationality+society ≈ ḥifẓ al-ʿaql; with property as a derivative interest under Aquinas as it is a primary necessity under Maqāṣid.

The convergence is *not* coincidental. Aquinas's natural-law theory is mediated through Aristotle (whom he reads through Averroes and Maimonides). Al-Ghazālī's articulation is mediated through *uṣūl al-fiqh* (legal-theory tradition) and Mu'tazilī rational ethics. Both traditions draw on the shared Hellenistic-Late-Antique conceptual heritage. Jasser Auda (2008) and George Hourani (1985, *Reason and Tradition in Islamic Ethics*) have both argued that the structural parallel reflects shared philosophical genealogy, not independent convergence — though the specific five-necessity articulation appears to be a Ghazalian innovation that has no direct precedent in pre-Islamic philosophy.

### Modern Reception: Maqāṣid as Reform Hermeneutic

In the 20th and 21st centuries, Maqāṣid theory has been deployed by reformist Islamic thinkers as a hermeneutic for re-interpreting traditional fiqh in the light of contemporary conditions. Muhammad Abduh (d. 1905), Rashid Rida (d. 1935), Allal al-Fasi (d. 1974), Yusuf al-Qaradawi, Jasser Auda, and Tariq Ramadan have all developed Maqāṣid-based readings that argue traditional rulings should be re-examined where their literal content no longer serves the underlying maqsad (purpose) under modern conditions.

This is theologically significant: Maqāṣid theory provides a principled Islamic-internal framework for legal change without abandoning the Sharia framework. It is the closest Islamic analogue to Christian "tradition-development" hermeneutics (John Henry Newman, John XXIII's *aggiornamento*) and to rabbinic *takkanot* (legislative enactments by sages). The current Maqāṣid reform movement is one of the most theologically substantive intellectual currents in contemporary Islamic thought.

### Sharia's Universal-Ethics Dimension

The five necessities articulate what is sometimes called Sharia's **universal-ethics** layer. Three of the five (life, intellect, property) are universal goods recognizable across traditions and protected by virtually every legal system. Lineage protection is widely-but-not-universally framed; religion protection is distinctly Islamic in its specific articulation but maps onto every tradition's analogous protection of religious-ritual order. Maqāṣid theory thus offers an Islamic framework for dialogue with non-Islamic legal-ethical traditions — universal goods + tradition-specific articulation — structurally parallel to the rabbinic Noahide framework ([[moral-noahide-laws-seven]]).
