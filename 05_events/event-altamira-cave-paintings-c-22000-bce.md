# Altamira Cave Polychrome Paintings (c. 36,000–13,000 BCE)

## What happened
Altamira Cave, a limestone cave near Santillana del Mar on the Cantabrian coast of northern Spain, contains painted and engraved imagery accumulated over an extended Palaeolithic interval. The cave was discovered in 1868 by a local hunter, Modesto Cubillas, but the painted ceiling was first noticed in 1879 by María Sanz de Sautuola (then eight years old), daughter of the amateur archaeologist Marcelino Sanz de Sautuola, who published the find that year. Sanz de Sautuola was ridiculed by the contemporary academic establishment — the paintings were considered too sophisticated to be Palaeolithic and were suspected of being forgeries. The authenticity of Palaeolithic cave art was only accepted by the academic mainstream after Cartailhac's 1902 *Mea Culpa d'un Sceptique* in *L'Anthropologie* — a turning point in the discipline's history.

The site is most famous for its **Great Ceiling** ("Sala de los Polícromos") — a low-roofed chamber covered in polychrome paintings of bison (some 16+ bison figures), horses, deer, a wild boar, and abstract signs, executed using natural ceiling-relief to give the figures three-dimensional volume. The polychrome bison technique (ochre, hematite, and charcoal applied with stumping and shading to model musculature) represents the technical apex of Iberian Upper Palaeolithic painting. Beyond the Great Ceiling, the cave contains deeper galleries with engravings, hand stencils, and abstract signs.

**Dating.** Conventional dating (based on associated archaeological deposits and stylistic correlation) placed the principal polychrome ceiling in the Magdalenian (~16,500–14,000 BP), with some Solutrean (~22,000–17,000 BP) and earlier components. Pike et al. 2012 (*Science*) U-Th dated calcite flowstone overlying selected Altamira marks and obtained minimum ages up to ~35,600 BP for one painted club-shaped sign — implying Aurignacian-age use of the cave alongside the famous Magdalenian polychrome work. **Altamira is therefore not a single-period site but a multi-millennial palimpsest.**

UNESCO World Heritage 1985 (extended 2008). The cave was closed to public visitation in 1977–1982 and again from 2002 to address microbial damage; a full-scale replica ("Neocueva") at the adjacent National Museum is the principal visitor experience.

## Religious consequences
- Altamira's polychrome ceiling is the iconographically most-developed Magdalenian-period imagery and is often paired with Lascaux as the climax of the Upper Palaeolithic painted-cave tradition. Both sites are dominated by *food fauna* (bison, horse, deer) rather than the dangerous fauna of Chauvet, reinforcing the thematic shift between Aurignacian and Magdalenian cave bestiaries that Leroi-Gourhan first systematized.
- The cave was *not a dwelling*. The painted ceiling chamber and the deeper galleries show no evidence of sustained habitation; the Aurignacian-age and Magdalenian-age occupation deposits cluster in the cave's entrance chamber. The painted spaces are *interior, structured-ritual locations* — material evidence for the same set-apart-space pattern attested at Chauvet, Lascaux, and most major painted caves.
- The repeated visit-and-mark pattern across ~20,000+ years (Aurignacian signs through Magdalenian polychromes) indicates that Altamira was a *long-lived persistent place* in the local cultural landscape — a precondition for and material trace of cumulative cultural-religious memory at the deep-time scale.

## Disputes
- **Dating of specific images.** The Pike et al. 2012 U-Th dating provides minimum ages from overlying calcite; specific *paintings* could be older than their overlying-calcite date but cannot be younger. The reconciliation of stylistic dating (Magdalenian polychrome ceiling) with the new U-Th minimum dates is not yet fully worked out for every image.
- **The Cartailhac affair as a cautionary tale.** The 1879–1902 rejection of Altamira's authenticity by the contemporary academic establishment is now invoked as a methodological lesson about *a priori* expectations distorting evidence-evaluation — a relevant frame when considering subsequent contested early dates (Neanderthal cave art in Iberia, Sulawesi cave art).
- **Interpretation.** As with all Upper Palaeolithic cave art: hunting-magic, totemic-identification, shamanic-vision (Lewis-Williams 2002), narrative-mythic illustration, and didactic-pedagogical readings all compete. None is decisive.
