# The Parthenon (447–432 BCE)

## Identity
The Parthenon is the principal temple on the Athenian Acropolis, dedicated to [[athena]] in her aspect as *Athena Parthenos* ("Athena the Maiden"). Built 447–432 BCE under the political leadership of Pericles, the architectural direction of Iktinos and Kallikrates, and the sculptural direction of [[phidias]], the temple replaced earlier Acropolis structures destroyed during the Persian sack of 480 BCE (the "Older Parthenon" / "Pre-Parthenon" was under construction when the Persians burned the Acropolis). The Parthenon is the canonical exemplar of the mature Doric order — and, by its incorporation of an Ionic frieze on the inner cella wall, the canonical case of cross-order synthesis in 5th-c. Greek temple architecture.

## Construction
The temple was commissioned under Pericles as the centerpiece of his program to rebuild the Acropolis monumentally after the Persian destruction — a programme funded substantially by the tribute-treasury of the Delian League, an Athenian-led naval alliance whose funds were transferred to Athens c. 454 BCE and increasingly deployed for domestic projects rather than collective defense (the political-financial controversy of this transfer is reported by Plutarch *Pericles* 12 and remains a major question in 5th-c. Athenian history).

The temple is constructed entirely of Pentelic marble (quarried at Mount Pentelicus, 16 km northeast of Athens). It is a peripteral Doric temple, 8 × 17 columns (the standard Doric ratio of n × (2n + 1) — here 8 × 17, an unusually elongated Doric ratio that produces a more rectangular footprint). The cella ("naos") is divided into two unequal rooms: the larger eastern room housing the colossal chryselephantine statue of Athena Parthenos, and the smaller western room (the *Parthenōn* proper — the room from which the temple takes its name — used as a treasury and the location of the Delian-League funds).

**Architectural refinements** ("optical corrections"): the Parthenon's stylobate (platform) is slightly convex (rising c. 6 cm at the center of the long sides, c. 11 cm at the center of the short sides); the columns lean very slightly inward (so that their axes would converge at a point c. 2.4 km above the temple if extended); the columns swell slightly in the middle (entasis); the corner columns are slightly thicker than the others. These refinements (analyzed since the 19th c. by Penrose 1851 and others) are usually interpreted as corrections to optical effects that would otherwise make the temple look "wrong" (concave stylobate, columns leaning outward) — though the question of how systematic vs. how aesthetic-perfectionist they are remains debated.

## Religious significance
**Cult image and the role of Phidias.** The temple housed the colossal chryselephantine (gold-and-ivory over wooden core) statue of Athena Parthenos, c. 12 m tall, by Phidias — completed c. 438 BCE. The statue depicted Athena standing armed (helmet, aegis, spear, shield), holding a Nike (Victory) in her extended right hand, with the shield bearing reliefs of the Amazonomachy on the outside and the Gigantomachy on the inside. The statue is lost (it survived to the 5th c. CE; the gold may have been removed earlier for re-coinage). Multiple Roman small-scale copies survive — the Varvakeion Athena (Athens National Archaeological Museum) is the most complete copy, though much smaller (c. 1 m) and of uneven quality.

**Iconographic programme.** The Parthenon carried three integrated sculptural programmes:
1. **The metopes** (92 panels around the exterior): four mythological battles — the Gigantomachy (east, gods vs. giants), the Amazonomachy (west, Athenians vs. Amazons), the Centauromachy (south, Lapiths vs. centaurs), and the Iliupersis / sack of Troy (north, Greeks vs. Trojans). The pattern: the Olympian / civic / human / Greek order against chaos / barbarism / animality / Asian disorder — a programmatic statement of Athenian-civic-religious identity post-Persian-Wars.
2. **The pediments**: east (the birth of Athena from the head of Zeus), west (the contest of Athena and Poseidon for the patronage of Attica).
3. **The Ionic frieze** (running around the top of the cella's exterior wall, inside the colonnade — a unique placement): the Panathenaic procession, the great festival in honour of Athena that culminated every four years in the presentation of a newly-woven peplos (sacred garment) to her ancient olive-wood statue housed in the Erechtheion (not to the Parthenon's Phidian Athena). The frieze depicts the procession in progress, with mortal Athenians on the long sides converging toward the gathered Olympian gods on the east — one of the rare instances in Greek temple sculpture of mortal Athenians directly depicted in a religious context.

**Festival and ritual context.** The Parthenon was not the principal cultic temple of Athena on the Acropolis — the older [[athena]]-Polias cult continued to centre on the olive-wood xoanon housed first in the destroyed pre-Persian temple and after 421 BCE in the Erechtheion. The Parthenon was therefore something closer to a magnificent votive thank-offering to Athena and a state-monumental display of Athenian-imperial wealth (with its treasury function explicit in the back cella). The Panathenaia festival included the great quadrennial procession depicted on the frieze and athletic / musical competitions; the Parthenon was the visual centerpiece but not the sole cultic site.

## Post-antique history
The Parthenon's continuous re-use is unusual:
- **Greek Orthodox church (c. 6th c. – 1204)** — converted to the Church of the Theotokos / Holy Wisdom, with major iconographic alterations (some sculptures defaced; mosaic/fresco programmes added in the apse).
- **Latin (Catholic) church (1204–c. 1456)** — under Frankish rule.
- **Ottoman mosque (c. 1456–1687)** — converted under Ottoman rule.
- **Destruction of 26 September 1687.** During the Morean War, the Venetian general Francesco Morosini bombarded the Acropolis; an artillery shell struck the Parthenon, which the Ottomans were using as a powder magazine; the resulting explosion destroyed the central section of the building. This is the moment of the building's collapse from intact-temple to ruin.
- **Removal of the Marbles (1801–1812).** Thomas Bruce, 7th Earl of Elgin, removed approximately half the surviving sculptural programme (~75 m of frieze, 17 pedimental figures, 15 metopes) under disputed Ottoman authorization; the marbles entered the British Museum 1816. The "Elgin Marbles" / "Parthenon Marbles" repatriation dispute is an ongoing major case in cultural-heritage law and remains unresolved.

## Disputes
- **The frieze's subject.** The procession is universally identified as the Panathenaic procession; the precise identity of some figures (especially the central east-frieze "peplos scene" of an adult and child handling a folded cloth) is debated. The traditional reading is the presentation of the peplos to Athena Polias; a minority reading (Connelly 1996) sees the central group as enacting a mythical-foundational sacrifice (the daughters of Erechtheus). Mainstream scholarship still endorses the peplos reading; the Connelly hypothesis is taken seriously but not consensus.
- **Funding from the Delian League treasury.** Plutarch *Pericles* 12 reports the Periclean defense ("Athens has paid its part; the others have no right to ask how the money is spent"); whether this is a fair summary of the financial mechanics or a polemical-moralizing retroactive criticism is contested in 5th-c. Athenian fiscal history.
- **"Optical refinements"** intentionality: see above. The refinements are real and measurable; whether they were deployed for aesthetic / corrective reasons (the standard view) or for symbolic-cosmological reasons (a minority view, e.g., some sacred-geometry literature) is largely settled in favour of the aesthetic-corrective reading.
- **Elgin Marbles legitimacy.** Was the 1801 Ottoman authorization (the *firman*, which survives only in an Italian translation) a sufficient legal basis for the removal? Both sides have detailed legal-historical arguments; this is a live dispute and the Atlas does not take a side.
