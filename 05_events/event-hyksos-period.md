# Hyksos Rule of Egypt (15th and 16th Dynasties)

## What happened

Between approximately 1650 and 1550 BCE, the 15th Dynasty of Egypt — the "Hyksos" — constituted the first extended rule of Egypt by a non-Egyptian people. The name derives from Egyptian *heka-khasut*, "rulers of foreign lands," rendered in Greek as *Hyksos* by Manetho (c. 280 BCE) and glossed by Josephus — quoting Manetho — as "shepherd kings." This gloss is philologically incorrect but proved historically influential in connecting the Hyksos to biblical tradition (*Contra Apionem* I.82–90).

The Hyksos were not a single invading army. Manfred Bietak's excavation at Tell el-Dab'a (confirmed as Avaris, Hyksos capital, in the eastern Nile Delta) reveals a gradual infiltration of West Semitic peoples beginning as early as the 12th Dynasty (c. 1950 BCE). By the mid-13th Dynasty, a culturally distinct community — marked by Canaanite-tradition pottery, donkey burials near residences (a Levantine pastoral custom absent from Egyptian contexts), and scarab manufacture — had achieved dominance in the Delta. A 2020 strontium isotope study (Stantis et al., *PLOS ONE*) of 75 burials at Tell el-Dab'a confirmed that the pre-Hyksos period shows the highest proportion of non-local immigrants, while by the 15th Dynasty itself many inhabitants were already Delta-born — indicating that the Hyksos rise to power was an internal political takeover by a settled immigrant community, not a dramatic conquest.

The 15th Dynasty kings (including Salitis, Bnon, Apachnan/Khyan, and Apophis/Apepi I) controlled Lower Egypt from Avaris while the native Theban 17th Dynasty ruled Upper Egypt. The Hyksos military advantage included the composite bow, the horse-drawn chariot, and bronze-bladed sickle-swords — technologies new to Egypt that the Thebans subsequently adopted and deployed in the reconquest.

## Religious consequences

The Hyksos period produced the most direct Egyptian-Levantine religious synthesis attested in the archaeological record. The Hyksos adopted Set (Egyptian god of storm, desert, and foreigners) as their patron deity, equating him with their Levantine storm-god Baal-Hadad — a syncretism that is the most documented case of Egyptian-Canaanite deity fusion in the second millennium BCE. Temples at Avaris show a Seth-Baal cult whose residue persisted into the 19th Dynasty: Ramesses I's family came from Avaris, and Seti I ("Man of Set") bears Set's name directly. Minoan frescoes discovered in the palace district indicate diplomatic or matrimonial contact with Crete, making Avaris a cosmopolitan hub connecting Egypt, Canaan, and the Aegean.

## Joseph narrative context

For maximalist scholars (Hoffmeier 1997), the Hyksos period is the historical setting within which the Joseph narrative (Genesis 37–50) is most plausibly anchored: a Semitic individual rising to high position in Egypt is most historically intelligible during a period of Semitic political dominance. Egyptian documentary detail in the Joseph story (slave prices, court titles, court protocol) is consistent with c. 1800–1600 BCE conditions. However, the mainstream treats the narrative as a late literary composition (Persian-period or late monarchic) with deliberate archaizing detail — the Egyptian background evidences careful composition, not contemporaneity.

## Documents in play

The Kamose Stelae (c. 1555 BCE, Karnak) and the autobiography of Ahmose son of Ebana (c. 1530 BCE, El-Kab) are the primary contemporary Egyptian accounts. Manetho's account, preserved in Josephus *Contra Apionem*, is the only ancient historiographic narrative — though it is a Ptolemaic-era Egyptian priestly reconstruction.

## Disputes

- Whether Manetho's "Hyksos" chapter derives from genuine temple-archive material or is a rhetorical construction shaped by inter-ethnic polemics of the 3rd century BCE is debated (Dillery 2015; Redford 1986).
- The identification of specific 15th Dynasty kings with those named by Manetho remains disputed.
- Whether the Hyksos exodus narrative (Kamose → Ahmose expulsion) is the historical kernel of the Exodus story, or merely a parallel, is the central debate in the [[theme-exodus-hyksos-thesis]].
