# Bhakti Movement (c. 700–1700 CE)

## What happened
A ~thousand-year arc of regional South Asian devotional religious renaissances that progressively transformed Hindu (and adjacent Sant, Sikh, and Sufi-Hindu syncretic) religious life from a Sanskrit-Brahmanical-ritual center to a vernacular-devotional-emotional center. The movement is not a single coordinated phenomenon but a series of regional renaissances, each composing devotional poetry in the regional vernacular and centered on a personal-divine focus (Krishna, Rāma, Śiva, Devī, or the formless nirguṇa divine), each developing local institutional forms (temple complexes, monastic lineages, devotional-singing communities), and each contributing to a cumulative cross-regional movement that, by ~1500, had reshaped religious life across the subcontinent. The Hindu nationalist / textbook framing of "the Bhakti movement" as a single coherent historical phenomenon was substantially crystallized in 20th-c. historiography (Hawley 2015 traces the genealogy of the term); the underlying regional renaissances are entirely real, but their conjunction as "one movement" is partly a 20th-c. interpretive construction.

The principal regional waves (Hawley 2015's schema, modified):
1. **Tamil South Indian wave (~7th–9th c.)**: the Vaishnava Āḻvārs ([[andal]], [[nammalvar]], 10 others) and the Shaiva Nāyaṉārs ([[tamil-three-nayanars]], [[manikkavacakar]], 60 others). The earliest deep root of the broader movement. Hardy 1983 argues this Tamil wave is the source-tradition for subsequent pan-Indian bhakti.
2. **Karnataka Vīraśaiva wave (~12th c.)**: [[basava]] and the Lingāyat / Vīraśaiva tradition; Kannada *vacanas*; radical anti-caste, anti-temple, anti-Vedic reform.
3. **Maharashtra Vārkarī wave (~13th–17th c.)**: Jñāneśvar, Eknath, Tukaram, Namdev (also a Sant who composed in Hindi); centered on Viṭṭhal (a form of Viṣṇu-Krishna) of Pandharpur; Marathi vernacular.
4. **South Indian Vedānta wave (~11th–13th c.)**: [[ramanuja]] (Tamil-Sanskrit), [[madhva]] (Karnataka-Sanskrit), [[nimbarka]] (probably Andhra → Vrindavan) — the philosophical-Vedantic systematization of bhakti theology.
5. **North Indian Sant wave (~14th–17th c.)**: [[kabir]], Ravidas, [[guru-nanak]], Dadu, Sundardas; Hindi-Bhojpuri-Awadhi vernacular; *nirguṇa* (formless-divine) devotion crossing Hindu-Muslim boundary.
6. **North Indian Krishna-bhakti wave (~15th–17th c.)**: [[vallabhacharya]], [[chaitanya-mahaprabhu]], [[mirabai]], [[surdas]]; Vrindavan / Bengal / Gujarat / Rajasthan centers; Brajbhāṣā vernacular; *saguṇa* (personal-form-divine) devotion to Krishna.
7. **North Indian Rama-bhakti wave (~16th–17th c.)**: [[tulsidas]]; Awadhi vernacular; the *Ramcharitmanas* (1574–1576) becomes the central religious-poetic text of the Hindi-speaking Hindu world.
8. **Bengal Śākta-bhakti wave (~17th–18th c.)**: [[ramprasad-sen]] and the Bengali Kali-bhakti tradition; Bengali vernacular; *nirguṇa-saguṇa* devotion to the Goddess.

## Religious consequences
- **Vernacularization of religious authority**: regional devotional canons in Tamil, Kannada, Marathi, Bengali, Brajbhāṣā, Awadhi, Punjabi increasingly displaced Sanskrit as the language of normative religious-poetic authority. Sri Vaishnavism's claim that the Tamil *Divya Prabandham* is canonically equal to the Sanskrit Veda (~12th c. under Ramanuja's school) is the structural prototype; analogous regional vernacular-canonical claims developed across the subsequent waves.
- **Devotional-emotional displacement of ritual-philosophical center**: the principal pre-bhakti religious mode (Vedic *yajña* + Vedantic *jñāna*-realization) was supplemented by, and for many devotional communities substantially displaced by, devotional-emotional *bhakti* as the central religious mode.
- **Anti-caste reformism in many waves**: the Vīraśaiva, Sant, and Lingāyat traditions were explicitly anti-caste; the Vaishnava-bhakti and Shaiva-bhakti traditions varied — Vaishnavism's *bhakti* theology asserts spiritual equality of devotees while institutional caste-practice generally persisted.
- **Hindu-Sufi syncretism in the North Indian Sant wave**: Kabir, Dadu, Nanak, and the Sant tradition share devotional vocabulary, polemical structure, and theological emphases with contemporary Indo-Persian Sufi poetry. The two devotional traditions cross-pollinated explicitly in late-medieval North India; the *Bijak* of Kabir is the most-cited single document of the synthesis.
- **Foundation of Sikhism**: the Sant tradition's *nirguṇa* devotional theology is the source-context from which Guru Nanak's Sikh tradition emerges; the Sikh Adi Granth incorporates Kabir, Ravidas, Namdev, and other Sants as canonical *bhagats* alongside the Sikh Gurus.
- **Reshaping of Hindu pilgrimage geography**: medieval-bhakti pilgrimage sites (Vrindavan for Krishna-bhakti, Pandharpur for Marathi Vārkarī, Tirupati for Tamil Vaishnava, Chidambaram for Tamil Shaiva, Puri for Gaudiya Vaishnavism, Banaras as multi-traditional center) became the principal nodes of pan-Indian religious geography.

## Cross-tradition consequences
- **Hindu-Sufi shared devotional idiom**: late-medieval Indo-Persian Sufi poetry (Chishti, Suhrawardi, the Indo-Persian *bhakti-marfa* literature) and North Indian Sant-Bhakti poetry developed in mutual influence in the same cultural-linguistic zone (the *Hindavī*-*Hindustani*-Awadhi vernacular sphere). The shared idioms: gendered yearning of the soul-bride for the Divine Beloved, the formless divine addressed by multiple names, the critique of external religious form, the antinomian inversion of social-religious hierarchy. The Akbar-period *sulh-i kull* state ideology institutionalized the cross-pollination at the elite-political level. See [[kabir]] and the Sant tradition; the [[guru-granth-sahib]]; the Indo-Persian Sufi tradition.
- **Bhakti as deep-substrate for modern Hindu modernist universalism**: [[ramakrishna]], [[swami-vivekananda]], Gandhi, and the 20th-c. Hindu modernist universalism explicitly draw on the bhakti tradition as the source of a pluralistic-devotional Hindu ethos. Vivekananda's Chicago 1893 address (see [[event-parliament-of-world-religions-1893]]) deploys bhakti-tradition vocabulary.
- **Modern Indian anti-caste politics**: the Vīraśaiva tradition ([[basava]]), the Sant tradition (Ravidas, Kabir), and the bhakti-anti-caste arc more broadly have been claimed by 20th-c. Dalit-Bahujan political movements (Ambedkar; modern Bahujan Samaj Party; Lingayat-religious-status debates) as the deep historical lineage for Indian anti-caste reform.

## Documents in play
See documents-produced and documents-affected above. The principal text-corpora: (1) the *Divya Prabandham* and *Tirumurai* Tamil canons; (2) the Marathi Vārkarī corpus; (3) the Kannada *vacana* corpus; (4) the Gauḍīya Vaiṣṇava textual corpus around Chaitanya; (5) the Hindi Sant corpus (Kabir, Dadu, etc.); (6) the Hindi Krishna-bhakti and Rama-bhakti corpora (Surdas, Mira, Tulsi); (7) the Sikh Adi Granth.

## Disputes
- **"Bhakti movement" as a coherent historical phenomenon vs. modern construction**: Hawley 2015 argues that the framing as a single coordinated movement is substantially a 20th-c. nationalist-historiographical move; the underlying regional renaissances are real but their conjunction as "one movement" is partly interpretive.
- **South-to-North diffusion (Hardy 1983) vs. multi-regional independent emergence (recent revisionism)**: Hardy's classic argument that the Tamil-South Alvar tradition is the source-spring from which the medieval-North-Indian bhakti tradition flows has been complicated by recent regional-historical work showing more multi-centric, less unilineally-south-to-north patterns.
- **Anti-caste universalism vs. continued caste-practice**: the disjunction between the bhakti texts' anti-caste rhetoric and the institutional caste-practice of bhakti communities is a long-running tension in modern bhakti historiography.
