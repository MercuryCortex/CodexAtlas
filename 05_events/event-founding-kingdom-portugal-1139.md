# Foundation of the Kingdom of Portugal (1139–1143)

## Event
The Kingdom of Portugal emerged from the County of Portugal — a frontier territory carved out of the Kingdom of León during the Christian Reconquista, granted in 1096 to Henry of Burgundy as a vassal fief. Henry's son **Afonso Henriques** (~1109–1185) seized control of the county from his mother Teresa of León in 1128 (Battle of São Mamede), began expanding aggressively southward against the Almoravid Moors, and on **July 25, 1139** won the **Battle of Ourique** — a victory over a coalition of Moorish armies in the Alentejo plains. Following this victory, his men acclaimed him *Rex* (King) on the battlefield — the traditional date of Portugal's founding as an independent kingdom. The Treaty of Zamora (1143) formalized Portuguese autonomy within the Iberian Christian political system, and the papal bull *Manifestis Probatum* (1179) gave Portugal its ultimate legitimacy as a papal fief, recognizing Afonso Henriques as rightful king.

## The Battle of Ourique: divine vision and national founding mythology

The battle of Ourique acquired, in the late medieval and early modern tradition, a miraculous accretion: the night before the battle, Christ appeared to Afonso Henriques and promised him victory against the five Moorish kings he faced. This **Christophany of Ourique** became the theological foundation stone of Portuguese national identity — the claim that Portugal was a divinely elected nation, chosen by Christ himself at its moment of founding.

The five besant-shaped coins (*quinas*) in the Portuguese coat of arms — five shields arranged in a cross pattern, each with five coins — were retrospectively interpreted as commemorating the five Moorish kings defeated at Ourique, with the coins representing Christ's five wounds. The cross formation became the visual encoding of the divine-election myth in the national symbol.

Alexandre Herculano (19th-century liberal historian) argued in his *História de Portugal* (1846) that the Christophany was a later legendary accretion with no medieval documentary basis — causing a furious ecclesiastical controversy. His critical-historical demolition was probably correct; the myth's power was untouched by the demolition.

## Cross-tradition significance

**The Ourique vision and the Portuguese divine-election theology**
The Christophany of Ourique is Portugal's version of Constantine's vision at the Milvian Bridge (312 CE: *in hoc signo vinces* — "in this sign conquer"). Both are battle-eve divine visions that retroactively sacralize the founding political moment of a Christian polity. Both generate a sustained tradition of divinely-elected national identity. Both were probably legendary accretions to genuine historical battles. The structural parallel to the Constantinian founding myth is the vault's best European parallel to the vision-legitimizes-political-founding pattern.

**The Ourique myth and Sebastianism**
The founding divine election at Ourique became one of the theological pillars of [[sebastianism-quinto-imperio]] — the argument that Portugal has a specific providential destiny. António Vieira's *Quinto Império* theology drew explicitly on the Ourique vision: if Christ appeared to found the kingdom, then Portugal is not just another European state but God's instrument. The Ourique myth → Sebastianist Fifth Empire ideology → Pessoa's *Mensagem* is a continuous theological thread in Portuguese cultural history.

**Reconquista as Crusade: the military-religious fusion**
The Reconquista was simultaneously a land-grab, a political consolidation, and a religious war — the same fusion of military and sacred that characterized the Holy Land Crusades (launched 1095, four years before the Portuguese county's establishment). The **Knights Templar** were militarily active in the Reconquista from the 1120s; after their suppression (1307–1312, see [[event-trial-of-templars-1307-1314]]), their Portuguese properties were not confiscated but transformed into the **Order of Christ** (1319, see [[event-order-of-christ-foundation-1319]]). The Portuguese Reconquista institution thus directly inherited the Templar military-religious structure that the founding of the kingdom had employed.

## The name Portugal: Portus Cale and the Celtic layer
The kingdom's name derives from **Portus Cale** — the Roman port settlement near the mouth of the Douro River (modern Porto/Gaia area). The evolution: *Portus Cale* → *Portucale* → *Portugale* → *Portugal*.

**"Portus"** (harbor/port, Latin) is transparent. **"Cale"** is the crux:
- The dominant scholarly view: "Cale" preserves the name of the **Gallaeci** (also *Callaici*, *Callaeci*) — the dominant Celtic tribal confederation of the northwest Iberian Peninsula. Roman sources use "Portus Callaicorum" or similar forms; "Cale" is a Latin shortening of the Callaician tribal-territory name. By this reading, **Portugal literally means "Port of the Gallaeci"** — a Celtic name for a Celtic people's harbor.
- **The Celtic deity possibility**: Some researchers have proposed that "Cale" preserves a pre-Roman deity or sacred place name associated with the settlement. The root *cal-* appears in Celtic place-names across Atlantic Europe in contexts suggesting "rock / height / fort" (cf. Welsh *caled* = hard; Gaelic *caill* = hazel/wood). A divine or sacred toponym at the Douro mouth is not documented epigraphically but is structurally consistent with the general pattern of sacred pre-Roman place-names at river mouths and headlands.
- **Greek *kalos*** ("beautiful"): A minority hypothesis; likely folk etymology.

The most probable reading: "Portugal" preserves a Celtic tribal name (*Gallaeci/Callaeci*) at least 2,500 years old — predating the Roman conquest of Iberia (~2nd c. BCE), predating the county, predating the kingdom. The nation named itself, unknowingly, after its pre-Christian Celtic inhabitants.
