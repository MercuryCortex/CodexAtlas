# Foundation of the Temple of Leontopolis

## What Happened
**[[onias-iv]]**, son of the murdered last legitimate Zadokite high priest [[onias-iii]], fled to Ptolemaic Egypt following the Maccabean revolt and the subsequent Hasmonean seizure of the Jerusalem high priesthood. He petitioned the Ptolemaic pharaoh (Ptolemy VI Philometor, r. 180–145 BCE) for permission and land to build a Jewish temple. His petition cited Isaiah 19:19 as prophetic sanction: "In that day there will be an altar to the Lord in the midst of Egypt, and a pillar to the Lord at its border."

The temple was built at **Leontopolis** (modern Tell el-Yahudiyya) in the Heliopolis nome (administrative district) of the Nile delta, approximately 20 km northeast of modern Cairo and within easy reach of Alexandria. Josephus describes it as modeled on but smaller than the Jerusalem Temple, with a tower rather than a ziggurat.

## Duration
The temple operated for approximately **220 years** — from its founding (~150 BCE) until **73 CE**, when the Romans ordered it closed. The closure came three years after the Romans destroyed the Jerusalem Temple (70 CE); they feared that Leontopolis might become a rallying point for Jewish military resistance, as many Sicarii (Jewish resistance fighters) had fled to Egypt. Josephus (*Jewish War* VII.433–436) records that the Roman prefect Lupus ordered the temple's closure; his successor Paulinus completed the confiscation and closure.

## Why This Is Extraordinary

### 1. The Only Extended Non-Jerusalem Jewish Temple
The **Elephantine Temple** (Upper Egypt, 5th c. BCE — a military colony's temple that predates the Jerusalem Temple's reconstruction and may have survived into the Persian period) is the only other significant case. Leontopolis is the only Jewish temple *in the post-Babylonian-exile period* that operated for an extended time outside Jerusalem, maintained by a legitimate priestly family, with full sacrificial cult. It challenges the standard narrative that Deuteronomy's "one place of worship" theology had fully triumphed — clearly some Diaspora Jews considered a second temple legitimate under the circumstances.

### 2. Zadokite Priestly Authority in Exile
The Leontopolis temple was staffed by **Oniad-Zadokite priests** — the family that had been expelled from Jerusalem by the Hasmonean usurpation. It represents the Zadokite priestly tradition's attempt to reproduce itself outside its natural institutional home. For the duration of its existence, there was a rival claim: Oniad priests maintaining that Zadokite legitimacy resided in Egypt, not in the Hasmonean-controlled Jerusalem.

### 3. Geographic Proximity to Alexandria
Leontopolis sits approximately 40 km from **Alexandria**, the most intellectually productive city of the ancient world. During the temple's 220-year lifespan, Alexandria produced: the **Septuagint** (Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible); the philosopher **Philo of Alexandria** (Jewish-Platonic synthesis); the **proto-Hermetic texts** (Egyptian-Greek-Jewish philosophical synthesis); the **Gospel of John** (possibly composed in Alexandria); the **Nag Hammadi tradition** (Gnostic Christianity). Whether the Oniad priestly community at Leontopolis had intellectual contact with these Alexandrian Jewish circles is not documented — but the physical proximity and temporal overlap make such contact nearly certain at the level of shared community, even if not at the level of doctrinal influence.

### 4. The Roman Closure as Symmetry
The Romans closed Leontopolis in 73 CE — three years after destroying the Jerusalem Temple in 70 CE. Both closures happened within the same generation. The **entire Zadokite-Temple institutional complex** — both the original in Jerusalem and its Egyptian exile-continuation — was eliminated by Roman military force within three years. The institutional chain from Aaron to the 1st century CE ended in 73 CE.
