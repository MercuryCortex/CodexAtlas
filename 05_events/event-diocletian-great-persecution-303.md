# Diocletian Great Persecution (303-311 CE)

## What happened
The most systematic, prolonged, and severe anti-Christian persecution in Roman history. **Initiated February 23, 303**, with [[diocletian-emperor|Diocletian]]'s first edict (destroy churches, burn scriptures, deprive Christians of legal rights and senatorial office), followed by a series of escalating edicts through 303–304:
- Spring 303: arrest clergy.
- November 303: require sacrifice from imprisoned clergy.
- Spring 304: universal sacrifice requirement (the *Decian* model, applied empire-wide).

Lactantius (*De mortibus persecutorum*) names **Galerius** (Diocletian's Caesar, the eastern junior emperor) as the principal anti-Christian driver. The persecution intensity varied sharply by region: in the **East** (Galerius's, then Maximinus Daia's territories) it was severe and lasted until 311; in the **West** (under Constantius Chlorus and his son Constantine) it was relatively mild and largely ended by 306.

**Galerius's Edict of Toleration** (Serdica, April 30, 311), issued shortly before his death from disease, ended the persecution in the East and acknowledged that the policy had failed. The Edict of Milan (313) under Constantine and Licinius formalized the new dispensation.

## Religious consequences
- **The traditor crisis**: The 303 edict to surrender scriptures created the *traditor* problem — those who handed over books to the authorities. In North Africa, post-persecution debates about whether *traditores* could resume ecclesiastical office (especially episcopal ordination) erupted into the **Donatist schism** of the 310s onward — a 100-year fracture in African Christianity.
- **Martyrdom theology codified**: the surviving martyrologies of this period (Eusebius's *Martyrs of Palestine*; many Acta Martyrum) become the foundational hagiographic library.
- **Confessor / martyr theological elevation**: those who suffered acquired enormous post-persecution authority.
- **Constantine's accession**: the political failure of the persecution helped establish the conditions for Constantine's eventual pro-Christian policy.
- **Scriptural canon consolidation**: post-persecution debates about which books had been *worth dying for* sharpened the emerging NT canon.

## Documents in play
- Lactantius, *De mortibus persecutorum* (~+315).
- Eusebius, *Martyrs of Palestine* and *HE* 8.
- Constantine and Licinius, *Edict of Milan* (313).
- (Lost) the original Diocletianic edicts.

## Disputes
- **Diocletian vs. Galerius as principal author**: Lactantius blames Galerius; modern scholarship varies.
- **Date of the original edict**: February 23, 303 — Lactantius's date.
- **East-West variation**: well-documented and substantial.
- **Scale of mortality**: hagiographically inflated; modern estimates more conservative.
