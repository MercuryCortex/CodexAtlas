# Martyrdom of Guru Arjan Dev Ji (1606)

## Who Was Guru Arjan?
**Guru Arjan Dev Ji** (1563–1606) was the **fifth Sikh Guru** and the first to be born into the Sikh tradition (all previous Gurus converted to Nanak's path as adults). His achievements transformed Sikhism from a devotional movement into an organized religious institution:

1. **Compilation of the Adi Granth** (1604) — he gathered and edited the hymns of the first four Gurus plus his own compositions and selected hymns from Hindu and Muslim saints (Kabir, Namdev, Ravidas, Farid, etc.) into the **Adi Granth**, the Sikh scripture. This is the text that later Guru Gobind Singh would install as the perpetual Guru (the *Guru Granth Sahib*), replacing the human guru line. **Guru Arjan's editorial act is the founding of the Sikh canon.**

2. **Construction of the Harmandir Sahib** (the Golden Temple, Amritsar, 1604) — built with four doors facing all four directions (open to all regardless of caste or religion), with its foundation stone laid by a Muslim Sufi saint, Mian Mir — a deliberate statement of Sikh universalism.

3. **Systematization of tithes (*masand* system)** — gave the Sikh community a financial-organizational structure independent of Mughal patronage.

4. **Mass conversions** — under Guru Arjan, Sikhism reached beyond the Punjab into Kabul, Kashmir, and Delhi; significant numbers of Khatri merchants converted.

## The Execution

**Emperor Jahangir** ordered Guru Arjan's arrest in 1606. Jahangir's own memoir (*Tuzuk-i-Jahangiri*) mentions the case, though his description of Guru Arjan's offense varies from later Sikh accounts:

*Jahangir's account*: Guru Arjan's shop of falsehood had attracted too many followers; he was raising sedition; Chandu Shah (a Mughal divan) had a personal grievance against the Guru.

*Sikh tradition*: The immediate trigger was Guru Arjan's blessing of Prince Khusrau — Jahangir's rebellious son, who had been defeated and blinded; giving blessing to a rebel made Guru Arjan complicit in treason. A fine of 200,000 rupees was demanded; Guru Arjan refused to pay it and refused to alter passages in the Adi Granth that Jahangir found offensive.

The execution by torture:
- Guru Arjan was made to sit on a red-hot iron plate (*tatta tawa*)
- Hot sand was poured over his body
- He was placed in boiling water
- After several days of torture, he was permitted to bathe in the Ravi River — and died in the river, or (in some accounts) was drowned, in late May 1606

His recorded words during the torture, from Sikh tradition: "Tera kiya meetha laage" — "Your will is sweet to me, O Lord."

## Immediate Consequences

The martyrdom of Guru Arjan transformed Sikh history with unusual speed. His son, **Guru Hargobind Ji** (the 6th Guru, 1595–1644), made the decisive response:

- He donned **two swords** (temporal and spiritual authority — *miri* and *piri*) — the first armed Sikh Guru
- He built the **Akal Takht** (Throne of the Timeless One) in Amritsar, directly across from the Harmandir Sahib — institutionalizing the Sikh tradition's claim to political as well as spiritual authority
- He **trained a military force** and engaged the Mughals in four battles (1621–1634)

**The pacifist devotional tradition of Guru Nanak begins acquiring a warrior dimension directly as a response to Guru Arjan's martyrdom.** The Khalsa of 1699 (created by Guru Gobind Singh in response to Guru Tegh Bahadur's beheading — see [[event-death-of-guru-tegh-bahadur-1675]]) completes the transformation that Guru Arjan's death begins.

## The Adi Granth as Surviving Witness
The most theologically significant consequence of Guru Arjan's execution: **the Adi Granth survives** — compiled just two years before his death. His own compositions in the *Granth* include the *Sukhmani* (Pearl of Peace), 24 long hymns that constitute one of the great devotional poems in Punjabi literature. The persecutors killed the compiler; the compiled work endured and was elevated to eternal Guru status. The execution made the scripture more authoritative, not less — a structural instance of persecution-as-legitimation at the textual level.
