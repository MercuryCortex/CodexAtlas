# Pyramid of Menkaure (Giza)

## Overview

The **Pyramid of Menkaure** is the smallest of the three Giza pyramids and the last to be built, completed ~2490 BCE. Despite being the smallest — approximately one-tenth the volume of the Great Pyramid — it is in many ways the most interesting architecturally, and it provides the best evidence that the Giza complex was designed as a unified program rather than three independent monuments.

**Dimensions:**
- Base: ~104.6 m per side (less than half of Khufu's base)
- Original height: ~65 m
- Angle: 51°20'25" (nearly identical to the Great Pyramid's 51°50')

**The granite casing:** The lower third of Menkaure's pyramid was cased not in white Tura limestone like the upper two pyramids, but in **red Aswan granite** — the same material used in the King's Chamber above. This was an enormous logistical effort (granite must be brought from Aswan, 800 km south) and gave the lower pyramid a dramatically different appearance. The upper two-thirds was Tura limestone. Whether this was the intended final design or an incomplete casing is debated — the pyramid may have been unfinished at Menkaure's death, with Shepseskaf completing it in the cheaper limestone (visible above the granite zone).

## Three subsidiary pyramids

Menkaure's pyramid is accompanied by **three subsidiary pyramids** to the south — more than any other Giza pyramid. These are sometimes called "Queens' Pyramids" but their occupants are unknown:
- The largest has a cross-shaped subterranean chamber (possibly for a wife)
- One may have been intended for Menkaure himself as a backup/satellite
- All were found empty

The presence of three satellites reinforces Giza's status as a planned necropolis rather than ad hoc royal burials.

## The interior and the coffin question

**Vyse and Perring (1837)** entered the pyramid and found a burial chamber containing:
- A basalt sarcophagus (beautifully carved in the "palace facade" style — one of the finest ancient Egyptian sarcophagi ever found)
- A wooden coffin with a cartouche of Menkaure
- Bone fragments

The basalt sarcophagus was loaded onto a ship for transport to the British Museum. **The ship sank in the Mediterranean in 1838.** The coffin and bones arrived safely — but analysis in the 20th century showed the bones were from the Late or Ptolemaic period (not Old Kingdom), and the coffin may have been a later restoration placed there after the original was disturbed. The original burial, if any, was long gone.

The sarcophagus's "palace facade" decoration is significant: this is the same pattern — panelled alternating recesses and projections imitating reed matting and woven walls — found in the **[[step-pyramid-djoser-saqqara|Step Pyramid's blue faience chambers]]** and on mastaba tomb facades. It survived as an architectural syntax across centuries.

## The Orion Correlation's weakest link

In Bauval's **[[theme-orion-correlation-theory|Orion Correlation Theory]]**, Menkaure's smaller, offset pyramid corresponds to *Mintaka* — the smaller, offset star of Orion's Belt. This is the most visually convincing part of the correlation: the three pyramids are not perfectly collinear, just as the Belt stars are not. The relative sizes correspond to the relative magnitudes.

Critics note that the offset direction is slightly wrong (the pyramids offset to the southwest, the Belt stars to the southwest — actually this does match), and that a three-point correlation with one unusual feature (offset + size difference) is not statistically remarkable.

## Valley temple and mortuary temple

Menkaure's pyramid complex includes:
- A **mortuary temple** (east face; substantially completed)
- A **causeway** leading to a **valley temple** (largely mud-brick; completed by Shepseskaf in a hurry with mud brick instead of stone — evidence of rushed completion after Menkaure's unexpected death)

**The triads:** Found in Menkaure's valley temple were the **Menkaure triads** — a remarkable series of schist sculptures showing Menkaure flanked by Hathor and nome deities. Four have survived; they are among the finest examples of Old Kingdom royal sculpture and now in the Cairo Museum and Museum of Fine Arts Boston.
