# Phoenician Foundation of Gades (c. 1100 BCE)

## Event
Around 1100 BCE, Tyrian Phoenicians established a settlement on a small island near the Atlantic mouth of the Guadalquivir River in southwestern Iberia. This settlement — **Gadir** in Phoenician (*gadir* = "enclosed/walled place"; a common Phoenician colonial toponym also appearing as Agadir, Gadara, Gades) — is **Gades**, modern **Cádiz**.

If the traditional date is correct, Gades is one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities in Western Europe — founded 300 years before the traditional date of Rome (753 BCE), 200 years before the Greeks established their Sicilian colonies, and coterminous with the late Bronze Age cultures of Atlantic Europe. The date is debated — classical sources (Velleius Paterculus) give 1110 BCE; the earliest clear archaeological Phoenician layers are c. 800 BCE — but some form of Phoenician presence in the Atlantic zone from ~1100 BCE is archaeologically plausible given the Huelva finds (*Huelva Hoard*, c. 900 BCE).

## The Temple of Melqart: The World's Western Sanctuary
The defining feature of Gades is the **Temple of [[melqart]]** — the primary western sanctuary of the Phoenician deity Melqart ("King of the City"), tutelary god of Tyre and patron of all Tyrian colonies. Ancient sources describe:

- **Perpetual fire** maintained on the central altar — no women admitted to the inner precinct; no swine allowed (both unique prohibitions in the ancient Mediterranean world)
- **Two bronze columns** flanking the entrance, inscribed with the cost of the temple's construction — Herodotus (II.44) explicitly notes these were not the fabled "Pillars of Heracles" tourists came to see; they were the sacred pillars of the actual cult
- **Annual *egersis*** ("awakening") festival: Melqart's ritual death and reawakening, involving the extinguishing and re-ignition of the sacred fire. Josephus (*Contra Apionem* I.118), citing the Phoenician historian Menander of Ephesus, dates the institution of the *egersis* to the reign of Hiram I of Tyre (c. 970–936 BCE) — Solomon's alliance partner in the Hebrew Bible — making this one of the oldest dateable ritual cycles in Western religious history.

## The Pillars of Heracles and *Ne Plus Ultra*
The Gades temple became entangled with the **"Pillars of Heracles"** mythology — the two boundary-markers (variously identified as the Strait of Gibraltar, the promontories of Calpe and Abyla on either side of the Strait, or the columns of the Gades temple itself) that marked the western limit of the known world. The Latin phrase *ne plus ultra* — "nothing beyond" — encapsulates the theological geography: this is the edge; the divine ocean begins here.

Herodotus, visiting in person, was explicitly told by the Gades priests that their deity was not the Greek hero Heracles but an older eastern god — yet the Greek identification was already established and unstoppable. By the time of Pindar and the Attic tragedians, "beyond the Pillars of Heracles" meant "beyond the world" — an entirely Phoenician-Tartessian geographical construction absorbed wholesale into Greek mythology and subsequently into the Roman, Islamic, and European imaginations.

**Plato's Atlantis** (*Timaeus* and *Critias*, ~360 BCE): the legendary sunken civilization Plato placed "beyond the Pillars of Heracles" in the Atlantic Ocean. The Atlantis narrative encodes the same mythological geography: the world's western edge concealing a lost civilization of extraordinary power. Tartessian archaeology — a wealthy Bronze Age civilization that vanished ~500 BCE, exactly the timeframe Plato's sources would have known as "the recent past" — is the likeliest real-world template. The Atlantis myth may be a Greek-filtered memory of Tartessian collapse.

## Why Gades Is a Cross-Tradition MASSIVE WIN

The Gades foundation and its Melqart temple is the physical-geographic nexus where multiple cross-tradition threads converge:

**1. Phoenician dying-and-rising theology at the world's western edge**
The *egersis* ritual — Melqart's annual death and awakening — is a dying-and-rising-god ceremony performed at the specific geographic point where the sun "dies" each evening: the western Atlantic shore. The solar theology encoded in Melqart's cult is not metaphorical — it is performed at the exact location where the sun descends into the ocean. The Gades temple is where ancient Mediterranean theology met its astronomical object. See [[dying-rising-god]].

**2. The Heracles myth transfer — Hebrew and Greek mythology from Phoenician source**
The Greek hero myths set in the far west (the **tenth labor**: Geryon's cattle on an island near Gades; the **eleventh labor**: golden apples of the Hesperides at the world's western edge; the **Pillars of Heracles**) are almost certainly Phoenician-Melqart mythology absorbed into the Greek heroic tradition through the Phoenician colonial presence. The Phocaean Greeks who made contact with Tartessian king [[arganthonius]] in the 7th-6th century BCE were trading in a landscape saturated with Melqart mythology — and brought it home to Athens as Heracles's western adventures.

The **Samson narrative** (Judges 13–16) is the Hebrew Bible's parallel absorption: Cross (1973) argued Samson (*Shimshon* = "sun-man") is a Hebraicized Melqart myth — the solar hero who kills lions, whose power is in his hair (solar rays), who pulls down the two pillars of the temple at his death. The two pillars of Dagon's temple ↔ the two columns of Melqart's temple at Gades. See [[melqart]].

**3. The geographical imagination of the Western tradition**
Every subsequent tradition that inherited the Greek geographical-mythological framework inherited the Phoenician conception of the western Atlantic edge as the world's sacred limit:
- Islamic geography: *al-Maghrib* ("the West"), Surat al-Kahf's description of Dhul-Qarnayn reaching the place of the sunset
- Medieval Christian geography: the *mappa mundi* with the known world ending at the western ocean
- The Camino de Santiago: Christian pilgrims walk to Cabo Finisterre — to the same Atlantic edge where Melqart was worshipped 2,000 years before Saint James
- The Age of Discoveries: Portuguese and Spanish navigators sail *past* the *ne plus ultra* — and the Portuguese Discoveries are launched from Gades's hinterland, from Lisbon and the Atlantic coast that Melqart's sailors first mapped

The civilization that would eventually sail past the Pillars of Heracles and circumnavigate the globe was born in the sacred geography Melqart established here.
