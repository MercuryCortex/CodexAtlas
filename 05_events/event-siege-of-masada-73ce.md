# Siege of Masada (73-74 CE)

The terminus event of the [[event-first-jewish-roman-war-66-73|First Jewish-Roman War]]. Roman governor Lucius Flavius Silva besieged the Sicarii community at the Masada plateau-fortress (originally Herodian; held by the Sicarii from 66 onward) with the Legio X Fretensis. After a siege lasting several months — culminating in a massive ramp the Romans constructed against the western face — the fortress was breached in spring 74 CE (the conventional 73 CE date follows Josephus but some scholars argue for 74). According to Josephus (*Jewish War* 7.8-9, the sole primary source), the Sicarii commander Eleazar ben Ya'ir convinced the community (~960 persons, including women and children) to commit collective suicide rather than face Roman enslavement; only two women and five children survived hidden in cisterns and gave the account to Josephus's informants.

## MASSIVE-WIN — sacred-defeat / mass-suicide cross-tradition pattern

Masada anchors one pole of the cross-tradition pattern of *sacred defeat as theological-political founding event*. The pattern: a small force resists overwhelming military power, chooses collective death over submission, and the defeat becomes a founding-myth of the surviving community's identity. See [[sacred-defeat]] for the cross-tradition theme.

Parallel instances:
- **[[event-numantia-133bce|Numantia, 133 BCE]]** — Iberian Celtic resistance to Roman siege; the Numantines also chose collective death rather than surrender; founding myth of Spanish/Iberian identity
- **Karbala 680** ([[event-karbala-680]]) — Husayn's small force at Karbala chooses martyrdom over allegiance to Yazid; structural pattern, founding event of Shi'i piety
- **Various First Nations final-stand events** in colonial-American history

The structural pattern: *defeat that becomes more theologically-politically generative than victory would have been*. The vault catalogs these as cross-tradition cases of how communities construct identity from foundational catastrophe rather than triumph.

## Historiographic discipline

Ben-Yehuda 1995 documents that the modern Israeli-national-mythological reception of Masada (the IDF oath at Masada; "Masada shall not fall again") substantially over-reads Josephus's narrative. The Sicarii were a specific revolutionary-Zealot faction, not "the Jews" generically; Josephus describes them critically; the mass-suicide narrative has been questioned by some scholars on archaeological + textual grounds. The Codex Atlas treats:
- The Roman siege + Sicarii defeat as historically secure
- The mass-suicide narrative as Josephus-attested but historiographically contested
- The modern national-mythological reception as a distinct topic requiring separate scholarly framework
