# Göbekli Tepe (c. 9,500–8,000 BCE)

## What happened
Göbekli Tepe ("Potbelly Hill"), a tell-mound on the Germuş range of southeastern Anatolia ~15 km northeast of Şanlıurfa, was identified as a significant prehistoric site by Harald Hauptmann in the early 1990s and excavated by Klaus Schmidt of the German Archaeological Institute (DAI) from 1995 until his death in 2014; excavation continues under Lee Clare. The site consists of a sequence of large stone enclosures featuring T-shaped megalithic pillars — the largest yet excavated are ~5.5 metres tall, 7–10 tonnes each — arranged in circular and rectangular layouts, often with elaborately carved relief sculptures of foxes, snakes, scorpions, vultures, wild boars, aurochs, cranes, and other fauna, plus abstract symbols and stylized human-arm-and-hand reliefs that strongly imply the T-pillars themselves are anthropomorphic (the "T" being a stylized head-and-shoulders).

Construction dates (calibrated radiocarbon on charcoal from fills and on plaster) span ~9,500–8,000 BCE — the Pre-Pottery Neolithic A through early Pre-Pottery Neolithic B. The site was deliberately backfilled and abandoned by ~8,000 BCE.

The site is in the region where einkorn wheat was independently domesticated (genetic-origin studies, Heun et al. 1997) — the domestication of cereal agriculture occurs *contemporaneously* with and *in the same landscape as* Göbekli Tepe construction. The temporal and spatial proximity of monument-building and cereal-domestication has been the basis for the much-discussed Schmidt thesis that *ritual organization preceded and may have driven the Neolithic transition* — i.e., that gathering for monument-building created the labor-and-feasting demand that motivated cereal cultivation, rather than the conventional sequence of agriculture-first then social-complexity.

Inscribed UNESCO World Heritage 2018.

## Religious consequences
- **If Schmidt's "temple" interpretation is correct**, Göbekli Tepe is the world's earliest known monumental ritual complex by a margin of ~6,000 years (predating Sumerian temples by ~5,000 years and Stonehenge by ~5,500 years). The T-pillars-as-anthropomorphic-beings reading (relief arms, hands, belts, loincloths, occasional necklaces) supports their interpretation as either deities, ancestors, or supernatural agents — the iconography does not specify which.
- **The animal reliefs** (predators, scavengers, dangerous fauna predominantly) iconographically resemble the dangerous-fauna emphasis of Chauvet rather than the food-fauna emphasis of Lascaux. Whether this is a deep cultural pattern or a coincidence of preservation is contested.
- **The feasting-and-building hypothesis** (Dietrich et al. 2012 *Antiquity*) — large stone troughs at the site contain calcium-oxalate residues consistent with cereal-mash fermentation, possibly the earliest material evidence for systematic beer production. The argument is that periodic gatherings for monument-building were lubricated by communal feasting (meat and beer) which drove both the social organization and the agricultural-intensification trajectory.
- **The Banning critique and the Clare reassessment.** Both shift the religious-consequences claim: rather than "pre-agricultural hunter-gatherers built a temple," the current best reading is closer to "an early-Neolithic community (transitioning to and from cultivation) built mixed-use buildings that included monumental T-pillars among other features, in a longer and more variable construction sequence than Schmidt initially proposed." The religious-significance claim is therefore softened from "world's first temple" to "world's earliest dated monumental T-pillar tradition with strong ritual elements" — which is itself sufficient to mark Göbekli Tepe as a major node in deep-prehistory religious-cognitive evolution.

## Documents in play
- None (pre-writing; the T-pillar reliefs and abstract signs are pre-writing iconography, not script).

## Disputes
- **"Temple vs. domestic": the central interpretive question.** Schmidt 2006/2012 argued the enclosures were dedicated cult buildings with no contemporaneous domestic occupation — a "pure ritual site." Banning 2011 (*Current Anthropology*) argues, point by point, that this reading is not securely demonstrated: alleged absence of domestic debris reflects sampling, plant-processing tools and bone accumulations were present, and many Pre-Pottery Neolithic sites in the region show mixed ritual-and-domestic use in single buildings (e.g., Çayönü, Nevalı Çori). Banning's reframe: Göbekli Tepe is the largest and earliest of a regional class of *mixed-use elevated communal-and-ritual buildings*, not a sui generis "first temple." Clare et al. 2024 (*Cambridge Archaeological Journal*) substantially endorses Banning's mixed-use position from inside the current excavation team. **Atlas position: present both, weight current consensus toward mixed-use; do not retain the marketing-line "world's first temple."**
- **Construction sequence.** Schmidt proposed a clear chronological sequence from large/early to small/late enclosures (Layer III → II → I). Current excavation has substantially complicated this — the enclosures appear to have been built and remodeled over longer periods, with some contemporaneity, and the "older/newer" categorical distinction is less clear.
- **The "ritual-drove-the-Neolithic" thesis** (Cauvin 2000 in a related form; Schmidt's specific Göbekli Tepe argument) is provocative but not securely established. The temporal-and-spatial correlation between Göbekli Tepe construction and cereal-domestication in the Karacadağ-Şanlıurfa region is real; the causal direction (ritual gatherings driving agriculture, or agricultural intensification enabling ritual gatherings, or both as joint products of a deeper process) is undetermined.
- **Pseudo-archaeological accretions.** Göbekli Tepe has attracted significant pseudoarchaeological attention (Graham Hancock's *Magicians of the Gods* 2015 frames it as evidence of a lost antediluvian civilization). The site is well-established by mainstream Pre-Pottery Neolithic A archaeology and does not require any extra-archaeological hypothesis. See [[phase-7-036-hancock-fingerprints-of-the-gods]] for the Hancock framing as a *separate phenomenon* in modern alternative-history; Göbekli Tepe itself is a documented Pre-Pottery Neolithic site.

## Connections
- [[event-natufian-burial-traditions-c-12500-9500-bce]] — *shared-milieu*; Late Natufian and earliest Göbekli Tepe layers are roughly contemporary in the terminal-Pleistocene / earliest-Holocene Southwest Asian cultural horizon.
- [[event-jericho-pre-pottery-neolithic-c-9000-bce]] — *shared-milieu*; both are PPNA-PPNB sites of monumental construction (Jericho's tower; Göbekli Tepe's enclosures) in the same broad timeframe and culture-area.
- [[event-catalhoyuk-c-7400-6200-bce]] — *parallel-motif* (Neolithic Anatolian ritual built environment; later, but in the same broad cultural-geographic continuum).
- **Mesopotamian temple architecture** (Eridu temple sequence; Sumerian ziggurats; see [[phase-1-001-kesh-temple-hymn]], [[phase-1-018-gudea-cylinders]]) — *parallel-motif*. The gap is ~5,000 years. Schmidt 2006 suggested architectural-structural continuity in southeastern Turkey / northern Mesopotamia (sunken-floor ritual enclosure → temple cella). Banning 2011 and most current scholarship are skeptical of any direct typological-transmission claim across such a gap. The link is best described as *thematic recurrence* of the monumental ritual enclosure in the same broad Mesopotamian-Anatolian culture-region across deep time, **not** a documented continuous tradition.
