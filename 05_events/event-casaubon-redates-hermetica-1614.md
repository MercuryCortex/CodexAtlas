# Casaubon Redates the Hermetica (1614)

## What happened
In 1614, the great Calvinist classical philologist Isaac Casaubon, working in London under the patronage of James I, published *De rebus sacris et ecclesiasticis exercitationes XVI* as a critical reply to Cardinal Baronius's massive Catholic *Annales Ecclesiastici*. Within the first exercitatio, sections X–XX, Casaubon demonstrated on philological grounds that the texts of the *Corpus Hermeticum* — which Renaissance scholars from Ficino onward had treated as pre-Mosaic revelations of Hermes Trismegistus, ancestor of all pagan-prophet wisdom — were in fact Hellenistic Greek compositions of the early Christian era. His arguments included: (1) the Hermetica use vocabulary, theological categories, and stylistic features distinctively of imperial-period Greek; (2) they show knowledge of the Septuagint Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible (3rd c. BCE), which cannot have existed in a pre-Mosaic age; (3) they show knowledge of Christian-era philosophical vocabulary; (4) the historical Hermes Trismegistus framework is a Hellenistic-Egyptian Hellenized-Thoth construction, not a historical pre-Mosaic prophet. The argument was effectively decisive among careful philologists.

## Religious consequences
- **End of the Renaissance Hermetic project as it had stood from Ficino to Bruno.** The Hermetica continued to be read and esteemed but as Hellenistic philosophical-religious documents, not as primordial revelation. The *prisca theologia* historiography collapsed.
- **Rear-guard resistance.** Athanasius Kircher's *Oedipus Aegyptiacus* (1652–1654) is the most ambitious post-Casaubon attempt to maintain the prisca-theologia framework; Yates (1964) treats it as the late-Renaissance rear-guard. Kircher acknowledged Casaubon's dating in scholarly notes but continued to use Hermes as authoritative.
- **Foundation of modern historical-critical method.** Casaubon's redating is one of the foundational acts of modern critical philology. Grafton (2011) argues that the patristic dimension of Casaubon's scholarship — the development of historical-critical method in dialogue with patristic literature — has been underestimated.
- **Shift in esoteric self-understanding.** Subsequent Western esotericism (the post-Casaubon Rosicrucian movement, 18th-c. Freemasonry, 19th-c. occultism) had to relocate its claimed antiquity onto different (and equally fragile) foundations — Egyptian temple wisdom, Druidic-Celtic survival, Atlantis, etc.
- **The Yates framing.** Frances Yates' *Giordano Bruno and the Hermetic Tradition* (1964) made Casaubon's 1614 redating the demarcation event between the Renaissance Hermetic moment and modern historical-critical study; this framing has been refined but remains a useful periodization.

## Documents in play
- *De rebus sacris et ecclesiasticis exercitationes XVI* (Casaubon, 1614).
- Baronius's *Annales Ecclesiastici* (1588–1607), the work Casaubon was replying to.
- Kircher's *Oedipus Aegyptiacus* (1652–1654) as principal rear-guard response.
- The Hermetica themselves, now recontextualized.

## Disputes
- **Did Casaubon end the Hermetic tradition?** Yates emphasized the rupture; current scholarship (Hanegraaff, Copenhaver) notes substantial continuities in 17th-c. and later Hermetic-Kabbalist literature, which simply did not let Casaubon's philology overturn the tradition's authority. The end of the tradition's *scholarly* legitimacy is real; the end of its esoteric-internal authority is later and more gradual.
- **Was Casaubon's argument truly decisive philologically?** Yes; subsequent classical-philological consensus dates the Hermetica firmly to the 1st–3rd centuries CE.
