# Hagia Sophia of Constantinople (532–537 and after)

## Identity
The Hagia Sophia ("Holy Wisdom" — Greek *Ἁγία Σοφία*; Turkish *Ayasofya*; Latin *Sancta Sophia*) is the great domed church-then-mosque-then-museum-then-mosque at the heart of Constantinople / Istanbul, built under Emperor [[justinian-i]] between 532 and 537 CE on the site of two earlier basilicas (the Constantian foundation of c. 360, burnt in 404, and the Theodosian rebuild of 415, burnt during the Nika riots of January 532). It is one of the architectural turning-points in world history — the engineering achievement of placing a 31-metre-diameter dome on pendentives over a near-square central space, a technical solution that defines the subsequent Byzantine, Orthodox, and Ottoman ecclesiastical-architectural tradition.

## Construction
Justinian commissioned Anthemius of Tralles and Isidore of Miletus — both mathematician-architects, not conventional master-builders — to design the replacement immediately after the Nika riots destroyed the Theodosian basilica. Construction proceeded extraordinarily rapidly: foundation begun February 532; consecration 27 December 537. Procopius (*De Aedificiis* 1.1) records Justinian's reported exclamation on entering the completed building — "Solomon, I have surpassed you" — frequently cited as the building's foundational anecdote, though it is reported only in late sources (Diegesis on Hagia Sophia, c. 9th c.) and is probably legendary rather than directly historical.

The structural innovation was the central dome supported on four pendentives over four massive piers, with eastern and western semi-domes extending the central space into a vast longitudinal nave. The first dome collapsed on 7 May 558 after an earthquake; Isidore the Younger (nephew of Isidore of Miletus) rebuilt it 558–562 with a steeper profile and a buttressing-rib system that survives substantially intact. Subsequent partial collapses and repairs occurred in 989 (eastern arch and dome), 1346 (eastern part); each repair preserves the basic Justinianic plan.

## Religious significance
**Christian-cathedral phase (537–1453).** Hagia Sophia was the cathedral of the Ecumenical Patriarchate of Constantinople, the principal church of the Eastern Roman / Byzantine Empire, and the site of the imperial coronations from at least the 7th century onward. It was the venue for the proclamation of doctrinal decisions (Iconoclast controversy 726–843 was fought through this church; the Triumph of Orthodoxy in 843 was proclaimed here; see [[event-iconoclast-controversy-726-843]]). The mosaic programme — figural Christ Pantokrator, Theotokos, archangels, emperors — is some of the finest surviving Byzantine monumental art. The building was briefly converted into a Latin-rite cathedral during the [[event-fourth-crusade-1204]] Latin occupation (1204–1261), during which significant pillaging occurred.

**Ottoman-mosque phase (1453–1934).** Following the [[event-fall-of-constantinople-1453]] (29 May 1453), Mehmed II entered the city and immediately converted Hagia Sophia into a mosque (Ayasofya Camii). Christian figural mosaics were progressively plastered over (some preserved beneath the plaster — many survived to be recovered in the 20th century); a *mihrab* was installed in the apse oriented toward Mecca (at a slight angle to the building's original eastern axis); four minarets were added (one by Mehmed II, one by Bayezid II, two by Selim II under the architect Sinan). The building exercised significant typological influence on the great imperial Ottoman mosque architecture (Süleymaniye, Sultan Ahmed, Selimiye — all explicitly cite Hagia Sophia's central-dome-on-pendentives scheme).

**Museum phase (1934–2020).** Under the secularist program of Mustafa Kemal Atatürk and the early Turkish Republic, Hagia Sophia was deconsecrated as a mosque and reopened as the Ayasofya Museum on 1 February 1935. The 1934 cabinet decree (CABINET DECREE NO. 2/1589) framed the conversion as a recognition of the building's universal cultural-historical value transcending any single confessional claim. The museum period was the era of the great mosaic-recovery and restoration campaigns — Whittemore's Byzantine Institute work in the 1930s–40s recovered figural mosaics including the great deesis in the south gallery.

**Reconversion to mosque (2020–present).** On 10 July 2020 the Turkish Council of State annulled the 1934 cabinet decree, and the building was reconsecrated as a mosque on 24 July 2020 under President Erdoğan. The Christian figural mosaics are covered with cloth or screens during Islamic prayer times. The reconversion was internationally controversial: criticized by UNESCO, the Greek government, the Russian Orthodox Church, the U.S. State Department, and the World Council of Churches as a unilateral confessional appropriation of a UNESCO World Heritage site; defended by the Turkish government as the restoration of a sovereign decision over a national monument.

## Disputes
- **The 2020 reconversion.** This is contested scholarly and political territory and **must not be smoothed over**. The Christian-cathedral tradition (537–1453, with the Latin interlude 1204–1261), the Ottoman-mosque tradition (1453–1934), and the museum tradition (1934–2020) are three distinct historical-religious configurations of the same building, each with its own theological and political meaning. The 2020 reconversion is sometimes presented as a "return to the building's correct status" (the Turkish official line) or as "an erasure of the museum period as a shared-heritage moment" (the international-protest line); the historical reality is that the building has been Christian, Latin-Christian, Sunni-Muslim, secular-museum, and Sunni-Muslim again, and any framing that flattens this sequence into a single continuous identity is theologically motivated rather than historical. **Atlas position: catalogue all four phases with their dates; the 1934–2020 museum phase is its own historical moment, not a bracketed pause.**
- **The "Solomon, I have surpassed you" anecdote.** Reported only in the Diegesis (Narratio de Aedificatione Templi Sanctae Sophiae), a 9th-century legendary compilation, not in Procopius. Almost certainly later legendary embellishment, but cited so frequently it has effectively become part of the historical narrative; we mark it as legendary.
- **Architectural dependency on earlier monuments.** Some scholars (e.g., Rasch 1985) trace Hagia Sophia's dome-on-pendentives scheme to earlier Roman engineering (the Pantheon's coffered concrete dome, Maxentius's basilica) and to small-scale antecedents like SS. Sergius and Bacchus (built by the same team 527–536, with a centralized octagonal-domed plan that is often read as the laboratory for Hagia Sophia). The Justinianic synthesis is original in scale and configuration but builds on a continuous late-antique architectural-engineering tradition.
