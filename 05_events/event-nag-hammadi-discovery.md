# Nag Hammadi Discovery

## What happened
In December 1945, Muhammad Ali al-Samman and his brothers, digging for fertilizer (*sabakh*) at the foot of the Jabal al-Tarif cliff near the village of al-Qasr (close to Nag Hammadi in Upper Egypt), unearthed a sealed earthenware jar containing thirteen leather-bound Coptic codices — approximately 1,150 papyrus pages of texts including some fifty-two tractates. The codices had been buried, on the standard reading, by Pachomian monks ~370 CE following Athanasius's Festal Letter 39 (367) ordering the destruction of non-canonical books, or by some related Coptic Egyptian Christian community resisting the consolidating orthodox canon.

The texts entered the antiquities market over the following years; Codex I (the Jung Codex) was purchased for the C.G. Jung Institute in Zurich in 1952; the others gradually came under the control of the Egyptian Coptic Museum in Cairo. UNESCO sponsored the photographic facsimile edition (1972–1984), and an international team under James Robinson produced critical editions and English translations.

## Religious consequences
The discovery transformed three fields:
- *Gnostic studies* — first direct access to primary Gnostic texts (previously known almost entirely via heresiological reports). The four manuscripts of the Apocryphon of John, the Valentinian texts (Gospel of Truth, Gospel of Philip, Tripartite Tractate), the Sethian corpus (Hypostasis of the Archons, On the Origin of the World, Trimorphic Protennoia, Allogenes, Zostrianos), and the Thomasine materials (Gospel of Thomas, Book of Thomas the Contender) reshaped understanding of late-antique Christianity.
- *Early Christianity broadly* — the Gospel of Thomas's discovery raised the question of pre-Synoptic sayings sources; the diversity of second-century Christianity became visible as never before.
- *Hermetic studies* — the [[phase-4-013-discourse-on-the-eighth-and-ninth|Discourse on the Eighth and Ninth]] (NHC VI,6), the [[phase-4-078-prayer-of-thanksgiving-nhc-vi-7|Hermetic Prayer of Thanksgiving]] (NHC VI,7) and the [[phase-4-079-coptic-asclepius-nhc-vi-8|Coptic Asclepius]] (NHC VI,8) — the three Hermetica bundled at the end of NHC VI — demonstrated the practical-ritual-liturgical side of Hermetic religion that the previously-known Greek Corpus + Latin Asclepius did not. Mahé's three-decade engagement with this Coptic-Hermetic material (1978–1982 critical editions; with the Armenian Definitions added in 1982) constitutes the second great 20th-c. addition to the Hermetic corpus.

## Documents in play
Almost the entire corpus of [[02_documents/_phase-4-late-antiquity]] documents linked above (Gospel of Thomas, Apocryphon of John, Gospel of Truth, Gospel of Philip, Hypostasis of the Archons, On the Origin of the World, Trimorphic Protennoia, Sophia of Jesus Christ, Discourse on the Eighth and Ninth) entered modern knowledge through this find.

## Disputes
- **Burial date and reason.** The Pachomian-monastery hypothesis (Robinson, Doresse) connects the burial to Athanasius's 367 Festal Letter. Recent reassessment (Goehring, Lundhaug, Jenott) revisits the connection: were the codices monastic or a non-monastic Christian community's library? The new consensus is more cautious about the Pachomian identification.
- **Exact circumstances of discovery.** The story of fratricidal blood-feud (Muhammad Ali's brothers killed their father's killer and ate his heart, then refused to dig with the police nearby) is colorful but partly reconstructed by later interviewers (especially Robinson 1977); the precise chronology of the 1945–46 events has been refined by later scholarship.

## Later scholarship (Phase 7 reception)

The cultural and scholarly legibility of the 1945 find unfolded across the second half of the 20th century in distinct phases:

- **1945–1958: hidden / partial-access period.** Codex I (the Jung Codex) purchased for the C.G. Jung Institute, Zurich, in 1952. Doresse 1958 (*Les livres secrets des gnostiques d'Égypte*) is the first book-length account but with limited textual access.
- **1958–1977: scholar-only period.** Hans Jonas's [[phase-7-013-gnostic-religion]] (1958) provided the dominant scholarly framework but relied largely on pre-1945 heresiological sources. UNESCO photographic facsimile edition (1972–1984). James M. Robinson directed the international Coptic-edition project.
- **1977: Robinson translation.** *The Nag Hammadi Library in English* (HarperSanFrancisco) opened the texts to non-specialists.
- **1979: Pagels popularization.** [[phase-7-014-gnostic-gospels]] by [[elaine-pagels]] made the find a mainstream Western cultural reference for the first time.
- **1980s–present: critical reception.** Michael Allen Williams's *Rethinking "Gnosticism"* (1996) and [[karen-king]]'s *What Is Gnosticism?* (2003) dismantled the Jonas-Pagels unified-Gnostic-Religion framing; David Brakke's *The Gnostics* (2010) consolidated the post-Williams scholarly position around the more limited Sethian category. The CGRR/Critical Gnostic Reception Recension and the SBL Coptic Hymnody Group continue specialist work.
- **2008+: Lundhaug-Jenott reassessment.** Hugo Lundhaug and Lance Jenott's *The Monastic Origins of the Nag Hammadi Codices* (2015) challenges the Pachomian-monastery hypothesis on philological-codicological grounds; the burial-context question remains live.
