# Red Pyramid of Sneferu (Dahshur)

## The first true smooth-sided pyramid

The **Red Pyramid** (~2600–2590 BCE) is the **first successfully completed true smooth-sided pyramid** in history — the culmination of Sneferu's architectural experiments at Meidum and Dahshur. After the Bent Pyramid's mid-construction angle change, Sneferu's builders began the Red Pyramid at the conservative angle of **43°22'** — the same angle as the *upper* portion of the Bent Pyramid — and maintained it from base to apex. It worked.

The pyramid is named after its distinctive **reddish limestone** core stones, visible today because the original white Tura limestone casing was stripped in the Middle Ages (the same fate as the Giza pyramids). At construction, it would have appeared as a gleaming white monument identical in form to the Great Pyramid — the direct prototype.

**Dimensions:**
- Base: ~220 m per side (the third-largest pyramid in Egypt)
- Height: ~105 m
- Angle: 43°22' (shallower than the Great Pyramid's 51°50' — making the Red Pyramid lower and broader)

## Three internal chambers

The Red Pyramid contains **three successive corbelled chambers** — all accessible from a single north-face entrance:

1. **First Antechamber:** Corbelled ceiling; approximately 8.3 m wide × 4.2 m deep × 12.3 m high. Empty.

2. **Second Antechamber:** Similar corbelled ceiling; same height (~12 m). Empty. Unusual feature: positioned transversely (running east-west rather than north-south), creating an L-shaped progression through the chambers.

3. **Burial Chamber:** Larger chamber with a corbelled ceiling approximately **15 metres** high — the tallest corbelled vault in any Egyptian pyramid. Approximately 4.2 m wide × 8.6 m long. The pharaoh's burial would have been here. Found completely empty and ransacked.

**Corbelled vault technology:** The corbelling technique — each successive course of stone overhangs the one below — is the same method used in the **[[grand-gallery-great-pyramid|Grand Gallery]]**'s extraordinary 8.74 m vault. The Red Pyramid's burial chamber, at 15 m high, demonstrates that Sneferu's builders had mastered this technique at large scale before the Great Pyramid was designed.

## The nasal bones: evidence of actual burial

In 1950, Ahmed Fakhry found **fragmentary human bones** in the burial chamber, including what appeared to be nasal bones and skull fragments. These have been tentatively identified as Sneferu's remains, making the Red Pyramid one of very few large Egyptian pyramids with any human remains in the burial chamber (the Great Pyramid's King's Chamber had none).

This finding complicates alternative theories that the great pyramids were never intended for burial — at least one pyramid builder was actually buried in his pyramid.

## The olfactory problem

The Red Pyramid has a distinctive and well-documented problem: it **smells strongly of ammonia** from bat guano accumulated over millennia. Modern visitors find the descent through the 63-metre long entrance passage followed by the low-ceilinged antechambers to be physically unpleasant. This practical detail — invisible in the archaeological literature — is a reminder that most ancient monuments now exist in states radically different from their original condition.

## The Sneferu → Khufu succession

The Red Pyramid's successful completion proved the design template that Khufu's architect **Hemiunu** would refine into the Great Pyramid:
- True smooth sides at a consistent angle: confirmed workable
- Corbelled chamber technology: proven at large scale
- North-face descending entrance: standardized
- Internal chamber succession: established as the theological sequence

The one thing Hemiunu changed most dramatically was the angle — from Sneferu's 43° to the Great Pyramid's 51°50'. This steeper angle, combined with the vast scale increase, required all the engineering innovations (the ascending passage, the Grand Gallery, the five relieving chambers) that make the Great Pyramid architecturally unique.
