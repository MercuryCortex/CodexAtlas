# The Trojan War

## Identity

The Trojan War is the master-event of the Greek heroic age: a ten-year siege of Troy (Ilios) in the Troad by a coalition of Achaean kings, launched to recover Helen, wife of Menelaus of Sparta, after her abduction (or elopement) with the Trojan prince Paris — a guest in Menelaus's house when he took her. The war ends with the city's destruction by the stratagem of the wooden horse. For the Greeks this was not literature but the anchor of their past: genealogies of historical families ran back to its heroes, Herodotus (2.145) counted it roughly 800 years before his own time, Thucydides (1.9-11) treated it as sober early history whose scale tradition had inflated, and the chronographic tradition fixed the fall of the city at 1184/3 BCE (Eratosthenes' computation). In the Atlas it is typed as a **legendary event**: its setting (~1200 BCE, the end of the Aegean Bronze Age) is precise in tradition, while its historicity is an open dispute conducted between a real excavated city at Hisarlik and a poem written down some 450 years after the horizon it describes.

## In the Greek corpus

No single Greek text narrates the whole war. The [[phase-2-008-homeric-epics|Iliad]] covers about fifty days of the tenth year — the wrath of Achilles, the death of Patroclus, the killing of Hector — and ends before the sack; the Odyssey looks back at the horse and the sack through the songs of Demodocus (Od. 8.492-520) and Menelaus's reminiscences (Od. 4.271-289). The rest of the story was carried by the **Epic Cycle**, now lost and known chiefly through Proclus's prose summaries and fragments (West 2003): the *Cypria* (the causes — Eris's apple, the Judgment of Paris, the abduction), the *Aithiopis* (Penthesileia, Memnon, the death of Achilles), the *Little Iliad* and *Ilioupersis* (the horse and the sack), the *Nostoi* (the returns), and the *Telegony*. [[phase-2-009-hesiod-theogony-works-and-days|Hesiod]] (Works and Days 156-173) makes the war one of the two catastrophes — with Thebes — in which Zeus destroyed the race of heroes, fought "for Helen's sake." Attic tragedy relentlessly mined the cycle (Trojan Women, Agamemnon, Philoctetes), and Herodotus preserves a deviant Egyptian logos (2.112-120) in which Helen never reached Troy at all — the Greeks fought ten years for a phantom, a rationalising variant going back to Stesichorus's Palinode.

A detail with cross-tradition weight: the *Cypria* opened by giving the war a cosmic cause. Earth, oppressed by the weight of an overabundant humanity, appealed for relief, and Zeus devised the Trojan War to lighten her burden (Cypria fr. 1 West). The war is thus, in its fullest Greek telling, a divinely engineered demographic catastrophe — a motif with Near Eastern and Indic relatives (see below).

## Historicity: Hisarlik and the Hittite dossier

The site of Troy was identified with the mound of **Hisarlik** in the northwest Troad by Frank Calvert and made world-famous by Heinrich Schliemann's excavations (1870s-1880s), continued by Wilhelm Dörpfeld (who championed the great walled citadel of **Troy VI**) and Carl Blegen's Cincinnati expedition (1932-1938), which favoured **Troy VIIa** — a cramped, crowded settlement destroyed by fire c. 1210-1180 BCE, with human remains in the streets and sling bullets in the destruction layer — as "Homer's Troy." Manfred **Korfmann**'s Tübingen excavations (1988-2005) transformed the picture, arguing from magnetometer survey and soundings for a substantial fortified lower town making Late Bronze Age Troy a real Anatolian city of consequence; a Luwian biconvex seal (found 1995) is the site's only Bronze Age writing. Korfmann's reconstruction provoked the sharp German "Troia-Debatte" of 2001-2002, with Frank **Kolb** charging that the lower town and its commercial importance were overdrawn (Kolb 2004).

The second evidentiary track is Hittite. A kingdom **Wilusa** — the name is widely equated with Greek *(W)ilios*, the older form of Ilios — appears in Hittite records as a vassal in northwest Anatolia; its king **Alaksandu** (Alaksandu Treaty, CTH 76, c. 1280 BCE) bears a name long compared to Alexandros, Homer's other name for Paris. The **Tawagalawa Letter** (CTH 181, mid-13th century) mentions a past conflict between the Hittite king and the king of **Ahhiyawa** — widely equated with the Achaeans — concerning Wilusa. The equations (defended in Latacz 2004; texts in Beckman, Bryce & Cline 2011) are onomastic and geographic, are widely though not universally accepted, and even if right prove only that Mycenaean Greeks and Hittites contended over a place probably called Wilusa: **no Bronze Age text records a Trojan War**, and the destroyer of Troy VIIa is archaeologically unidentified.

## Cross-tradition significance

Three distinct cross-tradition wires run through this event, of different kinds:

1. **The xenia wire (moral instance, not transmission).** The war is the Greek tradition's largest-scale statement of the sacred-hospitality law: Paris violated the guest-bond in Menelaus's house, and a city died for it. Menelaus calls on Zeus to avenge the guest-crime when he faces Paris (Il. 3.351-354), and invokes Zeus Xenios by name at Il. 13.623-625. The [[moral-sacred-hospitality-cross-tradition|sacred-hospitality hub]] already frames the war as a xenia-violation writ large; this node is that hub's negative exemplum — hospitality enforced by catastrophe. This is an instance-of-a-moral relation within one tradition, not a claim of transmission.

2. **The plan of Zeus and the overburdened Earth (transmission argued).** The *Cypria*'s opening — war devised by the high god to thin an overabundant humanity — closely parallels the Mesopotamian [[phase-1-006-atrahasis|Atrahasis]] epic, where the gods answer human overpopulation and noise with plague and flood. West (1997, pp. 480-482) argues the Greek motif is a West Asiatic borrowing of the Bronze-Age Levantine-Mesopotamian stream that fed early Greek epic. The same structure — Earth personified, begging to be relieved of her burden, answered by a great annihilating war — frames the Kurukshetra war in the [[phase-2-017-mahabharata-ramayana-oral-layers|Mahabharata]] (Adiparvan). Whether the Greek-Indic agreement is common Indo-European inheritance, parallel drift from a shared Near Eastern motif, or independent convergence is debated (West 2007 surveys the comparanda); the honest position is that the Greek-Mesopotamian link is the best-evidenced leg, and the Indic leg is a genuine but contested parallel.

3. **The war-over-an-abducted-wife plot.** The Helen plot — a wife taken across a boundary, a coalition war to recover her, the destruction of the taker's city — recurs in the Ramayana (Sita and Ravana). Comparative scholarship (West 2007) treats this as a possible inherited Indo-European narrative frame, against the null hypothesis that abduction-and-retrieval is simply a universal story-shape. No transmission claim is secure; the parallel is structural.

Downstream, the war's reception is itself a cross-tradition event: Rome adopted Trojan descent through [[aeneas]], turning the defeated city into imperial sacred prehistory (Erskine 2001), and medieval European dynasties from the Franks to the British followed suit with their own Trojan genealogies.

## Disputes

**Historicity** is the standing dispute, and this node deliberately encodes it rather than resolving it. The maximalist position (Korfmann; Latacz 2004) holds that Troy VI/VIIa was a significant Anatolian city, that Wilusa = Ilios and Ahhiyawa = Achaeans, and that the epic preserves the memory of real 13th-century Mycenaean-Anatolian conflict. The skeptical position descends from Finley's 1964 essay — the Homeric picture is an 8th-century poetic construct, its Bronze Age memory-content unprovable and probably slight — with Kolb (2004) pressing the archaeological case that Late Bronze Age Troy was too modest to be the city of the legend. A broad middle holds that *some* conflict-horizon at Wilusa/Troy is plausible, while "the Trojan War" as a single ten-year pan-Achaean siege is an epic condensation that cannot be confirmed. Separately, the **date** is layered: the traditional 1184/3 BCE is a Hellenistic computation, not evidence; the archaeological destruction of VIIa (c. 1210-1180) and the Hittite Wilusa notices (c. 1280-1220) are real but need not be the same event as each other, let alone as Homer's war.
