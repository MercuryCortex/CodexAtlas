# Australian Aboriginal Stolen Generations

## What happened
Across approximately six decades (~1905–1969, with different state-by-state legislation and varying intensities), Australian state and federal governments — implemented through Aboriginal Protection Boards and frequently delegated to Christian mission institutions — forcibly removed an estimated 10%–33% of Aboriginal children from their families. The children were placed in mission-run dormitories, foster homes, government institutions, or fostered/adopted by non-Indigenous families. The principal legislative instruments included the various state Aborigines Protection Acts (NSW 1909; SA 1911; NT 1911; Vic 1915; WA 1936; Qld 1939). The policy operated under explicit assimilation-and-elimination logic: "breeding out" of Aboriginal identity over generations. The *Bringing Them Home* report of 1997 documented the practice systematically; Prime Minister Kevin Rudd issued the National Apology on February 13, 2008.

## Religious consequences
The Stolen Generations directly targeted *religious-cultural transmission*:
- *Forced linguistic suppression.* Mission and institution discipline regularly punished use of Aboriginal languages; many languages experienced catastrophic intergenerational interruption. Religious vocabulary and conceptual structure cannot be transmitted without language.
- *Forced ceremonial suppression.* Removed children could not be initiated; could not learn country-specific Dreaming knowledge; could not transmit the religious tradition forward when they had children of their own. Across many nations, the Stolen Generations directly caused intergenerational interruption of religious-ceremonial transmission.
- *Christianization.* Forced exposure to Christianity (often Anglican or Catholic but also Lutheran, Methodist, etc.) replaced Aboriginal religious frameworks for many removed children. Some surviving Aboriginal Christian traditions descend from this history; the relationship between forced Christianization and authentic religious engagement is complex.
- *Long aftermath in 20th- and 21st-c. revitalization.* From the 1970s onward, language-revitalization and ceremonial-revitalization movements have attempted to recover what the policy disrupted, with variable success — some languages and ceremonies have been recovered substantially; others lost in the relevant generation are permanently lost. The Stolen Generations is therefore a religious-cultural disruption whose consequences continue.
- *Symbolic-political religious dimension.* The 1997 *Bringing Them Home* report's terminology of "genocide" (drawing on UN Genocide Convention article II(e), "forcibly transferring children of the group to another group") generated intense national-political controversy that continues. The religious-cultural-dimensional argument for genocide is precisely that the policy targeted religious-cultural transmission.

## Disputes
- **"Genocide" terminology.** Defenders of the term cite UN Genocide Convention article II(e) — "forcibly transferring children of the group to another group" — explicitly written into the Convention partly with attention to colonial child-removal practices, with Indigenous Australian implementation in mind. Critics argue assimilation policy was not intended-extermination and the term is inappropriately strong. Manne 2001 and Reynolds 2001 are major academic interventions on each side.
- **Numerical scope.** Estimates of children removed range from ~10% to ~33% of Aboriginal children of the relevant generations; the variation reflects differing definitions, state-by-state record quality, and time-period boundaries.
- **End date.** The policy did not end on a single date; child-removal practices declined unevenly through the 1960s and were formally ended in different states in different years; some scholars argue continued over-representation of Aboriginal children in present-day child-welfare removal continues a structural pattern.
