# Visigothic Kingdom of Hispania (418–711 CE)

## Overview
The Visigoths (one of the Germanic tribes that absorbed and succeeded the Western Roman Empire) ruled the Iberian Peninsula for 293 years — longer than Portugal's post-Reconquista history to date. In 418 CE, after their catastrophic sack of Rome (410 CE) and subsequent settlement in Gaul, the Visigoths were granted Hispania as a *foederati* territory; after losing Gaul to the Franks (507 CE, Battle of Vouillé), their kingdom became entirely Iberian. The Visigothic Kingdom is the political-cultural bridge between Roman Hispania and Islamic al-Andalus — the 300-year period that:
1. Synthesized Roman-Latin and Germanic Visigothic culture into what became Iberian identity
2. Transformed Iberia from an Arian Christian kingdom to a Nicene Catholic one
3. Persecuted and forcibly converted the Jewish community in ways that directly shaped the subsequent Islamic conquest
4. Produced **Isidore of Seville** — one of the most important scholars of the early medieval period

## The Arian-to-Catholic Conversion (589 CE)
The Visigoths practiced **Arianism** — the Christian doctrine condemned at the Council of Nicaea (325 CE) that held that Christ was the "firstborn" of God but not coequal with God. This put the Visigothic ruling class in permanent theological conflict with the Roman-Iberian Catholic population they ruled (who followed Nicene orthodoxy), with the Pope, and with the Frankish Catholic kingdom to the north.

**Recared I** (king 586–601) converted to Nicene Catholicism and renounced Arianism at the **Third Council of Toledo** (589 CE) — the most consequential religious act in Iberian history before the Reconquista. His conversion:
- United the Visigothic ruling class and the Roman-Iberian Catholic majority in a single church
- Made the Bishop of Toledo the primate of Iberia (a position that gave Toledo its extraordinary religious authority in the medieval period)
- Created the ideological framework for a Christian Iberian civilization that would survive the Islamic conquest and eventually produce the Reconquista states

The **Council of Toledo** (the series of councils held there from 400 to 702 CE — eighteen councils in total) became the primary organ of Iberian church governance and, eventually, of political legitimacy. The tradition of Toledan conciliarism was the structural ancestor of the Reconquista kingdoms' religio-political fusion.

## The Jewish Community and the Forced Conversions
The Visigothic Kingdom contains the first documented European state-sponsored forced conversion of Jews — creating a precedent and a population that would have profound consequences:

- **Sisebut** (king 612–621 CE): issued an edict ordering all Jews in the kingdom to convert to Christianity or leave. The forced conversions created a class of insincere converts (*conversos*) whose sincerity was immediately doubted and who maintained Jewish practice in private. This is the **first historical appearance** of the converso pattern that would define Spanish and Portuguese religious history for the next millennium.

- **Council of Toledo IV** (633 CE): decreed that while forced conversions were improper in principle, those who had already been converted should be held to their baptismal obligations — effectively locking in the forced converts. The children of converts were to be given over to monasteries to prevent Jewish home practice.

- The Jewish community's relationship to the Visigothic kingdom was therefore: marginalized, periodically persecuted, episodically subjected to forced conversion, with their internal practice supervised and suppressed. When the Islamic armies arrived in 711 CE, some accounts (and later polemics) describe Jewish communities welcoming the invaders or even facilitating their entry into certain cities — not from love of Islam but from relief from Visigothic persecution.

**The Long Converso Arc**: Visigothic forced conversions (613 CE) → Islamic conquest (711 CE, experienced partly as relief) → *dhimmi* status under Islamic rule (subordinate but protected) → Reconquista Christian rule → Spanish forced conversion/expulsion (1492) → Portuguese forced conversion (1496) → New Christian crypto-Judaism → Lurianic Kabbalah (emerged from the expelled community, many from Iberian converso families). The Visigothic period is the **first link in a chain** that runs continuously to the Safed Kabbalists of the 16th century. See [[event-portuguese-forced-conversion-1496]], [[isaac-luria]].

## Isidore of Seville and the Transmission of Knowledge
**Isidore of Seville** (c. 560–636 CE) — Archbishop of Seville under the Visigothic kingdom — is one of the most important intellectuals in the history of Western civilization, not for his originality but for his **encyclopedic transmission function**. His *Etymologiae* (also called *Origines* — "Origins") is a 20-volume encyclopedia of all classical knowledge, organized by etymology (the "meaning" of each subject revealed in its word-origin). It covers:
- The seven liberal arts (grammar, rhetoric, logic; arithmetic, geometry, music, astronomy)
- Theology, law, medicine
- Natural history, geography, architecture
- The pagan gods and mythology (crucially: Isidore preserves descriptions of classical religion precisely by framing them as historical errors to be avoided)

The *Etymologiae* was one of the most copied texts in medieval Europe — over 1,000 manuscripts survive. It was the primary transmission vehicle for what remained of classical learning in Western Europe during the "Dark Ages" between the fall of Rome and the Carolingian Renaissance. Without Isidore:
- Much of what medieval Europe knew about classical rhetoric, geometry, music theory, and medicine would have been lost
- The mythological and religious information (which Isidore preserves while condemning) would be reduced
- The encyclopedic genre that shaped medieval intellectual culture would lack its founding exemplar

Isidore is the **Iberian node in the classical-to-medieval transmission chain** — a parallel to the Arabic translation movement at Baghdad. The Western transmission of classical knowledge into medieval Europe went partly through Iberia (Isidore's Seville, 636 CE) just as the Eastern transmission went through Baghdad (Bayt al-Hikmah, 8th-9th c. CE). Both transmissions eventually converged in Toledo (the [[event-toledo-translations-1085-1150|Toledo translation movement]], 1085+), which translated from Arabic and also built on Isidore's Latin foundation.

## The Visigothic Collapse and the Islamic Opening
The Visigothic kingdom collapsed with startling speed: in 711 CE, Tariq ibn Ziyad's forces crossed the strait, defeated and killed king Roderic at Guadalete, and within seven years controlled most of the peninsula. The swiftness has three causes:

1. **Internal political crisis**: The Visigothic ruling class was divided by a succession dispute; one faction (sons of the previous king Wittiza) actually allied with the Islamic forces against Roderic. The conquest was partly a Visigothic civil war with Islamic participation.
2. **The Jewish community's grievances**: The persecuted converso and openly Jewish communities had no attachment to the Visigothic political order. Some accounts describe Jewish communities helping open city gates.
3. **The Roman-Iberian population's ambivalence**: After 300 years of Visigothic rule, the Romanized Catholic Iberian majority had absorbed the Visigoths culturally but had not fully merged with their aristocracy. The Islamic conquerors offered lighter initial tax burdens and did not immediately interfere with Christian practice.

The Visigothic kingdom is therefore a cautionary case in the fragility of religious-ethnic state projects built on coerced conformity rather than genuine community.
