# Jesuit Mission to Ethiopia and its Expulsion (1557–1632)

## What happened
Between approximately 1557 and 1632, the **Society of Jesus** conducted one of its most ambitious and ultimately one of its few unambiguous failures: a 75-year mission to convert the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church to Roman Catholicism. The mission unfolded in distinct phases:

1. **Bermudes interlude (1555–1565)** — João Bermudes, a Portuguese who had accompanied the 1520s embassy, claimed (probably falsely) the title of Patriarch of Ethiopia; expelled 1559.
2. **First Jesuit phase (1557–1597)** — Andrés de Oviedo arrives as Patriarch; resisted by Ethiopian church authorities; the mission stagnates.
3. **Páez phase (1603–1622)** — **Pedro Páez** (1564–1622), the most successful Jesuit missionary in Ethiopian history, arrives at the court of Emperor Susenyos. Páez was an exceptional linguist (fluent in Ge'ez, Amharic, and Oromo), an accomplished scholar (his *História da Etiópia*, completed by 1622, is one of the most thorough early-modern European treatments of Ethiopia), and a skilled diplomat. He gradually persuaded Susenyos toward Catholic theological positions.
4. **Susenyos's conversion (1622)** — Emperor Susenyos publicly converted to Roman Catholicism and decreed national conversion. This triggered civil war: a majority of Ethiopian clergy and laity rejected the conversion; female monastics including **Walatta Petros** led organized resistance; rebellions of major regions broke out.
5. **Mendes phase (1625–1632)** — After Páez's death (1622), **Afonso Mendes** arrived as Catholic Patriarch with broader-aggressive program: re-baptism of Tewahedo Christians; replacement of Ge'ez liturgy with Latin; suppression of Saturday-Sabbath; suppression of broader-OT-canonical scriptures; replacement of Tewahedo monastic and clerical discipline with Roman-Catholic norms. The cultural-religious heavy-handedness intensified resistance.
6. **Fasilides's expulsion (1632)** — In 1632 Susenyos abdicated in favor of his son Fasilides amid escalating civil war. Fasilides expelled all Jesuits (1632–1634), restored Tewahedo Christianity as state religion, and *closed Ethiopia to all European missionaries for two centuries*. The expulsion is the most decisive rejection of European Christian missionary influence by any African or Asian state in early-modern history.

## Religious consequences

1. **Tewahedo identity sharpens.** The Jesuit-mission encounter forced Tewahedo Ethiopia to articulate its distinctive theological-canonical-liturgical identity in explicit polemical contrast to Roman Catholicism. [[galawdewos-emperor]]'s *Confessio Claudii* (1555) and the 17th-c. anti-Jesuit polemical literature constitute the most systematic Tewahedo self-definition produced in any period.

2. **The Saturday-Sabbath issue stabilizes.** The Mendes-period suppression attempts solidified Ethiopian commitment to dual Sabbath observance.

3. **Ethiopia closes to European missionaries for two centuries (1632–1830s).** The successful expulsion produced a regime of religious isolationism that lasted until the Protestant and Catholic missions of the 1830s onward.

4. **Walatta Petros and female monastic resistance.** The 1632 expulsion was substantially driven by mass-popular and especially female-monastic resistance. The *Gadla Walatta Petros* (1672) — see [[phase-7-040-walatta-petros-hagiography]] — is the central textual witness.

5. **Páez's *História*** — one of the most important early-modern European descriptions of Ethiopia, including the first European description of the source of the Blue Nile (Páez observed it at Lake Tana, 1618), the first detailed European treatment of Ethiopian Christianity, and the first European Ge'ez and Amharic linguistic-scholarly work.

6. **Modern ecumenical implications.** The Catholic-Tewahedo encounter is a load-bearing case study in modern ecumenical theology and post-colonial religious studies. The Vatican II-era and post-1990-Christological-convergence engagements take this 17th-c. failure as constitutive prior context.

## Documents in play
- **Produced:** Páez's *História da Etiópia* (1622, published 2011 critical edition); Mendes's correspondence and treatises; the anti-Jesuit Ethiopian polemical literature; the *Gadla Walatta Petros* (1672, retrospective).
- **Affected:** Tewahedo theological self-definition.

## Disputes
- **Susenyos's personal vs. political conversion motives.** Susenyos was theologically engaged (Páez's correspondence shows substantive theological exchange) but also operated in a political environment where Catholic conversion was tied to Portuguese military-diplomatic alliance against rising regional pressures. The mix is debated.
- **Counterfactual: could a less heavy-handed Mendes have succeeded?** Páez's gradualist approach was working; Mendes's rapid program of Catholic normalization triggered the resistance. Whether a continued Páez-style mission would eventually have produced lasting Catholic conversion is impossible to determine; the rejection had deep roots beyond particular missionary tactics.
