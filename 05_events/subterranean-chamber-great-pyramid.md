# Subterranean Chamber (Great Pyramid of Khufu)

## Physical description

The **Subterranean Chamber** is the lowest of the Great Pyramid's three known chambers, cut entirely into the **living bedrock** beneath the pyramid rather than built with stone blocks. It is reached via the **Descending Passage** — the original entrance passage (entered through the original north-face entrance, now above the current tourist entry) — which drops at a steep angle of ~26.5° for approximately 105 m.

**Dimensions:**
- Length: ~14 m (irregular; the chamber is roughly rectangular but rough-hewn)
- Width: ~8.3 m
- Height: ~3.5 m (varies; the ceiling is rough)
- Unfinished: the floor is rough bedrock with a deep blind shaft ("The Pit") cut 16 m into the bedrock from the south end

**The Pit:** A square shaft ~2 m × 2 m cut straight down into the bedrock from the chamber floor, reaching 16 m deep. Unfinished. No connecting passage at the bottom. No finds of any kind.

## The great interpretive question: abandoned plan or deliberate theology?

The Subterranean Chamber is **unfinished** — tool marks are visible on the walls, the Pit's shaft bottom shows abandoned chiseling, and the floor is never smoothed. This has generated two radically different interpretations:

**Orthodox position (construction-change theory):** The original plan was to place the burial chamber in the bedrock — the conventional Egyptian pyramid design. Work began on the subterranean chamber and the Pit while the pyramid was being built above ground. Then Khufu (or his architects) changed the plan and relocated the burial chamber upward — first to the "Queen's Chamber" level, then ultimately to the granite "King's Chamber." The subterranean chamber was abandoned mid-construction.

This is supported by: the architectural sequence (the Subterranean Chamber's Descending Passage predates the Ascending Passage, which is a later addition that had to punch through the Descending Passage's ceiling), and parallels with Sneferu's Bent Pyramid (which shows two similar mid-construction design changes).

**Alternative interpretation (intentional unfinished state):** A minority position argues the rough, unfinished appearance is deliberate — the Subterranean Chamber represents the primordial chaos before creation (*Nun* in Egyptian cosmology), the unformed bedrock from which the pyramid — and the pharaoh's resurrection — emerges. The Pit may be a symbolic passage to the Duat (underworld) rather than an abandoned engineering shaft.

In **[[theme-pyramid-as-resurrection-machine]]** spatial theology: the Subterranean Chamber is the chthonic/Osirian level (the underworld, associated with Osiris as the dead king); the Queen's Chamber is the intermediate station; the King's Chamber is the solar transformation. The vertical axis of the pyramid is a cosmological diagram.

## The Well Shaft (Grotto)

From the bottom of the Grand Gallery, a narrow irregular shaft descends through the pyramid's masonry and into the bedrock, connecting back down to the Descending Passage near the Subterranean Chamber entrance. It is sometimes called the "Well Shaft" or "Escape Shaft."

**Purpose:** Completely unknown. Hypotheses:
- **Robber's tunnel:** Cut by ancient thieves to bypass the granite blocking plugs in the Ascending Passage (the plugs can be seen in place in the Ascending Passage from above; robbers apparently tunneled around them via this shaft)
- **Workmen's exit:** After the burial and the plugging of the Ascending Passage, workmen sealed inside needed an exit route; the Well Shaft was their escape path down to the Descending Passage
- **Ritual feature:** The shaft contains an irregular natural cavity ("the Grotto") — a void in the masonry that seems to have been incorporated into the shaft rather than bypassed

The Grotto's existence — a natural fissure in the rock that the builders routed the shaft through — is itself a minor mystery, since the builders could have gone around it.

## The Descending Passage and original entrance

The Descending Passage (original entrance) was sealed with polished limestone casing blocks that blended perfectly into the pyramid's exterior — it was invisible from outside. The entrance was known to ancient tourists (Herodotus mentions a secret entrance but couldn't find it). Al-Ma'mun's workmen (9th century CE) tunneled a completely different entry passage through the masonry — the tunnel visitors use today — before finally locating the Descending Passage from inside.

The original north-face entrance is now visible as an opening approximately 17 m above the base on the north face.
