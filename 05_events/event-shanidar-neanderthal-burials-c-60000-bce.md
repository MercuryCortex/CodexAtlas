# Shanidar Cave Neanderthal Burials (c. 75,000–45,000 BCE)

## What happened
Shanidar Cave, in the Baradost Mountains of Iraqi Kurdistan, was excavated between 1951 and 1960 by Ralph and Rose Solecki of Columbia University, with re-investigation by a Cambridge–Liverpool team led by Graeme Barker from 2014 onward. The site preserved the remains of at least ten Neanderthal (*Homo neanderthalensis*) individuals — adults, adolescents, and infants — designated Shanidar 1 through Shanidar 10, plus the recently recovered articulated upper-body remains Shanidar Z (Pomeroy et al. 2020). Several individuals show evidence of deliberate placement in shallow depressions within a recurring rear-cave cluster. Shanidar 4 (an adult male buried c. 60,000 BCE in conventional dating) was associated by Arlette Leroi-Gourhan's 1968–1975 palynological analysis with anomalous concentrations of pollen from at least eight species of flowering plants (yarrow, cornflower, ragwort, grape hyacinth, joint pine, hollyhock, woody horsetail, and St Barnaby's thistle), several of which have ethnobotanical medicinal uses — leading Solecki to popularize the site as the location of a Neanderthal "flower burial." Shanidar 1 ("Nandy") is independently notable as a severely injured adult who survived for years after major trauma (right-arm amputation, healed cranial wounds, blindness in the left eye), suggesting prolonged group care.

## Religious consequences
- **If the flower-burial interpretation holds**, Shanidar 4 represents the earliest plausible evidence for symbolic-ritual treatment of the dead anywhere in the human-lineage record, predating *Homo sapiens* behavioral modernity. Even on the most skeptical reading, the repeated clustering of bodies in one cave-corner across millennia is itself evidence of *recurrent mortuary practice* — not random disposal.
- The Shanidar 1 evidence for sustained care of a severely impaired adult is consistent with social bonds, gerontocratic respect, or simply group cohesion — but does not by itself entail religious belief.
- The site has been load-bearing in the broader argument (Solecki, later Trinkaus, Hayden, Spikins) that Neanderthals were behaviorally and cognitively equivalent to early *Homo sapiens* in capacity for symbolic culture — an argument now increasingly accepted on independent evidence (Iberian cave-art dating of red hand-stencils to >64,000 BCE, Hoffmann et al. 2018 *Science*; engraved bone at Einhornhöhle; pigment use), though the *specific* Shanidar floral-offering claim remains contested.

## Documents in play
- No texts (~58,000–55,000 years before the earliest writing systems).
- The "documents" here are archaeological strata, skeletal remains, pollen samples, and excavation reports.

## Disputes
- **The flower-burial interpretation is the central disputed claim.** Leroi-Gourhan 1975 attributed the pollen concentrations to deliberate floral deposition by mourners. Sommer 1999 (*Cambridge Archaeological Journal*) argued the pollen clusters are better explained as fodder caches and burrow-floor accumulations of the Persian jird (*Meriones persicus*), a rodent whose burrows were documented in the excavation. Pettitt 2011 treats the floral claim as unproven. Pomeroy et al. 2020, while supportive of intentional burial at Shanidar more broadly, are cautious about the specific floral-offering reading and note that the original pollen samples and stratigraphic associations cannot now be fully reverified. **Current scholarly position**: deliberate burial at Shanidar is well-supported; the flower-offering specifically is *possible but not established*.
- **Whether Neanderthal "burial" is mortuary ritual or simple body disposal** has been debated since Gargett's 1989 critique. The Shanidar cluster, plus comparable evidence from La Chapelle-aux-Saints, La Ferrassie, Kebara, and Dederiyeh, has shifted the consensus toward intentional burial; but whether that intent was *religious* (in the sense of belief in afterlife or supernatural agency) or pragmatic (sanitary disposal, predator defense, grief response) remains an interpretive question that the archaeological record by itself cannot fully resolve.
- **Date range.** The Shanidar Middle Palaeolithic layer (Layer D) is dated approximately 75,000–45,000 BCE; individual burials are not all of the same date. The YAML range reflects the layer; the Shanidar 4 burial is conventionally placed ~60,000 BCE.

## Connections
- [[event-natufian-burial-traditions-c-12500-9500-bce]] — *parallel-motif* (intramural burial, body-clustering), with the explicit caveat that ~50,000 years of intervening practice are largely undocumented. No direct continuity claim is warranted.
- Later Mesopotamian funerary practice (e.g., the Royal Tombs of Ur, ~-2600; see [[phase-1-018-gudea-cylinders]] context) — *parallel-motif* only. The vast intervening time-depth precludes direct-transmission claims; what survives is the recurrence of cave/chamber burial in the same broad region (the Zagros piedmont and adjacent Mesopotamian plain), which is itself a noteworthy if non-causal pattern.
