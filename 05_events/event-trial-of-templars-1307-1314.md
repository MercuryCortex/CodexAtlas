# Trial of the Templars

## What happened
On the morning of **Friday, 13 October 1307**, royal sergeants in France simultaneously arrested every available member of the [[tradition-knights-templar|Order of the Temple]] — perhaps 5,000+ men, including Grand Master [[jacques-de-molay]], who had returned from Cyprus only months before. The arrests were authorized by sealed orders prepared by [[philip-iv-of-france|King Philip IV]] and his jurist Guillaume de Nogaret, in coordination with the Dominican Inquisitor of France, Guillaume de Paris.

The charges, prepared in advance, included:
- Heresy (specifically: denial of Christ, spitting / trampling / urinating on the cross at initiation)
- Idolatry (veneration of an image variously called *Baphomet*, a head, a cat — see [[baphomet-controversy]])
- Sodomy (sanctioned within the Order)
- Obscene initiation kisses (mouth, navel, base of spine)
- Receiving absolution from the Order's own (lay) officers rather than ordained priests

Under torture, the Paris arrestees gave large-scale confessions matching the prepared formulary; Molay himself confessed (though he later retracted). Pope [[pope-clement-v]] — caught between his obligation to defend the Order (a papal-direct-allegiance institution) and his political dependence on Philip IV — initially attempted to take control of the process. At **Chinon in August 1308**, Clement V's cardinal delegates examined Molay and four other senior officers; the cardinals' report (the [[phase-5-038-chinon-parchment-1308|*Chinon Parchment*]], Avignon Reg. 308, rediscovered in the Vatican Secret Archive in 2001 by Barbara Frale) records that the officers were **conditionally absolved** of the heresy charge once they affirmed essential Catholic faith.

But Philip pressed on. The **Council of Vienne** ([[event-council-of-vienne-1311-1312|1311–1312]]) was convened to formally judge the Order; Clement, unable to obtain a conciliar conviction, suppressed the Order administratively without conviction (*Vox in excelso*, 22 March 1312). Templar assets were transferred to the [[tradition-hospitallers|Hospitallers]] (*Ad providam*, 2 May 1312). Individual Templars were dispensed to monasteries or, in some cases, returned to lay life. Molay and Geoffroi de Charney — who retracted their confessions on the public scaffold of Notre-Dame de Paris — were burned as relapsed heretics on a small island in the Seine on **18 March 1314**.

## Religious consequences
- **Effective end of the [[tradition-knights-templar|Knights Templar]].** Iberian partial-survival (the Portuguese **Order of Christ**, the Aragonese **Order of Montesa**) preserves Templar holdings and traditions in those regions.
- **Major precedent for royal-vs-papal jurisdictional contest.** Philip's coercion of Clement V is a key data-point in the late-medieval *Avignon papacy* dynamic and the subsequent Babylonian Captivity of the Church (~1309–1377) leading toward the Western Schism.
- **The institutional Inquisition as instrument of royal politics.** Guillaume de Paris's collaboration with Nogaret demonstrates the deployment of the inquisitorial apparatus for crown purposes — a precedent with long subsequent history.
- **Mass-mythological fuel.** The Templar trial generates one of the longest-running legendary streams in Western religious history: from the medieval *libelles* through 18th-c. Masonic Strict Observance through 19th-c. occult-Templarism (Lévi, Hammer-Purgstall) through 20th-c. Holy-Blood-Holy-Grail / Priory of Sion / *Da Vinci Code* mass culture. See [[templar-gnostic-transmission-hypothesis]] and [[baphomet-controversy]].

## Documents in play
- The trial transcripts proper — the *Processus Contra Templarios*, Frale 2007 critical edition. Note the **divergence by jurisdiction**: French confessions match the prepared formulary; English, Aragonese, German, and Cypriot proceedings largely *do not*, suggesting French confessions are an artifact of French royal torture rather than evidence of Templar doctrine.
- The Chinon Parchment — [[phase-5-038-chinon-parchment-1308]] — papal-cardinal absolution of the senior officers (1308); misfiled in the Vatican Archive for nearly seven centuries until Frale's 2001 identification.
- Clement V's bulls — *Pastoralis praeeminentiae* (1307), *Faciens misericordiam* (1308), *Vox in excelso* (1312), *Ad providam* (1312), *Considerantes dudum* (1312).

## Disputes
- **Did the Templars practice the rites confessed under torture?** Consensus position post-Barber 2006 / Frale 2007: no, with one important qualification — *some* element of the admission rite (renunciation-test, kiss of obedience) may have had a defensible internal-discipline rationale (capture-resistance training, hierarchical-submission ritual) that hostile inquisitors interpreted as heretical denial. The "Baphomet head" charge has no defensible historical basis (Barber 2006 ch. 7; see [[baphomet-controversy]]).
- **Was Clement V complicit or coerced?** Mixed reading. Demurger 2007 emphasizes coercion; Barber 2006 emphasizes a degree of papal political maneuvering for face-saving rather than substantive defense of the Order. The Chinon Parchment (Frale 2004) substantially complicates the older view that Clement V simply abandoned the Templars — he absolved the senior officers privately but lacked the political weight to defend them publicly.
- **What did Philip get out of it?** Templar treasure (substantial), Templar lands (mostly transferred to Hospitallers, but with extensive French royal taxation), and the political demonstration of royal supremacy over a papal-direct institution. Read with the broader Philip IV financial-coercion pattern (the 1306 expulsion of Jews; the 1311 expulsion of Lombards; the chronic debasement of French coinage).
