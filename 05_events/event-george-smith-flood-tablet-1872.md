# George Smith Reads the Babylonian Flood Tablet

## What happened
On 3 December 1872, George Smith (a self-taught British Museum cuneiformist) delivered a lecture to the Society of Biblical Archaeology in London announcing that he had identified, on a cuneiform tablet from the Library of Ashurbanipal (excavated 1849–1854 by Layard and Rassam), a Babylonian flood narrative remarkably parallel to Genesis 6–9. The tablet was what is now known as Tablet XI of the Standard Babylonian Gilgamesh epic; the flood narrative is Utnapishtim's account to Gilgamesh. The lecture was widely reported (the *Daily Telegraph* funded a follow-up Smith expedition to Nineveh to find missing fragments; Smith recovered additional tablets). Smith published *The Chaldean Account of Genesis* in 1876 — the first popular presentation of the Mesopotamian-Hebrew religious-literary parallels.

## Religious consequences
The 1872 announcement was a public sensation comparable in 19th-century religious-cultural impact to the Darwinian controversies of 1859–1870s. For the first time it was demonstrably (not merely speculatively) the case that the Hebrew Bible's foundational narratives had Mesopotamian precursors. The subsequent *Babel und Bibel* controversy (Friedrich Delitzsch's lectures 1902–1904, supported and then problematized by Kaiser Wilhelm II) institutionalized the comparative-religion question in German Protestant biblical scholarship. The Codex Atlas vault's Phase 1 ↔ Phase 2 transmission discussion (Atrahasis-Genesis flood, Enuma Elish-Genesis cosmogony) descends directly from this discovery.

## Documents in play
The Babylonian flood narrative (now identified as Gilgamesh Tablet XI's Utnapishtim story, drawn from the older Atrahasis composition) entered modern knowledge through this 1872 identification. Atrahasis itself was substantially recovered later (Lambert-Millard 1969).

## Disputes
- **Direct dependence vs. shared milieu**: Genesis-Gilgamesh / Genesis-Atrahasis relationship — direct literary dependence (older Wellhausen-school view), shared Near Eastern grammar (Smith 2010), or polemical-demythologization (Levenson, Hartman).
