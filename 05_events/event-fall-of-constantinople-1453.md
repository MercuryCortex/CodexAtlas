# Fall of Constantinople (1453)

## What happened
Sultan Mehmed II's 53-day siege (April–May 1453) of Constantinople, defended by the last Byzantine Emperor Constantine XI Palaiologos with ~7000 troops against an Ottoman army of perhaps 80,000+ and a substantial fleet. The city's walls were breached on 29 May 1453 by sustained bombardment from Ottoman heavy artillery (notably the "Basilica" gun built by the Hungarian Orban) and by assault. Constantine XI died in the fighting; the city was sacked for three days per Ottoman custom; Mehmed entered the Hagia Sophia and immediately converted it to a mosque. Constantinople became Istanbul and the Ottoman capital. The fall ended over a thousand years of continuous Byzantine Roman imperial rule.

## Religious consequences
- **End of Byzantine Christianity as a state religion.** Constantinople's Patriarchate continued under Ottoman *millet* administration but no longer as an imperial-ecclesiastical institution.
- **Greek manuscript diaspora.** Byzantine scholars (Bessarion, Chrysoloras' students, the Lascaris brothers) had been migrating west since the late 14th c. (Council of Florence 1438–1439 was an important transmission moment); the 1453 fall accelerated this. The post-1453 westward flow of Greek manuscripts and Greek-reading scholars feeds the Italian Renaissance's discovery of ancient Greek philosophy, theology, and literature. Without this manuscript diaspora, Ficino's 1463 Hermetica translation, his 1484 complete Latin Plato, and his 1492 Latin Plotinus are unimaginable.
- **Symbolic shift.** The fall of the "Second Rome" was interpreted variously: in Latin Christendom as God's judgment on Eastern Orthodox schism; in Russian Orthodoxy as the transfer of imperial sacrality to Moscow (the "Third Rome" doctrine of Filofei of Pskov, 16th c.); in Islamic theology as the divinely-promised conquest the Prophet had foretold.
- **Ottoman religious establishment.** Mehmed II reorganized the Ottoman religious-political structure to incorporate the conquered Byzantine populations under the *millet* system, in which non-Muslim religious communities (Greek Orthodox, Armenian Orthodox, Jewish, later others) had legal autonomy under their own religious heads.

## Documents in play
- Greek manuscripts brought west 1453+: the *Corpus Hermeticum* (which reached Cosimo de' Medici ~1460 via Leonardo da Pistoia), substantial Plato and Plotinus manuscripts, Greek patristic and Byzantine works.
- Russian "Third Rome" texts (16th c.) — Filofei of Pskov's letters.
- Sultan Mehmed's Greek-Arabic-Persian intellectual program at the Ottoman court — significant in its own right and bringing Italian humanists (Gentile Bellini at Mehmed's invitation 1479) into contact with Ottoman patronage.

## Disputes
- **Was the manuscript diaspora *caused* by 1453?** Wilson (1992) and others have shown that the major flow began earlier (1438+ Council of Florence; ongoing late-14th c. migration) and that 1453 accelerated rather than initiated the phenomenon.
- **Symbolic vs. material consequence.** Some historiography emphasizes the *symbolic* rupture (end of the Christian empire); others emphasize the material continuities (Ottoman governance of the Greek population through the existing ecclesiastical structures).
