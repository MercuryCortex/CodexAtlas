# Sabbatean Apostasy (1666)

## What happened
In May 1665, the unstable charismatic mystic Shabbatai Tzvi of Smyrna was proclaimed Messiah by Nathan of Gaza, a young and brilliant Lurianic-Kabbalist; the proclamation ignited a worldwide Jewish messianic movement that swept across the Ottoman, Italian, Dutch, German, and Polish-Lithuanian Jewish communities through 1665–1666. Entire communities sold their property in expectation of imminent migration to the Holy Land; the year 1666 had been calculated by multiple Christian and Jewish chronologies as apocalyptic. In February 1666 Shabbatai entered Ottoman territory in fulfillment of his messianic role; the Ottoman authorities, alarmed by the agitation, arrested him at Gallipoli and brought him before Sultan Mehmed IV at Adrianople in September 1666. Under the threat of execution, Shabbatai chose conversion to Islam, becoming *Aziz Mehmed Efendi* and accepting an Ottoman court pension. The apostasy shattered the public messianic movement; large numbers of believers reverted to normative Judaism in shock and shame; the anti-Sabbatean rabbis (Jacob Sasportas, others) intensified their denunciations. But a substantial believing minority — theologized by Nathan as having witnessed the messiah's deepest redemptive descent into the realm of evil — sustained Sabbateanism for generations.

## Religious consequences
- **Most consequential messianic crisis in post-classical Jewish history.** Scholem's framing: the Sabbatean explosion-and-collapse opened a permanent fissure in Jewish religious life, with covert Sabbatean theology persisting into Hasidism (contested), Frankism (1755+), and Donmeh crypto-Jewish-Muslim communities in Salonica.
- **Theological transformation of Lurianic Kabbalah.** Nathan's *Drush ha-Tanninim* and related writings produced the heterodox-Sabbatean transformation of Lurianic categories: the messianic descent into the *kelippot* (realm of evil shells) as itself the work of *tikkun*. "Redemption through sin" (Scholem's phrase) became the doctrinal logic.
- **Hardening of rabbinic anti-Sabbatean orthodoxy.** The 17th- and 18th-c. rabbinic struggle against Sabbatean infiltration (Jacob Emden's denunciations of Jonathan Eybeschütz, etc.) reshaped European Jewish institutional life.
- **Donmeh.** Several hundred Sabbatean families in Salonica formally converted to Islam following Shabbatai (1683 onward in waves), forming the crypto-Jewish-Muslim Donmeh community that persisted into the 20th century (substantially relocated to Istanbul after the 1923 Greek-Turkish population exchange).
- **Long shadow.** The Sabbatean experience is read by Scholem and others as a key step on the way to Jewish modernization — antinomian energies that, once liberated from the Sabbatean framework, fed into Enlightenment-era Jewish modernization.

## Documents in play
- Nathan of Gaza's theological corpus (*Drush ha-Tanninim*, *Iggeret Magen Avraham*, etc.).
- Sasportas's *Zizath Novel Zvi* (1666–1667), the principal contemporary anti-Sabbatean polemic.
- Vast pamphlet literature (Hebrew, Ladino, Yiddish) generated by the crisis.
- Ottoman court records.

## Disputes
- **Did Sabbateanism feed Hasidism?** Scholem argued yes (covert Sabbatean reservoir flowing into early Hasidism); Mendel Piekarz and others have contested the directness of the link. The current consensus accepts substantial Sabbatean-Hasidic crosscurrents while granting Hasidism's own distinct origins.
- **Modernization thesis.** Scholem's reading of Sabbateanism as a step on the road to Jewish modernization is partly accepted, partly contested.
- **Mass conversions to Islam.** The number of Sabbatean converts to Islam is variously estimated; the Donmeh community was substantial but smaller than the believing-but-not-converted population.
