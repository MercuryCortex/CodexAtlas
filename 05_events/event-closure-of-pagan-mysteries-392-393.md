# Closure of the Pagan Mysteries (Theodosian Edicts 391–393)

## What happened
A sequence of **three imperial edicts under [[theodosius-i|Theodosius I]]** in 391–392 CE comprehensively criminalized public and private pagan religious practice across the Roman Empire, effectively ending the **legal existence of the ancient Mediterranean mystery-cults**:
1. **16.10.10 (24 February 391)** — Rome-specific ban on sacrifices and on entry into pagan temples.
2. **16.10.11 (16 June 391)** — Egypt-specific ban, important for the Isis cult and the Alexandrian Sarapeum (destroyed by Christian crowds in 391 — Theophilus of Alexandria's notorious campaign).
3. **16.10.12 (8 November 392)** — **the comprehensive ban**: all forms of pagan worship including private domestic cult, sacrifice, divination, and the maintenance of cult-images. Penalties for offenders included property forfeiture and capital sentences.

These edicts were preceded by the **[[event-edict-of-thessalonica-380|Edict of Thessalonica (380 CE)]]** which had made Nicene Christianity the official Roman state religion, and accompanied by the **gradual suppression of the Altar of Victory** (removed from the Roman Senate 357, restored 361, finally removed 382, with Symmachus's failed defense of 384). The 391–392 edicts represent the **legal completion** of the Christianization-of-the-Empire process that the [[event-edict-of-milan-313|Edict of Milan (313)]] and [[event-council-of-nicaea-325|Council of Nicaea (325)]] had initiated and that Constantine, Constantius II, Valentinian I, and Gratian had advanced incrementally.

The **physical mystery-cult closure** followed in the immediate aftermath:
- **Eleusis** (the Eleusinian Mysteries, [[tradition-eleusinian-mysteries]]) — celebrated for the last time c. 392 CE; physical destruction completed by **Alaric's Visigothic invasion 396 CE**, when the sanctuary was sacked, the Telesterion destroyed.
- **Alexandrian Sarapeum** (Isis-Serapis cult) — destroyed by Christian crowds under Theophilus 391 CE.
- **Mithraea** across the empire — many forcibly closed by Christian crowds; archaeological evidence at Ostia shows ritual statues smashed and altars overturned.
- **Cybele cult** — Phrygianum at Rome closed; the *taurobolium* dedications cease ~390 CE.
- **Philae temple of Isis** in Egypt — a single exception, allowed to continue under treaty with the Nubians until Justinian's closure in **537 CE** — the *last* working ancient-Mediterranean polytheistic temple.

## Religious consequences
- **End of the legal existence of the ancient Mediterranean mystery-cults** — Eleusinian, Dionysian, Mithraic, Isis (in most of the empire), Cybele-Attis.
- **Physical destruction of the cult-buildings** in the 390s–410s, with substantial loss of cult-literature, cult-objects, and architectural-iconographic evidence.
- **The Christianization-of-the-Mediterranean process** that began with Constantine reaches its juridical completion.
- **Substantial continuity of the cults in modified-private form** persists into the 5th–6th c. (the Lex Iulia Maiestatis is invoked against pagan diviners in Justinian's *Corpus Iuris Civilis*; Anastasius and Justinian continue periodic closure-edicts).
- **The iconographic and conceptual substrate of the mysteries is partly absorbed into Christianity** — Isis-Lactans → Mary-Theotokos; Mithraic Sol Invictus radiate-iconography → Christ-Pantokrator radiate-halo; Mithras *natalis* 25 December → Christmas; Eleusinian mystery-initiation soteriology → Christian baptismal initiation soteriology.

## Documents in play
- *Codex Theodosianus* 16.10 (the principal legal corpus on anti-pagan edicts).
- *Codex Iustinianus* 1.11 (subsequent re-codification).
- Christian narrative sources (Sozomen, Socrates Scholasticus, Theodoret of Cyrrhus) — sympathetic.
- Pagan witnesses (Eunapius *Lives of the Sophists* — hostile; Zosimus *Historia Nova* — hostile).

## Disputes
- **Did 391–392 actually end pagan religion?** Cameron 2011 substantially revises the older "sudden death of paganism" narrative — substantial pagan-cult continuity into the 5th–6th c. is documented. The edicts were legally comprehensive but enforcement was uneven.
- **Did 391–392 specifically end the mystery-cults?** Effectively yes — the institutional mystery-cults required public-cult infrastructure, which the edicts removed.
