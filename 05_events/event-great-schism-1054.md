# The Great Schism (East–West Split)

## What happened
On 16 July 1054, the papal legate Cardinal Humbert laid a bull of excommunication against Patriarch Michael Cerularius of Constantinople on the altar of Hagia Sophia; the Patriarch in turn excommunicated the papal legates. The mutual excommunications — formally aimed at individuals rather than churches — became symbolic of the deeper Western-Latin and Eastern-Greek divergence that had been building for centuries: the *filioque* addition to the Nicene Creed (Western "and from the Son"); papal claims to universal jurisdiction vs. Eastern conciliar / pentarchic ecclesiology; differences in liturgical practice (unleavened vs. leavened Eucharistic bread; clerical celibacy; specific liturgical languages); and the longer-term political divergence between Carolingian-papal Western Europe and the Byzantine Empire. The mutual excommunications were not lifted until 1965 by Pope Paul VI and Patriarch Athenagoras.

## Religious consequences
- Crystallized the institutional separation of Catholic (Latin Roman) and Orthodox (Greek and Slavonic) Christianity that had been developing for several centuries.
- Made subsequent reunion attempts (Council of Lyon 1274; Council of Florence 1439) politically possible but ecclesiastically fragile — the Council of Florence's reunion was rejected in the East under Ottoman conquest pressure.
- The Fourth Crusade's 1204 sack of Constantinople deepened the rupture into a popular bitterness that has never fully healed.
- The doctrinal fault lines — *filioque*, papal primacy, essence-energies (which would crystallize fully in the Palamite controversy of the 14th c.) — define Orthodox-Catholic theological dialogue to the present.

## Documents in play
- The mutual bulls of excommunication of 16 July 1054.
- The earlier patriarchal letters of Cerularius and the *Tomos* of 1054.
- The much earlier *Photian Schism* documents (~860–880, the previous major Constantinople-Rome breach, eventually formally healed but remembered).

## Disputes
- Whether 1054 is the *real* date of the schism or merely a symbolic moment. Most modern scholars (Dvornik, Meyendorff) argue the schism was a long process with no single moment; 1054 is a convenient marker that contemporaries did not necessarily see as decisive.

## Forward to the Crusades
The schism set the political-ecclesiological framework that the [[event-first-crusade-1095-1099|First Crusade]] (1095–1099) attempted to bridge — Urban II's call at [[event-council-of-clermont-1095|Clermont]] was framed as aid to Byzantine Emperor Alexios I Komnenos. The attempted bridge collapsed catastrophically at the [[event-fourth-crusade-1204|Fourth Crusade's]] Sack of Constantinople (1204), after which Latin-Greek relations entered a deeper rupture that has never fully healed. The 1965 mutual lifting of the 1054 excommunications by Paul VI and Athenagoras was a partial rather than total reconciliation.
