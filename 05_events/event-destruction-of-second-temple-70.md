# Destruction of the Second Temple

## What happened
The Roman siege of Jerusalem under Titus (April–September 70 CE) — the climactic event of the First Jewish-Roman War (66–73). After a brutal five-month siege accompanied by famine and intra-Jewish factional violence, Roman forces breached Jerusalem's walls. The Second Temple, the center of Jewish religious life since its rebuilding (~516 BCE) and reconstruction by Herod the Great, was burned and torn down (traditional date 9th of Av — Tisha B'Av — though Josephus dates the burning to 10th of Av; the discrepancy is one of the principal historical-liturgical disputes).

## Religious consequences
- *End of Temple Judaism.* The sacrificial cult that had defined Jewish religion was permanently abolished. No replacement Temple has been built; Jewish religious practice reorganized around the synagogue, study, and prayer.
- *Rise of Rabbinic Judaism.* The Yavneh academy under Yochanan ben Zakkai and his successors (~70–135) preserved Pharisaic-rabbinic tradition, culminating in the Mishnah (~200, [[phase-4-031-mishnah]]) and ultimately the two Talmuds ([[phase-4-032-jerusalem-talmud]], [[phase-4-033-babylonian-talmud]]).
- *Reshaping of early Christianity.* The destruction occurs in the gap between Paul's letters (~50s) and the writing of the Gospels (~70–100); it shapes the eschatological-apocalyptic register of the Synoptic Apocalypse (Mark 13 and parallels) and the gospel writers' attitudes toward the Temple and Judaism. The destruction enabled Christianity's gradual separation from Judaism.
- *End of priestly-Sadducean religion.* The Sadducees, whose authority was tied to the Temple cult, effectively disappear after 70.

## Documents in play
- Josephus's *Jewish War* is the principal source (Josephus himself was a participant on the Jewish side at the start, captured, transferred his loyalty to the Romans, and witnessed the siege).
- The Synoptic Apocalypse (Mark 13; Matthew 24; Luke 21) — whether composed before or just after 70 shapes interpretations of Jesus's predictions.
- All subsequent rabbinic literature operates in the post-Temple horizon.

## Disputes
- **Date of Temple burning.** 9th vs. 10th of Av — the liturgical commemoration takes the 9th; Josephus the 10th.
- **Titus's responsibility.** Josephus claims Titus tried to prevent the burning; rabbinic sources (Talmud Gittin 56b) and some modern scholarship hold Titus personally responsible.
