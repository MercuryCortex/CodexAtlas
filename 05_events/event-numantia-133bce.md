# Siege and Destruction of Numantia (133 BCE)

## The Event
Numantia was a Celtiberian city-state in the upper Duero valley of northeastern Iberia — a hilltop settlement of roughly 8,000 inhabitants (warriors and civilian population together) that had frustrated Roman military power for twenty years through a combination of guerrilla warfare, fortified resistance, and repeated defeat of Roman armies in the field. Between 154 and 137 BCE, Numantia inflicted multiple humiliating defeats on Roman consular armies — including forcing Roman soldiers to pass under the yoke (the supreme humiliation in Roman military culture) and compelling the Roman Senate to repudiate treaties signed by Roman commanders.

In 134 BCE, the Senate sent **Publius Cornelius Scipio Aemilianus Africanus** — the same general who had destroyed Carthage in 146 BCE — to end the Numantine resistance. Scipio's method was purely technical: he refused battle with the famously dangerous Numantine fighters and instead built a complete circumvallation — 9 km of walls, towers, camps, and ditches entirely surrounding the city — and waited. For over a year, approximately 60,000 Roman soldiers starved 8,000 Celtiberians.

In 133 BCE, Numantia fell. The survivors — having burned their city and reportedly killed themselves and each other rather than surrender to slavery — were so emaciated, diseased, and broken that Scipio could parade only a handful of survivors in his triumph. Appian: *"The Numantines, being destitute of food, went mad from hardship, eating the bodies of those who had died."* The city was razed to its foundations.

## Scipio Aemilianus: The Man Who Destroyed Two Civilizations
Scipio Aemilianus is one of the most consequential and disturbing figures in Roman history: in his single career, he destroyed both **Carthage** (146 BCE — the North African superpower and Rome's greatest rival) and **Numantia** (133 BCE — the symbol of Iberian resistance). Both destructions were total: the cities were razed, the populations killed or enslaved, the sites cursed and left empty (Carthage was reportedly plowed with salt; Numantia was abandoned for centuries).

The coincidence of one general executing both destructions gives his career a structure that subsequent history has found both admirable (Roman efficiency) and monstrous (systematic cultural genocide). Polybius, his friend and biographer, was present at the destruction of Carthage and described Scipio weeping as the city burned — quoting Homer's *Iliad* on Troy's fall and saying he feared Rome would someday meet the same fate. This self-aware imperial destroyer — who understood what he was doing, felt something like grief for it, and did it anyway — is a figure the vault should note.

## The Viriato Connection
**[[viriato]]** — the Lusitanian guerrilla leader who had resisted Roman conquest from ~147–139 BCE and was the immediate predecessor of Numantian resistance — was murdered by Roman bribery six years before Numantia's destruction. His killing violated *fides* (Roman honor/loyalty), just as the Romans' treaty violations had provoked his resistance in the first place. Viriato's murder and Numantia's destruction together constitute the **founding-trauma narrative** of pre-Roman Iberian resistance to conquest: the guerrilla hero, then the city of last resort, both eliminated by Rome in the same decade.

The 19th-century Iberian nationalist tradition (Portuguese and Spanish alike) revived both as symbols: Viriato as Portugal's founding resistance hero; Numantia as Spain's equivalent. The parallel to other nationalist resistance-martyrdom figures (Vercingetorix in Gaul; Arminius/Hermann in Germany) shows this is a post-Romantic nationalist mythological pattern applied retroactively to pre-Roman indigenes — not a continuous cultural memory.

## Cervantes and the Long Cultural Memory
Miguel de Cervantes wrote **La Numancia** (~1585 CE) — a dramatic depiction of the siege, with allegorical figures of Spain, Fame, War, and the River Duero speaking alongside the human characters. Written during Spain's imperial height (the Armada was launched three years later), it uses the Numantine tragedy as a parallel to Spanish grandeur: the country that once produced such heroic self-sacrifice now commands the world's greatest empire.

The play's central paradox — total defeat becomes total victory because the defeated preserve their honor — is the founding-trauma logic that any culture applies to its moments of annihilation: what cannot be won militarily is won morally. The Numantine self-destruction becomes, in Cervantes's reading, the first Spanish act of world-historical significance.

**Structural parallel to other founding-trauma-as-sacred-defeat narratives**:
- **Thermopylae** (480 BCE): 300 Spartans' delay of Xerxes becomes Greece's defining moral moment
- **Masada** (73 CE): Jewish mass suicide becomes "Masada shall not fall again"
- **Numantia** (133 BCE): Celtiberian mass death becomes Spain's founding moral event
- **Viriato's murder** (139 BCE): becomes Portugal's founding injustice narrative
- **Battle of Kosovo** (1389): Serbian defeat becomes Serbian national identity
- **Alamo** (1836): Texas defenders' death becomes American expansion mythology

The "sacred defeat" as founding narrative is a universal mythological pattern: the group that loses maintains or gains its identity through the heroism of its losing.
