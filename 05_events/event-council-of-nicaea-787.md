# Second Council of Nicaea (787)

## What happened
Convened by the Empress Irene (regent for her son Constantine VI) and Patriarch Tarasius at Nicaea in September–October 787, ending the first phase (726–787) of Byzantine Iconoclasm. About 350 bishops attended. The council reversed the Iconoclast Council of Hieria (754) and defined that:
1. Images of Christ, the Theotokos, the angels, and the saints are to be set up in churches and other places, on walls and on doors, in private houses and along the roads.
2. These images are to be given **veneration** (*proskynesis*) — kisses, prostrations, incense, lights — which is a relative honor terminating on the prototype they represent. They are **not** to be given the worship (*latreia*) due to God alone.
3. The iconoclast emperors (Leo III, Constantine V, Leo IV) and the iconoclast patriarchs (Anastasius, Constantine II, Nicetas I) were named and anathematized.
22 disciplinary canons were also enacted.

## Religious consequences
- **The seventh and final ecumenical council** of the Catholic and Orthodox traditions (though Iconoclasm resurged 815–843; the Triumph of Orthodoxy in 843 ratified Nicaea II's settlement).
- **Sacred image-making becomes definitively part of Eastern (and Latin) Christian practice.** The Orthodox iconographic tradition (Constantinople, Crete, the Balkans, Russia) develops on the platform of Nicaea II.
- **Reception in the Latin West was complex.** A poorly translated Latin version of the acta reached the Carolingian court and was read as endorsing image-worship; the response, the *Libri Carolini* (~792, drafted by Theodulf of Orleans under Charlemagne's direction), rejects both the iconoclasts and (what it thinks is) the iconophile council, holding a moderate Frankish position: images are useful for instruction but should not be venerated. The Frankish hesitation about Nicaea II conditioned medieval Latin theology of images (Aquinas, *ST* III.25.3, ratifies the seven-council tradition).

## Substrate connections
- **Iconoclasm and iconophilia are the patristic re-encounter with the deep substrate of Mediterranean religious image-practice.** The Iconoclasts grounded their position in the Old Testament Second Commandment and in continuities with **Jewish aniconism** and **Islamic aniconism** (Islam was the immediate political-religious presence on Byzantium's eastern frontier and a documented influence on Leo III's iconoclast initiative). The Iconophiles grounded their position in:
  - The Incarnation: God-in-the-flesh makes matter capable of bearing the divine.
  - The continuity with **Greco-Roman cult-image practice**, against which early Christian aniconism had defined itself but which had been gradually re-absorbed (the Dura-Europos house-church c. 235 already has Christian frescos).
  - Pseudo-Dionysian and Maximian theology of hierarchy and matter-mediation.
- The image-veneration ratified at Nicaea II is structurally similar to ancient Mediterranean cult-image practice (Greek *agalmata*, Roman *signa* of the gods), with the explicit theological qualification that the honor is *relative* (transferred to the prototype) — a distinction Greek and Roman theologians of cult images had also articulated (Porphyry, *On Images*). The substrate continuity is real and the theological self-distinguishing is real.
- The Marian iconography that flourishes in the post-787 tradition draws on substrate registers of Mediterranean Mother-Goddess iconography (Isis-and-Horus visual prototypes for Mary-and-Christ are well-attested in late-antique Egyptian Christian art; cf. Mathews & Muller 2017).

## Documents in play
- **Conciliar acta** — Greek (Mansi XII–XIII; new critical edition Lamberz 2008–) and Latin.
- **The *Libri Carolini*** — the Frankish reaction.
- **Pope Hadrian I's *Hadrianum*** — the Roman response to the *Libri Carolini*.

## Disputes
- **Reception in the Latin West.** As noted: the Carolingian reception was partly garbled by translation problems, and the *Libri Carolini* hold a position distinct from the council's. Whether Nicaea II is "the seventh ecumenical council" in Catholic theology is settled (yes); the medieval-Frankish reception is messier.
- **The continued resurgence of Iconoclasm (815–843).** Nicaea II did not finally end Iconoclasm; the second iconoclast period under Leo V and his successors required a second restoration in 843 (the "Triumph of Orthodoxy").
- **The Aniconism / iconography history of early Christianity.** Whether early Christianity was strongly aniconic (mainstream view, until Dura-Europos) or always tolerant of images (revisionist) is an ongoing scholarly question (Jensen 2000).
