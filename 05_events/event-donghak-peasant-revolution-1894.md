# Donghak Peasant Revolution (1894)

## What happened
The armed uprising of Donghak adherents and Korean peasants against feudal corruption, brutal taxation, and foreign economic interference in Joseon Korea. The revolution began in the Jeolla Province in early 1894 under the peasant general Jun Bong-jun (*Jeon Bongjun*), who raised hundreds of thousands of fighters under the Donghak spiritual banner and the practical grievance of the Goryeo tax system's brutal extraction.

When the Joseon government found itself unable to suppress the uprising militarily, it appealed for Qing Chinese military assistance. Japan, citing the 1885 Tientsin Convention (which required both China and Japan to notify each other before sending troops to Korea), immediately dispatched its own forces. The arrival of both armies on the peninsula ended the uprising but triggered the **First Sino-Japanese War (1894–95)** — the geopolitical earthquake that ended the Sinocentric East Asian world order and began Japan's rise to regional dominance.

## Religious significance
The revolution is the political climax of [[tradition-donghak]]'s *gaebyeok* eschatology: the "Opening of Heaven" — the cosmic renewal that Donghak doctrine held was imminent — was sought through armed transformation of the social order. The uprising instantiated the doctrine that *innaecheon* ("Humans are Heaven") had direct political implications: if every human contains the divine, then the existing feudal hierarchy and its foreign-enabled corruption was a cosmic offense.

Choe Je-u (the founder, [[choe-je-u]]) had been executed in 1864 for preaching this very doctrine. Thirty years later, his followers were enacting it at scale.

## Geopolitical cascade
The sequence is one of the vault's clearest examples of theology driving history at continental scale:

1. Donghak spiritual movement (1860 → 1894) builds peasant organization under eschatological urgency
2. Peasant revolution (1894) overwhelms Joseon government's military capacity
3. Joseon government requests Qing Chinese intervention
4. Japan invokes Tientsin Convention and deploys troops simultaneously
5. First Sino-Japanese War (1894–95): Japan defeats Qing China
6. Treaty of Shimonoseki (1895): Korea formally severed from Qing tributary system; Taiwan ceded to Japan
7. Korea's progressive absorption into Japanese influence culminates in formal annexation 1910

A religious movement founded in 1860 to resist "Western Learning" ultimately (through the 1894 revolution it generated) accelerated the end of Korean independence and the rise of the very Japanese imperialism it had sought to resist — a tragic irony documented in Korean nationalist historiography.

## Aftermath
The revolution was suppressed militarily; Jun Bong-jun was captured and executed in 1895. The second patriarch Choe Si-hyeong was executed in 1898. The movement reorganized under the third patriarch Son Byong-hui as *Cheondogyo* (天道教, "Religion of the Heavenly Way") in 1905 — rebranding as a peaceful religion while retaining the core doctrines. Cheondogyo then played a significant role in the **March First Independence Movement (Samil Undong)** of 1919 against Japanese colonial rule.
