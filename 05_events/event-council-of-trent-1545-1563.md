# Council of Trent (1545–1563)

## What happened
The nineteenth ecumenical council of the Catholic Church, convened by Pope Paul III at Trent (then in the prince-bishopric of Trent within the Holy Roman Empire, now Trento in northern Italy) and meeting in three discontinuous periods: Period I (1545–1547, transferred briefly to Bologna 1547), Period II (1551–1552), and Period III (1562–1563). The council's task was to respond to the Protestant Reformation — both by defining Catholic doctrine against Protestant positions and by reforming Catholic ecclesiastical discipline. Roughly 270 bishops attended at the peak; the council produced 25 sessions of decrees on doctrine and reform. Among the most consequential decrees: Session IV (1546) on Scripture and tradition (declaring the Vulgate authoritative and tradition co-equal with Scripture as source of revelation, against Protestant *sola scriptura*); Session VI (1547) on justification (the longest and most theologically detailed decree, against Lutheran *sola fide*); Sessions XIII, XXI, XXII on the Eucharist and the sacrifice of the Mass; Session XXIII on Holy Orders; Session XXIV on Marriage; Session XXV on Purgatory, the cult of saints, relics, indulgences, and the Index of Forbidden Books.

## Religious consequences
- **Doctrinal definition of Catholicism against Protestantism.** The Tridentine decrees fixed Catholic doctrine on the points contested by Lutherans, Reformed, and Anglicans for the next four centuries (until Vatican II, 1962–1965).
- **Reform of Catholic discipline.** Bishops were required to reside in their dioceses (a major reform against medieval pluralism), seminaries were established for clerical training, the Roman Catechism standardized parish instruction, the Tridentine Mass became the universal Roman Rite.
- **The Index of Forbidden Books.** The Tridentine Index (1564) systematized the censorship of Protestant, heterodox, and various other works; it remained operative until 1966.
- **The Counter-Reformation movements.** The Society of Jesus (Jesuits, founded 1540, but most active under Tridentine authority), the Capuchin reform of the Franciscans, the Discalced Carmelite reform (Teresa of Ávila and John of the Cross), the Theatines, the Oratorians, all gained institutional momentum from the Tridentine reform program.
- **Confessional fixation in Europe.** Trent ensured the permanence of the Catholic-Protestant divide. The post-Tridentine period was the era of the *cuius regio, eius religio* principle (Peace of Augsburg 1555; Peace of Westphalia 1648).

## Documents in play
- All twenty-five sessions of decrees, doctrinal canons, and reform decrees.
- The Tridentine Catechism (*Catechismus ex Decreto Concilii Tridentini ad Parochos*, 1566).
- The Sixto-Clementine Vulgate (1592, the definitive Tridentine Latin Bible).
- The Roman Breviary (1568) and Roman Missal (1570).
- The Tridentine Index of Forbidden Books (1564).

## Disputes
- **Was Trent strictly a Counter-Reformation or also a Catholic Reformation?** O'Malley (2013) emphasizes that the reform impulse predated and exceeded the Protestant response.
- **The doctrinal moves of Session VI (Justification).** Catholic-Protestant ecumenical dialogue (esp. the 1999 Joint Declaration of the Lutheran World Federation and the Vatican on Justification) has revisited the Tridentine decree's relationship to current Catholic and Lutheran soteriology.
