# Formation of the Arya Samaj (10 April 1875)

## What happened
On 10 April 1875 at the Doctor Mansookhbhai Maker's house in Girgaon, Bombay, Swami [[dayananda-saraswati]] formally founded the *Ārya Samāj* ("Noble Society"). The founding meeting was attended by ~10 founding members and established a charter of *Niyam* (Ten Principles) that codified the Society's doctrinal orientation: (1) all knowledge originates from God (Ishwar); (2) God is the source of all true knowledge; (3) the Vedas are the books of all true knowledge; (4) one should always be ready to accept truth and to renounce untruth; (5) all acts should be done according to dharma; (6) the principal aim of the Society is to do good to the world; (7) one should treat all persons with love and justice; (8) one should dispel ignorance and promote knowledge; (9) one should not be content with merely one's own welfare; (10) one should consider the welfare of society binding on oneself. These principles framed a strict Vedic-monotheistic, anti-idolatrous, anti-Puranic, anti-caste-by-birth, pro-vernacular-education, pro-Hindu-women's-rights reform program. The institutional center shifted to Lahore in 1877 (Punjab Arya Samaj) where the movement found its principal organizational base; the DAV (Dayanand Anglo-Vedic) school system was founded in the 1880s as the educational wing.

## Religious consequences
- **First institutional Hindu reform organization with mass reach**: while the Brahmo Samaj (1828, [[ram-mohan-roy]]) preceded it institutionally, the Brahmo Samaj remained an elite-Bengali phenomenon; the Arya Samaj achieved mass-scale institutional reach across North India, especially Punjab, Haryana, UP, and the global Indian diaspora.
- ***Śuddhi* reconversion**: Dayananda instituted the *śuddhi* (purification / reconversion) ritual allowing people who had converted to Christianity or Islam to "return" to Hinduism. This was a major theological-institutional innovation — pre-modern Brahmanical Hinduism had generally treated conversion-out as irreversible — and reshaped 20th-c. South Asian conversion politics. The Hindu nationalist movements (Hindu Mahasabha 1915, RSS 1925) drew on Arya Samaj *śuddhi* infrastructure.
- **Vedic monotheism vs. Puranic Hinduism**: Arya Samaj's strict Vedic-monotheistic theology and its polemic against Puranic Hinduism (idolatry, temple-worship, the avatar doctrine, the popular goddess traditions, the bhakti devotional traditions) made it a sharp internal-Hindu reform-vs.-orthodoxy line of conflict that continues into the present.
- **Anti-caste-by-birth reform**: Arya Samaj's rejection of birth-determined caste (while retaining *varṇa* as a functional category) was a substantial reformist position; the Mission's *gurukula* and DAV educational institutions were among the first to admit students across caste lines.
- **Hindu nationalist precursor**: V.D. Savarkar's *Hindutva* (1923, see [[phase-7-029-hindutva]]) draws explicit ideological inheritance from Dayananda; the contemporary Sangh Parivar treats the Arya Samaj as one of its founding precursors. Lala Lajpat Rai, Bhagat Singh's family, and many other late-19th- / early-20th-c. North Indian Hindu nationalists were Arya Samajis. The movement's relationship to Hindu nationalism is one of the most-studied historical questions in modern Indian political-religious history.
- **Theosophical Society alliance and rupture (1879–1881)**: when Helena Blavatsky and Henry Steel Olcott arrived in India in 1879, the Theosophical Society initially formed an institutional alliance with the Arya Samaj. By 1881 the alliance ruptured over doctrinal incompatibilities — the Theosophists' polytheistic-occult orientation was incompatible with Dayananda's strict Vedic monotheism. The Theosophists subsequently established their permanent Indian headquarters at Adyar (Madras, 1882). The brief Arya-Theosophy alliance is a documented moment in the 19th-c. entanglement of Indian Hindu reform with Western esotericism.

## Cross-tradition consequences
- **Protestant-structural Hindu reform**: the Arya Samaj's strict scripturalism ("Return to the Vedas" — *Vedoṅ kī or lauto*), its anti-clerical critique of Brahmanical ritual specialism, its emphasis on vernacular accessibility (Hindi-Urdu lectures, vernacular education, women's literacy), and its institutional-organizational structure show striking structural parallels to 16th-c. Protestant Christian Reformation. Jordens 1978, Jones 1976 trace the structural parallels; direct Protestant-missionary influence is also documented (Dayananda's polemics against Christianity in chapter 14 of the *Satyārtha Prakāśa* show extensive familiarity with Protestant missionary critiques of Hinduism).
- **Diaspora export**: Arya Samaj missions to the Caribbean (Trinidad, Suriname, British Guiana), Fiji, Mauritius, East Africa, and South Africa exported Hindu-modernist reform across the global Indian indentured-labor diaspora — comparable in structure to Christian missionary diaspora expansion. The Trinidad and Mauritius Arya Samaj institutions remain among the principal Hindu religious organizations in those countries.
- **Anti-Islamic polemic and the modern Hindutva trajectory**: chapter 14 of the *Satyārtha Prakāśa* contains the most aggressive 19th-c. Hindu-reform critique of Islam in any major Indian-language text; the polemical model fed into 20th-c. Hindu-Muslim communal politics and into Hindutva ideology. The Mughal-period legacy of Hindu-Muslim cross-pollination ([[kabir]], the Sant tradition, the *sulh-i kull* of Akbar, the Bhakti-Sufi shared idiom) was disavowed by the Arya Samaj's stricter Vedic-restorationist position.

## Documents in play
- Dayananda's *Satyārtha Prakāśa* (Light of Truth, 1875, rev. 1882) — the foundational creedal text.
- The *Ārya Samāj Niyam* (Ten Principles) — the founding institutional charter.
- Dayananda's *Ṛgvedādi-bhāṣya-bhūmikā* (1876) — interpretive principles for Vedic interpretation.
- Subsequent Arya Samaj literature: the *Ārya Samāchār* periodicals from the 1880s onward.

## Disputes
- **Arya Samaj's contribution to Hindu nationalism**: how much the Arya Samaj is a precursor of Hindutva (the standard Hindu-nationalist self-presentation), vs. how much it is a distinct reformist tradition co-opted by Hindutva (the standard Arya-Samaji self-presentation), is a continuing political-historiographical question.
- **Mass-membership figures**: precise membership numbers across the late-19th and 20th c. are difficult to establish; estimates vary widely.
