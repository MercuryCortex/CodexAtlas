# Ficino's Translation of the Corpus Hermeticum (1463)

## What happened
Around 1460, the Medici-employed manuscript-hunter Leonardo da Pistoia returned from Macedonia with a Greek manuscript of fourteen treatises of the *Corpus Hermeticum* (which the post-1453 Greek manuscript diaspora was making available to Italian collectors). Cosimo de' Medici, aged and ailing, ordered his court Platonist Marsilio Ficino — already at work on a complete Latin translation of Plato — to interrupt the Plato project and translate Hermes first. Cosimo's stated reason (preserved in Ficino's dedicatory letter) was that he believed Hermes Trismegistus to be older than Plato and indeed older than Moses, and wanted to read the primordial revelation before he died. Ficino completed the translation in 1463; Cosimo died in 1464. The translation circulated in manuscript through the 1460s and was printed at Treviso in 1471 (the first Latin printing of any ancient text translated by a Renaissance humanist). The 1471 *Pimander* was reprinted sixteen times before 1500 and dozens more in the 16th century — one of the most widely circulated philosophical books of the early printing era.

## Religious consequences
- **Foundation of Renaissance Hermeticism as a self-conscious intellectual movement.** Ficino's preface to the *Pimander* frames Hermes as the first of the *prisci theologi* (Hermes → Orpheus → Aglaophamus → Pythagoras → Plato → Christ), grounding the central Renaissance esoteric historiography for the next 150 years.
- **Massive expansion of the Western theological repertoire.** Hermetic categories — the unknowable Father, Logos cosmogony, soul-ascent through the spheres, the human as *deus terrenus* (earthly god) — enter Western European Christian discourse at scale.
- **Christianization of pagan revelation.** Ficino reads Hermes as a pagan-prophet predecessor of Christ, building on the patristic tradition (Lactantius, Augustine) that had taken Hermes seriously. This Christianizing reading of the Hermetica is what makes the Renaissance Hermetic project doctrinally compatible with Catholicism.
- **Setting the stage for Pico, Reuchlin, Agrippa, Bruno.** Every subsequent Renaissance Hermetic-Kabbalist figure presupposes the Ficinian *Pimander*.

## Documents in play
- The Greek manuscript Ficino used (likely Laurentianus 71,33 plus possibly one sister codex; survives in the Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana).
- The 1471 Treviso first edition and its many reprints.
- Ficino's dedicatory letter and the *Argumentum*, which articulate the *prisca theologia* framework.

## Disputes
- **Date of completion.** Ficino's own statement gives 1463 (translation); print is 1471. The exact internal stages of revision before printing are debated.
- **The "prisca theologia" thesis.** Yates' framing of Renaissance Hermeticism as a coherent magical-philosophical movement has been refined by Copenhaver, Hanegraaff, and others; the trajectory is real, the homogeneity is contested.
- **Survival of the 1453 manuscript-diaspora narrative.** Some scholars emphasize that Hermetic transmission to Italy predates 1453 (Italian humanists were already collecting Byzantine manuscripts in the 1430s–1440s).
