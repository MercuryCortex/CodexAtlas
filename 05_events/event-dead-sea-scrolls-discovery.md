# Dead Sea Scrolls Discovery

## What happened
Between 1947 and 1956, eleven caves near the Wadi Qumran on the northwestern shore of the Dead Sea yielded approximately 930 manuscripts (most heavily fragmentary), dating from the third century BCE to the first century CE. Cave 1 was discovered by Bedouin shepherds (traditional account: Muhammad edh-Dhib, ~1947); Cave 4 (1952) yielded ~15,000 fragments representing some 600 manuscripts. The site of Qumran itself was excavated by Roland de Vaux and the École Biblique de Jérusalem 1951–1956, though the connection between the scrolls and the Qumran settlement is debated.

The corpus divides into: (1) biblical manuscripts — every book of the Hebrew Bible except Esther is represented, often in multiple copies; (2) sectarian writings — texts apparently produced by the Qumran community (Community Rule, War Scroll, Hodayot, Damascus Document, Temple Scroll, MMT, Pesher commentaries); (3) other Jewish writings of the Second Temple period (Enochic literature, Jubilees, biblical paraphrases).

## Religious consequences
- *Hebrew Bible textual criticism* — pushed direct manuscript witnesses for the Hebrew Bible back from medieval Masoretic codices (~1000 CE) to the Second Temple period; demonstrated that the consonantal text had been substantially stable since at least the early common era, while also revealing significant textual fluidity in the late-Second-Temple period before stabilization.
- *Second Temple Judaism* — revealed Second Temple Judaism as plural, sectarian, apocalyptic, and theologically diverse — quite unlike the picture provided by either later rabbinic literature or the Christian New Testament alone.
- *Christian origins* — provided crucial context for John the Baptist and early Christianity in a shared milieu of Jewish apocalyptic-eschatological sectarianism.

## Documents in play
The corpus is associated with [[phase-3-011-dead-sea-scrolls]] documents — most Dead Sea Scrolls materials are Phase 3 (pre-100 CE) and outside the strict Phase 4 scope. Listed here because the *1947–1956 discovery event* parallels the 1945 Nag Hammadi event as a 20th-century recovery of late-antique religious literature; the two transformed mid-20th-century religious studies in tandem.

## Disputes
- **Authorship.** The Essene hypothesis (de Vaux, Cross) remains the majority view but has been challenged: alternative proposals include Sadducean origin (Schiffman), Jerusalem-temple library hidden during the 66–70 revolt (Golb), or sectarian-mixed origin.
- **Connection of scrolls to Qumran site.** Whether the scrolls were produced at Qumran or deposited there from elsewhere.

## Later scholarship (Phase 7 reception)

- **1947–1955: initial publication.** Cave 1 manuscripts (Great Isaiah Scroll, Habakkuk Pesher, Manual of Discipline / Community Rule, Genesis Apocryphon) published 1950–1956.
- **1955–1991: international team / closed-access period.** The principal Cave 4 fragments were assigned to a small international editorial team under Roland de Vaux and successors; the slow pace of publication became scandalous by the 1980s ("Dead Sea Scrolls scandal").
- **1991: forced opening.** Hershel Shanks and the Biblical Archaeology Society's release of photographs (1991), the Huntington Library's release (1991), and Robert Eisenman's facsimile edition (1991) broke the closed-access regime. Emanuel Tov restructured the international publication team.
- **1991–2010: rapid publication.** The 40-volume *Discoveries in the Judaean Desert* (DJD) Oxford series was substantially completed in this period under Tov and successors.
- **Major Phase 7 scholarly figures**: Roland de Vaux (excavation), Frank Moore Cross (early-period scholarship), Géza Vermes (translation), James VanderKam, Joseph Fitzmyer, Lawrence Schiffman (Sadducean-hypothesis revisionism), Florentino García Martínez, Emanuel Tov, Eibert Tigchelaar, Carol Newsom (sectarian-text scholarship), John J. Collins (apocalypticism).
- **2010+: digital and museum era.** Leon Levy Dead Sea Scrolls Digital Library (Israel Antiquities Authority, 2012 onward); ongoing forgery-detection issues (Museum of the Bible 2017 acquisition of fragments later determined to be modern forgeries, demonstrated 2020 by Colette Loll's investigative team).
