# Discovery of Tutankhamun's Tomb (KV62, 1922)

## What happened
On **4 November 1922**, Howard Carter's water-boy discovered the top of a stone step in the Valley of the Kings. By **26 November 1922**, Carter, his patron the Earl of Carnarvon, Lady Evelyn Herbert, and Arthur Callender had broken through the sealed door and Carter — peering through a small breach into the antechamber — saw "wonderful things" (his own much-quoted phrase from his diary). The tomb of Pharaoh [[tutankhamun]] (~-1341 to ~-1323) had been substantially intact for over 3,200 years.

Excavation, conservation, and cataloguing of the ~5,400 artefacts in the tomb took **ten years** (1922–1932). The find included the famous **gold death-mask**, the nested anthropoid coffins, the granite sarcophagus, the four shrines surrounding the burial chamber, and — crucially for the Codex Atlas's scorpion-investigation — the **gilded canopic shrine** flanked on its four sides by **free-standing gilded statues of the four canopic protectresses**: [[isis-egyptian]] (south), [[nephthys]] (north), [[neith]] (east), and [[selket]] (west). Each statue stands ~90 cm tall with arms outstretched in the protective canopic-shrine pose. The Selket statue carries the scorpion crisply modelled atop her tripartite wig — the single most-photographed iconographic representation of the Egyptian scorpion-goddess and the cult-image that anchors modern global recognition of Selket / Serqet.

## Religious / scholarly consequences
- **Iconographic-stability moment.** The KV62 finds gave 20th-c. scholarship a single, perfectly-preserved late-18th-Dynasty (~-1323) iconographic corpus — including the canonical four-canopic-goddesses ensemble. The Selket-statue is one of the **most-reproduced cult-images** in the entire world archaeological record (Reeves 1990; Eaton-Krauss & Graefe 1985).
- **Modern Egyptomania.** The 1922 discovery sparked a global wave of Egyptian-revival aesthetics in jewellery, fashion, architecture (Art Deco), and popular religion (1920s–30s Theosophy, occultism). The "curse of Tutankhamun" tabloid narrative (Carnarvon's death from blood-poisoning in April 1923 was its proximate trigger) is itself a piece of 20th-c. occult-receptional history rather than ancient Egyptian belief.
- **Egyptological methodology.** Carter's painstaking decade-long conservation-and-cataloguing approach — with Harry Burton's photographs and individual numbered object-cards for each of ~5,400 artefacts — became a methodological touchstone for modern archaeological excavation.
- **Source of Egyptian Book of the Dead manuscript witnesses.** The KV62 [[phase-1-010-book-of-the-dead]] papyri are among the best-preserved New Kingdom witnesses to the text (~-1323).
- **Selket's modern visibility.** Before 1922 Selket was a specialist topic in Egyptological scholarship; after 1922 her gilded statue became one of the most-photographed Egyptian artefacts and her cross-tradition recognition as **the** Egyptian scorpion-goddess (against e.g. Mesopotamian [[ishara]]) was substantially driven by the iconographic clarity of the KV62 statue. The Codex Atlas's astrological Mars-wedge anchor for the scorpion-archetype rests on the KV62 iconographic record.

## Documents in play
- **Carter & Mace 1923–1933** *The Tomb of Tut-Ankh-Amen* (3 vols.) — Carter's own publication.
- **Griffith Institute Tutankhamun's Tomb Series** (10 vols., 1963–2008) — definitive scholarly catalogue; vol. 2 (Eaton-Krauss & Graefe 1985) on the shrines including the four canopic goddesses.
- **Griffith Institute online archive** — digitised diaries, photographs, and object-cards. https://www.griffith.ox.ac.uk/discoveringTut/

## Disputes
- **The "curse" narrative** is a 1920s tabloid construct, not Egyptian theology. The reputation has decayed in scholarship but persists in popular literature.
- **Lord Carnarvon's role and the antiquities-export controversy.** The 1922–25 dispute between the Carnarvon estate and the Egyptian government over the disposition of the finds (in the wake of the 1922 partial Egyptian independence) reshaped Egyptian antiquities law: the tomb's contents went, for the first time, **entirely to the Egyptian Museum, Cairo** rather than being split with the foreign excavator's institution.
- **Cause of Tutankhamun's death.** Various theories — assassination, chariot accident, malaria + bone disorder (the most recent 2010 DNA-and-CT analysis). Outside the scope of this event-node.
