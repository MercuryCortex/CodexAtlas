# Muhammad's Night Journey and Ascension (al-Isrāʾ waʾl-Miʿrāj, c. 619 CE)

## Summary

The **Night Journey and Ascension** (*al-Isrāʾ waʾl-Miʿrāj*) is one of the most significant events in Islamic theology and one of the most important documented instances of cross-tradition religious transmission in late antiquity. It is the event in which **the Jewish heavenly-journey tradition** (Ezekiel's chariot → Hekhalot mysticism → Enochic apocalypse) enters Islam and is transformed into a founding prophetic experience — and from which it will later influence Dante's *Paradiso*.

The Mi'raj is described in the Quran (Sura 17 and Sura 53:1-18) and narrated in detail in the earliest Islamic biography, Ibn Ishaq's *Sīra* (c. 768 CE). The probable date is c. 619 CE — during the "Year of Sorrow" (*ʿĀm al-Ḥuzn*) in which both Muhammad's wife Khadija and his uncle Abu Talib died, leaving him politically vulnerable in Mecca.

## The Event

### The Night Journey (*al-Isrāʾ*)

Muhammad is transported from the **Sacred Mosque in Mecca** to the **Temple Mount in Jerusalem** (*al-Masjid al-Aqṣā*, the Farthest Mosque) in a single night, carried by the **Buraq** — a white celestial steed with a woman's face and peacock's tail, larger than a donkey but smaller than a mule, whose stride reaches as far as its eye can see. Gabriel accompanies him.

At Jerusalem, Muhammad leads the prophets (Adam, Jesus, Moses, Abraham) in prayer as their imam — the scene that establishes his position as the seal and summation of the entire prophetic tradition.

### The Ascension (*al-Miʿrāj*)

From the Temple Mount, Muhammad ascends through **seven heavens**:

| Heaven | Metal | Prophet encountered |
|--------|-------|---------------------|
| First | Silver | Adam |
| Second | — | Jesus and John |
| Third | — | Joseph |
| Fourth | — | Idris (Enoch) |
| Fifth | — | Aaron |
| Sixth | — | Moses |
| Seventh | — | Abraham (at the *Bayt al-Maʿmūr*, the heavenly Ka'aba) |

At each level, Gabriel requests entry from the guardian angels; the gatekeepers ask who has come; Gabriel identifies Muhammad and vouches for his mission.

Beyond the seventh heaven: the **Lote Tree of the Uttermost Boundary** (*Sidrat al-Muntahā*) — the cosmic boundary beyond which no created being can pass. Gabriel stops here; Muhammad alone proceeds beyond the veil to God's presence. The divine voice commands 50 daily prayers; Moses (who knows the human capacity for religious discipline from his experience with the Israelites) counsels Muhammad to negotiate it down; the final number is five.

## The Jewish Background

**Peter Schäfer's definitive 2009 study** (*Mohammed's Journey to Heaven*, De Gruyter) establishes what scholars had long suspected: the Mi'raj shows specific formal parallels with the Hekhalot literature ([[phase-5-013-hekhalot-literature]]) that are too precise to be coincidental:

- The **seven-heaven structure** (exactly seven, in sequence, each with a gatekeeper)
- The **credential-challenge** at each gate (Gabriel's dialogue with the guardian angels mirrors the Hekhalot ascender's use of divine seals and Names)
- **Enoch's presence** (at the fourth heaven in the Mi'raj; Enoch is central to both Hekhalot literature and the Islamic prophetic chain as *Idris*)
- The **Lote Tree** as the limit of creation echoes the Hekhalot texts' descriptions of the boundary beyond which ordinary consciousness cannot proceed

Muhammad had extensive contact with Jewish communities in Arabia — the Jewish tribes of Medina (Banu Qaynuqa, Banu Nadir, Banu Qurayza), Jewish individuals like Waraqah ibn Nawfal (his wife's cousin, a Christian-Jewish scholar who recognized Muhammad's experience), and the broader Jewish Arabian culture that preserved Enochic and Hekhalot material in oral and written form.

## The Cross-Tradition Chain This Event Creates

The Mi'raj becomes the **transmission node** for the heavenly-journey tradition from Judaism into Islam, and from Islam into medieval Christian culture:

1. **Hekhalot → Mi'raj**: Jewish heavenly-journey structure → Islamic prophetic ascension
2. **Mi'raj → Sufi mysticism**: Ibn Arabi's *Kitāb al-Isrāʾ* (*Book of the Night Journey*) allegorizes the Mi'raj as a template for mystical union — each heaven a stage of philosophical-spiritual ascent toward *wahdat al-wujud* (unity of being)
3. **Mi'raj → Persian visual culture**: the *Miʿrāj-nāma* illustrated manuscripts (15th century, Herat) produce some of the most extraordinary images in Islamic art — the winged, flame-haloed Muhammad among the angels — influencing the visual vocabulary of Islamic mysticism
4. **Mi'raj → Dante**: the *Kitāb al-Miʿrāj* was translated into Castilian (c. 1260) and Latin (c. 1264) for King Alfonso X of Castile — making the Mi'raj narrative available in Latin as the *Liber Scalae Machometi*. Dante's *Paradiso* ([[phase-5-056-divine-comedy]]) — with its nine ascending spheres, prophets and saints at each level, and culminating vision of divine light — is structurally the Mi'raj in Christian-Ptolemaic dress

## Significance

The Mi'raj demonstrates one of the vault's core claims: **the world's mystical traditions are not independent inventions but a conversation**. The founding text of Jewish mystical experience — Ezekiel's chariot vision in Babylon, received by a priest in exile among the nations — passed through 1,200 years of Jewish apocalyptic and mystical development, entered Arabia where Muhammad encountered the tradition, became the structural model for a founding Islamic prophetic experience, and eventually shaped the greatest poem of Western Christian literature.

The six nodes of the chain are: **Ezekiel** → **1 Enoch** → **Hekhalot** → **this event** → **Ibn Arabi** → **Dante**.
