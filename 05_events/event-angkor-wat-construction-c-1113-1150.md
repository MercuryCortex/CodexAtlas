# Angkor Wat (c. 1113–1150)

## Identity
Angkor Wat (Khmer "City Temple") is a massive temple complex in Angkor, Siem Reap Province, Cambodia, built under Suryavarman II of the Khmer Empire in the first half of the 12th century. By footprint enclosed within its outer moat (c. 1.5 × 1.3 km), it is the largest religious monument in the world. The central temple structure consists of three rising rectangular galleries surmounted by a quincunx of five towers (one central, four corner), conceived as an architectural model of Mount Meru, the axis mundi of the Hindu-Buddhist cosmos.

Inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1992 (part of the Angkor Archaeological Park).

## Construction
Built by Suryavarman II (r. 1113–c. 1150), one of the greatest Khmer kings, who unified the Khmer state through extensive military campaigns and patronized Vaishnava religion as state cult — a partial break from the predominantly Shaiva royal cult of his predecessors. The monument was conceived simultaneously as:
- A state temple dedicated to **[[vishnu]]** (uniquely among the major Angkor monuments, oriented west toward the setting sun and the realm of the dead — most Khmer temples face east);
- The Devarāja cult monument and possibly the funerary temple of Suryavarman II himself, identified after death with Vishnu under the posthumous name Paramavishnuloka ("[He who has gone to] the supreme world of Vishnu");
- A three-dimensional model of Mount Meru — the five towers as the five peaks of Meru, the three concentric galleries as the cosmic continents, the outer moat as the cosmic ocean.

Inscriptions naming the monument by its medieval name (Vrah Vishnuloka) and connecting it to Suryavarman II are limited; the principal evidence for the dating and the Vaishnava-funerary-temple interpretation comes from the architectural and iconographic programme, contemporary epigraphy from other sites, and the bas-relief identification of Suryavarman II in a procession scene on the south gallery.

The bas-relief programme of the outer gallery (over 600 m of continuous relief carving) is one of the most extensive narrative-relief programmes in world art, including:
- The Battle of Kurukshetra from the Mahabharata (west gallery, north half);
- The Battle of Lanka from the Ramayana (west gallery, south half);
- The Procession of Suryavarman II (south gallery, west half);
- The Heavens and Hells (south gallery, east half — 32 hells of Hindu cosmology);
- The Churning of the Ocean of Milk / Samudra Manthana (east gallery, south half — the longest single relief, ~50 m, ~88 asuras and ~92 devas churning Mount Mandara on the back of the turtle Kūrma);
- The Battle of Vishnu and the Asuras (east gallery, north half);
- The Battle of Krishna and Bana (north gallery, east half);
- The Battle of the Devas and Asuras (north gallery, west half).

## Religious significance
**Vaishnava state cult.** Under Suryavarman II, the Khmer royal religion shifted toward Vaishnavism — a departure from the predominantly Shaiva Devarāja cult of earlier and most later Khmer kings (Jayavarman II's foundational establishment in 802 of the kamratĕṅ jagat ta rāja "lord of the universe who is king" linga cult was overwhelmingly Shaiva). Angkor Wat is the most spectacular surviving monument of this Vaishnava interlude.

**Buddhist reconfiguration after the late 12th century.** Following Suryavarman II's death and the brief reign of Yaśovarman II, the kingdom passed through a period of disorder; Jayavarman VII (r. c. 1181–c. 1218) re-took the kingdom from the Cham occupation and definitively established **Mahayana Buddhism** as the new state cult (his great foundations are Angkor Thom, Bayon, Ta Prohm, Preah Khan, Neak Pean, all primarily Buddhist with Mahayana iconography of [[avalokitesvara]] and [[prajnaparamita-goddess]]). Angkor Wat was not abandoned but was progressively reconfigured: by the 13th–14th centuries some sanctuaries within the complex were re-dedicated to Buddhist use; standing Buddha images (many in the Theravada style consistent with the post-14th-century Khmer turn toward Theravada Buddhism) were installed in the four central galleries and elsewhere. The monument has functioned continuously as a Buddhist site since the late 13th century, currently a Theravada Buddhist temple. **It is the only major Angkor monument never to have been entirely abandoned** — a continuous religious site for ~900 years.

**Continuity through religious turnover.** Angkor Wat is one of the clearest cases in world architectural history of a monument that has functioned successively as Vaishnava temple → Mahayana Buddhist site → Theravada Buddhist site, with the iconographic programme being reframed rather than destroyed at each turn. The Vaishnava bas-reliefs remain visible and intact; later Buddhist images coexist with the inherited Hindu cosmological programme.

## Disputes
- **Funerary-temple status.** The west-orientation of the monument (toward the realm of the dead in Hindu cosmology) and the posthumous name of Suryavarman II ("Paramaviṣṇuloka") are the principal evidence that Angkor Wat was simultaneously a state temple and the funerary monument of the king-as-Vishnu. Cœdès accepted this; some later scholars (e.g., Charles Higham) regard the funerary identification as plausible but not definitive. **Atlas position: catalogue as probable / standard reading.**
- **The astronomical-cosmological measurement programme** (Mannikka 1996; Stencel-Gifford-Morón 1976). Whether the temple's proportional system precisely encodes the yuga calendar / the 432,000-year mahayuga / specific solar and lunar alignments is contested — the measurements do match the cosmological numbers but the question is whether this is intentional encoding or post-hoc numerical pattern-finding. Mainstream Cambodianists treat the cosmological intent as established at the general level (the monument *is* a Mount Meru model) but are cautious about the specific yuga-numerical claims.
- **The "khan-rebellion" myth.** Some popular literature presents Angkor Wat as having been built by gods overnight or as having received contributions from extraterrestrial / antediluvian sources — Tier-4 reception literature only (Hancock's *Heaven's Mirror* 1998 includes Angkor in this frame). Mainstream scholarship rejects this entirely; the monument is securely dated by epigraphy, ceramics, and architectural sequence to the 12th c. and is the product of the documented Khmer construction-engineering tradition.
