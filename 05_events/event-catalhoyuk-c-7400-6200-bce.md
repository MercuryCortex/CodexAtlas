# Çatalhöyük (c. 7,400–6,200 BCE)

## What happened
Çatalhöyük ("forked mound") is a Neolithic proto-urban tell-settlement of two adjacent mounds (East Mound, the principal Neolithic occupation, ~13 hectares; West Mound, Chalcolithic) on the Konya Plain of central Anatolia, ~50 km southeast of modern Konya. The East Mound was occupied from ~7,400 to ~6,200 BCE (late PPNB through early Pottery Neolithic) by a sedentary, agricultural community of as many as 3,500–8,000 persons at peak — making it one of the largest known settlements of its date anywhere in the world. The site was first identified by James Mellaart in 1958 and excavated by him 1961–1965; new long-term excavation began under Ian Hodder in 1993 (Çatalhöyük Research Project, 1993–2018) and has continued in modified form since.

**Architectural and ritual-archaeological features:**

- **Densely packed mud-brick houses with no streets** — access was via roof-hatches; the rooftops formed the public circulation network. Houses were rebuilt repeatedly on the same footprint over multiple generations, creating the deep stratigraphic mound.
- **Intramural burial** — the dead were buried beneath the platforms and floors of houses (the *Natufian → PPNA → PPNB → Çatalhöyük* mortuary tradition continues, with Çatalhöyük's specific elaborations). Selected skulls were sometimes removed, plastered, painted, and curated, in continuity with the broader PPNB plastered-skull tradition (see [[event-jericho-pre-pottery-neolithic-c-9000-bce]]).
- **Wall paintings and reliefs** depicting hunting scenes, leopards, vultures over headless human bodies, a possible "townscape with volcano" (the Hasan Dağ panel, controversial — see Disputes), bucrania (cattle-skull-and-horn installations) embedded in walls and on platforms, and elaborate geometric patterns.
- **Bucrania** — actual aurochs / Bos primigenius skulls and horn-cores, sometimes plastered, mounted on walls and on benches; in some houses multiple bucrania are clustered. The "shrine rooms" of Mellaart's typology were essentially houses with high concentrations of bucrania, wall paintings, and elaborate burials.
- **Figurines** — over 2,000 small clay and stone figurines, predominantly animal (cattle, especially) but with a substantial minority of human and humanoid figures, including the famous "Seated Woman of Çatalhöyük" (Mellaart's "Mother Goddess on a leopard throne") and a range of slim, schematic human figures of indeterminate sex.

The site is inscribed UNESCO World Heritage 2012.

## Religious consequences
- **Çatalhöyük is the most-evidenced single Neolithic case for *household-based* religion in continuity with the dead.** The intramural burials beneath floors, the curated skulls, the bucrania installations, and the wall paintings all cluster within the *house unit* rather than in dedicated communal-shrine buildings. Hodder's "history houses" framework (certain houses functioned as multi-generational ancestral-memory anchors with cumulative burial counts) is the current best interpretive synthesis.
- **The Mellaart "Mother Goddess" interpretation has been substantially revised.** Mellaart 1967 framed Çatalhöyük as the great Neolithic capital of a Mother Goddess cult, with the Seated Woman as the principal cult image, and saw direct continuity to later Anatolian Cybele / Phrygian mother-goddess traditions (and beyond, to Aegean and Mediterranean goddess religion). Subsequent excavation and figurine analysis (Hodder & Meskell 2011, Meskell & Nakamura 2005) found that (a) human figurines are a minority of the corpus, (b) figurines of clearly identifiable sex of either gender are a minority within the human-figurine subset, (c) most figurines were discarded in midden contexts rather than venerated, (d) the iconography is heterogeneous and resists a single-deity reading. The current consensus: there is a recognizable *female-figure* sub-tradition within Çatalhöyük's broader figurine corpus, but the leap from "female figurines exist" to "a unified Mother Goddess cult" is not supported by the evidence as Hodder's team has reread it.
- The Çatalhöyük record nonetheless preserves rich *animal-symbolic* ritual material — bucrania, leopard imagery, vulture-and-corpse imagery, possible bull-leaping precursor scenes — that has been the basis for arguments about cattle-cult and predator-power symbolism that resonate later (Bronze Age Aegean bull-leaping, Anatolian storm-god bull associations). These are *thematic recurrences in the same broad region*, not documented continuous transmission.

## Disputes
- **The "Mother Goddess" controversy is the central interpretive dispute.** Mellaart 1967 vs. Hodder 2006 / Hodder & Meskell 2011 represent the two principal positions, with substantial movement of the scholarly consensus toward Hodder. **A separate, more radical, position** (Marija Gimbutas 1989 *The Language of the Goddess* and successors) built an entire Neolithic-European "Goddess civilization" interpretive program in which Çatalhöyük was a key node; this Gimbutas program is now substantially rejected in mainstream Neolithic archaeology (Conkey & Tringham 1995, Meskell 1995 critique), though it has had enduring influence on popular and feminist-spiritualist reception. **Atlas position: present Mellaart's and Hodder's positions in tension; flag Gimbutas-style readings as a separate reception-history phenomenon belonging to 20th-century goddess-spirituality, not to mainstream Neolithic archaeology.**
- **The Hasan Dağ "townscape and volcano" wall painting.** Mellaart interpreted a wall painting in Building VII.14 as a plan-view of Çatalhöyük itself with the nearby Hasan Dağ volcano in eruption — making it, if correct, the earliest known landscape painting and earliest known volcano-eruption depiction. Schmitt et al. 2014 (*PLOS ONE*) showed Hasan Dağ was geologically active in the relevant period (a tephra-flow date of ~6,900 BCE). Critics (Meece 2006) argue the panel is more plausibly a leopard-skin pattern. The eruption-painting reading is *possible* and recently *re-supported* by geological dating, but not securely established.
- **Çatalhöyük as "earliest city."** The settlement is *proto-urban* in scale (one of the largest Neolithic settlements known) but lacks the differentiated administrative, monumental, and economic infrastructure of later cities (no palaces, no temples-as-such, no clear status hierarchy in burials, no writing). Whether to call it a "city" is a definitional question; the technical term *proto-urban agglomeration* is more careful.
- **Population estimates** range widely (3,500–8,000 at peak); the upper-end figures depend on assumptions about contemporaneous occupation of all visible houses, which is contested.

## Connections
- [[event-jericho-pre-pottery-neolithic-c-9000-bce]] — *parallel-motif* and *shared cultural-region*; the Çatalhöyük intramural-burial-and-curated-skulls tradition is part of the broader PPNB / late-Neolithic Southwest Asian mortuary horizon that includes the Jericho plastered-skull tradition. Not direct continuity (different sub-regions, different specifics), but documented thematic recurrence.
- [[event-gobekli-tepe-c-9500-bce]] — *parallel-motif*; both are Anatolian Neolithic ritual-architectural sites; Göbekli Tepe is ~2,000 years earlier and ~600 km east. Whether anything cultural-continuous connects them is undocumented.
- [[event-cucuteni-trypillia-c-5500-3000-bce]] — *parallel-motif*; later proto-urban Neolithic-Chalcolithic settlement with a comparable abundance of female figurines and ongoing "goddess religion" interpretive disputes.
- **Later Anatolian mother-goddess traditions** ([[cybele]], the Phrygian Mother of the Gods) — *contested-influence*. Mellaart 1967 claimed direct continuity from Çatalhöyük's "Mother Goddess" to Cybele; Hodder and most current scholarship reject the specific continuity claim. The Cybele tradition is securely attested from ~the 7th c. BCE in Phrygia (~6,000+ years after Çatalhöyük's abandonment); no documented continuous transmission exists. **Both positions should be presented.** The Atlas wires this as a *contested-influence* edge: state Mellaart's claim, state the rejection, do not endorse either.
