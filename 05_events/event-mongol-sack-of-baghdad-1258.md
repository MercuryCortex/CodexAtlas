# Mongol Sack of Baghdad

## What happened
In January–February 1258 the Mongol general Hulegu Khan (grandson of Genghis Khan) besieged Baghdad, capital of the Abbasid Caliphate. The city fell after a twelve-day siege; the Mongols sacked Baghdad for a week, killing the inhabitants (death-toll estimates range from 90,000 to 800,000+, with modern scholars generally favoring numbers in the lower end of the range but still in six figures), destroying mosques, palaces, and libraries — including, according to traditional accounts, the Bayt al-Hikma (House of Wisdom) library founded by the early Abbasid caliphs. Caliph al-Mustaʿsim, the last Abbasid caliph, was executed (according to tradition, rolled in a carpet and trampled by horses, to avoid spilling royal blood on the ground). The Abbasid Caliphate, the symbolic center of Sunni Islam since 750, was ended.

## Religious consequences
- Symbolic end of classical Sunni Islamic civilization. The Abbasid Caliphate had been politically attenuated for centuries (Buyid then Seljuk control, by the 13th c. effective Mongol overlordship) but its symbolic role as the focal point of Sunni political-religious continuity ended absolutely.
- Massive loss of Islamic manuscript culture; whether the Bayt al-Hikma specifically was destroyed at this moment or had already substantially diminished earlier is debated (it had been declining for centuries; the 1258 sack was a final blow).
- Shifted the center of Sunni Islamic intellectual life decisively eastward (to Mongol-tolerant or Mongol-converted areas — eventually Mamluk Cairo became the new center) and to Persianate Sufi networks (the Mongol catastrophe drove an enormous expansion of organized Sufism as the institutional vector of Islamic continuity through political chaos).
- The Mongol conversion to Islam (the Ilkhanate under Ghazan Khan, 1295) inaugurated a paradoxical recovery: by 1300 the same dynasty that destroyed Baghdad was patronizing major new Islamic intellectual projects (Rashid al-Din's universal history).

## Documents in play
- The reported destruction of Bayt al-Hikma is the most consequential putative loss; the Tigris was reported to have run black with ink from manuscripts thrown into the river.
- Many manuscripts were rescued (notably by the Twelver Shi'a scholar Nasir al-Din al-Tusi, who became a Mongol advisor and saved substantial scientific and philosophical material; he later founded the Maragheh observatory under Hulegu's patronage).

## Disputes
- Death-toll estimates vary enormously.
- Extent of intellectual destruction is genuinely uncertain; some scholars (Saliba, Ragep) argue Islamic science continued robustly post-1258, against older narratives of cultural-civilizational collapse.
