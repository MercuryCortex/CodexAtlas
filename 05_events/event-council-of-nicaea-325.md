# Council of Nicaea (325)

## What happened
The first ecumenical council of Christianity, convened by Emperor Constantine in May–June 325 at Nicaea in Bithynia, attended by ~250–318 bishops (traditional 318 echoes Abraham's 318 retainers in Genesis 14:14). The primary doctrinal business was the Arian controversy: was the Son co-eternal and consubstantial (*homoousios*) with the Father, or a created intermediate being subordinate to him? The council condemned Arius's subordinationist position and produced the original Nicene Creed affirming the Son as "begotten not made, *homoousios* with the Father." Twenty disciplinary canons addressed clerical issues, jurisdictional disputes, and the date of Easter (decided to be celebrated independently of the Jewish Passover calendar).

## Religious consequences
- Codified Christological orthodoxy in a formula (*homoousios*) that became the test of catholic faith and the boundary against subsequent Arian, semi-Arian, Pneumatomachian, and Monophysite controversies.
- Established the *ecumenical council* as the supreme deliberative body of the Christian world (the format used through Chalcedon 451, Constantinople II 553, etc.).
- Strengthened the alliance of imperial power and theological orthodoxy — Constantine's role as convener established a pattern of imperial involvement in doctrinal disputes that ran throughout late antiquity.

## Disputes
- **Exact attendance.** Ancient sources vary 250–318.
- **Constantine's own theological understanding.** Whether Constantine grasped or merely enforced *homoousios* is debated.
- **Lasting victory.** The Arian controversy did not end at Nicaea — it intensified through the next half-century and was substantially resolved only at the First Council of Constantinople (381).
