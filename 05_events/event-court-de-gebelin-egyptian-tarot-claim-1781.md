# Court de Gébelin Invents the Egyptian Tarot (1781)

## What happened

In 1781, [[antoine-court-de-gebelin]] — Huguenot pastor, Enlightenment comparative philologist, Freemason of the Neuf Sœurs lodge — published volume 8 of his nine-volume ***Le Monde primitif***. Inside it was a short essay, ***Du Jeu des Tarots***.

Court de Gébelin reported that at a friend's house he had been shown a pack of tarot cards and had recognised in them, on sight, a survival of ancient Egypt: the lost **Book of Thoth**, preserved in card form after the temples fell, its 22 trumps encoding Egyptian priestly doctrine, its name derived from Egyptian *tar* ("road") and *ro* ("royal") — the **royal road**. He added that the pack had been carried into Europe out of Egypt by the people then called Bohemians.

Printed with his essay, under the byline "Le Comte de M***", was a second piece by [[comte-de-mellet]]. It proposed something Court de Gébelin had not: that the **22 trumps correspond to the 22 letters of the Hebrew alphabet**. It also gave the earliest published description of how to lay cards out and read them.

Between them, the two essays in one volume of one book, in one year, created: the tarot's Egyptian antiquity, the tarot's Kabbalistic architecture, and the tarot's divinatory method. None of the three existed before 1781.

## The documented reality

- **The pack is a 15th-century northern Italian card game.** Playing cards reach Europe from the Mamluk sultanate in the 1370s; a fifth suit of fixed-rank allegorical trumps is added in northern Italy, probably in the 1420s. The earliest secure documentary reference to *carte da trionfi* is the **1442** court ledger at Ferrara (Dummett 1980). The earliest surviving cards are the [[visconti-sforza-tarocchi-deck]], painted for the Milanese ducal court in the 1440s–1450s: Petrarchan triumphs, Christian virtues, Visconti heraldry. No Egypt.
- **The Egyptian script was undeciphered.** Champollion's *Lettre à M. Dacier* is **1822** — 41 years after the essay. Court de Gébelin's Egyptian etymologies could not have been checked by anyone alive, including him. See [[jean-francois-champollion]].
- **Nobody read fortunes with tarot for roughly three centuries.** The pack was played, taxed, banned, imitated and exported as a *game*; tarocchi and its descendants are still played today in France, Italy, Austria and Hungary. Divinatory use has no evidence before the 18th century (Decker, Depaulis & Dummett 1996).
- **The practice preceded the myth.** [[etteilla-jean-baptiste-alliette]] had been reading ordinary 32-card piquet packs professionally since **1770** — eleven years earlier. He adopted the Egyptian frame in 1783–1785 and built the first divinatory tarot system on it.
- **The Roma claim is chronologically impossible.** Roma presence in western Europe is documented from the early 15th century, after cards were already circulating; and the 16th-century notices of Roma divination describe **palmistry** ([[palmistry-chiromancy]]), not cards.

## Tier and type

This vault records the Egyptian-origin claim as a **T4 claim — popular, rejected**. It is not a contested academic minority position (T2) and not an alternative-school reading with published defenders (T3). It has **no academic defenders at all**, and the refutation is documentary rather than interpretive: an account book in Ferrara dated 1442 and a decipherment dated 1822.

It is included, not deleted, because it is believed by millions and printed on the boxes of decks sold today. The vault's function here is the disclaimer machine: state the claim, name its author, date it, and serve the rebuttal beside it.

The **Kabbalah correspondence** is tiered separately. It is not a false *historical* claim in the same way — it is a **documented 1781 retrofit**, authored by de Mellet, systematised by [[eliphas-levi]] in 1854–1856 and elaborated by the Golden Dawn from 1888. As a piece of intellectual history it is T1-documented; as a claim to *ancient* transmission it is T4-rejected. Both statements are true of the same object, which is why they are carried on separate wires.

## Why this event matters beyond tarot

The vault holds the other half of this event 167 years earlier. In 1614, [[isaac-casaubon]] proved on philological grounds that the *Corpus Hermeticum* was Hellenistic, not pre-Mosaic — see [[event-casaubon-redates-hermetica-1614]] — and the Renaissance edifice of *prisca theologia* collapsed. That node's own text observes that subsequent Western esotericism "had to relocate its claimed antiquity onto different (and equally fragile) foundations."

**1781 is where it landed.** And the landing site was chosen, whether or not anyone chose it consciously, with structural cunning: a deck of pictures has no Greek to date. Casaubon's method — internal linguistic evidence — has nothing to bite on. The tarot's claimed antiquity was, for a century and a half, genuinely unfalsifiable by the tools that had killed the previous claim. It took the archival card-historians of the twentieth century, working from tax records, printers' bills and court inventories, to close it.

The transmission therefore runs **backwards to the popular story**. Not Egypt → tarot. But: Mamluk card game → Italian trump game → French printers → an Enlightenment philologist's misfire → *a claimed Egypt*. The Egyptian connection in the real chain is real and utterly mundane — playing cards did come to Europe from Islamic Egypt, in the 1370s, as a game, with no book of Thoth in them.

## Disputes

- **Who deserves the credit or blame.** Popular accounts merge the two 1781 essays and attribute both the Egyptian thesis and the Hebrew correspondence to Court de Gébelin. The Hebrew mapping is de Mellet's. Decker, Depaulis & Dummett (1996) separate them; this vault follows that separation.
- **Whether an earlier esoteric layer exists in the Italian pack.** Some scholarship (Place 2005, 2012) reads Renaissance Neoplatonic and Christian-mystical content into the original trump sequence — a much weaker claim than Court de Gébelin's, and a legitimate one, since 15th-century Milan was a Neoplatonic milieu. Dummett (1980) and Caldwell, Depaulis & Ponzi (2010) hold that the Renaissance sources themselves read the trumps as a hierarchy of virtues and temporal powers, not as an initiation system. The dispute is about the depth of allegory, not about Egypt.
- **Whether Court de Gébelin acted in good faith.** Nothing in the record suggests otherwise; the tarot essay applies exactly the method of his other eight volumes.
