# Burning of the Library of Alexandria

## What happened
There is no single "burning of the Library of Alexandria." The library — actually a complex of institutions associated with the Ptolemaic Mouseion — declined and was damaged or destroyed over six and a half centuries through at least four major events:

1. **48 BCE.** Julius Caesar's Alexandrian War. Plutarch, Aulus Gellius, and Ammianus Marcellinus report that fire spread from Caesar's burning of the Egyptian fleet in the harbor and burned a quantity of books. The *scope* is contested: some ancient sources speak of 40,000 books, others of the entire library; modern scholarship (Bagnall 2002) holds the damage was significant but partial — likely to dockside warehouses of books rather than the main library complex.
2. **272 CE.** Aurelian's recapture of Alexandria from Zenobia of Palmyra severely damaged the Brucheion quarter where the Mouseion stood.
3. **391 CE.** Theophilus, bishop of Alexandria under Theodosius's anti-pagan legislation, destroyed the Serapeum, the daughter-library temple of Serapis — though the precise library content present at the time is debated.
4. **642 CE (?).** A medieval legend (Bar Hebraeus, 13th c.) attributes a final destruction to the Arab conqueror Amr ibn al-As acting on orders from Caliph Umar ("If they agree with the Quran, they are superfluous; if they disagree, they are heretical; in either case, burn them"). Modern historians overwhelmingly regard this story as apocryphal — invented centuries after the conquest, probably as Christian-Muslim polemic.

## Religious consequences
- *Symbolic.* The "burning of the Library" is the most-invoked emblem of the destruction of ancient knowledge in Western cultural memory, often deployed in religious-polemical narratives (Gibbon's Christian-blaming, modern Muslim-blaming).
- *Actual textual loss.* Most actual loss of ancient texts was the slow attrition of papyrus, parchment, and copying labor over centuries — not the spectacular destruction the popular narrative emphasizes.

## Documents in play
- Plutarch, *Life of Caesar* 49 — 48 BCE damage.
- Ammianus Marcellinus, *Res Gestae* 22.16 — sober Roman testimony.
- Eunapius, *Lives of the Sophists* — Serapeum 391.
- Bar Hebraeus, *Chronicon Syriacum* (13th c.) — the apocryphal Amr ibn al-As story.

## Disputes
This entry is structured around the *historiographical dispute itself*: there was no single Library-burning event. The popular narrative compresses centuries of slow decline plus localized destructions into one dramatic catastrophe, usually attributed for polemical reasons to whichever group the narrator opposes (anti-Christian, anti-Muslim, anti-Roman). Modern critical historiography (Bagnall 2002, MacLeod 2000) rejects the simple-burning narrative and reconstructs a process of decline.
