# Fourth Buddhist Council under Kaniṣka (~100-150 CE)

## What happened
Under the patronage of the Kushan emperor [[kanishka|Kaniṣka I]] (r. ~127-150), a Buddhist council was convened in Kashmir, with 500 Sarvāstivāda monk-scholars compiling and standardizing the Sarvāstivāda canonical tradition. The council produced the *Mahāvibhāṣā* (*Great Commentary*) — a massive Abhidharma commentarial compendium that became the foundational text of the Sarvāstivāda school. The Council is recognized as the Fourth Buddhist Council by the Sarvāstivāda and later Mahāyāna traditions; the Theravāda tradition does not recognize it (and instead counts a different Fourth Council, the Theravāda Sri Lankan council of ~-29 BCE that produced the first writing-down of the Pali canon at Aluvihāra). The Kashmir Council is generally considered the institutional moment of Buddhist canonical Sanskritization — the transition from Prakrit (Gandhārī, Pali, etc.) to Sanskrit as the literary language of northern Indian Buddhism — and is closely associated with the cultural-religious milieu in which Mahāyāna Buddhism crystallizes as a distinct movement.

## Religious consequences
- **Sarvāstivāda canonical consolidation**: the *Mahāvibhāṣā* becomes the foundational text of the Sarvāstivāda Abhidharma tradition. Through the Sarvāstivāda's enormous influence on later Buddhist scholasticism, the *Mahāvibhāṣā* indirectly shapes both [[vasubandhu|Vasubandhu's]] *Abhidharmakośa* (which substantially synthesizes Sarvāstivāda doctrine and was translated into Chinese by [[xuanzang]] 651-654) and the entire subsequent Indian Buddhist commentarial tradition.
- **Sanskritization**: Sarvāstivāda canonical literature was redacted into Sanskrit at this period (whether at this council specifically or in the broader Kushan-Gandhāran milieu it represents). This is a major linguistic-canonical transition; before, Buddhist literature had been transmitted primarily in Prakrits (Pali, Gāndhārī, Magadhi). After, Sanskrit becomes the dominant literary language of northern Indian Buddhism, with consequences for the subsequent Mahāyāna corpus (which is entirely Sanskrit/Buddhist Hybrid Sanskrit).
- **Mahāyāna emergence**: the Kushan-Gandhāran milieu under Kaniṣka is the institutional matrix in which Mahāyāna Buddhism crystallizes. The earliest extant Mahāyāna manuscripts are Gāndhārī Mahāyāna texts (Schøyen Collection; British Library Kharosthi mss) from this milieu (~1st-3rd c. CE). Kaniṣka's coinage includes the "BODDO" type — the earliest dated Buddha image-coinage. While the Council itself is Sarvāstivāda (not Mahāyāna), it is part of the broader Kushan-period institutional consolidation that supports Mahāyāna's emergence.
- **Greco-Buddhist Gandhāran art consolidation**: the Kaniṣka period is the high-water mark of Gandhāran Buddhist sculpture — the workshop tradition that creates the first anthropomorphic Buddha images in Apollo-styled form. This is the iconographic vocabulary that becomes universally East Asian Buddhist art via Silk Road transmission (see [[tradition-greco-buddhism]]).

## Documents in play
- ***Mahāvibhāṣā*** (大毘婆沙論, T 1545) — produced at the council; preserved in Chinese in Xuanzang's translation (656-659 CE); ~200 fascicles. The Sanskrit original is lost.
- **Sarvāstivāda canonical literature** — Sanskritized in this period; Gilgit Sanskrit Buddhist manuscripts (~5th-6th c. CE) are partial witnesses to the Sarvāstivāda corpus as standardized at this council.
- **[[vasubandhu|Vasubandhu's]] *Abhidharmakośa*** (~4th c. CE) — the great synthesis of Sarvāstivāda Abhidharma that draws substantially on the Mahāvibhāṣā tradition.

## Disputes
- **Historicity**: the Sarvāstivāda Fourth Council is attested in Sarvāstivāda and Chinese Buddhist tradition; the Theravāda does not recognize it. Modern scholarship (Frauwallner 1956; Williamen et al. 1998; Williams 2008) accepts the broad outline — Kushan-period Sarvāstivāda canonical consolidation and production of the Mahāvibhāṣā — while remaining cautious about elaborate Kushan-tradition canonical claims.
- **Date**: traditional dating ranges from ~78 CE (early Kushan-era hypothesis) to ~150 CE (late Kaniṣka). Falk 2001's astronomical establishment of Kaniṣka's accession at 127 CE places the council in the 127-150 window.
- **Location**: Kashmir is universal; precise site (Kuṇḍalavana / Jālandhar — Xuanzang reports the latter) is disputed.
- **Kaniṣka's personal role**: was Kaniṣka a Buddhist convert who convened the council? Cribb's reading (Bactrian-Iranian polytheist Kushan with strong Buddhist patronage among other religious patronages) vs. the maximalist Buddhist hagiographic claim. The institutional patronage is uncontested; personal conversion is debated.
- **Relation to Mahāyāna**: was the council a Mahāyāna event or a Sarvāstivāda event? The latter — institutionally Sarvāstivāda. The Mahāyāna milieu is parallel and contemporaneous but the council itself is not Mahāyāna.
