# Aśokan dhamma-missions to the Hellenistic kingdoms (~-256 BCE)

## What happened
In approximately the 13th year of his reign (~-256), [[asoka-maurya|Aśoka]] dispatched dhamma-envoys to multiple regions outside his immediate empire — including, by his own epigraphic claim in **Major Rock Edict XIII**, five named Hellenistic kingdoms of the Mediterranean and Levantine world. The edict's relevant passage:

> "And it is conquest by Dhamma that the Beloved of the Gods considers to be the best conquest. And it (conquest by Dhamma) has been won here, on the borders, even six hundred yojanas away, where the Greek king Antiochos rules, and beyond there where the four kings named Ptolemy, Antigonos, Magas, and Alexander rule..."

The five named kings — identified by Bühler 1893, Hultzsch 1925, Tarn 1938, Eggermont 1969:
- **Antiochus II Theos** of the Seleucid empire (r. -261 to -246)
- **Ptolemy II Philadelphus** of Ptolemaic Egypt (r. -283 to -246)
- **Antigonus II Gonatas** of Macedonia (r. -276 to -239)
- **Magas of Cyrene** in modern Libya (r. -276 to -250)
- **Alexander II of Epirus** in northwest Greece (r. -272 to -255)

The mutual-reign overlap window narrows the edict's composition to ~-256 (when all five were simultaneously reigning). Parallel Aśokan dhamma-missions per the Pali Mahāvaṃsa chs. 4-5 also went to Sri Lanka (Mahinda), Suvarṇabhūmi (Burma / Thailand), the Himalayan region, Kashmir-Gandhāra, and other South Asian regions — a coordinated multi-directional proselytization program. The Kandahar Greek-Aramaic bilingual edict (also Aśokan, found 1958) demonstrates the linguistic infrastructure for the Hellenistic missions: Aśoka's dhamma was deliberately translated into Greek and Aramaic for the Hellenistic frontier population, using the Greek philosophical term *eusebeia* ("piety") as the translation-equivalent of *dhamma*.

## Religious consequences
- **The first attested Indian religious outreach to the Hellenistic West**: this is the foundational moment of [[tradition-greco-buddhism]]. The dhamma-missions establish the diplomatic, linguistic, and proselytic infrastructure within which subsequent Indo-Greek Buddhism (~-200 to +50), Greco-Buddhist Gandhāran art (~+1 to +400), and the Buddhist conversion of Indo-Greek and Kushan rulers ([[menander-i-soter|Menander I]], [[kanishka|Kaniṣka]]) operates.
- **Sri Lankan Theravāda foundation**: Mahinda's mission to Sri Lanka (~-247) founds the Anurādhapura Mahāvihāra and the Sinhalese Theravāda lineage — the unbroken chain that becomes [[tradition-theravada-buddhism]] and through it the Buddhist transmission to Burma, Thailand, Cambodia, Laos, and modern global vipassanā.
- **Southeast Asian transmission**: the Suvarṇabhūmi mission (Soṇa and Uttara) is the foundational charter for Southeast Asian Buddhism. The historical reality of a Mauryan-era mission to Lower Burma/Thailand is debated; the Mahāvaṃsa account is treated as substantially mythologized by Bronkhorst 2007 but as carrying historical core by Lahiri 2015.
- **Greek-language Buddhist outreach**: the Kandahar bilingual edict's use of *eusebeia* as the Greek translation of *dhamma* is the foundational *terminus technicus* choice of Hellenistic-Buddhist translation. Whether dhamma-envoys actually reached the named Hellenistic courts and what their reception was — debated. Tarn 1938 was skeptical; Halkias 2014 + Lahiri 2015 take the Aśokan claim more seriously, noting that the Hellenistic world's later Buddha-mentions (Clement of Alexandria's "Boutta of the Indians"; later interest from Pyrrhonist circles) are circumstantially consistent with a longer-arc reception.

## Documents in play
- **[[phase-3-030-asokan-edicts|Aśokan Major Rock Edict XIII]]** (~-256) — the primary source. Inscribed at Girnar, Kalsi, Shahbazgarhi, Mansehra, Erragudi, and Yerragudi-fragments.
- **Aśokan Kandahar Greek-Aramaic bilingual edict** (~-256-250; found 1958) — Aśoka's dhamma message translated into Greek and Aramaic for the Hellenistic-frontier population.
- **Pali Mahāvaṃsa chs. 12-13** (~5th c. CE Sri Lankan chronicle) — the principal Theravāda narrative source for the dhamma-missions, especially Mahinda's mission to Sri Lanka.
- **Pali Dīpavaṃsa** (~4th c. CE) — earlier Sri Lankan chronicle, parallel content.

## Disputes
- **Historical reality of the Hellenistic missions**: did dhamma-envoys actually reach the named Hellenistic courts? Tarn 1938 was skeptical, treating Rock Edict XIII as Aśokan rhetorical bombast. Modern scholarship (Lahiri 2015; Halkias 2014; Olivelle 2023) generally takes the Aśokan claim at face value, noting that (a) the precise overlap of the five kings' reigns rules out post-hoc fabrication, (b) the contemporary Kandahar bilingual edict demonstrates a deliberate Greek-language translation infrastructure, and (c) the later Hellenistic-world Buddha-mentions (Clement; Hippolytus; the "gymnosophist" tradition) are circumstantially consistent.
- **Reception**: what reception did the missions get? No Hellenistic-side source mentions an arriving Buddhist mission. This is consistent with either (a) the missions arrived but were absorbed into the broader category of "Indian wisdom" without leaving distinct documentary trace, or (b) the missions arrived only as the edict's rhetorical performance, not as a sustained Aśokan diplomatic-religious initiative. Modern scholarship is open to either reading; the truth is probably in between.
- **Sri Lankan mission timing**: traditional Pali dating is ~-247; modern scholarship accepts the ~-250-240 range. The foundational charter status of the mission is uncontested even if precise dates are uncertain.
- **Identity of "Yonaka Dhammarakkhita"**: the Aparantaka-bound missionary's name *Yonaka* ("Greek") suggests a Greek-origin or Greek-region-origin missionary — possibly an Indo-Greek convert. The Mahāvaṃsa preserves this identification without elaboration. Halkias 2014 treats it as load-bearing evidence of Greek-Buddhist personnel in the Aśokan saṅgha.
