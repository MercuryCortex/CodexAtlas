# Death of Hypatia (March 415)

## What happened
In March 415, Hypatia of Alexandria — the leading philosopher of the city, a Neoplatonist mathematician and astronomer in her late 50s, daughter and successor of the mathematician Theon — was attacked while traveling through the city, dragged from her chariot to the Caesarion (a former temple of the imperial cult that had been converted to a church), stripped, killed with broken roof-tiles or potsherds (*ostraka*), her body torn apart and burned. The attack was carried out by a Christian mob with strong identification with the patriarchate of Alexandria, then headed by Cyril; Socrates Scholasticus (*HE* VII.15) names Peter the Reader as the ringleader.

The political context: Cyril had been engaged in a power struggle with Orestes, the imperial prefect, since 412 — over the patriarchate's appropriation of judicial authority, over the expulsion of the Alexandrian Jewish community (414), and over the role of the *parabalani* (a lay brotherhood attached to the patriarchate, used as enforcers). Hypatia was Orestes's close friend and political adviser. Her murder neutralized the most influential pagan figure backing Orestes; Orestes himself disappears from the historical record shortly thereafter.

## Religious consequences
- **A landmark in the violent end of late-antique paganism.** Hypatia is, in the popular and scholarly imagination, the symbol of philosophical, female, and pagan Alexandria destroyed by Christian mob violence. Watts 2017 cautions that the symbolic load is heavier than the actual evidence — Hypatia was not the last pagan philosopher (Damascius, Simplicius, etc. all post-date her) — but the symbolic weight is real and historically influential.
- **Cyril of Alexandria's reputation.** Even hostile sources (Damascius) and friendly ones (Socrates) record uneasiness about the patriarch's relationship to the killing. The imperial response was a temporary reduction of the patriarchal *parabalani* (by Theodosian Code 16.2.42, autumn 416), suggesting Constantinople held Cyril responsible.
- **A foreshadowing of 529.** The Justinianic Closure of the Athenian Academy in 529 completes, institutionally, what 415 had done to one of the principal pagan philosophical centers.

## Substrate connections
- Hypatia stood at the **continuation of the great Alexandrian Platonist mathematical-philosophical tradition** — her commentaries on Diophantus, Apollonius, and Ptolemy; her father Theon's edition of Euclid (the textually dominant edition for the next 1,500 years); her teaching of philosophy in the Plotinian tradition. With her death, Alexandrian pagan Neoplatonism does not end (her students continue), but it is decisively wounded in its most public and political form. Watts 2017.
- The destruction of pagan Alexandria — the 391 destruction of the Serapeum (under Bishop Theophilus, Cyril's uncle), the 415 killing of Hypatia, the gradual loss of philosophical patronage — represents **the largest single subtraction of substrate from the late-antique Christian world**. Hellenistic Alexandria had been one of the densest concentration of philosophical, religious, scientific, and esoteric traditions in human history (Hermetic literature, Philonic Judaism, Origenist Christianity, Plotinian Platonism, the Library and Museum). The 4th–5th-c. closure of these institutions by Christian authority is one of the historically pivotal substrate-erasure events.

## Documents in play
- **Socrates Scholasticus, *HE* VII.15** — the primary near-contemporary account (Christian author writing ~440); openly condemns the act.
- **Damascius, *Vita Isidori* (Life of Isidore)** — preserved in Photius (cod. 242); pagan-philosophical perspective, blames Cyril more directly.
- **John of Nikiu, *Chronicle* 84** — a 7th-c. Coptic Christian source defending the killers' actions on the grounds that Hypatia was a "Satanic" sorceress.
- **The letters of Synesius of Cyrene** — Hypatia's student and friend; principal source for her teaching and intellectual circle.

## Disputes
- **Cyril's direct involvement.** Sources range from direct responsibility (Damascius) to climate-setting (Socrates) to denial (Coptic tradition).
- **Hypatia's age at death.** 60 (Suda); some scholars argue early 60s, some late 30s — the 30s reading would put her birth around 380, the 60s reading around 350. The case for the older reading (Watts 2017, Dzielska 1995) is now the consensus.
- **The instruments of death.** *Ostraka* (broken roof-tiles) is the most common reading of Socrates's terminology; Damascius (more lurid) speaks of being dragged through the streets with her flesh scraped off with sherds.
