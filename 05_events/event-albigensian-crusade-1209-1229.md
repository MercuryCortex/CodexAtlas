# Albigensian Crusade

## What happened
A two-decade military and inquisitorial campaign (1209–1229) launched by Pope Innocent III against the Cathars (also called Albigensians, from the city of Albi in Languedoc) and the protecting nobility of the County of Toulouse and surrounding Occitan territories. The casus belli was the 1208 assassination of the papal legate Peter of Castelnau on Raymond VI of Toulouse's lands. Northern French nobility, led by Simon de Montfort the Elder (1209–1218) and later by King Louis VIII (1226), conducted brutal campaigns: the massacre of Béziers (1209, traditionally invoked through the legendary order *Caedite eos! Novit enim Dominus qui sunt eius* — "Kill them all; God will know his own"); sieges of Carcassonne, Toulouse, and Cathar fortress-strongholds (Lavaur, Minerve, Termes, Cabaret, Montségur — the last falling in 1244, after the crusade's formal end). The Treaty of Paris (1229) ended the military phase by transferring Languedoc to direct royal control. The Inquisition (papal Inquisition founded 1234 by Gregory IX, structured by Innocent IV's *Ad extirpanda* 1252) continued the persecution; the last named Cathar perfectus, Guillaume Bélibaste, was burned 1321.

## Religious consequences
- Effective extermination of organized Catharism in Western Europe by ~1320.
- Systematic destruction of Cathar literature — the surviving Cathar texts ([[phase-5-020-interrogatio-iohannis]], [[phase-5-023-liber-de-duobus-principiis]], Cathar Latin New Testament fragments) are recovered only through accidental preservation in inquisitorial archives or in monastic libraries that did not know what they contained.
- Foundation of the Dominican order (1216) and the institutional Inquisition (1234) as the principal anti-heretical institutions of the medieval Church; both have substantial historical legacies.
- Politically, the extermination of southern French aristocratic independence and the absorption of Languedoc into the French royal demesne.

## Documents in play
- Substantial inquisitorial records preserved at Toulouse, Carcassonne, and elsewhere — these are now the principal sources for reconstructing Cathar belief and practice. The records' nature (extracted under coercion, organized through inquisitorial categories) is a major historiographic issue (Pegg 2001 develops this critique).

## Disputes
- The actual coherence of Cathar belief as a distinct dualist religion vs. the inquisitorial *construction* of "Catharism" as a heresy-category. Moore 2012 and Pegg 2001 / 2008 argue the latter substantially; Lambert 1998 and Stoyanov 2000 defend the former.
- The massacre at Béziers — the death-toll (variously reported between several thousand and 20,000) is uncertain.
- **[[tradition-knights-templar|Templar]] non-participation.** Templars held substantial properties in Languedoc but did not participate in the Crusade armies. Whether this absence reflects doctrinal sympathy with Cathars (the speculative thesis), Outremer-commitment preoccupation (the consensus position, Barber 1994 / Nicholson 2001), or local landlord-tenant complexity (the granular position) is debated. The non-participation is a real historical fact; its interpretation is the [[templar-gnostic-transmission-hypothesis|Templar-Gnostic transmission hypothesis]] in one of its most defensible variants.
