# Haitian Revolution (Bois Caïman to Haitian Independence)

## What happened
A successful slave revolution in the French colony of Saint-Domingue, beginning August 22, 1791 (a week after the August 14 Bois Caïman ceremony), and ending January 1, 1804, with Jean-Jacques Dessalines's declaration of Haitian independence — the first independent Black republic and the second independent state in the Americas. Across 13 years of warfare against French colonial forces (including Napoleon's massive 1802 expeditionary force), British forces (1793–98), and Spanish forces (1793–95), the enslaved Africans of Saint-Domingue defeated three European empires in succession. The Haitian Revolution is the only successful slave revolution in modern history and was the most consequential challenge to the Atlantic slave system before its abolition.

## Religious consequences
- *Vodou as constitutive revolutionary religion.* The Bois Caïman ceremony of August 14, 1791 — led by Dutty Boukman and Cécile Fatiman — is treated as the founding ritual moment of both Haitian Vodou and the Haitian Revolution; the two traditions cannot be cleanly separated. Vodou served as the religious-cultural infrastructure that coordinated revolutionary mobilization across plantation, ethnic-African, and linguistic divisions.
- *Catholic Church-state realignment.* The Haitian Church's relation to Rome was contested for decades; the Concordat of 1860 formally restored relations on terms acknowledging some Vodou-influenced religious-cultural reality.
- *Trans-Atlantic abolitionist catalyst.* The Haitian Revolution accelerated abolitionism in Britain (1807 slave-trade ban, 1833 Emancipation Act), France (final abolition 1848), and influenced US abolitionist thought (and slaveholder paranoia — slaveholder fears of "another Haiti" shaped pro-slavery politics across the antebellum South).
- *Pan-African / Black-national religious-political imaginary.* Haiti became foundational for subsequent Black-national and pan-African movements — Garveyism, Rastafarianism, the négritude movement, post-WWII African and Caribbean independence movements all drew on Haitian-revolutionary precedent.
- *Vodou's continued contested status.* Haitian state, Catholic Church, and external observers have variously suppressed, tolerated, and (since 2003) officially recognized Vodou; the long aftermath of the Revolution is partly the long aftermath of the religious question.

## Documents in play
- The Boukman prayer (transmitted orally, recorded in various 19th-c. sources beginning with Antoine Dalmas 1814 and Hérard Dumesle 1824).
- Haitian Declaration of Independence (January 1, 1804).
- Dessalines's 1805 Constitution (notable for outlawing slavery permanently and for symbolic religious-cultural provisions).
- Indigenous (Indigenista-Haitien) historiography from Vastey, Madiou, Ardouin, etc.

## Disputes
- **Bois Caïman historicity.** Hoffmann 1990 controversially argued the ceremony was substantially mythologized; Geggus 2002 (leading current historian) accepts the ceremony's reality but is cautious about details. Mainstream consensus: the religious-revolutionary inauguration in mid-August 1791 occurred, the specific details are partly mythologized.
- **Religious vs. political weighting.** Whether Vodou is *constitutive* of the Revolution or one mobilizing factor among many is intensively debated.
- **Long aftermath.** The 19th-c. isolation, indemnity to France (1825), repeated foreign interventions (US occupation 1915–34), and ongoing instability are causally connected to the Revolution in various contested ways.
