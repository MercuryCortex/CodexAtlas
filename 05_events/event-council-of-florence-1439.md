# The Council of Florence and the Platonic Renaissance (1439)

## Summary

The **Council of Ferrara-Florence (1438–1439)** was called by Pope Eugenius IV to negotiate reunion between the Eastern (Byzantine Orthodox) and Western (Roman Catholic) churches, with both sides hoping that a unified Christendom might resist the Ottoman advance on Constantinople. It failed theologically — the reunion agreement collapsed almost immediately on return to Constantinople. But it succeeded in something its conveners never intended: **it transferred Byzantine Neoplatonic scholarship to Italian soil**, setting in motion the chain of events that produced Marsilio Ficino, the Platonic Academy, the translation of the *Corpus Hermeticum*, and the entire Hermetic Renaissance.

The Council is the **hinge event** between two epochs in the Western mystical tradition:
- Before 1439: the living chain of Neoplatonic philosophy runs through Byzantine Constantinople (Proclus → Byzantine commentators → Plethon)
- After 1439: it runs through Florence (Plethon → Cosimo de' Medici → Ficino → Pico → Agrippa → Bruno → Rosicrucianism → Masonry)

## The Intellectual Event Within the Political Event

Accompanying the Byzantine diplomatic delegation was **[[gemistos-plethon]]** — then in his eighties, an extraordinary scholar who had spent decades studying Platonic and Neoplatonic philosophy in Constantinople and Mystras. Plethon was not there in an official capacity but as an intellectual celebrity.

During the Council, Plethon gave informal lectures to Florentine humanists on the philosophy of Plato — specifically, on the differences between Plato and Aristotle (the topic of his text *De Differentiis Aristotelis et Platonis*). His argument: **Aristotle's philosophy had corrupted the true Platonic tradition**, which alone provided the metaphysical foundations for both theology and natural philosophy. The Neoplatonic triad (the One / Intellect / Soul) was the true structure of reality; Christian theology was best understood through Platonic rather than Aristotelian categories.

This was not a new claim, but Plethon's Greek fluency, his access to original Platonic texts, and his evident conviction that this was a living philosophical system (not merely ancient history) electrified his Italian audience.

## Cosimo de' Medici's Decision

**Cosimo de' Medici** (1389–1464), the effective ruler of Florence and the most powerful patron of learning in Europe, was profoundly affected by Plethon's lectures. The later tradition — recorded by Ficino himself — holds that Cosimo conceived the idea of founding a Platonic Academy in Florence on the spot, to revive Plato's Academy in Italy.

Whether this precise moment of inspiration is historical or legendary, the outcome is documented: Cosimo de' Medici:
1. Funded Marsilio Ficino's entire career from an early age, providing him with a villa (Careggi) and a library
2. Commissioned Ficino to translate all of Plato's dialogues into Latin
3. When Greek manuscripts of the *Corpus Hermeticum* arrived from Macedonia in 1462, **ordered Ficino to stop the Plato translation and translate Hermes Trismegistus first** — because he believed Hermes was older than Plato and the source of Plato's wisdom

That last decision — prioritizing Hermes over Plato because of the *prisca theologia* principle that Plethon had articulated — is the single most consequential editorial decision in the history of Western esotericism.

## The Fall of Constantinople as Accelerant (1453)

Fourteen years after the Council of Florence, the Ottoman capture of Constantinople (1453) sent a second wave of Greek scholars westward — including **Cardinal Bessarion** (who brought his extraordinary Greek library to Rome, eventually bequeathing it to Venice as the nucleus of the Biblioteca Marciana). The fall confirmed the western relocation of Byzantine scholarship and made Ficino's Platonic project a preservation mission as well as an intellectual one.
