# Burning of Jan Hus (July 6, 1415)

## What Happened
On **July 6, 1415**, at the **Council of Constance** (the church council convened to resolve the Western Schism and reform the church), **Jan Hus** — rector of Charles University Prague and the leading voice of the Czech reform movement — was burned at the stake after being condemned for heresy.

The sequence:
1. Hus had traveled to Constance in 1414 **under a letter of safe-conduct** from Holy Roman Emperor Sigismund
2. Despite the safe-conduct, he was arrested upon arrival and imprisoned for 7 months
3. At his trial, he was given an opportunity to recant; he refused, stating he could not recant doctrines he had never held, and that he could not recant views he believed to be true
4. Found guilty of heresy on 30 counts (largely based on Wyclif's teaching attributed to him)
5. July 6, 1415: stripped of priestly vestments, a paper crown painted with devils placed on his head, taken to the stake
6. Eyewitness accounts report that as the flames rose, Hus sang psalms until he could no longer be heard
7. His ashes were thrown into the Rhine to prevent veneration

The violation of the safe-conduct — a legal document issued by the Emperor — shocked contemporaries. The standard medieval justification was that "faith need not be kept with heretics" (*fides non est servanda hereticis*).

## Jan Hus's Reforms
Hus's theological program was not radical by later Reformation standards:
- **Communion in both kinds** (*sub utraque specie* — bread AND wine for the laity, where Rome reserved the cup for priests alone) — the central Hussite demand
- **The authority of Scripture over the authority of the Pope** — Wyclif's principle, applied to the Bohemian context
- **Criticism of simony** (the buying and selling of church offices) and clerical immorality
- **Czech-language preaching** at the Bethlehem Chapel, Prague — vernacular ministry as a form of empowerment

He was influenced by **John Wyclif** (1328–1384), the English reformer who had attacked papal authority and transubstantiation; Hus was less radical than Wyclif on transubstantiation but inherited the Scripture-over-Pope framework.

## The Hussite Wars — Persecution's Immediate Consequence
Hus's execution triggered one of the most dramatic persecution-as-legitimation sequences in Christian history:

- **1419**: The **First Defenestration of Prague** — Hussite protesters throw Catholic city councillors from windows; the Hussite Wars begin
- **1420–1434**: **Five Crusades** launched against the Hussites by the Holy Roman Empire — all five **defeated** by the Hussite armies under Jan Žižka (who became blind during the wars and still won)
- **1436**: The **Compacts of Basel** — the Catholic Church officially grants Bohemia communion in both kinds (the cup to the laity) — Hus's central demand, conceded 21 years after his death
- The Hussite movement becomes the **first successful Reformation** — a century before Luther

**The Council of Constance executed Jan Hus to eliminate a reform movement. It instead created a military-political revolution that won the church's concession of Hus's central demand.**

## The Luther Connection
Martin Luther famously declared at the Leipzig Debate (1519): **"We are all Hussites without knowing it."** Luther had read Hus's *On the Church* in Erfurt and recognized his own position already articulated a century earlier. Hus's burning at Constance (1415) → Luther's Wittenberg (1517) is not a coincidence but a direct intellectual and spiritual genealogy.

The Bohemian Brethren (*Unitas Fratrum*), direct descendants of the Hussite movement, became the **Moravians** — the 18th-century Protestant missionary movement that would profoundly influence John Wesley and the birth of Methodism. A line runs: Hus burned (1415) → Hussite wars → Bohemian Brethren → Moravians → Wesley's conversion → Methodist movement worldwide.

## The Safe-Conduct Betrayal as Political-Theological Problem
The violation of Hus's imperial safe-conduct raised a question that occupied canon lawyers for decades: can a church council override an imperial promise to a heretic? The Council of Constance said yes. The damage to Catholic political credibility among Central European princes contributed significantly to the soil in which Luther's Reformation grew a century later.

Emperor Sigismund was haunted by the broken pledge for the rest of his reign — it permanently damaged his standing in Bohemia and contributed to the nearly 20-year Hussite Wars that devastated his empire.
