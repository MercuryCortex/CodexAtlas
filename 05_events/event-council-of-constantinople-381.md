# First Council of Constantinople (381)

## What happened
Theodosius I, having issued the Edict of Thessalonica (380) making Nicene Christianity the state religion, convoked a synod of eastern bishops at Constantinople in May–July 381 to settle the long-running theological controversies of the post-Nicene era. About 150 bishops attended, all from the eastern provinces (Rome and the West were not represented; the council was originally regarded as a regional synod and only retroactively elevated to ecumenical status). It (1) reaffirmed the Nicene Creed of 325, (2) added or reworked language on the Holy Spirit to condemn the *Pneumatomachoi* ("Spirit-fighters") who had denied the divinity of the Spirit, producing the Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed now said at most Christian liturgies, (3) condemned a list of contemporary heresies (Eunomians, Anomoeans, Arians, Pneumatomachians, Sabellians, Marcellians, Photinians, Apollinarians), and (4) issued seven (in some collections, four) disciplinary canons — the third of which ranked Constantinople "second only to Rome" in honor, foreshadowing the long East-West ecclesiastical struggle.

## Religious consequences
- **Sealed the Trinitarian settlement.** The Spirit's divinity was now formally proclaimed; the developed Cappadocian Trinitarian theology (three *hypostases* in one *ousia*) became orthodox.
- **Closed the Arian controversy in the East.** The 60-year doctrinal struggle launched at Nicaea was, in catholic-orthodox terms, settled. Arianism survived only among the Germanic peoples (Goths, Vandals, Lombards) until the 6th–7th c.
- **Established Constantinople's ecclesiastical status.** Canon 3 set up the East-West tension that would peak at Chalcedon 451 (Canon 28) and again in the medieval period.
- **The Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed** is the form that has been said for 1,600 years across Christian liturgies (with the later western *Filioque* addition).

## Substrate connections
- The Cappadocian Trinitarian theology that the council ratified is methodologically structured around the Aristotelian-Stoic vocabulary of *ousia* / *hypostasis* / *prosopon* — Greek philosophical substance-metaphysics applied to the Christian God. The form of the Niceno-Constantinopolitan settlement is unintelligible without the philosophical substrate.
- The Constantinopolitan setting itself — the New Rome, founded by Constantine on the site of Byzantium — is geographically loaded: the city's pagan cults (most famously of Hekate, savior-deity of the city in 340 BCE in the legend Constantine deliberately appropriated), and the wider Greek-Thracian religious substrate, were assimilated into the imperial-Christian capital's ideology. Limberis 1994, *Divine Heiress*, on Constantinopolitan Mary in the substrate of Hekate / Tyche cult of the city.
- The condemnation of *Pneumatomachoi* (esp. Macedonius, the deposed bishop of Constantinople of the 350s) is the formal closure of a long debate over the Spirit's status, which had been complicated by the easy alignment of the Greek philosophical *pneuma* (Stoic world-spirit) with subordinationist readings of the Holy Spirit. The catholic Spirit is now decisively co-divine, not a high creature.

## Documents in play
- The **Nicene-Constantinopolitan Creed** itself — the conciliar text.
- The **seven canons** (four in Latin transmission, seven in Greek).
- *Tomus* of the council to Theodosius (the official letter conveying the decisions).
- Theodosius's edict *Cunctos populos* (380) is the political precondition.

## Disputes
- **The creed's relation to the Nicene.** Whether the 381 creed is a revision of the 325 creed or a separate, parallel document is a complex textual question. Modern critical work (Kelly 1972, *Early Christian Creeds*) shows it is closer to a local Constantinopolitan baptismal creed expanded with Nicene Trinitarian language than to a direct revision of N325.
- **The council's "ecumenical" status.** It was a regional Eastern synod at the time; it was Chalcedon (451), 70 years later, that ratified Constantinople 381 as the Second Ecumenical Council. Latin sources only acknowledge its theological-creedal authority, not always its canonical authority (esp. Canon 3 on Constantinople's rank).
- **Apollinarianism's condemnation.** The council condemned Apollinarianism (the third condemnation in three years, after Rome 377 and Antioch 379), but the formal anti-Apollinarian formula ("perfect God and perfect man, with a rational soul") is sometimes attributed only to the council's correspondence rather than to its official creedal acts.
