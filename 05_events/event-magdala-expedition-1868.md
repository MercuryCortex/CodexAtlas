# British Magdala Expedition (1868) and Manuscript Removal

## What happened
In 1867–1868, a British Imperial expeditionary force commanded by Field Marshal Sir Robert Napier marched into the Ethiopian highlands to free a group of European hostages held by Emperor **Tewodros II** at the mountain fortress of **Maqdala** (or *Magdala*; Amhara region). Tewodros — whose tortured diplomatic relations with Britain had culminated in his 1864 imprisonment of European missionaries and envoys including the British consul Charles Cameron — committed suicide on 13 April 1868 as British forces took the fortress. In addition to liberating the hostages, the British force removed approximately **350 to 500 Ge'ez manuscripts** from Maqdala's library, plus numerous gold processional crosses, royal regalia (including Tewodros's crown), tabotat (replicas of the Ark of the Covenant tablets — sacred objects of every Ethiopian church), and the *Maqdala Library* generally.

The manuscripts and objects were dispersed to British institutions:

- **The British Museum / British Library** (largest single holding; Wright's 1877 catalogue describes ~530 Ethiopian manuscripts in the BM collection at that date, many from Magdala);
- **The Bodleian Library, Oxford** (substantial holdings);
- **The Royal Collection at Windsor** (Tewodros's crown);
- **The India Office Library** and other secondary holdings;
- **Private collections** (some Magdala material was acquired privately by officers and dispersed through subsequent sales).

The Magdala manuscripts include some of the most important Ge'ez Bible codices, hagiographies, liturgical books, and historical chronicles in any Western collection — alongside the Bodleian's earlier (Bruce, 1773) Ge'ez Enoch manuscripts and the Vatican Library's earlier Ethiopic holdings.

## Religious / scholarly consequences

1. **The most extensive Western Ge'ez manuscript collection assembled.** The British Magdala acquisitions, combined with the earlier Bruce holdings and the 19th-c. continued collecting, gave British scholarship the world's largest Western collection of Ge'ez manuscripts — making 19th-c. British Ge'ez philology (Wright, Charles, Budge) possible.

2. **August Dillmann and the German tradition benefit.** German Ge'ez scholarship (Dillmann's *Liber Henoch Aethiopice* 1851; *Lexicon Linguae Aethiopicae* 1865; *Grammatik der äthiopischen Sprache* 1857) was already established but was substantially intensified by access to Magdala manuscripts via British colleagues.

3. **Foundational publications.** Wright 1877 (BM catalogue); Budge's translations including the *Kebra Nagast* (1922 — based on the BM Magdala holdings), the *Sinkessar / Book of the Saints* (1928), and many other works; Charles's 1912 *Book of Enoch* (the Bruce manuscripts plus Magdala-period additional witnesses).

4. **The Ethiopian collective-cultural-heritage trauma.** From the Ethiopian side, Maqdala is one of the central modern instances of *colonial-era cultural-religious appropriation*. Restitution demands have been ongoing since the late 19th c. Some material has been returned in recent decades — most notably the Aksum Obelisk (returned by Italy 2005 from a separate 1937 removal), some manuscripts and regalia returned from individual British and Continental institutions in piecemeal restitutions through the 2000s–2020s. The bulk of the Magdala-acquired manuscripts remain in British collections.

5. **The *tabotat* dimension.** Tabotat (Ark-of-the-Covenant replicas) are *sacred objects in active religious use* — each Ethiopian church requires a tabot to be a functioning consecrated church. The British removal of tabotat from Maqdala is, from the Ethiopian-religious perspective, a substantially more serious act than manuscript removal. The British Museum returned some tabotat to the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church in 2002–2007.

## Documents in play
- **Affected:** ~350–500 Ge'ez manuscripts in British collections, foundational for 19th-c. and 20th-c. Western Ge'ez scholarship.
- **Catalogued:** Wright 1877 BM catalogue; subsequent inventories by Strelcyn and others.

## Disputes
- **Exact number and content of Magdala manuscripts.** Estimates range 350–500; Pankhurst 1973 gives the standard scholarly inventory. The complete record is partly incomplete because some material was dispersed privately.
- **Restitution.** Ongoing political-cultural-religious negotiation. The Ethiopian government and the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church have repeatedly requested return; British institutions have returned selected items in recent decades but not the bulk of the manuscript collection.
- **Comparative cases.** The Magdala removal is parallel to but more extensive than other 19th-c. colonial-era European acquisitions of religious heritage from non-European cultures (e.g., the British retention of the Benin Bronzes; the German retention of the Bust of Nefertiti; etc.).
