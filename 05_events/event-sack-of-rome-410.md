# Sack of Rome by Alaric's Visigoths (410)

## What happened
Over three days in August 410, Alaric I's Visigothic army entered and looted Rome — the first foreign sack of the city in some 800 years (since the Gauls in 387 BCE). Casualties were limited; major basilicas were respected (Alaric was an Arian Christian, the basilicas of Peter and Paul served as refuge); but the symbolic impact across the Mediterranean was profound. Jerome wrote: "the city which had captured the whole world has itself been captured."

## Religious consequences
- **Pagan accusation**: pagans throughout the empire argued the sack was the gods' punishment for Rome's abandonment of the traditional cults under the Christian-state policies of the 4th century (Theodosius's anti-pagan legislation, the [[event-edict-of-thessalonica-380]]).
- **Augustinian response**: Augustine of Hippo began *De civitate Dei* (City of God) in 413 in direct response to the pagan charge. The work develops the theology of the *two cities* — the City of God and the City of Man — and re-frames Rome's fall as a moral question rather than a cosmic-political catastrophe. The work was completed in 426 and became foundational for Western Christian political theology.
- **Periodization**: the event is one of the conventional markers of the end of antiquity / beginning of late antiquity; Gibbon's *Decline and Fall* uses it as a major hinge.

## Disputes
- The actual extent of physical destruction (modern historiography has revised it downward; the *symbolic* shock was disproportionate to the material damage).
- The 410 sack vs. the 455 Vandal sack and the 476 deposition of Romulus Augustulus as the *real* end-of-Rome marker.
