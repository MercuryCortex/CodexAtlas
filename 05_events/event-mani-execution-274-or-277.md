# Execution of Mani

## What happened
After three decades of Sasanian royal favor under Shapur I (~242–270) and Hormizd I (270–271), Mani's protection ended under Bahram I (r. 273–276). At the instigation of the powerful Zoroastrian high priest Kartir (whose own inscriptions claim credit for persecuting Manichaeans among other heresies), Bahram summoned Mani to court at Gundeshapur (Bēth Lapaṭ) and imprisoned him. Mani died in prison after twenty-six days of confinement — the exact circumstances of death (deliberately killed, succumbed to mistreatment) are debated, but the result was the same. His body was mutilated and exhibited at the gate of the city; the Manichaean community refers to this event as the *Crucifixion* of Mani, modeling his death on Christ's.

The traditional Sasanian date is 274; the Coptic and Iranian Manichaean traditions transmit dates of 276 or 277. The range used here brackets the live disagreement.

## Religious consequences
- *Persecution of Manichaeans in Iran.* Kartir's anti-Manichaean campaign drove the religion from its homeland over the following century; Manichaeism became principally a religion of diaspora — spreading west through the Roman Empire and east along the Silk Road into Central Asia and ultimately China.
- *Mani as paradigmatic martyr.* The Manichaean community's veneration of Mani's death structurally parallels Christian veneration of Christ's death; Manichaean hymnody dwells on the death and its salvific meaning.
- *Confirmation of the Zoroastrian-Manichaean rupture.* Mani had attempted to present his teaching as a fulfillment of Zoroastrian revelation (the Šābuhragān deliberately uses Zoroastrian vocabulary); Kartir's persecution closed that door, defining Manichaeism as a competitor religion rather than a Zoroastrian extension.

## Disputes
- **Date.** 274 (Sasanian Christian tradition) vs. 276 or 277 (Coptic and Iranian Manichaean traditions).
