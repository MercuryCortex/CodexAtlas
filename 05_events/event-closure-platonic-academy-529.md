# Justinian's Closure of the Athenian Schools (529)

## What happened
In 529 CE the emperor Justinian I issued imperial legislation (preserved in *Codex Justinianus* I.5.18; I.11.10) prohibiting the teaching of philosophy at Athens and confiscating endowments of the schools. The legislation targeted pagan (non-Christian) instruction; the Neoplatonic school of Athens — heir to Plato's Academy and headed at this point by Damascius (last scholarch), with Simplicius, Priscianus Lydus, and others as members — ceased its public operation. Damascius and a group of Neoplatonists fled to the Sasanian court of Khosrau I in Persia in 532, returning the next year under the terms of the Roman-Persian peace treaty (which guaranteed their freedom of religion in the Eastern empire). Whether the closure was a single dramatic event or one moment in a longer Christianizing pressure on Hellenic philosophical education is debated (Watts 2006 argues for the latter).

## Religious consequences
- **Symbolic end of pagan philosophical education** in the Eastern Roman empire; the Christianizing trajectory of late antique educational institutions is sealed.
- **Persian episode**: the Neoplatonists' exile and return is one of the few episodes of direct Greek-philosophical contact with the Sasanian intellectual world; some Greek-philosophical translation activity in early Sasanian Pahlavi may stem from this contact (the case is debated).
- **Transmission**: Neoplatonic texts continued to circulate within Christian intellectual contexts (Pseudo-Dionysius, Maximus Confessor, Eriugena); the closure ended the pagan-philosophical institutional context but not the text-tradition.

## Disputes
- The exact scope of the 529 legislation.
- The relationship between the 529 closure and the earlier (Theodosius-period) anti-pagan legislation.
- Whether the Alexandrian Neoplatonic school (which continued under nominally Christian leadership: Olympiodorus, Elias, David) was affected.
