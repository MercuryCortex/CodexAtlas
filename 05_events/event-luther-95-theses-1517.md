# Luther's 95 Theses (1517)

## What happened
On 31 October 1517, the Wittenberg Augustinian friar Martin Luther composed in Latin 95 propositions for academic disputation, critiquing the theory and abusive practice of indulgences — particularly the indulgence-campaign authorized by Archbishop Albrecht of Mainz, preached by Johann Tetzel, the proceeds of which were partly destined for the construction of St. Peter's in Rome and partly for Albrecht's own fiscal arrangements with the Fugger bank. Luther sent the theses with a covering letter to Albrecht; per the traditional account (Melanchthon's 1546 narrative), he also posted them on the door of the Wittenberg Castle Church the same day. Within weeks, the theses had been printed at Leipzig and Nuremberg; within months they had been translated into German and were circulating across the Empire. The controversy that followed (the 1518 Heidelberg Disputation; the 1518 Augsburg meeting with Cardinal Cajetan; the 1519 Leipzig Disputation with Johann Eck; the 1520 papal bull *Exsurge Domine* threatening excommunication; the 1521 Diet of Worms and imperial Edict) made Luther the symbolic head of an irreversible rupture from Rome.

## Religious consequences
- **The Protestant Reformation.** The 95 Theses are the symbolic — and to a substantial extent the actual — starting point of the Protestant Reformation. By 1530 (Augsburg Confession), a substantial part of northern Germany, Scandinavia, and parts of Switzerland and central Europe had broken with Rome.
- **End of medieval Catholic Christendom.** The unitary medieval Western Catholic Church gave way to permanent confessional plurality; the Thirty Years' War (1618–1648) and the Peace of Westphalia (1648) institutionalized the plurality.
- **Vernacular religion.** Luther's subsequent German Bible translation (NT 1522, complete 1534) and the broader Reformation vernacular project transformed European reading, education, and literary culture.
- **Catholic Counter-Reformation.** The Council of Trent (1545–1563) responded with doctrinal clarification (Session VI on Justification, 1547, directly responds to Lutheran soteriology) and disciplinary reform; the Society of Jesus (founded 1540) became the principal Catholic intellectual and missionary order.

## Documents in play
- The 95 Theses themselves.
- Luther's subsequent 1517–1521 corpus: the *Resolutiones disputationum de indulgentiarum virtute*, the three 1520 treatises (*To the Christian Nobility of the German Nation*, *Babylonian Captivity*, *Freedom of a Christian*).
- Papal documents: *Exsurge Domine* (1520), *Decet Romanum Pontificem* (1521 excommunication).
- The Diet of Worms records and Edict (1521).

## Disputes
- **Did Luther post the theses on the door?** The posting is attested only by Melanchthon's later (1546) account. The composition and dispatch to Albrecht are firm. Most historians treat the posting as plausible but not narratively decisive.
- **Did Luther intend rupture in 1517?** Brecht and Roper emphasize that Luther in October 1517 was still positioning himself as a reform-minded Augustinian within the Catholic Church; the rupture deepened over 1518–1521.
