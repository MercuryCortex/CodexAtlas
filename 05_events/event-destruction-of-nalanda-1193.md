# Destruction of Nālandā monastic university (1193)

## What happened
Between approximately 1193 and 1202 CE, the Ghurid (Turko-Persian Muslim) general **Bakhtiyar Khalji** — a subordinate commander under Muhammad of Ghor — destroyed the great monastic universities of eastern India: **Nālandā** (Bihar), **Vikramaśīla** (Bhagalpur), and **Odantapurī** (Bihar Sharif). Nālandā, founded ~427 CE under the Gupta emperor Kumāragupta I and continuously active for ~770 years, had been the principal institution of Indian Mahāyāna and Vajrayāna Buddhist learning, hosting thousands of monastic-scholars at its peak under the Pāla dynasty (~750-1150). Its library, housed in three multi-storey structures known as **Ratnasāgara** ("Sea of Jewels"), **Ratnodadhi** ("Ocean of Jewels"), and **Ratnaranjaka** ("Jewel-Decorated") — per the testimony of [[xuanzang]] who studied there 633-643 — was burned by Bakhtiyar's forces. Persian sources (*Tabaqat-i Nasiri* of Minhāj-i Sirāj Jūzjānī, ~1260) report that the library "smoldered for three months." The Tibetan pilgrim Dharmasvāmin visited the ruined site in ~1234-35 and reported only ~70 surviving monks living among the ruins under the elder Rāhulaśrībhadra. By the end of the 13th c., institutional Buddhism in India was effectively extinct as a coherent monastic-religious tradition (small Buddhist communities persisted in the Himalayan regions and along the eastern coast, but the great monastic-university system was gone).

## Religious consequences
- **End of institutional Indian Buddhism**: the destruction of Nālandā + Vikramaśīla + Odantapurī within ~10 years (~1193-1202) is the institutional terminal event of Indian Buddhism. After ~1300, Buddhism survives in India only in remote Himalayan pockets (Ladakh, Sikkim, Arunachal Pradesh, Bhutan) and in the Tibetan refugee population (post-1959); the great Indian monastic-universities are gone permanently.
- **Catastrophic textual loss**: the Nālandā library held the bulk of Indian Buddhist Sanskrit literature accumulated over ~770 years. **Tibetan translations preserve perhaps ~80% of what was once the Indian Buddhist Sanskrit corpus.** The 20% that was lost includes the bulk of the Sarvāstivāda Sanskrit canonical corpus, large parts of the Yogācāra commentarial literature, and many tantric texts. The Tibetan Kangyur (~108 vols) + Tengyur (~225 vols) is now the most complete extant repository of Indian Mahāyāna and Vajrayāna canonical literature — a preservation chain that runs Indian Sanskrit → Tibetan translation → Tibetan continuous transmission → modern critical editions.
- **The preservation-chain parallel to Ge'ez Enoch**: the Nālandā destruction is structurally parallel to other "religious-text-preservation-via-exile-translation" stories in this vault — most directly the Ge'ez preservation of 1 Enoch wedge (Ethiopian Christianity preserved 1 Enoch when every other tradition lost it; see [[phase-4-081-mashafa-henok-geez-1-enoch]] and the Ethiopian wedge in [[tradition-ethiopian-orthodox-tewahedo]]). Without Tibetan preservation, vast swaths of Indian Mahāyāna and Vajrayāna would be lost as completely as the Greek originals of most pre-Socratic philosophy.
- **Diaspora to Tibet, Nepal, Southeast Asia**: the last great Nālandā-Vikramaśīla scholars (notably Śākyaśrībhadra, the last great abbot of Vikramaśīla, who fled to Tibet ~1204) brought the surviving textual culture into the Tibetan and Nepalese traditions. The 13th-c. Tibetan translation movement (the latter half of the Phyi-dar "Later Diffusion") salvages what it can of the Indian Buddhist Sanskrit literature.
- **Symbolic-historical: the "end" of Buddhism in the land of its birth** — a powerful narrative theme in subsequent Buddhist historiography, especially in modern reform movements (Ambedkar's 1956 Buddhist conversion of Dalits; the modern Indian Buddhist revival).

## Documents in play
- **Persian *Tabaqat-i Nasiri*** (~1260) of Minhāj-i Sirāj Jūzjānī — the principal contemporary Persian-Muslim chronicle of Bakhtiyar Khalji's eastern conquests. Brief but explicit: "Most of the inhabitants of the place were Brahmans with shaven heads. They were put to death. Large numbers of books were found there, and, when the Muhammadans saw them, they called for some person to explain their contents, but all the men had been killed. It was discovered that the whole fort and city was a place of study."
- **Dharmasvāmin's biography** (~1234-35) — the Tibetan pilgrim's eyewitness account of post-destruction Nālandā, recording the survival of ~70 monks in the ruins under Rāhulaśrībhadra. Translated Roerich 1959.
- **Tāranātha's *History of Buddhism in India*** (1608) — the late Tibetan retrospective historical account; partly legendary but preserves substantial information not available elsewhere about the late-Indian Buddhist monastic universities.

## Disputes
- **Scale of the destruction**: did Bakhtiyar destroy the whole complex in one campaign, or did the destruction extend across years and multiple campaigns? Modern scholarship (Davidson 2002; Truschke 2017) treats the destruction as a multi-year process, with the immediate 1193 attack initiating a sustained period of institutional collapse over the subsequent decade.
- **The "burning of the library for three months"**: hagiographic exaggeration or material fact? Even at minimum the Nālandā library was a multi-storey wooden-and-palm-leaf structure with hundreds of thousands of manuscripts; a major fire would burn for an extended period. The "three months" figure cannot be precisely verified but the order-of-magnitude destruction is uncontested.
- **Cause as religious vs. military**: was Bakhtiyar's attack religiously motivated (anti-Buddhist iconoclasm) or militarily-strategic (the great monasteries were also fortified treasury-and-administrative centers)? Modern scholarship (Truschke 2017; Davidson 2002) treats it as primarily military-political with iconoclastic overtones, not as a primarily religious campaign.
- **Quantitative textual loss**: the "~80% preserved via Tibetan / ~20% lost" estimate is approximate. Some scholars (Snellgrove 1987) place the preservation figure higher; others (Davidson 2002) lower. The qualitative loss is uncontested: most of the Sarvāstivāda Sanskrit canonical corpus is lost, the late Indian tantric corpus is preserved only fragmentarily.
