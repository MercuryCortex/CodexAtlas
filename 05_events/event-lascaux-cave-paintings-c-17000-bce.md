# Lascaux Cave Paintings (c. 19,000–15,000 BCE)

## What happened
Lascaux Cave, in the Vézère Valley of the Dordogne in southwest France, was discovered on 12 September 1940 by four teenagers — Marcel Ravidat, Jacques Marsal, Georges Agniel, and Simon Coencas — who entered through a fox-hole into a sealed limestone cavity. The cave contains approximately 600 painted figures and 1,500 engravings of horses, aurochs, stags, bison, ibex, a single bear, a single rhinoceros, abstract signs, and a famous human figure (the Shaft Bird-Man). The principal painted spaces are the **Hall of the Bulls** (Salle des Taureaux), with its monumental aurochs (one over 5 metres long), the **Axial Gallery**, the **Passageway**, the **Apse**, the **Nave**, the **Chamber of the Felines**, and the **Shaft of the Dead Man** (Puits du Sorcier).

The Shaft scene depicts a bird-headed (or bird-masked) human figure with an erect phallus, apparently falling backward, with a wounded bison nearby (entrails hanging out, a spear through it) and a rhinoceros, with an obscure bird-on-a-stick beside the human. This is one of the few unambiguous human figures in Upper Palaeolithic cave painting and the most-discussed therianthropic image after the Hohlenstein-Stadel Löwenmensch.

**Dating.** Lascaux's painted figures are conventionally placed in the early Magdalenian (~17,000 BP), with some elements possibly Solutrean (~21,000–17,000 BP). Direct radiocarbon dating of the pigments is constrained (the cave has been closed to research-grade sampling for conservation reasons since 1963); current best estimates are based on stratigraphic context, stylistic correlation, and selective dating of associated organic remains. The range ~19,000–15,000 BCE in YAML is the conservative scholarly range.

UNESCO World Heritage 1979 (as part of "Decorated Caves of the Vézère Valley"). The cave was opened to the public 1948–1963, after which microbial damage from visitor breath, body heat, and lighting forced its permanent closure. Replicas Lascaux II (1983), Lascaux III (touring), and Lascaux IV (2016, the *Centre International de l'Art Pariétal Montignac-Lascaux*) now serve public visitation.

## Religious consequences
- The Lascaux Shaft Bird-Man is the most-cited single image in the *Homo sapiens* therianthrope tradition that includes the Hohlenstein-Stadel Löwenmensch (~38,000 BCE), the Chauvet Sorcerer panel (~32,000 BCE), the Sulawesi hunting-scene therianthropes (~44,000 BCE), and the Trois-Frères "Sorcerer" engraving. The recurrence of human-animal-hybrid imagery across continents and millennia is one of the most robust patterns in the Upper Palaeolithic religious-cognitive record.
- The Hall of the Bulls is sometimes argued (Aujoulat 2005) to depict a *seasonal cycle* of mating and rutting behaviors — horses in winter coat, aurochs and stags in autumn rut, ibex in spring — which would make Lascaux an *astronomically-or-seasonally structured ritual space* rather than a hunting-magic display. The seasonal-cycle reading is one hypothesis among several.
- Lascaux's near-absence of carnivore depictions and its emphasis on food-fauna and horned-bovid imagery is iconographically continuous with Altamira and contrasts with Chauvet's dangerous-fauna emphasis. Whether this represents a deep cultural shift between Aurignacian and Magdalenian religious imagination, or simply changing ecologies and prey-targeting, is a contested interpretive question.
- The Shaft is in the cave's most remote and most physically difficult chamber, reached by a ~6 metre vertical drop. This is one of the strongest cases for a *deliberately set-apart, hard-of-access* ritual space in the Upper Palaeolithic record.

## Disputes
- **Dating.** No direct radiocarbon dates exist for most Lascaux pigment (sampling restricted by conservation). The Magdalenian-c.17,000-BP attribution rests on stylistic-comparative grounds and associated archaeological deposits in the cave entrance. A revised earlier-Solutrean component for some images is possible but not established.
- **The Lewis-Williams shamanism reading of the Shaft scene** (the Bird-Man as a shaman in trance, the bird as a soul-flight psychopomp, the bison-and-rhino as spirit-encounters) is influential but not consensus. Alternatives: (a) a mythological-narrative illustration whose story is lost; (b) a hunting-accident memorial; (c) a hero-and-prey paired motif (Bahn 2010); (d) a death-and-renewal scene (Aujoulat). The investigation-posture stance: the Shaft is *securely* the earliest known multi-figure-narrative wall-painting in Europe; specific *interpretations* are open.
- **Leroi-Gourhan's structural-iconographic schema** (male/female animal pairings, central/peripheral position symbolism, four-style chronology) was foundational in the 1960s–70s and is now substantially modified — the rigid male/female-animal correspondences are no longer accepted, but the more general observation that Lascaux is *structured* and not random remains.
- **Conservation crisis.** The cave's degradation since opening (Fusarium and Pseudomonas microbial outbreaks, black-stain formation) is a separate and ongoing scientific-and-management controversy.
