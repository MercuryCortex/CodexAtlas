# Mahabodhi Temple at Bodh Gaya (c. 260 BCE – present)

## Identity
The Mahabodhi ("Great Awakening") Temple at Bodh Gaya, Bihar, marks the spot traditionally identified as the place where Siddhartha Gautama achieved buddhahood under the Bodhi tree (a *Ficus religiosa* / *pippala* fig). The site has been continuously identified as the principal place of the Buddha's enlightenment from at least the 3rd century BCE Asokan period to the present, making it the most important pilgrimage site of all Buddhist traditions. The Mahabodhi precinct includes the main temple (a tall pyramidal-tower *śikhara*-form structure, present built form 5th–6th c. CE with continuous restoration), the Bodhi tree (descendant by repeated cuttings of the tree that stood in the Buddha's time), the Vajrasana / diamond throne (a stone slab marking the precise spot of meditation, traditionally identified as Asokan-era and one of the oldest physically identifiable Buddhist cult objects), the railing surrounding the Bodhi tree (Sunga-period c. 2nd–1st c. BCE, with later replacements and now mostly preserved fragments in the Bodh Gaya Archaeological Museum and the Indian Museum, Calcutta), and the encircling stupas and votive monuments accumulated over the site's long pilgrimage history.

Inscribed UNESCO World Heritage 2002.

## Construction
**Asokan-era foundation (c. 260 BCE).** Asoka Maurya, after his Buddhist conversion following the Kalinga War (c. 261 BCE), visited Bodh Gaya (recorded in the Asokan inscriptions and in the Asokavadana tradition) and established the Vajrasana / diamond throne, a small temple or shrine, and the original railing-and-stupa elements. The Asokan layer is the earliest archaeologically attested phase; the Vajrasana itself, a polished sandstone slab with Mauryan-style decoration, is securely datable to the late 3rd c. BCE.

**Sunga-period elaboration (c. 2nd–1st c. BCE).** The stone railing around the Bodhi tree dates to this period (the carved relief style is identifiably Sunga); the railing's iconographic programme depicts aniconic representations of the Buddha (footprints, the empty throne, the wheel, the bodhi tree itself) consistent with pre-iconic Buddhist visual conventions.

**Gupta-era main temple (c. 5th–6th c. CE).** The current Mahabodhi temple in its broad form — a tall śikhara-pyramidal-tower structure with a square base, c. 55 m high — dates to the late Gupta period. It is one of the earliest surviving Indian temple structures of major scale and is architecturally distinctive: unlike most Indian temples of the period (which use the southern Dravida or northern Nagara orders), the Mahabodhi tower is sui generis, possibly preserving an earlier architectural tradition specific to the site.

**Medieval pilgrimage and decline (c. 7th–13th c. CE).** Xuanzang's 7th-c. visit (recorded in *Da Tang Xiyu Ji* / *Records of the Western Regions*) describes a thriving monastic complex; Burmese kings (notably Hsinbyushin in 1810) and Sri Lankan kings made restoration missions over the centuries. After the [[event-destruction-of-nalanda-1193]] and the broader Muslim-period decline of Indian Buddhism, the site fell into significant disuse from c. 13th c. onward, though never entirely abandoned — local Bengali / Bihari Buddhist and Hindu traditions continued to maintain a presence.

**Modern restoration (1870s–1880s).** Major restoration under Alexander Cunningham and J.D. Beglar of the Archaeological Survey of India, partly in dialogue with a Burmese restoration mission of 1881 that had begun on its own initiative. Cunningham-Beglar restoration is the source of much of the visible upper-tower stonework and the four corner-towers (the "minarets" of the present structure — these are later reconstructions of presumed Gupta features and are partly speculative).

## Religious significance
**Centre of all Buddhist traditions.** Mahabodhi is the principal pilgrimage destination for **all** Buddhist traditions — Theravada, Mahayana, Vajrayana — and is not the property of any one Buddhist sect. The site is identified in canonical and post-canonical Buddhist literature (Pali Vinaya, Mahaparinibbana Sutta, Lalitavistara, the Asokavadana) as the precise location of the Buddha's awakening; the four-fold pilgrimage of the Buddhist devotee canonically takes in Lumbini (birth), Bodh Gaya (enlightenment), Sarnath (first teaching), and Kushinagar (parinirvana).

**The Bodhi tree.** The current Bodhi tree is not the original tree under which Siddhartha sat — that tree was destroyed (according to Buddhist tradition by King Pushyamitra Shunga c. 2nd c. BCE in an anti-Buddhist persecution; or by Bengali king Shashanka c. 600 CE in another anti-Buddhist incident; the textual evidence for both episodes is itself contested). The line has been preserved by repeated cuttings and transplantings; the Sri Lankan tree at Anuradhapura, planted from a cutting brought by Sanghamitta (Asoka's daughter) c. 240 BCE, is itself one of the oldest verified human-planted living trees in the world and has been the source of replacement-cuttings sent back to Bodh Gaya at various times.

**Royal-religious patronage continuity.** Mahabodhi has received patronage from Mauryan, Sunga, Kushan, Gupta, Pala, Burmese, Sri Lankan, Tibetan, Mongol-Ilkhanid, modern Burmese, modern Sri Lankan, modern Japanese, and modern Thai royal and state actors across more than two millennia — one of the densest cross-tradition pilgrimage continuities in world religious history. Each tradition has built representative shrines and monasteries in the immediate vicinity, and the contemporary cluster of national Buddhist temples surrounding the main site (Thai, Burmese, Sri Lankan, Japanese, Tibetan, Vietnamese, Korean, Chinese, Bhutanese, etc.) is itself a kind of architectural map of contemporary world Buddhism.

## Disputes
- **Site-management arrangement.** Since 1949 the site has been administered under the Bodh Gaya Temple Act, which establishes a Mahabodhi Temple Advisory Board with mixed Hindu-Buddhist trustee composition (with a Hindu majority and the District Magistrate as ex officio chairperson — the District Magistrate of Gaya is, by convention, Hindu). This arrangement has been contested by Buddhist organizations (international Buddhist conferences from the 1990s onward, the All-India Buddhist Society, and others) who argue that the principal site of the Buddha's enlightenment should be under Buddhist control. The dispute is ongoing and politically delicate; the Atlas catalogues both positions without endorsing either.
- **Bodhi-tree replacement history.** Tradition holds the original tree was destroyed by Shashanka c. 600 CE and that the present tree descends from a cutting from the Anuradhapura tree, which itself descended from a cutting from the original tree carried to Sri Lanka by Sanghamitta c. 240 BCE. Whether all these episodes are securely historical (Shashanka-as-anti-Buddhist-persecutor is partly attested in Buddhist sources but contested in modern scholarship; the Sanghamitta-cutting transmission is securely attested) varies by episode.
- **Asokan dating of the Vajrasana.** The diamond throne is securely Mauryan in style (Mauryan polish, Mauryan-period iconography); whether it is precisely Asokan or post-Asokan-Mauryan is a question of decades, not centuries — the standard reading is c. 260–230 BCE.
- **Authenticity of the post-1880 reconstruction.** The 1880s Cunningham-Beglar restoration produced what we now think of as "the Mahabodhi temple"; how much of the upper tower and the four corner towers reflects the actual Gupta original vs. 19th-c. archaeological reconstruction is a real historiographic question. Most authorities accept the main body of the tower as substantially Gupta-period with extensive 19th-c. restoration of decorative elements.
