# Ghost Dance and Wounded Knee Massacre

## What happened
The 1890 Ghost Dance movement originated in the January 1, 1889 vision of Wovoka, a Northern Paiute prophet in Nevada (see [[wovoka]]). Wovoka taught a circular dance and an ethical-eschatological message: if Indigenous peoples performed the dance, lived peacefully, and refrained from war, the world would be renewed — the buffalo and ancestors restored, the white settlers removed, Indigenous nations restored. The movement spread rapidly across Plains and Western Indigenous nations 1889–1890; among the Lakota (Sioux), recently confined to severely-restricted reservations (Pine Ridge, Rosebud, Cheyenne River, Standing Rock) under starvation conditions, the Ghost Dance was adopted in modified, more militant form (the bullet-proof "Ghost Shirts" being a Lakota-specific addition that Wovoka did not teach).

US government officials read the Lakota Ghost Dance as preparation for armed uprising; the agent at Pine Ridge called for military intervention. On December 15, 1890, the Hunkpapa Lakota leader Sitting Bull was killed by Indian police during an attempt to arrest him at Standing Rock. The Miniconjou leader Big Foot (Spotted Elk) led ~350 of his people south toward Pine Ridge, intercepted by the 7th Cavalry at Wounded Knee Creek on December 28. On the morning of December 29, 1890, as soldiers attempted to disarm the group, gunfire erupted; the 7th Cavalry massacred ~250–300 Lakota, the majority women and children, in approximately two hours. Twenty-five soldiers died, many from friendly fire and from Lakota return-fire. The 7th Cavalry initially received 20 Medals of Honor for the action.

## Religious consequences
- *End of large-scale Ghost Dance among the Lakota.* The massacre effectively ended the public Ghost Dance among the Lakota; the movement persisted in modified form in other nations (Caddo, Pawnee, Kiowa traditions retained Ghost Dance elements for decades).
- *Symbolic-political endpoint.* Wounded Knee is often treated as the symbolic endpoint of the late-19th-c. Plains-Indian-wars period; the immediate occasion for ending overt military resistance.
- *Ongoing political-religious significance.* The Wounded Knee occupation of 1973 by AIM (American Indian Movement) deliberately invoked the 1890 massacre; the Lakota legal and political effort to revoke the 1890 Medals of Honor and obtain federal apology is ongoing.
- *Ethnographic project.* James Mooney's *The Ghost-Dance Religion and the Sioux Outbreak of 1890* (1896) — based on his 1891–92 visit to Wovoka and Ghost Dance communities — is the principal ethnographic record and one of the foundational works of American anthropology of religion.
- *Indigenous revitalization-religion typology.* The Ghost Dance is the paradigm case of "revitalization movement" in Anthony F.C. Wallace's later (1956) influential typology.

## Disputes
- **Responsibility for the massacre.** Mainstream historical consensus: the 7th Cavalry's actions were disproportionate; the dynamics of the attempted disarming were tense but the response was massacre, not battle. Some 19th-c. and into 20th-c. accounts described it as a "battle"; the term "massacre" has been the standard scholarly usage since at least Brown 1970 (*Bury My Heart at Wounded Knee*).
- **Christian influence on Wovoka's vision.** Wovoka grew up in part with the David Wilson family (Mormon and Presbyterian-influenced). How much of his vision is Numic-shamanic, how much Christian-derived, is intensively studied. See [[wovoka]].
- **Lakota militancy.** Whether the Lakota Ghost Shirt tradition reflects Lakota theological innovation or distortion of Wovoka's pacific message; consensus is that it reflects Lakota-specific reshaping under starvation-and-dispossession conditions.
