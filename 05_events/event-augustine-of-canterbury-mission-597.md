# Augustine of Canterbury's Mission to England (596–604)

## What happened
In 596 Pope Gregory the Great dispatched a band of about 40 monks from his own monastery of St Andrew's on the Caelian Hill in Rome, headed by the prior Augustine, to evangelize the pagan Anglo-Saxons of Britain. The mission arrived in Kent in 597. King Æthelberht of Kent — whose Frankish Catholic wife Bertha had brought her own bishop and was worshipping at the surviving Roman-era church of St Martin's at Canterbury — received them, was baptized himself, and gave them the use of Canterbury as their base. By 601 Gregory had sent reinforcements (Mellitus, Justus, Paulinus); by 604 Augustine had founded the sees of Canterbury, Rochester, and London. He died in 604.

## Religious consequences
- **The (re-)Christianization of Anglo-Saxon England.** British Christianity (Celtic, going back to Roman Britain) had survived in Wales and the west; Anglo-Saxon England, settled by pagan Germanic peoples from the late 4th c., had become re-paganized. The Augustinian mission, working through royal courts, re-converted the south; the Irish Columban / Aidanite mission from Iona (635, Lindisfarne) converted the north. The conflicting Roman and Celtic ecclesiastical practices were reconciled at the Synod of Whitby (664) in favor of Rome.
- **The Canterbury archbishopric** Gregory established becomes the senior English see for the next 1,400 years.
- **Bede's *Ecclesiastical History*** (731) makes the Augustinian mission the pivot of English ecclesiastical history.
- **The Gregorian liturgical, monastic, and educational pattern** Augustine brought becomes the template for English Christian practice.

## Substrate connections — explicit Christian doctrine on substrate-Christianization
- The single most important text in this batch on **explicit doctrinal endorsement of substrate-Christianization**: **Gregory the Great's letter to Abbot Mellitus** (601, *Reg.* XI.56, preserved in Bede *HE* I.30), instructing Augustine **not to destroy pagan temples but to convert them into churches**, retain the festival days but assign them new Christian meanings, and "let them rebuild themselves dwelling-places about the church" — explicitly: "the idol-temples of this people should not be destroyed, but the idols within them should be... if those temples are well built, they may be converted from the worship of demons to the service of the true God." This is **the proof-text for the Christian-imperial substrate-Christianization policy**. Markus 1997: 78–82.
- Bede records (*HE* I.30) the survival under Christian forms of:
  - **Old Anglo-Saxon festival names**: Ēostre (whose name became Easter; *De Temporum Ratione* 15) — Bede's own testimony, in passing, on a substrate goddess whose name was preserved in the major Christian festival.
  - **Thunor / Woden** day-names — preserved in English weekday vocabulary (Thursday, Wednesday, Friday's *Frīgedæg*) within Christianized society.
  - **Temple sites converted**: Canterbury's first Christian usage of the Roman-Romano-British church of St Martin's; subsequent royal-Christian use of older sacred-political sites.
- The **continuity of holy-well, sacred-tree, calendar-festival** patterns in English Christianity — much of medieval English folk-Christianity sits on Anglo-Saxon pre-Christian substrate — is documented from this mission forward (Hutton, *The Stations of the Sun* 1996; Mayr-Harting 1991: 250–82).
- **Queen Bertha's pre-existing Catholic worship** at St Martin's, Canterbury — a survival from Roman-Britain Christianity through five centuries of Anglo-Saxon paganism — represents a **physical continuity of Christian sacred site** from Roman through pagan to renewed Christian use. The St Martin's archaeological evidence (Brooks 1984) confirms Roman-era Christian construction.

## Documents in play
- **Bede, *HE* I.23–33** — primary narrative source.
- **Gregory the Great, *Registrum* (Letters)** Books VI, IX, XI — especially the letter to Mellitus (*Reg.* XI.56) on temple-conversion policy and the *Libellus Responsionum* addressed to Augustine's queries.
- **Anglo-Saxon legal codes** (Æthelberht's laws, ~600) — the earliest English-language legal text, written under post-baptismal Christian influence.

## Disputes
- **Authenticity of the *Libellus Responsionum*.** Often disputed in the 20th c.; Meyvaert (1986) and subsequent scholarship vindicate basic Gregorian authorship.
- **Scale and pace of conversion.** Bede's picture of rapid royal-conversion-followed-by-mass-baptism is partly hagiographic; actual conversion proceeded over generations and varied by kingdom.
- **Relations with British / Celtic Christianity.** Bede's pro-Roman framing of the conflict with the British bishops (whom Augustine demanded conform to Roman Easter dating and baptismal practice) is partisan; the Welsh / British church had legitimate prior claims.
