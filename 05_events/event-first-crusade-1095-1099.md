# First Crusade

## What happened
A multi-year armed pilgrimage from Latin Europe to the Levant (1095–1099), launched by Pope Urban II at the [[event-council-of-clermont-1095|Council of Clermont]] (November 1095), undertaken by a coalition of Frankish, Norman, Provençal, Flemish, and Lotharingian nobles with their retinues — eventually numbering perhaps 30,000–60,000 fighting and non-fighting participants. The campaign reached Constantinople (where Alexios I Komnenos extracted oaths from the Frankish leaders), crossed Anatolia (Battle of Dorylaeum 1097), besieged and took Antioch (1097–1098), and on 15 July 1099 stormed Jerusalem, massacring the city's Muslim and Jewish populations. The conquest founded four **Crusader states** (the *Outremer*): the Kingdom of Jerusalem, the County of Tripoli, the Principality of Antioch, and the County of Edessa.

Antecedent and ancillary events of the First Crusade complex:
- The **People's Crusade** (spring 1096, Peter the Hermit) — a popular uprising that preceded the main army; massacred along the way (especially in the **Rhineland massacres of 1096** — pogroms against Rhineland Jewish communities, the first major mass Jewish-Christian violence in Northern Europe), then destroyed by the Turks at Civetot (October 1096) before the main armies arrived.
- The **Princes' Crusade** (autumn 1096 onward) — the main military force.

## Religious consequences
- **Latin Christian sovereignty over Jerusalem** for the first time (1099–1187, then briefly again 1229–1244).
- **Foundation of the [[tradition-knights-templar|Knights Templar]] (~1119) and the militarization of the [[tradition-hospitallers|Hospitallers]]** (~1120) — without Outremer pilgrim-protection, no military-religious orders.
- **Pivot of Christian theology** — the just-war tradition (Augustine, Aquinas later) is operationalized into *holy* war. The plenary indulgence becomes a permanent feature of medieval Catholic practice.
- **Catastrophic Latin–Eastern relations.** Despite ostensibly aiding Byzantium, Latin behavior — especially the keeping of conquered territory rather than returning it to imperial control — sours Latin–Byzantine relations and contributes (through three generations) to the [[event-fourth-crusade-1204|Fourth Crusade's]] sacking of Constantinople.
- **Rhineland Jewish massacres of 1096.** The first major mass-violence against European Jewish communities; precedents the recurrent pogroms of later medieval Europe. Documented in Hebrew chronicles (Solomon bar Simson, Eliezer bar Nathan).
- **Latin–Islamic contact zone.** The Outremer becomes a sustained two-century contact zone between Latin Christianity and the Eastern Christian, Muslim, Jewish, Mandaean, Druze, and Nizari Ismaili communities. Whatever theological transmission occurred — and the question is contested ([[templar-gnostic-transmission-hypothesis]]) — happened here.

## Documents in play
- ***Gesta Francorum*** — anonymous, by a Norman participant in Bohemond's retinue, the principal eyewitness source.
- **Fulcher of Chartres** — clerical participant; the more elite-perspective account.
- **Raymond of Aguilers** — chaplain to Raymond of Toulouse.
- **Anna Komnene's *Alexiad*** — the principal Byzantine source, hostile to the Latin crusaders.
- **Ibn al-Athir** (later, Mosul) and **Ibn al-Qalanisi** (Damascus) — the principal Arabic sources, hostile to the Franks.

## Disputes
- **Crusader-massacre scale at Jerusalem (1099).** Latin sources speak of streets running with blood; Arabic and Hebrew sources confirm massacre but with varying numbers. Asbridge 2004 ch. 11 gives the historiographic state-of-play.
- **Did the Crusade succeed *despite* or *because of* its disorganization?** Frankopan 2012 emphasizes the Byzantine logistical and strategic enabling-conditions; older scholarship credited Latin religious enthusiasm.
