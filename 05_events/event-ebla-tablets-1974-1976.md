# Ebla Tablets Discovery

## What happened
The Italian Archaeological Mission to Syria, led by Paolo Matthiae (Sapienza University of Rome), excavating at Tell Mardikh in northern Syria from 1964 onward, identified the site as ancient **Ebla** (early-second-millennium attestations from Mesopotamian texts had named the city) and uncovered the Royal Palace G archive in 1974–1976. The archive contained ~17,000 cuneiform tablets and fragments dating ~2400–2300 BCE — making them, alongside contemporary Mesopotamian and the Tell Brak material, among the earliest Semitic-language administrative and literary texts known. The Eblaite language is an East Semitic language (closer to Akkadian than to West Semitic, contra some early-1970s sensationalist claims).

## Religious consequences
Substantial impact on the early-third-millennium religious-cultural map:
- **Pantheon documentation**: extensive god-lists, ritual offerings to specific deities (Dagan, Reshep, Hadda, Ishtar/Inanna, Shamash, Kura, Ashtapi — many later familiar from West Semitic religion).
- **Sumerian-Semitic interaction**: the Eblaite scribes used Sumerian logograms with Semitic phonetic readings, illustrating the early-third-millennium Mesopotamian-Levantine literary-religious contact.
- **OT-comparative claims contested**: Giovanni Pettinato (the first epigrapher) made several sensationalist claims connecting Ebla material to biblical patriarchal narratives, place names, and a deity *Ya* (alleged precursor of YHWH); subsequent scholarly review (Archi, Biggs, Lambert) rejected the dramatic claims as based on misreadings. The mature scholarship treats Ebla as an important early-third-millennium West-Mesopotamian site without specific biblical-patriarchal connections.

## Documents in play
The Ebla archive is not directly represented as Phase-1 nodes in this vault (the materials are largely administrative); the religious-cultural impact is contextual.

## Disputes
- **Pettinato "Ebla and the Bible" claims (1976–1980s)**: largely retracted under scholarly review.
- **Eblaite language classification**: East Semitic (consensus) vs. proto-West-Semitic (Pettinato early position, now generally rejected).
