# Beheading of Guru Tegh Bahadur (November 11, 1675)

## Background
**Guru Tegh Bahadur Ji** (1621–1675) was the **ninth Sikh Guru** — son of the sixth Guru, Hargobind, and father of the tenth Guru, Gobind Singh (who would create the Khalsa). The Mughal Emperor **Aurangzeb** (r. 1658–1707) had reversed the relative tolerance of his father (Shah Jahan) and grandfather (Jahangir) toward non-Muslim religious communities, implementing an aggressively Islamizing policy:

- **Jizya** (poll tax on non-Muslims) reinstated in 1679
- **Temples demolished** — including the Kashi Vishwanath temple at Varanasi and the Keshava Deva temple at Mathura
- Forced conversions of Hindus, particularly in Kashmir

## The Interfaith Context of the Martyrdom
The extraordinary feature of Guru Tegh Bahadur's execution is its **interfaith motivation** — the most remarkable aspect of this martyrdom in the vault:

According to Sikh tradition (Khushwant Singh vol. 1; Sikh records including the *Gurbilas Patshahi 10*):

A delegation of **Kashmiri Brahmin Pandits** came to Guru Tegh Bahadur in Anandpur (~1675) in desperation. Aurangzeb's governor of Kashmir, Sher Afghan Khan, was systematically forcing conversions of the Kashmiri Hindu community. The Brahmins asked the Guru for help — for a protector.

The Guru's 9-year-old son (the future Guru Gobind Singh) reportedly said: "Who is more worthy to give this sacrifice than you?" The Guru accepted.

Guru Tegh Bahadur **sent word to Aurangzeb that if the Emperor could convert *him*, the Hindu Pandits would convert voluntarily** — making himself the test case for forced conversion, and thereby making his body a shield for Hindu religious freedom.

He was arrested, brought to Delhi, and imprisoned for three months. Three of his companions were tortured and killed before him:
- **Bhai Mati Das** — sawn in half (vertically) alive
- **Bhai Sati Das** — wrapped in cotton wool and burned alive
- **Bhai Dayala** — boiled alive in a cauldron

Guru Tegh Bahadur refused to convert or perform a miracle to save himself. On **November 11, 1675**, he was **publicly beheaded** in Chandni Chowk (the main marketplace of Delhi). A Sikh named Jaita recovered the severed head and carried it 250 km to Anandpur.

The site of the beheading: **Gurdwara Sis Ganj Sahib** in Delhi, built on the spot — still one of the most visited Sikh shrines in the world.

## The Khalsa — The Direct Consequence
The martyrdom of Guru Tegh Bahadur created the political and theological conditions for the Sikh tradition's most decisive transformation.

His son and successor, **Guru Gobind Singh**, on **Baisakhi (April 13, 1699)**, at Anandpur Sahib, created the **Khalsa** — the community of initiated Sikhs:

- **Five Ks** (*Panj Kakars*): Kesh (uncut hair), Kangha (wooden comb), Kara (steel bracelet), Kachera (undergarment), Kirpan (steel sword) — a distinctive visible identity that makes every Khalsa member immediately recognizable
- **Amrit Sanchar** (initiation ceremony, "nectar of immortality")
- **Abolition of the masand system** (the intermediary priest-representatives Guru Arjan had established)
- The declaration that the Guru's authority would pass after Gobind Singh's death to the **Guru Granth Sahib** — the scripture becomes the eternal Guru

The Khalsa's foundational purpose: **to defend the weak regardless of their religion.** The interfaith martyrdom of Guru Tegh Bahadur — killed for defending Hindus — is inscribed in the Khalsa's reason for existence.

**Sikhism under Guru Nanak (1469–1539) was a pacifist bhakti devotional movement.** The persecution sequence — Guru Arjan tortured to death (1606), Guru Tegh Bahadur beheaded for defending Hindus (1675) — transforms it into a **warrior-saint community** (sant-sipahi) with an explicit mandate to resist oppression. This is the vault's most dramatic instance of persecution fundamentally transforming a tradition's character rather than merely strengthening it.

## The "Hind di Chaddar" — Shield of India
Guru Tegh Bahadur is given the title **"Hind di Chaddar"** — "Shield of India" — by Guru Gobind Singh, who composed the *Bachitra Natak* (Wonderful Drama) as autobiography. The title captures the interfaith dimension: a Sikh Guru who died for the religious freedom of Hindus is called the protector of India's civilization.

This is the vault's only example of a **martyrdom explicitly for the religious freedom of another tradition** — the Guru as cross-tradition martyr. It foreshadows (structurally) the Bahá'í principle of religious tolerance and the modern inter-faith martyrdom discourse.
