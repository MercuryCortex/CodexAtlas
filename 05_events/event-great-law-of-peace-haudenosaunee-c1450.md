# The Great Law of Peace (Gayanashagowa)

## What It Is

The **Gayanashagowa** ("Great Binding Law" or "Great Law of Peace") is the oral constitution of the Haudenosaunee Confederacy (*Gayanashagowa* in Onondaga; "People of the Longhouse"). It established — at a date traditionally set c. 1450 CE, with scholarly estimates ranging from 1090 to 1600 CE — a federated union of five nations (later six) under a constitutional governance structure that:

- United five sovereign nations (Seneca, Cayuga, Onondaga, Oneida, Mohawk) under a federal council
- Encoded representative governance: each nation sends delegates (sachems/chiefs) to the Grand Council at Onondaga
- Established checks and balances: deliberation moves from house to house; the Onondaga serve as "firekeepers" who confirm or return decisions
- Protected freedom of speech and conscience in deliberation
- Institutionalized women's political authority: Clan Mothers (*Gantowisas*) appoint and may remove the male sachems; the political system structurally requires women's participation at the selection tier

The law was transmitted through **wampum** — shell-bead belts that function as mnemonic covenant-documents, encoding the terms of agreements with visual patterns that trained wampum-readers could interpret with precision. The Grand Council at Onondaga continues to meet. The Great Law has been in continuous operation for approximately 500–900 years, making it one of the oldest continuously functioning constitutional governance systems in the world.

## The Peacemaker's Mission

The founding narrative: **Deganawidah** (the Peacemaker) — born of a virgin mother among the Huron but sent to the Haudenosaunee — receives a vision from the Creator: the nations are locked in cycles of revenge warfare (*the Mourning War*) that destroy them. He brings a message of three principles:

1. **Righteousness** (*Skennen* — peace) — right thought and right action in council
2. **Health** (*Kariwiio* — good message/good mind) — the Good Mind as the foundation of governance  
3. **Power** (*Kasastensera* — power) — collective strength arising from union

**Hiawatha** (not Longfellow's fictional Ojibwe hero — a different historical person) is the Peacemaker's Onondaga co-founder. Hiawatha was himself a Mohawk chief transformed by grief (the murder of his daughters) into a wandering madman; Deganawidah finds him, condoles him (restoring his Good Mind through the Condolence Ceremony), and together they carry the Great Law to each nation. The last and most resistant convert is **Tadodaho** — the Onondaga war chief, described as so twisted by malice that his body is physically bent and his hair full of snakes. Deganawidah and Hiawatha straighten his body and mind through the power of the Good Mind. He becomes the permanent Onondaga firekeeper — the holder of the Great Council's central fire — in perpetuity; every subsequent Onondaga firekeeper takes the name Tadodaho as a title.

## The Governance Structure

The Gayanashagowa establishes a remarkably sophisticated governance architecture:

**Bicameral-equivalent deliberation**: Matters are first debated by the Mohawk (Keepers of the Eastern Door) and Onondaga; then sent to the Cayuga and Oneida; then returned to the Onondaga for confirmation or referral for re-deliberation. This creates a multi-stage deliberative process that checks hasty decisions.

**The pine tree chiefs**: The Great Law also allows for "pine tree chiefs" — individuals raised to council status by merit rather than clan-mother appointment. This establishes a meritocratic element alongside the hereditary structure.

**The women's veto**: Clan mothers not only appoint sachems but may "dehorn" (remove) any chief who fails his people — a structural accountability mechanism with no counterpart in European feudal governance of the same period.

**The Condolence Ceremony**: The political system is grounded in a grief-resolution ritual. Before any new council, the Condolence Ceremony formally restores any community in mourning to full deliberative capacity (the Good Mind). Governance and psychology are not separated.

## The Influence Debate — Haudenosaunee and American Democracy

This is the vault's most consequential cross-tradition influence case: whether, and to what degree, the Gayanashagowa directly shaped the United States Constitution.

### Documentary Evidence for Influence

| Document | Content |
|----------|---------|
| Benjamin Franklin, Albany Plan of Union (1754) | Franklin hosted Haudenosaunee at Albany Congress 1754; his Albany Plan for colonial union follows the confederacy model; he wrote explicitly: "It would be a strange thing if six nations of ignorant savages should be capable of forming a scheme for such a union and be able to execute it in such a manner that it has subsisted ages and appears indissoluble, and yet that a like union should be impracticable for ten or a dozen English colonies." |
| Thomas Jefferson's notes (~1786–87) | Jefferson's reading notes and letters during the Constitutional Convention period discuss Indigenous governance structures |
| James Madison, *Federalist No. 18* (1788) | Madison specifically discusses the Haudenosaunee Confederacy as a cautionary example of confederate weakness (arguing for a stronger federal government) — but this document proves the Founders studied the Haudenosaunee model in deliberate detail |
| US Senate Concurrent Resolution 76 (1988) | "the confederation of the original thirteen colonies into one republic was explicitly modeled upon the Iroquois Confederacy, as were many of the democratic principles which were incorporated into the Constitution itself" — Congressional acknowledgment |

### Structural Parallels

| Feature | Gayanashagowa | US Constitution / Government |
|---------|---------------|------------------------------|
| Federal structure | Confederacy of sovereign nations; local matters handled locally; confederacy handles external relations | Federal system with states' rights (10th Amendment) |
| Multi-stage deliberation | Mohawk/Onondaga → Cayuga/Oneida → Onondaga confirmation | Senate + House + Presidential confirmation |
| Executive accountability | Clan mothers "dehorn" failing chiefs | Impeachment procedure |
| Protection of deliberative speech | Sachems' speech in council protected | First Amendment freedom of speech |
| Leadership by consent | Chiefs appointed by Clan Mothers representing the people | Elected representatives (though women excluded until 1920) |
| Freedom of religion | Longhouse spiritual practice not mandated | First Amendment free exercise |

### The Scholarly Debate

**For substantial influence** (Johansen 1982; Johansen and Grinde 1991):
- Franklin's explicit statement at Albany 1754 constitutes direct acknowledgment
- Jefferson studied Haudenosaunee governance as part of his political philosophy
- The structural parallels are too numerous to be coincidental given documented contact
- The 1988 Senate Resolution is Congressional acknowledgment

**Against substantial influence** (Tooker 1988; Levy 1996):
- The constitutional framers' primary intellectual sources are Locke, Montesquieu, and English common law — extensively documented in their own writings
- The structural parallels are general enough to arise independently from common political problems
- Franklin's 1754 statement uses the Haudenosaunee rhetorically (as a rebuke to colonial parochialism), not as a design blueprint
- The Gayanashagowa had no women's suffrage at the delegate level; attributing the Constitution's design to it risks obscuring the specific contributions of Enlightenment political theory

**Fenton (1998) — the definitive scholarly verdict**: The Great Law is a genuine and extraordinary political achievement; the Founding Fathers demonstrably knew of it and were influenced by the *general concept* of confederate union; the specific mechanisms of the US Constitution derive primarily from English-Lockean sources. The influence is real but should be characterized as *one factor among many* rather than as the template.

**Investigative finding**: The influence debate is itself a MASSIVE WIN for the vault, because it surfaces the suppressed history of Indigenous political thought. Whether the influence was architectural (direct borrowing of structural features) or atmospheric (a proof-of-concept that a confederate union could work), the fact that the oldest constitutional democracy in continuous operation served as context for the founding of the United States' constitutional democracy is under-documented in mainstream political history. That suppression is itself a historical phenomenon worth recording.

## Cross-Tradition Political Theology Comparisons

The Gayanashagowa is significant not just as a political document but as **political theology** — governance grounded in cosmological vision (Sky Woman's mandate) and psychological principle (the Good Mind).

| Tradition | Political Theology | Key Concept |
|-----------|-------------------|-------------|
| Haudenosaunee | Great Law of Peace as implementation of Sky Woman's cosmological mandate | Good Mind (*Hahgwehdiyu*) as political virtue |
| Athens | Democratic deliberation as expression of *logos* (reason) | *Isonomia* (equality before the law) |
| Zoroastrian | Righteous governance as expression of *Asha* (cosmic truth-order) | *Asha Vahishta* as political principle |
| Hebrew | Torah as covenant constitution; Mosaic law as political theology | *Mishpat* (justice) as divine mandate |
| Islamic | *Shura* (consultation) as governance principle derived from Quranic revelation | *Ahl al-Hall wal-'Aqd* (those who loosen and bind) as consultative council |

The Haudenosaunee case is unique in linking political governance to **psychological restoration** (the Condolence Ceremony as prerequisite for deliberation) — a feature that distinguishes it from all European and Near Eastern political theology traditions.
