# Rise of Political Islamism (1928–present)

## What happened
The emergence and trajectory of *political Islam* — the family of 20th-c. Sunni and Shia movements that frame Islam not (only) as personal-religious commitment or traditional jurisprudential framework but as a comprehensive political program for state and society. The standard scholarly framing identifies four overlapping waves:

1. **Foundational period (1928–1949).** Hassan al-Banna founds the Muslim Brotherhood (*al-Ikhwan al-Muslimun*) in Ismailia, Egypt, 1928. The Brotherhood combines Salafi-modernist religious reform with anti-colonial nationalist mobilization. Parallel: Abul A'la Maududi founds Jamaat-i-Islami in British India 1941; explicit project of Islamic state.
2. **Qutbist radicalization (1949–1970s).** Following al-Banna's 1949 assassination, [[sayyid-qutb]] develops a more radical theology (*Milestones* / [[phase-7-028-milestones]], 1964) that re-applies the classical category of *jahiliyya* (pre-Islamic ignorance) to contemporary nominally-Muslim regimes and declares revolutionary opposition obligatory. Qutb is executed by Nasser's regime 1966; his thought becomes the seed of subsequent Sunni-jihadist movements.
3. **Iranian Revolution and the Shia track (1979).** Khomeini's *vilayat-e faqih* doctrine produces the world's first explicitly-Islamist state ([[event-iranian-revolution-1979]]) — Twelver Shia clerical political authority during the occultation of the Hidden Imam.
4. **Salafi-jihadist global wave (1979–present).** The Afghan war against the Soviet Union (1979–1989) trans-nationalizes Sunni jihadism (Azzam, bin Laden); al-Qaeda emerges in the late 1980s; 1990s North African and Egyptian jihadist insurgencies; 9/11 (2001); the Iraq War and the rise of al-Qaeda in Iraq → Islamic State (declaring caliphate 2014); ongoing fragmentation and post-territorial phase.
5. **2010s political-Islamist wave and reversals.** The Arab Spring (2010–2012) briefly brought elected-Islamist governments (Morsi/Brotherhood in Egypt 2012–2013; Ennahda in Tunisia; AKP-aligned outcomes in Turkey) before the 2013 Egyptian military counter-coup, Tunisian secular reassertion, and broader regional reversals. By the mid-2020s political Islamism is institutionally weaker than in the 2010s but continues as a major political force especially in Turkey (AKP-Erdoğan), Iran, and various non-state Islamist actors.

## Religious consequences
- Reframed Islamic political theology against centuries of post-Abbasid pragmatic accommodation with non-Islamic-state political authority. The classical Sunni position (recognize whatever Muslim ruler maintains order; pious quietism otherwise) and the Twelver-Shia quietist position (no legitimate political authority during the occultation) were both substantially destabilized.
- Produced major new theological-legal genres: the Brotherhood's *da'wa* literature; Qutbist *jahiliyya* / *takfir* / *hakimiyya* (sovereignty-of-God-as-political-program); Khomeinist *vilayat-e faqih*; Salafi-jihadist *fiqh al-jihad* and post-classical "individual jihad obligation" arguments.
- Renewed worldwide interest in early-Islamic political-theological sources ([[phase-4-034-quran]], [[phase-5-007-sahih-al-bukhari]], early caliphate history) as resources for and against the Islamist project.
- Provoked counter-developments: post-Islamist religious thought (Khaled Abou El Fadl, Tariq Ramadan, Abdolkarim Soroush); state-sponsored "moderate Islam" projects (UAE, Saudi Vision 2030, Egyptian al-Azhar reforms); a revived Islamic intellectual liberalism. Olivier Roy's *post-Islamism* thesis (2017) argues Salafi-jihadism is increasingly less Islamic-theological than nihilist-existential.

## Disputes
- **"Islamism" definition.** Whether to subsume Khomeinism, Brotherhood Sunni-democratic Islamism, and Salafi-jihadism under one category is debated. Kepel uses broad framing; Roy distinguishes "Islamism" (mainstream political-religious) from "neofundamentalism" / "post-Islamism" (Salafi-jihadism).
- **Continuity with classical Islam.** Whether political Islamism is a *revival* of an authentic premodern Islamic political theology or a 20th-c. *innovation* (selectively reading and breaking with classical scholarship) is contested; the academic mainstream tends toward the latter.
- **Causes.** Anti-colonial reaction, modernization shock, Saudi petrodollar export of Salafi religious infrastructure, U.S. Cold War instrumentalization of Sunni mujahidin in Afghanistan, Israeli-Palestinian conflict, and Sykes-Picot border legacies are variably emphasized by different scholars.
