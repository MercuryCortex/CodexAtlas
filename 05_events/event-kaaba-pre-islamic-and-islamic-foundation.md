# The Kaaba: Pre-Islamic Sanctuary and Islamic Foundation

## What happened
The Kaaba ("the cube") is a roughly cuboid stone building at the centre of the Masjid al-Haram in Mecca, draped since the early Islamic period in the black cloth *kiswa* and containing in its eastern corner the Black Stone (*al-Hajar al-Aswad*) — a meteoritic or basaltic fragment encased in a silver mount and kissed or saluted by pilgrims during the *tawaf* circumambulation. The structure has been rebuilt repeatedly (Quraysh c. 605 CE, post-Yazid-siege 683, Abdullah ibn al-Zubayr 684, al-Hajjaj 693, Murad IV 1630, and many minor restorations); the present structure preserves an approximation of the 7th-century footprint.

In its pre-Islamic configuration the Kaaba was the central sanctuary of a polytheistic federation centered on Mecca and overseen by the Quraysh tribe. Arabic historiographic tradition (Ibn al-Kalbi *Kitab al-Asnam*, Ibn Ishaq via Ibn Hisham *Sira*) records that the Kaaba and its immediate environs housed a large number of idols representing tribal deities; the chief god of the Meccan sanctuary in this period is identified as **Hubal** (a moon-or-rain god of disputed origin, possibly Aramaic/Nabataean). The three goddesses [[al-uzza]], [[allat]] (al-Lat), and [[manat]] — the "daughters of Allah" referred to in Q 53:19–20 — were associated with shrines elsewhere in the Hijaz (Ta'if, Nakhla, Qudayd) but were part of the broader sanctuary-network that included Mecca. [[allah]] in this period is attested in pre-Islamic poetry and inscriptions as a high god, possibly already a creator-figure understood as supreme above the lesser deities; whether Allah was specifically the "owner" of the Kaaba prior to the rise of Hubal is debated.

Muhammad's Meccan preaching (c. 610–622 CE) was a strict monotheist rejection of the polytheistic cult, while preserving the Kaaba itself as the *qibla* (direction of prayer) — first re-oriented from Jerusalem to Mecca in 624 CE (Q 2:142–150). After the conquest of Mecca in 630 CE (8 AH), Muhammad entered the city, ordered the destruction of the idols in and around the Kaaba, and rededicated it as the central sanctuary of the unitarian Abrahamic cult — the foundation event of Islamic sacred geography. The Quranic claim (Q 2:125–127, Q 3:96–97) identifies the Kaaba as the first house of worship established for humanity, built or refounded by [[abraham-patriarch]] (Ibrahim) and [[ishmael-son-of-abraham]] (Isma'il); this Abrahamic foundation claim is theologically central to Islam and historically contested by external scholarship.

## Religious consequences
- **Foundation of Islamic sacred geography.** The Kaaba becomes the universal Muslim *qibla* (Q 2:144) and the destination of the [[hajj]] pilgrimage (Q 22:26–27), which itself absorbs and reframes pre-Islamic Meccan pilgrimage practices (*tawaf*, the running between Safa and Marwa, the standing at Arafat, the stoning at Mina) within a strictly monotheist Abrahamic frame. This is one of the clearest instances of religious-form continuity across a tradition-change: the ritual architecture is preserved; the theological interpretation is replaced.
- **Closure of pre-Islamic Arabian polytheism in the Hijaz.** The 630 cleansing of the Kaaba is the symbolic end of the central Meccan polytheistic cult; what survived in peripheral form (e.g., remnant pagan elements in some folk practices) is treated by Islamic tradition as *jahiliyya* — the "age of ignorance" — and as something definitively superseded.
- **The Black Stone (al-Hajar al-Aswad).** Set in the Kaaba's eastern corner; its origin is uncertain — meteoritic-stone hypothesis is widely cited but not securely demonstrated. Pre-Islamic veneration of betyls (sacred standing stones) is well-attested across the ancient Levant and Arabia; the Black Stone is best read as the surviving betyl of the pre-Islamic sanctuary, reinterpreted within the Islamic frame as a stone descended from paradise (a hadith in al-Tirmidhi).
- **Daughters-of-Allah problem (Q 53:19–20 and the "satanic verses" episode).** The Quranic critique of al-Lat, al-Uzza, and Manat is the principal Islamic-textual evidence for their pre-Islamic role; the "satanic verses" incident reported by Ibn Sa'd, al-Tabari, and others — Muhammad briefly conceding intercessory standing to the three goddesses and then retracting — is itself a window into the theological negotiation by which strict monotheism was distinguished from the inherited polytheistic substrate. See [[satanic-verses-incident]].

- Ibn al-Kalbi (d. 819 CE) *Kitab al-Asnam* ("Book of Idols") — Tier-1-via-translation; the principal Arabic source listing the idols of the pre-Islamic Kaaba; itself a polemical-historiographic work and must be read with the Hawting 1999 caveat.

## Disputes
- **Pre-Islamic religious affiliation of the Kaaba — contested scholarly territory.** Traditional Islamic and 19th–20th century Western scholarship treats the pre-Islamic Kaaba as a straightforwardly polytheistic shrine containing 360 idols centered on Hubal, with al-Lat, al-Uzza, and Manat as intercessory daughter-goddesses. Hawting 1999 *The Idea of Idolatry* argues this picture is largely a later Muslim polemical construction projecting back onto the pre-Islamic situation a more starkly idolatrous frame than the historical evidence supports; he proposes that Muhammad's polemic was instead directed at fellow Arabian monotheists whose theology he regarded as deficient. **Atlas position: present both views; mark the polytheistic-cult-of-Hubal-plus-daughters narrative as the traditional reading and Hawting's revision as a serious scholarly contestation, not the consensus but not dismissible.** [claim: contested]
- **Abrahamic foundation claim.** Q 2:125–127 and Q 3:96–97 identify Abraham and Ishmael as the builders of the Kaaba. External historiographic and archaeological evidence does not support a 2nd-millennium-BCE Hebrew-patriarchal foundation; the building's earliest archaeologically documented phases are late pre-Islamic (probably late antique). The Abrahamic-foundation claim is a *theological-genealogical* claim grounding Islamic identity in the Abrahamic monotheist line, not a historical-archaeological one. Both are catalogued.
- **Identity of "Allah" in pre-Islamic Mecca.** Robert Hoyland and Patricia Crone have variously argued that *Allah* in pre-Islamic Arabia was already understood as a high god distinct from but standing above the lesser deities (a *hanif* monotheist substrate that Muhammad inherited and intensified); alternative reconstructions treat Allah as one god among many until the Islamic period. The textual evidence is fragmentary (pre-Islamic poetry, a handful of inscriptions).
- **Black Stone — origin.** Meteorite, basalt, agate, or impactite: the stone has not been subjected to modern scientific analysis (access is restricted on religious grounds); the meteoritic claim is plausible but not demonstrated.
