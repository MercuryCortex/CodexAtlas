# Edict of Milan (313)

## What happened
In February 313, Constantine I (ruling the western half of the empire) and Licinius (ruling the east) met at Milan to coordinate policy after the defeat of Maxentius. The resulting agreement, promulgated as a letter from Licinius to his eastern governors (preserved by Lactantius in Latin and Eusebius in Greek), restored to Christians the legal status of an *religio licita*: freedom to worship without persecution, restoration of confiscated property (churches, cemeteries, communal goods), and equal toleration for "every religion." It was not, strictly, a Christian charter — it was a universal toleration edict — but its effect, in the political context of an emperor (Constantine) now actively favorable to Christianity, was the end of imperial persecution of the Christian church.

## Religious consequences
- **Ends the era of persecution.** The Diocletianic Great Persecution (303–311) and its sporadic continuations cease in the West immediately and in the East with Licinius's enforcement (until his later 320s anti-Christian policy resumes, prior to his defeat by Constantine in 324).
- **Begins the Constantinian shift.** Within a decade Christianity has imperial patronage; within two decades the emperor convenes ecumenical councils; within seventy years (Theodosius, 380) it is the state religion. The shift from persecuted minority to state-church religion takes place across this trajectory.
- **Reframes the Roman religious-political order.** The previous Roman pattern — religious-civic conformity backed by imperial coercion — is now extended to a religion (Christianity) that defines itself by exclusive monotheism, with massive long-term consequences for the legal status of paganism, Judaism, and intra-Christian dissent.
- **Restitution of property.** The clause requiring return of confiscated Christian property created the practical infrastructure (basilicas, cemeteries, episcopal sees) of the imperial-Christian church.

## Substrate connections — the *real* religious-political continuities
- The Edict's emperor — Constantine — was still using **[[sol-invictus]]** iconography on coinage (the *SOLI INVICTO COMITI* solar coinage continued to be struck through ~325). The 312 Milvian Bridge vision is variously reported by Eusebius (*Vita Constantini* I.28: a cross of light) and Lactantius (*De mortibus persecutorum* 44: the chi-rho monogram seen in a dream); the Arch of Constantine (315) shows Sol the unconquered sun, no Christian iconography. The historical Constantine of the early 310s was a Sol-worshipping monotheizing emperor who shifted, by stages and through political calculation as much as through conversion, toward exclusive Christian commitment. Wallraff 2001, Bardill 2012, Barnes 2011. **The substrate of imperial Christianity in its founding moment is solar-monotheistic Greco-Roman religion**, not (yet) the dogmatic Nicene Christianity that crystallizes after 325.
- The Constantinian preference for Christianity is part of a longer trend of late-Roman religious **monotheizing** — Stoic, Platonist, solar-cultic, Mithraic, Hermetic — that prepared the cultural ground for Christianity's reception. Cumont, Athanassiadi & Frede 1999 *Pagan Monotheism in Late Antiquity*.

## Documents in play
- *Lactantius, De mortibus persecutorum* 48 — Latin text of the edict.
- *Eusebius, Historia Ecclesiastica* X.5 — Greek text.
- Constantine's own subsequent legislation in the *Codex Theodosianus* extends and elaborates.

## Disputes
- **Was there a single "Edict of Milan"?** Modern scholarship (Anastos, Mitchell) emphasizes that the surviving text is Licinius's letter to his eastern subjects implementing a policy agreed orally at Milan — there is no single document called "the Edict of Milan." The policy is real; the legal-document framing is a later simplification.
- **Galerius's 311 Edict of Toleration.** This earlier (April 311) edict by the dying Galerius had already ended the Great Persecution legally; the Milan agreement extends and confirms.
- **Constantine's "conversion."** When (if ever) Constantine became a Christian in any committed sense is a perennial dispute. Baptized on his deathbed (337) by an Arian bishop; explicit Christian self-identification only after 324.
