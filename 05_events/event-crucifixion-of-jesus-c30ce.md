# Crucifixion of Jesus of Nazareth

## What Happened
Jesus of Nazareth was executed by **Roman crucifixion** outside Jerusalem, at a site called **Golgotha** (Aramaic: "Place of the Skull"; Latin: Calvaria / Calvary), in the governorship of **Pontius Pilate** (~26–36 CE). The date is conventionally placed between 30 and 33 CE; the scholarly consensus clusters around **30 CE** (Brown) or **33 CE** (Humphreys). The execution followed a multi-stage legal-political process: arrest at Gethsemane, a Jewish hearing (before the Sanhedrin or a subset), referral to Pilate, a brief appearance before **Herod Antipas** (Luke's account), and Pilate's sentencing. Death occurred on or before the eve of Passover (the chronology differs between the Synoptics and John — a long-standing scholarly crux). A Roman centurion supervises the execution. Jesus is buried in a tomb donated by Joseph of Arimathea.

## Historical Evidence
The crucifixion is **the best-attested single event in the life of Jesus**. It is confirmed by:
- All four canonical Gospels (Mark 15, Matt 27, Luke 23, John 19) — independent passion-tradition streams
- The pre-Pauline creed of 1 Cor 15:3–5 ("Christ died for our sins according to the scriptures; he was buried") — formulated within ~3–5 years of the event
- Tacitus *Annals* XV.44 (~116 CE): "Christus, from whom the name had its origin, suffered the extreme penalty during the reign of Tiberius at the hands of one of our procurators, Pontius Pilatus"
- Josephus *Antiquities* XVIII.63–64 (the *Testimonium Flavianum*, disputed but partially authentic): "Pilate condemned him to be crucified"
- Lucian of Samosata (~165 CE): refers to Christians worshipping "that crucified sophist"

Virtually no serious historian doubts that Jesus was crucified under Pilate. **This is a stronger historical consensus than for most events of antiquity.**

## Roman Crucifixion as Context
Martin Hengel's *Crucifixion* (1977) establishes the historical reality: **crucifixion was the most degrading death in the Roman legal repertoire**, reserved for slaves, rebels, and the non-citizen dregs of society. It was specifically designed to be public, prolonged, and maximally humiliating — a deterrent theater of imperial power. Paul's formulation "scandal of the cross" (*skandalon*, 1 Cor 1:23) registers the genuine social-cultural shock: a crucified Messiah was an oxymoron. Jewish messianism expected a triumphant Davidic king, not a criminal's death.

The physical process: scourging → forced to carry the patibulum (crossbeam) → nailing through the wrists/forearms and feet → asphyxiation as the hanging body loses the ability to breathe adequately → death within hours to days. The *coup de grâce* (breaking the legs, John 19:31–33) accelerates death by removing the body's ability to push up and breathe.

## Theological Interpretations (the five major models)
The theological interpretation of the event generates the entire history of Christian soteriology — see [[crucifixion-theology]] for the full analysis:
1. **Christus Victor** (Greek Fathers, Aulén 1931) — victory over death and the devil
2. **Satisfaction** (Anselm, *Cur Deus Homo*, 1098) — satisfies God's offended honor
3. **Penal substitution** (Calvin) — Christ bears the divine penalty for human sin
4. **Moral influence** (Abelard) — inspires moral transformation
5. **Theologia crucis** (Luther, 1518) — God revealed *sub contrario* in suffering; opposed to *theologia gloriae*

## The Islamic Interpretation — A Critical Divergence
The Quran (4:157–158) asserts: **"They did not kill him, nor did they crucify him, but it was made to appear so to them"** — the *shubbiha lahum* (substitution) doctrine. Classical Islamic interpretation holds that someone else (Judas, Simon of Cyrene, or another) was crucified in Jesus's place, while Jesus was raised to God. This makes the crucifixion — the best-attested event in Jesus's life — the point of maximum theological divergence between Christianity and Islam. Both traditions agree that crucifixion was prepared; they disagree on whether it happened to Jesus.

## The Al-Hallaj Structural Parallel — ALERT
The most extraordinary cross-tradition parallel in the vault: **[[al-hallaj]]** (858–922 CE), the Sufi mystic who proclaimed "Ana al-Haqq" ("I am the Truth" — using a divine name of God), was tried by the Islamic religious establishment for blasphemy and in 922 CE was:
1. Publicly flogged
2. Crucified (exposed on a cross)
3. Beheaded
4. Burned
5. His ashes scattered in the Tigris

Louis Massignon titled his definitive 4-volume study **"The Passion of al-Hallaj"** — naming the structural parallel explicitly. Both Jesus and al-Hallaj were:
- Mystics who claimed a unique identification with the divine
- Tried by the religious legal establishment of their own tradition
- Found guilty of blasphemy
- Executed by crucifixion as part of the sentence
- Subsequently venerated by mystical sub-traditions as paradigmatic martyrs of divine love
- Interpreted by their traditions' mainstream as either orthodox heroes (Jesus in Christianity) or dangerous exemplars (al-Hallaj in Sunni Islam, though beloved in Sufi tradition)

The parallel is not a claim of historical connection — it is a structural isomorphism showing how the logic of **divine-identity claim + religious-establishment opposition + execution = founding martyrdom** repeats independently across traditions.

## Impact
The crucifixion is the **generative event of Christian theology**. Every subsequent doctrinal development — atonement, incarnation, resurrection, Trinity, sacraments, martyrdom theology — is organized around the meaning of this death. The cross becomes the primary Christian symbol worldwide. The event also directly generates [[martyrdom-theology]]: Jesus as the paradigmatic martyr whose death the community imitates (Paul's "fill up what is lacking in Christ's afflictions," Col 1:24).
