# Destruction of the Serapeum of Alexandria (391 CE)

## What happened
In 391 CE, under **Patriarch Theophilus of Alexandria** (r. 385–412) and with the institutional support of emperor **Theodosius I**'s anti-pagan legislation (the Theodosian decrees of 391 banning pagan worship), a **Christian mob — led by Theophilus and including the *parabalani* (lay shock-troops attached to the Alexandrian church)** — attacked and destroyed the **Serapeum of Alexandria**, the great temple of [[serapis]] on the Acropolis-hill of Rhakotis, one of the **most famous religious buildings of the ancient world**. The temple housed the **colossal cult-statue of Serapis sculpted by Bryaxis** (4th c. BCE) — one of antiquity's most renowned cult-images, described by Clement of Alexandria as containing materials from every part of the world. The statue was dragged out, dismembered, and burned in pieces across Alexandria. The Serapeum complex (which also housed the **"Daughter Library" of Alexandria** — a subsidiary book collection of the great Library, holding tens or hundreds of thousands of scrolls) was demolished, its stones quarried for Christian church-building, and a Christian church was erected on the site.

## Religious consequences
The destruction of the Serapeum is the **single most consequential single event** in the final transition from Egyptian-Greco-Roman polytheism to Christian domination of Egypt:

1. **Effective end of public Egyptian pagan religion.** Egyptian temple religion did not end *immediately* at 391 — the temple of [[isis-egyptian]] at Philae continued until **Justinian's closure in 535**; rural Egyptian temple cult persisted in the countryside (documented by Frankfurter 1998); pagan philosophical schools continued at Alexandria until **Hypatia's murder in 415** and beyond. But the destruction of the Serapeum was understood by contemporaries — both Christian (Rufinus, Theodoret) and pagan (Eunapius, Zosimus) — as the **decisive symbolic event** marking the end of Egyptian state-religious paganism.

2. **Effective end of the Hellenistic-imperial Isis-Serapis state cult.** The Serapis cult, established by [[ptolemy-i-soter]] (~-300) as the deliberate Greco-Egyptian state religion of Ptolemaic Egypt, survived through six centuries of Roman imperial patronage. The destruction of the Alexandrian Serapeum ended the institutional center of this cult. Sub-temples (Sarapeia) elsewhere across the empire were closed or destroyed in the following decades.

3. **Destruction of the Daughter Library.** The Library complex attached to the Serapeum was destroyed alongside the temple. **This is the actual historical "burning of the Library of Alexandria"** that popular accounts often mis-date to Julius Caesar (-48) or the Arab conquest (~+642). Caesar's fire damaged some warehouse-stored books; the Arab conquest did not destroy books (the Library was already gone by 642); **the 391 destruction is the major historical book-loss event** in Alexandria's late-antique history.

4. **Catalyzing event for the philosophical-pagan resistance.** The Serapeum destruction galvanized late-antique pagan philosophical opposition. The siege of the Serapeum was led by **the philosopher Olympius**, who organized pagan resistance and reputedly died in the temple's defense. Eunapius's *Lives of the Sophists* (~+400) reads the destruction as the symbolic moment when Greek philosophical-religious civilization was overthrown by Christian barbarism.

5. **Hermetic and Egyptian-religious diaspora.** The destruction did NOT end Hermetic literature ([[phase-3-021-hermetic-corpus-earliest]], [[phase-4-012-asclepius]] continue in private transmission); rural Egyptian cult continues; but the **institutional center of Greco-Egyptian state-religious culture is destroyed**. The Hermetic *Asclepius* dialogue's famous lamentation ("Egypt will be filled with foreigners; the gods will leave Egypt and return to heaven... only stones will be left to tell of Egypt's piety") is read as proleptic of exactly this kind of event.

6. **Christian appropriation of the site.** A Christian basilica was built on the Serapeum site; sacred topography passed from pagan to Christian use — an institutional pattern that becomes standard across the Mediterranean in the next two centuries. The Serapeum destruction is the **paradigm event** for what becomes a Mediterranean-wide pattern (the *temple-to-church* transformation Hahn-Emmel-Gotter 2008 documents systematically).

7. **Theodosian anti-pagan legislation.** The Serapeum destruction is institutionally enabled by **Theodosius I**'s comprehensive anti-pagan legislation of -391 (the *Theodosian Code* 16.10 ff.), which bans all forms of pagan worship across the empire. The Serapeum's destruction is the **first major test-case** of these laws and demonstrates the imperial-church alliance's willingness to use organized force against pagan institutions.

## Documents in play
- [[phase-3-021-hermetic-corpus-earliest]] / [[phase-4-012-asclepius]] — the Hermetic corpus's lamentation prophecies (esp. *Asclepius* 24–25) read as proleptic of exactly this kind of event.
- **No documents are produced by this event in a positive sense**; the destruction is a *book-loss event* affecting the Daughter Library.

## Disputes
- **Scope of book-loss.** Estimates of how many scrolls were lost at the Serapeum's Daughter Library vary widely — from tens of thousands to several hundred thousand. The total is uncertain; the loss is real and major.
- **Date specificity.** Conventional date 391 CE; some sources prefer 392; the year is uncontested.
- **Theophilus's role.** Whether Theophilus personally led the violence (Rufinus's account) or institutionally enabled it from a more distance (some modern scholarship) is debated. The institutional responsibility is uncontested.
- **The death of Olympius the philosopher.** The pagan-sympathetic source Eunapius reports Olympius died defending the temple; modern scholarship is less certain about the specific death narrative but accepts the leadership-of-pagan-defense.
