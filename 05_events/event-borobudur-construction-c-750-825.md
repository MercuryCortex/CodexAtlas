# Borobudur (c. 750–825 CE)

## Identity
Borobudur is a monumental stepped-pyramid Buddhist temple complex on the Kedu Plain of Central Java, c. 40 km northwest of Yogyakarta, built under Sailendra-dynasty patronage in the late 8th to early 9th century CE. It is the largest Buddhist monument in the world by volume — c. 55,000 m³ of andesite stone — and one of the most iconographically complete surviving statements of Mahayana cosmology and soteriology. Inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1991.

The monument occupies a single hill, sheathed in stone to produce a stepped square-based pyramidal volume rising to a central main stupa surmounting a circular upper platform. Total footprint c. 123 × 123 m; height c. 35 m. The monument is laid out as a three-dimensional mandala — a vertical compression of the Mahayana cosmological scheme into climbable architecture.

## Construction
Built in stages c. 750–825 CE under Sailendra-dynasty Buddhist royal patronage in Central Java during the period of Sailendra dominance (the Sailendras were Mahayana Buddhist, while the contemporary Sanjaya dynasty of central Java was Shaivite — Borobudur and the Shaivite Prambanan temple complex c. 850 are the major surviving monuments of this religious-political dyad). The monument was abandoned by c. 1000 CE — probably in connection with the eastward shift of central-Javanese political power and the regional turn toward Hindu / Shaivite patronage, and the c. 1006 eruption of Mount Merapi which buried the monument in volcanic ash and tropical vegetation. Borobudur was "rediscovered" in 1814 under Thomas Stamford Raffles's British colonial administration; cleared and surveyed under Cornelius and others 1814–1830s.

Significant restorations: van Erp's Dutch colonial campaign 1907–1911 (stabilization of the upper platform); the UNESCO / Government of Indonesia full-scale restoration 1973–1983 led by Soekmono (complete disassembly of the lower galleries, drainage and stabilization of the substructure, reassembly).

The legendary architect "Gunadharma" is named only in much later Javanese chronicles and is not historically attested.

## Religious significance
**Mandala-as-pilgrimage-architecture.** The monument's program is a three-dimensional Mahayana cosmological-soteriological mandala. The pilgrim ascends through three vertical zones:

1. **Kāmadhātu** (the realm of desire) — the lower, partly-buried encased plinth, decorated with the Karmavibhanga relief series depicting karmic cause-and-effect in the human moral world. (Partly hidden behind the encasement wall, which was added during construction itself, possibly for engineering or for theological-pedagogical reasons.)
2. **Rūpadhātu** (the realm of form) — the four square-plan terraces above, with 1,460 narrative reliefs on the inner and outer walls of the galleries, depicting (in ascending order): the Lalitavistara (life of Sakyamuni Buddha), Jataka-Avadana (former lives of the Buddha and exemplary bodhisattvas), and Gandavyuha (the pilgrimage of the lay youth Sudhana to fifty-three spiritual teachers — the climactic narrative of the Avatamsaka Sutra).
3. **Arūpadhātu** (the formless realm) — the three circular upper platforms supporting 72 bell-shaped perforated stupas, each containing a seated Buddha statue, surrounding the central main stupa (which was empty or contained an unfinished / fragmentary Buddha statue, depending on which excavation report one accepts).

**504 Buddha statues** are distributed across the monument: 432 in niches on the four square terraces (108 on each side, oriented in the four cardinal directions and showing four directional mudrās plus the dharmacakra mudra on the upper square terrace — corresponding to the Five Tathagata / Pancha-Buddha scheme of the Vajradhatu mandala), and 72 in the perforated stupas of the upper circular platforms.

**Pilgrimage performance.** The pilgrim circumambulates each terrace clockwise (pradakṣiṇā), ascending from the moral-causal world of Kāmadhātu through the narrated dharma-path of Rūpadhātu to the apophatic Arūpadhātu and the central stupa, retracing in architectural space the Mahayana path from samsaric entanglement to enlightenment. The Gandavyuha-Sudhana reliefs of the upper galleries are the iconographic anchor: the pilgrim is Sudhana, the architecture is the path.

## Disputes
- **Function: temple, stupa, or both?** Borobudur lacks an interior pilgrim-space; the central main stupa is solid (or contained a single Buddha statue) and is not enterable. It is therefore not a temple in the typical Indian or Southeast Asian sense (no cult-image-housing garbhagriha) and not a relic-stupa in the standard sense (no documented relic deposit, though small relic finds have been reported during restorations). The best current synthesis (Miksic 1990; Long 2009) is that Borobudur is *both* a stūpa (in the iconographic sense: a cosmic axis crowned by the bell-form) *and* a path-temple (in the performative sense: an architecture to be walked through to enact the bodhisattva path).
- **Mandala scheme.** Multiple competing mandala-identifications: Vajradhatu (Snodgrass), Garbhadhatu (older proposals), a syncretic Sailendra-specific scheme (Lokesh Chandra), or a generic Mahayana cosmological scheme rather than any one specific tantric mandala (Miksic). No single identification is uncontroversially accepted.
- **The covered foot.** The Karmavibhanga relief series of the lowest tier was encased during construction itself, probably between c. 800 and c. 820. Why: engineering reinforcement (structural-instability hypothesis, Soekmono); deliberate theological-pedagogical concealment of the karmic-cause-and-effect series so that pilgrims would not be deterred (Krom's older hypothesis); a later devotional-cosmological revision (van Erp). Probably engineering, with the theological framing as later rationalization.
- **Date and patronage.** The Karangtengah inscription (824 CE) attributes the monument to a Sailendra ruler whose name is variously read as Samaratungga, but the inscription is fragmentary and the attribution is the best reading rather than certain.
