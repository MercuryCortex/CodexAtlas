# Ambedkar's Mass Conversion to Buddhism (1956)

**On October 14, 1956**, B.R. Ambedkar — chairman of India's constitutional drafting committee, social reformer, and lifelong opponent of the caste system — converted to Buddhism alongside approximately **500,000 followers** at the Deekshabhoomi ground in Nagpur, Maharashtra. This is the largest single-day mass religious conversion in recorded history. Ambedkar died six weeks later on December 6, 1956.

## The event

Ambedkar received his refuge and precepts from the Sri Lankan Theravadin monk U Chandramani, then turned and administered the vows to the assembled crowd of approximately 500,000 Dalit followers. He also recited 22 additional vows that he had composed, explicitly rejecting Hinduism and the brahminical order:

Selected vows:
1. "I shall have no faith in Brahma, Vishnu, and Mahesh nor shall I worship them."
4. "I do not and shall not believe that God took birth."
5. "I do not and shall not believe that Lord Buddha was the incarnation of Vishnu."
13. "I shall endeavor to establish equality."
22. "I believe that I am having a re-birth."

The 22 vows constitute a formal theological severance from Hinduism and a founding confession of Navayana Buddhism. The vow that "I do not believe that Lord Buddha was the incarnation of Vishnu" directly addresses the standard Hindu strategy of absorbing Buddhist critique by making the Buddha an avatar of Vishnu — a strategy Ambedkar explicitly identified and refused.

## Cross-tradition significance

### The largest mass conversion in history as theological act
No other single-day religious conversion event approaches this scale. The closest parallels are:
- The mass baptism of Kievan Rus by Vladimir I (~988 CE) — forced conversion of an entire principality
- The Cao Dai movement's founding (1926) — gradual mass conversion of Vietnamese
- The Taiping Rebellion's millennial conversions — coercive

Ambedkar's conversion was voluntary, philosophically grounded, and explicitly anti-coercive — each convert having chosen to follow Ambedkar's theological lead. It is the world's most documented example of religion-as-emancipation-strategy enacted at scale.

### Return of Buddhism to its birthplace
Buddhism originated in India (~6th–5th century BCE) and was largely extinguished there by the 12th–13th centuries CE (university destruction at Nalanda 1193, Vikramashila 1203, combined with Brahmanical re-absorption). Ambedkar's conversion was a geographical return: Buddhism came home 700+ years after near-extinction in the land of its birth, under explicitly anti-caste terms that the Buddha himself — who accepted outcaste members into the sangha — would have recognized.

### The Nagpur site
The Deekshabhoomi ("Ground of Initiation") in Nagpur has become one of the most important pilgrimage sites in modern Buddhism worldwide. On every October 14, hundreds of thousands of Dalit pilgrims visit. It is a site of *political-religious memory* — like the Edmund Pettus Bridge in Selma or Robben Island — where the sacred and the political are inseparable.
