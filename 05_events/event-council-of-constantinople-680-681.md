# Third Council of Constantinople (680–681)

## What happened
Convoked by Emperor Constantine IV after the failure of the Monothelite policy that had been pushed by his grandfather Heraclius (the *Ekthesis* of 638) and his great-grandfather's successor (the *Typos* of Constans II, 648). Monothelitism — the doctrine that the incarnate Christ has only **one will** (the divine), even though he has two natures (divine and human) — had been advanced as a compromise to win back the non-Chalcedonian East after the Arab conquests had carried Syria and Egypt out of the empire and effectively voided the political calculus that had favored the compromise. The council met across 18 sessions from November 680 to September 681 and defined: in Christ are **two natural wills and two natural operations**, "not contrary one to the other... but his human will follows, and not as resisting and reluctant, but rather as subject to his divine and omnipotent will." Sergius, Cyrus, Pyrrhus, Paul, and Peter were anathematized; **and the council also anathematized Pope Honorius I (d. 638)** for having, in correspondence with Sergius, indicated assent to "one will" language.

## Religious consequences
- **Closure of the seventh-century Christological controversies** (Monoenergism / Monothelitism). Chalcedon's two-natures Christology now had its two-wills / two-operations corollary explicit.
- **Vindicates Maximus the Confessor**, who had been the architect of the theological resistance to Monothelitism at the Lateran Synod of 649 and was punished by mutilation (tongue and right hand) and exile, dying in 662 — 19 years before his theology was formally ratified.
- **The condemnation of Honorius** is a sticking point for Roman papal-infallibility theology — an ecumenical council and subsequent popes formally anathematized a Pope of Rome. The fact figures in the 1870 First Vatican Council debates over the dogma of papal infallibility.
- **Effectively ends the Greek-language ecumenical-council pattern** for handling Christological controversy. The 8th-c. Council of Nicaea II (787) is iconological, not Christological.

## Substrate connections
- The Monothelite settlement was a political-theological response to the loss of Syria-Egypt to the Arab conquest (634–642). The doctrinal solution failed because the political problem it was designed to solve had become moot: the Monophysite East was no longer inside the empire. The conciliar settlement of 680–681 thus marks the moment at which **Byzantine Christology stops trying to accommodate the now-extra-imperial Miaphysite traditions** (Coptic, Syriac, Armenian) — sealing the post-Chalcedonian East-vs-Oriental-Orthodox division as permanent.
- Maximus the Confessor's two-wills theology, which the council ratified, draws on:
  - Cappadocian Trinitarian metaphysics (will as a property of nature, not of person).
  - Stoic and Aristotelian moral psychology (the will as rational appetite, *gnomic* will distinguished from natural will).
  - The Dionysian / Origenist tradition of the soul's progressive alignment with the divine.
  This is one of the most philosophically sophisticated patristic deployments of Greek philosophical anthropology in service of Christology.

## Documents in play
- **Conciliar acta** — Greek and Latin, preserved.
- **The *Ekthesis* of Heraclius (638)** and **the *Typos* of Constans II (648)** — the Monothelite imperial decrees the council overturns.
- **The Lateran Synod of 649 acta** (composed in significant part by Maximus the Confessor) — the prior Latin condemnation.
- **Pope Agatho's letter to Constantine IV** — the formal Roman position.

## Disputes
- **The condemnation of Honorius.** Catholic responses range from "he taught Monothelite doctrine and was rightly condemned" to "his condemnation was for negligence, not for heretical teaching." The fact of the conciliar anathema is not in dispute; its theological significance for papal infallibility is.
- **Maximus's theological method.** Whether his two-wills doctrine genuinely follows from Chalcedon or constitutes a new development is debated among Catholic, Orthodox, and Oriental Orthodox theologians today.
