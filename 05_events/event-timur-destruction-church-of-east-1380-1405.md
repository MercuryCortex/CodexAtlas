# Timur's Destruction of the Church of the East (1380–1405)

**Between 1380 and 1405**, Timur-i-Leng ("Timur the Lame," Tamerlane, r. 1370–1405) conducted a series of military campaigns across Central Asia, Persia, the Caucasus, and Anatolia that **effectively destroyed the Church of the East** — the Syriac-speaking Christian denomination that had, at its height in the 9th–13th centuries, been the geographically most extensive Christian tradition in the world, with communities stretching from Antioch and Baghdad to India and Tang-dynasty China.

## What was destroyed

Before Timur, the **Church of the East** (*Kīlīttā d-Maḏnḥā*, "Church of the East") had:
- Its Catholicosate at Ctesiphon/Seleucia on the Tigris (later Baghdad after the Abbasid conquest)
- Bishops in Persia, Arabia, Central Asia, India (the Thomas Christians / Malabar coast), and China (where the Tang-dynasty Nestorian mission had planted churches by 635 CE and erected the [[event-nestorian-stele-781|Xi'an Stele]] in 781 CE)
- A monastic tradition and theological school at Nisibis that rivaled Alexandria and Constantinople in learning
- The [[phase-4-108-peshitta|Peshitta]] as its scriptural tradition — the oldest continuously-used Bible translation

Timur's campaigns, conducted under the banner of Sunni Islamic orthodoxy, explicitly targeted Christian communities alongside Shia Muslims, Hindus, and anyone who resisted his armies. The massacres at Tikrit (1393), Amid (Diyarbakır), and across Anatolia and Mesopotamia annihilated the Christian populations of regions that had been continuously Christian since the 1st century CE. Timur is said to have constructed towers of skulls from Christian communities.

## The before-and-after

Before Timur: The Church of the East was the world's most geographically extensive Christian denomination — communities from Lebanon to Beijing, with the Patriarch of the East potentially representing more Christians than the Pope or the Patriarch of Constantinople when measured by geographic reach.

After Timur: The Church of the East survived only in isolated mountain communities (the Hakkari highlands, southeastern Turkey) and in India (the Thomas Christians, who were far enough away to survive). By 1500, what had been a 750-year Silk Road Christian civilization was reduced to a few hundred thousand survivors in remote highlands. The church did not recover and today survives as the Assyrian Church of the East (~400,000 members worldwide).

## Cross-tradition significance

### The most under-documented religious catastrophe in world history
The Timurid destruction of the Church of the East is almost entirely absent from Western history curricula. The reason is structural: Western Christianity (Roman Catholic and later Protestant) had already lost contact with Eastern Christianity by the 14th century, and the Church of the East's communities were too geographically distant to generate Western records of their destruction. Philip Jenkins (*The Lost History of Christianity*, 2008) argues that the systematic amnesia about the Church of the East — once the world's most globally distributed Christian tradition — is the most consequential gap in popular understanding of Christian history.

### Parallel with other tradition-destruction events
The Timurid destruction of Nestorian Christianity is structurally parallel to:
- The Albigensian Crusade (1209–1229) — systematic destruction of Catharism in southern France
- The Ottoman campaigns against Yazidis (72 *firmans*, 1640–1918) — repeated attempts to eliminate a small endogamous religious community
- The 2014 ISIS genocide of Yazidis at Sinjar — same geographic zone, same pattern

In all cases: a dominant military power defines a small religious community as heretical or deviant, and uses that definition to justify organized elimination. The Nestorian case is distinctive in scale: this was not a small community but the world's most geographically distributed Christian tradition.
