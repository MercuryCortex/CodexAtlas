# Decipherment of Akkadian Cuneiform

## What happened
A 55-year decipherment effort, building on Grotefend's preliminary work on Old Persian cuneiform (1802–1815), Rawlinson's heroic copying of the trilingual (Old Persian, Elamite, Akkadian) Behistun Inscription in western Iran (Mt. Behistun, dangerously high cliff face) over multiple expeditions 1835–1847, Hincks's theoretical breakthroughs on the syllabic-logographic mixed structure (1846 onward), and culminating in the 1857 **"Cuneiform Jury" test** at the Royal Asiatic Society, London: Talbot proposed that the four leading cuneiformists (Rawlinson, Hincks, Talbot himself, Oppert) be given the same previously-unread Akkadian text in sealed envelopes; they returned translations that substantially agreed. The Royal Asiatic Society committee judged the test successful, formally establishing the decipherment's validity.

## Religious consequences
Akkadian decipherment opened the entire Mesopotamian religious literature — Enuma Elish, Gilgamesh, Atrahasis, Code of Hammurabi's prologue/epilogue, Babylonian wisdom literature, the omen literature — to systematic philological reading. The cumulative impact on biblical studies was immense: George Smith's 1872 identification of a Babylonian flood narrative (in Gilgamesh Tablet XI) on cuneiform tablets from the Library of [[ashurbanipal]] triggered immediate public sensation and forced 19th- and early-20th-century biblical scholarship to engage seriously with Mesopotamian-Hebrew comparativism (the *Babel und Bibel* controversy of Delitzsch 1902–1904 is one downstream marker). Sumerian (recovered later, through Akkadian-Sumerian bilingual texts) extended the recovery to the earliest known religious literature.

## Documents in play
The entire Phase 1 Mesopotamian textual layer (Kesh Temple Hymn, Pyramid Texts as ANE context, Enuma Elish, Atrahasis, Gilgamesh, Code of Hammurabi, Amarna Letters) entered modern legibility via the cuneiform decipherment.

## Disputes
- **Priority among Hincks, Rawlinson, Oppert**: long-running scholarly priority dispute. Hincks's theoretical contributions were arguably the most important but he received less public credit than Rawlinson; Oppert's framing of "Akkadian" as the name of the language (vs. Rawlinson's "Babylonian-Assyrian") prevailed.
