# Byzantine Iconoclast Controversy (726–843)

## What happened
A 117-year struggle, in two main phases (726–787 and 815–843), over whether Christian images of Christ, the Theotokos, the angels, and the saints were to be made, displayed in churches, and given liturgical veneration.

**First iconoclasm (726–787).** Emperor Leo III, possibly under influence of Islamic and Jewish aniconism on the empire's eastern frontier and after the catastrophic Thera-Santorini volcanic eruption of 726 (read theologically as divine judgment on idolatry), initiated the removal of images, most famously the Christ icon over the *Chalke* gate of the imperial palace in Constantinople. His son Constantine V was a fiercer iconoclast — theologically sophisticated, persecuting iconophile monks and producing his own theological *Peuseis*. The Council of Hieria 754 ratified iconoclasm. Empress Irene reversed the policy after Constantine V's death; the Council of Nicaea II (787) defined the iconophile position.

**Second iconoclasm (815–843).** Emperor Leo V the Armenian, after military setbacks (read as judgment on iconophile policy), reinstituted iconoclasm. Theodore the Studite led iconophile resistance and was repeatedly exiled. The Empress Theodora, after the death of her husband Theophilus, definitively restored icon veneration in 843 — the "Triumph of Orthodoxy," celebrated annually in the Orthodox calendar on the First Sunday of Lent.

## Religious consequences
- **Eastern Orthodox iconographic tradition** is the post-843 settlement: icons as legitimate liturgical art, theologically grounded in the Incarnation, technically constrained by traditional canons. This tradition (Constantinople, then Russia, the Balkans, Crete, Mount Athos) develops on the platform Nicaea II / 843 secured.
- **Massive destruction of pre-iconoclast religious art.** Most of what is now known of pre-726 Byzantine icon production is reconstructed from surviving evidence in regions outside Byzantine control during iconoclasm (esp. Saint Catherine's at Sinai; Roman churches). The damage to the cultural-art record is enormous and irreversible.
- **Theological articulation of incarnational matter-theology.** John of Damascus's *Three Treatises on the Divine Images* (the foundational iconophile defense): the Incarnation makes matter capable of bearing the divine; image-veneration (*proskynesis*) is distinguished from worship (*latreia*) due God alone. This theology becomes core Eastern Orthodox doctrine.
- **Latin-Greek estrangement deepened.** The Frankish *Libri Carolini* (792) rejected both iconoclasm and (the Frankish reading of) iconophilia. Mutual misunderstanding accumulated through to the Great Schism of 1054.

## Substrate connections — the cross-tradition tracing
- **Iconoclasm is the patristic Christian re-encounter with the deep substrate of Jewish-and-Islamic aniconism.** The Second Commandment ("Thou shalt not make any graven image") is the explicit iconoclast biblical warrant. The Christian church, having domesticated images by stages through 4th–6th c. practice, now has to articulate why this is compatible with the Old Testament prohibition. Islamic aniconism (Caliphal coinage reform 696, prohibition of human-figural representation in mosques) is the immediate political-religious neighbor on Byzantium's eastern frontier — Leo III is a soldier-emperor from the Anatolian frontier with strong Syrian / Anatolian formation. The substrate connection is direct: **iconoclasm is, in significant part, a Jewish-Islamic-style aniconism re-entering Christianity under political-military pressure**. Brubaker & Haldon 2011: 79–155.
- **Iconophilia draws on Greco-Roman cult-image practice substrate.** The veneration practices Nicaea II ratified — incense, lights, prostration, kisses — are continuous with older Mediterranean cult-image practice (Greek *agalmata*, Roman *signa* of the gods). The iconophile theology (relative honor terminating on prototype) was anticipated by pagan philosophical defenders of cult images (Porphyry, *On Images*; Iamblichus, *De Mysteriis*). Pelikan 1990 traces the continuities.
- **The Marian iconography that flourishes post-843 has Mother-Goddess substrate visual prototypes** — Isis-and-Horus visual schema for Mary-and-Christ is extensively documented (Mathews 1993; Mathews & Muller 2017, *The Dawn of Christian Art in Panel Paintings and Icons*). The geographic-iconographic continuity from Egyptian Mother-Goddess imagery to Byzantine Marian imagery is one of the cleanest visible cases of substrate persistence in Christian devotional art.
- **The Old Testament second-commandment substrate** of iconoclasm is its own continuation: Jewish aniconism is itself a development from a polemical reaction to Ancient Near Eastern cult-image practice; iconoclast Christianity inherits this polemical posture against image-cult.

## Disputes
- **Causes of Leo III's iconoclast initiative.** Islamic influence (older view, e.g. Vasiliev), internal Christian-theological reasoning (Brubaker & Haldon's contemporary view), or apocalyptic / political response to the Thera eruption — all have been argued. Brubaker & Haldon 2011 emphasize internal Byzantine factors over Islamic influence.
- **Scale of iconoclast persecution.** Earlier scholarship (Ostrogorsky, Treadgold) emphasized massive monk-persecution; Brubaker & Haldon argue the persecution was more limited and more focused on specific elite iconophile resisters.
- **Theological coherence of iconoclast position.** Constantine V's *Peuseis* show sophisticated Christological reasoning (an image of Christ either implies Nestorianism — depicting only the human — or Monophysitism — depicting the divine, an idolatrous impossibility). The position is theologically serious, not crude.
