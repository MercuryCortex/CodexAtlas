# Battle of Hattin (4 July 1187)

## What happened
On 4 July 1187, Saladin's Ayyubid army annihilated the combined field force of the Crusader Kingdom of Jerusalem on a waterless ridge near the Horns of Hattin (a double-peaked volcanic hill above the Sea of Galilee). The Crusaders — approximately 20,000 men including the full military might of the Kingdom and its military orders — were surrounded, denied water, and defeated in detail after two days of marching in summer heat.

**Casualties among the Templars:** approximately 230 Templar knights were captured and executed by Saladin's orders (Saladin offered the choice of execution or conversion; the Templars chose execution). This is the largest single-day loss of Templar knights in the Order's entire 200-year history.

**The True Cross:** The relic believed to be the actual cross of the Crucifixion — carried into battle as a divine guarantee of victory — was captured by Saladin. It was never returned and its fate is unknown. The theological shock of the True Cross's capture was immense across Christendom.

**Three months later:** Jerusalem fell to Saladin on 2 October 1187. The Kingdom of Jerusalem, established by the First Crusade in 1099, had lasted 88 years.

## Why this matters for the Templar chain

The Battle of Hattin is the **structural hinge** of the entire Templar story:
- **Before Hattin:** The Templars are the martial guardians of the Kingdom; they have a reason to exist; they hold fortresses; they police pilgrimage routes
- **After Hattin:** The Kingdom is largely gone; the Templars' operational purpose is destroyed; 100 years of recovery attempts (Third Crusade, subsequent Crusades) fail; when Acre falls (1291), the Templars lose their last military rationale — and the suppression of 1307–1314 becomes thinkable

The Hattin defeat is thus the **root cause** of the Templar suppression: not Philip IV's greed (though that mattered) but the Order's loss of military purpose when there was no more Crusader state to defend. An institution without a mission is vulnerable. By 1307, the Templars had been sitting on immense wealth in Europe for 16 years since Acre, without a clear function. Philip IV's accusations found purchase in that vacuum.

## The chivalric paradox: Saladin
Saladin's conduct at and after Hattin was widely recorded — both in Arabic and Latin sources — as exemplary chivalric behavior. He allowed the surviving rank-and-file soldiers to go free; he executed only the Templars and Hospitallers (military orders) and Raynald of Châtillon (who had repeatedly violated truces). He later allowed Christian pilgrims access to Jerusalem after its surrender. The European chivalric literature that emerged in the 13th–14th centuries often cites Saladin as a paragon of courtesy and honor — the enemy whose virtues shamed the Crusaders. This is the origin of the literary Saladin tradition that runs from boccaccio's *Decameron* through countless romantic treatments.

The paradox: the man who destroyed the Templars' kingdom was remembered by later Christian tradition as more chivalric than many Crusaders.
