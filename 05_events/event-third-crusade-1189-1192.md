# Third Crusade

## What happened
A coalition crusade (1189–1192) launched in response to **Saladin's victory at Hattin (4 July 1187)** and his subsequent capture of Jerusalem (October 1187). The three principal Western European monarchs — Richard I of England, Philip II Augustus of France, and Holy Roman Emperor Frederick I Barbarossa — committed to the campaign, generating the *Kings' Crusade* nickname. Frederick drowned crossing the Saleph river in Cilicia (June 1190); the bulk of his army turned back. Richard and Philip arrived at Acre in spring 1191 and completed the siege of Acre (which the Latin Kingdom remnant had been conducting since 1189). Philip departed for France in late 1191; Richard continued the campaign, defeated Saladin at the Battle of Arsuf (September 1191), but never attempted Jerusalem itself, recognizing that holding it after capture would have been logistically impossible. The Treaty of Jaffa (September 1192) ended the campaign: Saladin retained Jerusalem, Latin Christians retained pilgrim access, the Latin coastal strip (Acre, Tyre, Jaffa) was preserved.

## Religious consequences
- **Restored but reduced Latin Kingdom.** The Latin Kingdom continues 1192–1291 as a thin coastal strip, capital at Acre. No Latin Jerusalem after 1187 (except briefly 1229–1244).
- **Saladin's reputation.** Saladin becomes the most positively-imaged Muslim ruler in Latin Christian memory — uniquely so for the medieval period. Dante places him in Limbo (*Inferno* 4.129), not Hell proper.
- **Templar / Hospitaller Cyprus.** Richard's conquest of Cyprus (1191) from the Byzantine usurper Isaac Komnenos creates the Latin Lordship of Cyprus, eventually a refuge for the [[tradition-knights-templar|Templars]] (briefly, 1291–1306) and the [[tradition-hospitallers|Hospitallers]] (1291–1310).
- **The myth of Saladin in later Western literature.** Walter Scott's *The Talisman* (1825), 19th-c. Orientalist literature, the 20th-c. cinematic Saladin (Youssef Chahine, 1963; Ridley Scott, 2005).

## Documents in play
- Latin: *Itinerarium Peregrinorum*; Ambroise's verse chronicle.
- Arabic: Baha al-Din ibn Shaddad's Saladin biography is unusually well-disposed and detailed for a contemporary court source.

## Disputes
- **Why did Richard not attempt Jerusalem?** Twice he approached, twice he turned back. Gillingham 1999 argues this was a strategically sound recognition that taking Jerusalem with a small force was easy but holding it impossible.
- **The Massacre at Acre (1191).** Richard executed ~2,700 Muslim prisoners after the Acre surrender when ransom negotiations stalled. Möhring 2008 and Gillingham 1999 differ on the level of war-crime intentionality.
