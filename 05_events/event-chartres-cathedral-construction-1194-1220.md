# Chartres Cathedral (1194–1220)

## Identity
The Cathedral of Notre-Dame de Chartres is the principal exemplar of the early-13th-century French Gothic, located 80 km southwest of Paris in Eure-et-Loir. The present structure is the fifth or sixth cathedral on the site, raised between 1194 and c. 1220 after a catastrophic fire on 10 June 1194 destroyed the Romanesque cathedral consecrated 1037. Earlier cathedrals on the same site were burnt in 743, 858, and 1020 (the 1020 fire prompting the Romanesque rebuild). The 1194–1220 reconstruction preserved the Romanesque western façade (the Royal Portal, c. 1145–1155, with the famous "Beau Dieu" tympanum and column-figure jamb-statues — these are not part of the Gothic rebuild but inherited from the previous structure), the crypt, and the relic of the Sancta Camisia.

The cathedral was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1979.

## Construction
The 1194 fire reportedly destroyed the cathedral but spared the Sancta Camisia (the linen reportedly worn by the Virgin Mary at the Annunciation or the Nativity, given to the cathedral by Charles the Bald in 876) — a survival interpreted by Bishop Renaud de Mouçon and the chapter as a sign that the Virgin willed a more magnificent church. The relief operation generated extraordinary lay donor support: the cathedral was largely rebuilt within 26 years (1194–1220), an extremely fast Gothic construction. The cathedral was consecrated 24 October 1260 in the presence of Louis IX.

Architectural features that make Chartres the canonical Gothic exemplar:
- **Rib-vaulted nave** of unprecedented height (36 m) and stability.
- **Flying buttress system** in its mature form — the earliest deployment of multi-tier flying buttresses as the primary structural support, enabling the elimination of the gallery story and the corresponding expansion of clerestory windows.
- **The most complete medieval stained-glass programme in Europe** — 176 windows, c. 2,600 m² of glazing, the great majority surviving from the 1194–1220 campaign; the celebrated *bleu de Chartres* blue pigment.
- **Sculpture programme**: the Royal Portal (c. 1145–1155, pre-fire) on the west; the early-13th-century north (Old Testament, life of the Virgin) and south (New Testament, last judgment) transept porches. Roughly 4,000 sculpted figures total.

## Religious significance
**Marian cult center.** Chartres was one of the four principal Marian pilgrimage centres of medieval France (with Le Puy, Rocamadour, and Notre-Dame de Boulogne), drawing pilgrims from across Latin Christendom. The Sancta Camisia (often glossed as the "tunic" but more accurately the *camisia* / under-garment / shift of the Virgin) is the central relic; tradition holds it was sent to the cathedral by Charles the Bald in 876, having reached Constantinople from Jerusalem. The relic was a major source of revenue, processions, and theological identity for the cathedral; the four annual Marian feasts (Purification, Annunciation, Assumption, Nativity of Mary) structured the cathedral's liturgical year. Pilgrim badges with Marian iconography were a major component of the medieval Chartres economy.

**The "school of Chartres" cathedral school.** Chartres was a major centre of 12th-century learning under successive chancellors (Fulbert in the early 11th c.; Bernard of Chartres c. 1124; Thierry of Chartres c. 1141; Gilbert de la Porrée; William of Conches; Bernardus Silvestris). The school cultivated a Platonist-Christian curriculum integrating Macrobius, Calcidius's translation of Plato's *Timaeus*, the *quadrivium*, and the early scientific naturalism that produced texts like Thierry's *Tractatus de sex dierum operibus* and Bernardus Silvestris's *Cosmographia*. Whether the cathedral architecture itself reflects the school's theology-of-light is debated (see Disputes).

**Theology of light.** Von Simson 1956 argues that 12th–13th c. Gothic — beginning with Abbot Suger's program at Saint-Denis 1140s, decisively elaborated at Chartres post-1194 — is the architectural expression of a Pseudo-Dionysian and Neoplatonic theology in which God is the *Lux* through which all things are; the stained-glass-filled interior of the Gothic cathedral is an enacted theological argument that the divine light enters the world through differentiated colour and pattern. Whether the master masons consciously deployed this theology or whether it is a post-hoc theological reading of an engineering-driven architectural shift is contested (the Mâle and von Simson reading is the standard one; Bony 1983 and others have argued for a more strictly architectural-engineering driver).

## Disputes
- **Druidic / pre-Christian foundation hypothesis.** The 19th-c. Romantic and 20th-c. esoteric tradition (Charpentier 1972 is the canonical modern statement) holds that Chartres was built on a pre-Christian Druidic sanctuary centred on a sacred well and a black-stone or black-Madonna pre-Christian goddess cult. Mainstream archaeology and historical scholarship rejects this — the alleged "Druidic" elements (the well in the crypt is real but is the cathedral's own; the "Black Madonna" Notre-Dame-de-Sous-Terre is a late medieval statue, not pre-Christian; the Carnute / Druidic association rests on Caesar *BG* 6.13 placing the Druidic assembly "in the territory of the Carnutes" — Chartres is in that territory but Caesar does not specify the site). The hypothesis remains popular in esoteric and tourist literature; cataloged as Tier-4 reception, not historical foundation. [claim: contested in popular discourse, rejected in mainstream scholarship]
- **Theological intentionality of the design.** Von Simson 1956 (Neoplatonic / Dionysian intentionality) vs. Bony 1983 / Williams 1993 (architectural-engineering autonomy with theological readings layered later). The Atlas position: both readings are catalogued; the theology-of-light reading is well-attested in Suger's writings about Saint-Denis but cannot be directly attributed to the Chartres masons without overreach.
- **Master masons' identity.** Building accounts from the 1194–1220 campaign do not survive in a form that names the architects; stylistic analysis has identified at least two distinct master-masons by their characteristic profiles ("the Bronze Master" and "the Green Master" of older scholarship; "the Royal Portal Master" survives only as a reconstructed pre-1194 figure). No verified name.
