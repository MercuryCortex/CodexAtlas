# Middle Persian (Pahlavi)

The southwestern Middle Iranian language of the Sasanian Empire (224-651 CE) and the post-Sasanian Zoroastrian commentarial tradition; the principal bridge between [[language-old-persian|Old Persian]] of the Achaemenid royal house and the New Persian of the Islamic Iranian world. Pahlavi is also the language of [[phase-4-016-shabuhragan|Mānī's Šābuhragān]] — the founding cosmological treatise of Manichaeism — and one of the two principal languages (alongside [[language-syriac|Syriac]]) of Sasanian-era religious life.

## What Middle Persian is

Middle Persian = "Pahlavi" in most traditional Iranian-studies usage, though strict linguistic terminology distinguishes between *Middle Persian* (the language) and *Pahlavi* (the script + the Zoroastrian commentarial corpus written in that script). The language is the direct continuation of Old Persian after the Achaemenid collapse, attested first in the early Sasanian royal inscriptions (Šābuhr I, Narseh, Kerdīr; 3rd c. CE) and then in two parallel literary corpora:

1. **The Zoroastrian Pahlavi books** — Bundahišn, Dēnkard, Arda Wirāz Nāmag, Mēnōg ī Xrad, Dādestān ī Dēnīg, Pahlavi Rivāyats. These are compiled mostly in the 9th-10th centuries CE, after the Arab conquest, by Zoroastrian priests preserving Sasanian theological learning. They preserve the Sasanian-period theological canon in a slightly archaized register.
2. **The Manichaean Middle Persian corpus** — Mānī's own works (especially the [[phase-4-016-shabuhragan|Šābuhragān]] presented to Šābuhr I) plus hymns + liturgies for the Manichaean community. Preserved mostly through the Turfan-oasis Central Asian manuscript finds.

## The heterographic Pahlavi script

The Pahlavi script is one of the most unusual writing systems in linguistic history: it is *heterographic*. Aramaic words are written in their Aramaic spelling but read aloud as their Iranian equivalent — the Aramaic word *MLKA* is written letter-by-letter (mem-lamed-kaph-aleph) but pronounced *šāh* "king". This bizarre arrangement is the fossilized residue of the Achaemenid chancellery tradition (where Iranian-speaking officials had Aramaic scribes write their dictation in Aramaic) carried forward through 800+ years into Sasanian and post-Sasanian usage.

The result: reading Pahlavi is a kind of bilingual deciphering exercise. Modern editions transcribe with Aramaic ideograms in capitals (*MLKA*) and Iranian phonetic complements in lowercase (*šāh*), reflecting this dual-language fossil layer in the script itself.

## The Zand and Zoroastrian preservation

The single most important Pahlavi corpus from a religious-transmission perspective is the *Zand* — the Sasanian-era running translation + commentary on the [[language-old-avestan|Avestan]] canon. The Avesta itself was preserved orally in Avestan, but the comprehensive priestly understanding of it — what each line meant, how each ritual prescription applied — was carried in the Pahlavi *Zand* that accompanied every Avestan recitation. After the Arab conquest disrupted Zoroastrianism's institutional infrastructure, it was the Pahlavi *Zand* texts (and the post-9th-c. Pahlavi books summarizing them) that preserved the Zoroastrian tradition into the early-modern period when Parsi + Iranian Zoroastrian communities continued the chain.

## The Manichaean expansion

Mānī (216-276 CE), founder of Manichaeism, wrote in Middle Persian + Syriac. His [[phase-4-016-shabuhragan|Šābuhragān]] is a Middle Persian cosmological summary written specifically for the Sasanian court — one of the very rare cases of a founder of a major religion presenting his teaching to a king in the king's own language. Manichaeism spread from this Sasanian-court base westward (into the Roman world, where Augustine was briefly an adherent before converting to Christianity), eastward (where it reached Tang China and survived in Central Asia until ~14th c.), and northward (Sogdiana, Uyghur). The Turfan-oasis manuscript finds preserve large parts of the Middle Persian Manichaean corpus.

## The Abbasid translation bridge

The early Abbasid translation movement (~750-1000 CE) that delivered Greek philosophy + science to Arabic owed an under-acknowledged debt to Middle Persian. Sasanian Iran had inherited and translated Greek learning (especially under Khosrow I, 531-579, who established the medical school at Gondēšāpur and welcomed exiled Athenian Neoplatonist refugees). Much of what passed into Arabic through the House of Wisdom in Baghdad arrived via Pahlavi intermediation — Greek → Pahlavi → Arabic — alongside the more famous Syriac route (Greek → Syriac → Arabic). The Iranian-Islamic synthesis that produced Sufi poetry, Persianate court culture, and the *Shāhnāmeh* of Ferdowsi rests on this Sasanian-Iranian inheritance carried forward through Middle Persian.
