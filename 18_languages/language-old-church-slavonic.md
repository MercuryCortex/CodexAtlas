# Old Church Slavonic

The literary Slavic language deliberately created in 863 CE by the brothers Cyril (Constantine the Philosopher) and Methodius for their Byzantine-sponsored mission to Great Moravia — the founding language of Slavic Christianity, the medium of the Glagolitic and Cyrillic scriptural + liturgical canon, and the structural counterpart to [[language-latin-ecclesiastical|Ecclesiastical Latin]] in the Eastern Christian world. The only major liturgical language of antiquity whose origin can be precisely dated and attributed.

## What Old Church Slavonic is

Old Church Slavonic is unique among major liturgical languages in that we know the year (863), the agents (Cyril + Methodius), the patron (Byzantine Emperor Michael III in response to Prince Rastislav of Moravia), and the dialect base (the Macedonian-Bulgarian Slavic variety spoken around 9th-c. Thessalonica) of its creation. Cyril designed the Glagolitic script — a deliberately-original alphabet that visually resembles neither Greek nor Latin — and the brothers translated the Gospels + Psalter + liturgical texts from Koine Greek into the new written register.

Linguistically, OCS is a very-late Common-Slavic literary register, captured at a moment (c. 850-900) before the major Slavic dialect divisions had differentiated; for this reason OCS is structurally close to the *unrecorded* common Slavic of the late first millennium and serves as the principal source for the comparative reconstruction of Proto-Slavic and Balto-Slavic.

## The Cyril-Methodian mission

The mission to Moravia (863-885) was politically explosive: it explicitly competed with Carolingian Latin missionary work in central Europe and was caught up in the contemporary Photian schism between Constantinople and Rome. Cyril died in Rome in 869, Methodius continued the mission (and was briefly imprisoned by German bishops), and after Methodius's death in 885 the mission's enemies expelled the Slavonic-rite clergy from Moravia. Their disciples — Clement of Ohrid, Naum, Sava, Gorazd, Angelarius — fled south into Bulgaria, where the language found a permanent home at the courts of the Bulgarian rulers Boris I and Simeon I.

In Bulgaria the script was simplified — the Glagolitic was retired in favor of a streamlined Cyrillic, which adopted Greek-style letterforms for the sounds that exist in both Greek + Slavic and added new letters for the Slavic-specific consonants (b, ž, š, šč, c, č, ě, jers, nasal vowels). The two scripts together — [[alphabet-glagolitic-cyrillic|Glagolitic + Cyrillic]] — carried the language to its eventual destinations.

## The eastward expansion + the recensions

OCS spread from Bulgaria into Serbia, into Kievan Rus' (after the 988 baptism of Vladimir + the Christianization of the Eastern Slavs), into Romanian-speaking Wallachia + Moldavia (Slavonic liturgy until ~1700), and to a smaller extent into Glagolitic-rite Croatia. Each region developed a regional *recension* — Bulgarian OCS, Serbian OCS, Russian OCS — that absorbed local phonological + lexical features while preserving the core literary norm. Russian Church Slavonic (the Synodal recension of the 18th c.) is the variety still used in the Russian + Ukrainian + Belarusian Orthodox Churches today.

## The structural parallel to Ecclesiastical Latin

OCS and [[language-latin-ecclesiastical|Ecclesiastical Latin]] play almost-identical roles in their respective civilizational spheres:

- Both are *translation languages* anchored by a Greek source (Septuagint + NT for Latin; Septuagint + NT for OCS).
- Both are *liturgical norms* preserved across a thousand-plus years as the operating language of an entire Christian civilization (Latin Christendom; Orthodox Slavdom).
- Both calque Greek theological vocabulary into native morphology — Latin *consubstantialis* ↔ OCS *jedinosǫštьnъ* both translate Greek *homoousios*.
- Both eventually accommodate vernacularization (Vatican II 1962-65 for Latin; the 19th-c. nationalist movements + the Patriarchate of Moscow's gradual Russian-vernacular accommodations for Church Slavonic).

The choice between Latin and Slavonic for the Slavic peoples in the 9th-10th centuries was the load-bearing decision that produced the Catholic/Orthodox boundary across eastern Europe — Poland, Croatia, Hungary, Czech-Slovak lands chose Latin; Bulgaria, Serbia, Kievan Rus' chose Slavonic. That boundary remains religiously + culturally legible 1100 years later.
