# Old Norse (with the runic Proto-Norse stage)

The language of the [[phase-8-014-poetic-edda|Poetic Edda]], [[phase-8-015-prose-edda-snorri|Snorri's Prose Edda]], the sagas, and the runestones — the North Germanic language in which the only substantial mythology of pre-Christian Northern Europe survives. Its earlier runic stage, **Proto-Norse**, is what the [[alphabet-elder-futhark|Elder Futhark]] inscriptions record from c. 150 CE; the two stages are treated together here because the script row and the literary corpus belong to one continuous linguistic tradition.

## Two stages, three scripts

- **Proto-Norse (c. 150-700 CE).** The language of the earliest Elder-Futhark inscriptions — spear-heads, brooches, bracteates, standing stones. Forms like *ek hlewagastiz holtijaz horna tawido* ("I, Hlewagastiz of Holt, made the horn," Gallehus gold horn, c. 400) are still remarkably close to reconstructed Proto-Germanic.
- **Old Norse proper (c. 700-1350).** After a drastic sound-change period (syncope), the Viking-Age language is written in the reduced 16-rune Younger Futhark on c. 2,500 runestones — including the Rök stone (c. 800, the longest runic inscription, opening with allusions to lost heroic lore) and the Jelling stones (c. 965), on which Harald Bluetooth declares he "made the Danes Christian."
- **The manuscript stage (12th-14th c.).** The entire mythological and saga literature is written in the Latin alphabet ([[alphabet-latin]]) in Christian Iceland and Norway. The runes never carried the mythology; the church's script did.

## The source-critical paradox

Everything substantial we know of Norse religion is written in Old Norse *by Christians*, roughly two centuries after Iceland's conversion (1000 CE). Snorri Sturluson opens the Prose Edda with an explicitly Christian euhemerist frame before expounding the myths. The Eddic poems are linguistically and metrically older than their 13th-c. Codex Regius manuscript — how much older is the central debate of the field. The structural parallel with [[language-old-avestan|Old Avestan]] and [[sanskrit-vedic|Vedic Sanskrit]] is exact in shape: a sacred oral corpus surviving only through a much later writing-down, with the redactional filter itself part of the evidence.

## Cross-tradition findings

- **Týr is the sky-father's name.** Old Norse *Týr* < Proto-Germanic *Tīwaz < PIE *Dyḗus — etymologically the same god-name as Zeus, Iuppiter, and Vedic [[dyaus-pita|Dyauṣ Pitṛ́]] (Fortson 2010). The Norse pantheon's attested head is [[odin|Odin]], but the language preserves the older Indo-European headship in the name of the diminished war-god [[tyr]] — a within-tradition displacement legible only through comparative linguistics.
- **The weekday calque carries the interpretatio.** Old Norse and the other Germanic languages translated the Roman planetary week (Týr→Mars, Odin→Mercury, Thor→Jupiter, Frigg→Venus): *týsdagr*, *óðinsdagr*, *þórsdagr*, *frjádagr* — English Tuesday through Friday. The deity-level identifications live on the deity nodes; the language carries the documentary fossil.
- **Runic letter-lore as sacred knowledge.** Hávamál's account of Odin winning the runes by self-sacrifice on the world-tree makes the script itself an object of religious revelation — the Norse member of the cross-tradition pattern of divine-origin writing systems treated at [[alphabet-elder-futhark]] and [[runes-divination-norse]].
- **Inherited combat myth.** Thor against the world-serpent Jǫrmungandr continues the Indo-European serpent-combat pattern (Indra-Vṛtra, Zeus-Typhon) in alliterative Germanic verse (West 2007).
