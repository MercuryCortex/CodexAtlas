# Pali (Pāḷi)

The canonical language of [[tradition-theravada-buddhism|Theravāda Buddhism]] — a Middle Indo-Aryan literary register descended from a sister-vernacular of Vedic Sanskrit, used for the [[phase-2-016-early-buddhist-suttas|Pali Canon (Tipiṭaka)]], for [[buddhaghosa|Buddhaghosa's]] 5th-c. commentarial corpus, for the Sinhalese-Burmese-Thai-Cambodian-Lao Buddhist textual + liturgical tradition into the present. Pali is one of the two principal canonical languages of early Buddhism (alongside Buddhist Hybrid Sanskrit on the Mahāyāna side) and one of the most extensively transmitted-without-extinction ancient languages in human history.

## Pali, Sanskrit, and the Middle-Indo-Aryan layer

Pali is *not* a direct descendant of Classical (Panini-codified) Sanskrit. It is a Middle Indo-Aryan vernacular — the same linguistic stage as the Aśokan-edict dialects, the Jain canonical languages ([language-ardhamagadhi-prakrit|Ardha-Māgadhī] and others), and the various regional Prakrits. Compared to Vedic Sanskrit, Middle Indo-Aryan shows:

- Simplification of consonant clusters (Skt. *dharma* → Pali *dhamma*; Skt. *karma* → Pali *kamma*; Skt. *prajñā* → Pali *paññā*)
- Loss of the dual number (Skt. has singular/dual/plural; Pali has only singular/plural)
- Vowel simplification (Skt. *r̥* → Pali *a* or *i* or *u*)
- Significant verbal simplification

Bronkhorst 2007 argues that the cultural-linguistic context in which the Buddha taught was the eastern-Indian "Greater Magadha" region, which preserved a distinct Indo-Aryan tradition from the Vedic-Brahmanical northwest. Pali carries traces of this milieu.

## The Pali Canon and oral transmission

The Pali Canon (Tipiṭaka, "Three Baskets") was transmitted orally by the Buddhist Saṅgha for ~400 years before being committed to writing in Sri Lanka under King Vaṭṭagāmaṇi at the Aluvihare cave c. -29 BCE. The three Piṭakas are:

1. **Sutta Piṭaka** — the discourses of the Buddha, organized in five Nikāyas (Long, Middle-length, Connected, Numerical, Minor). Approximately 5,000 suttas; includes [[phase-2-029-dhammapada]] and the Jātaka birth-stories.
2. **Vinaya Piṭaka** — the monastic disciplinary code (227 rules for monks, 311 for nuns + procedural rules).
3. **Abhidhamma Piṭaka** — seven systematic-philosophical books elaborating the suttas' analytical psychology + ontology into a comprehensive Buddhist scholastic system.

The Pali Canon is the oldest substantially-complete Buddhist canon and the primary canonical reference for all modern study of early Buddhism, though scholarly opinion is divided on whether it preserves the Buddha's actual teaching verbatim, faithfully, or only the redaction of an early sectarian community (the Sthaviravāda / Theravāda ancestors).

## Buddhaghosa and the commentarial layer

The 5th-century-CE Theravāda monk [[buddhaghosa|Buddhaghosa]] (working in Sri Lanka) composed the *Visuddhimagga* (the standard Theravāda systematic treatise) and the canonical commentaries on the Nikāyas, the Vinaya, and the Abhidhamma. His Pali style is the standard scholastic register for Theravāda commentary; he established Pali as a living literary-scholastic language that continues into the present across the Theravāda Buddhist world.

## Pali and Buddhist Hybrid Sanskrit — the parallel sacred languages

The Mahāyāna sutra corpus (Lotus Sutra, Heart Sutra, Diamond Sutra, etc.) is composed in [[language-buddhist-hybrid-sanskrit|Buddhist Hybrid Sanskrit]] (Edgerton 1953) — a register that retro-Sanskritizes Middle Indo-Aryan (Pali-like) vocabulary into a Sanskrit-leaning literary form. The two languages preserve two different streams of the Indian Buddhist tradition: Pali for Theravāda (oldest preserved canonical corpus); Buddhist Hybrid Sanskrit for Mahāyāna (the sutra tradition that becomes the source for the East Asian + Tibetan Buddhist canons). The relationship is parallel — neither is a translation of the other; both reach back to a Middle-Indo-Aryan oral-canonical layer that has not survived independently.
