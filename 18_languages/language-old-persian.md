# Old Persian

The southwestern Old Iranian language of the Achaemenid Persian royal house, attested c. -550 to -330 BCE — the language of Cyrus's successors Darius I, Xerxes I, and the later Achaemenid kings. Old Persian is most famous as the third register of the trilingual Behistun Inscription (alongside Elamite and Akkadian) which was the decipherment key to all three cuneiform systems in the 19th century.

## A purpose-built cuneiform alphabet

Old Persian's writing system is unique in cuneiform history: it is *semi-alphabetic*. Where Sumerian + Akkadian + Elamite cuneiform had thousands of logographic + syllabic signs, Old Persian cuneiform has only 36 letters — three vowel signs (a, i, u) and 33 consonant signs (each implicitly carrying a vowel that can be modified by a following vowel sign), plus 8 logograms and word-dividers. The whole system was *invented* under Darius I c. -520 specifically to record Old Persian for royal monumental display; this is one of the rare attested cases of a sovereign deliberately commissioning a new writing system for ideological purposes.

The script does not appear in administrative documents — those are in Elamite or Aramaic — and falls out of use entirely after Artaxerxes III (~ -350). Its purpose was royal-monumental display, and it is wedded to that purpose.

## Behistun and decipherment

The Behistun Inscription (-520 to -518; carved on a cliff in western Iran 100m above the road from Babylon to Ecbatana) preserves Darius I's account of his accession in three languages — Old Persian + Elamite + Babylonian Akkadian. Henry Rawlinson + Edward Hincks + Jules Oppert + others used the Old Persian text (the simpler alphabetic system) as the wedge by which Akkadian cuneiform was deciphered in the mid-19th century. Without Old Persian, the entire Mesopotamian-Babylonian-Assyrian textual world might still be opaque.

## The Daiva Inscription and Zoroastrian polemic

Xerxes I's "Daiva Inscription" (XPh, c. -480) records the king's destruction of a cult of *daivas* somewhere in his realm — the same Iranian theological vocabulary ([[language-old-avestan|daēva-]]) by which the Avestan Gāθās condemn false gods. This is the strongest piece of royal-inscriptional evidence that the Achaemenid house participated in a Zoroastrian-aligned theological reform, although the question of how orthodox the Achaemenid royal religion was relative to "true" Zoroastrianism is one of the perennial debates of Iranian religious studies (Boyce 1982 vs Kellens 2002).

## The Achaemenid sponsorship of post-Exilic Judaism

The political-administrative context of Old Persian is foundational for [[hebrew-biblical|Biblical Hebrew]] in its post-Exilic phase. Cyrus's conquest of Babylon (-539) ends the Babylonian Exile; the Achaemenid administration sponsors the rebuilding of the Jerusalem Temple, the editing of Torah, the Ezra-Nehemiah reforms. Persian loanwords surface in Late Biblical Hebrew (*pardes* "garden / paradise", *dāth* "law"); the Cyrus Cylinder + the Ezra 1 Cyrus decree are the documentary anchors. Achaemenid Persian sponsorship is one of the load-bearing pieces of Second-Temple Judaism's political genealogy.

## Transmission downstream

Old Persian → Middle Persian (Pahlavi) → New Persian is a continuous spoken-language descent across ~2,500 years. The Sasanian period (224-651 CE) inherits the Achaemenid royal-Iranian ideological inheritance through Middle Persian; the modern Iranian-Persianate cultural sphere (including Sufi mystical poetry, [[hafez]]'s *Divan*, Ferdowsi's *Shāhnāmeh*) descends from this continuous Iranian linguistic chain.
