# Classical Tibetan

The literary register of Tibetan — chos skad, "the dharma language" — designed in the 7th century CE specifically to receive Buddhist Sanskrit literature into Tibet, codified by the 9th-century royal translation projects, frozen as a stable literary norm from the 11th century onward, and the medium of one of the largest single Buddhist textual corpora on Earth (the Kangyur + Tengyur, ~330 volumes). The principal surviving witness to enormous regions of late Indian Buddhist literature whose Sanskrit originals have been lost.

## What Classical Tibetan is

Tibetan is a Sino-Tibetan language of the Tibeto-Burman branch — distant cousin to [[language-classical-chinese|Classical Chinese]], Burmese, the Kuki-Chin languages, and the many smaller Tibeto-Burman families of the Himalayas + Southeast Asia. The Tibetan literary norm was codified c. 632-650 under King Songtsen Gampo (c. 605-650), by the minister Thonmi Sambhota who was reportedly sent to India to study Buddhist Sanskrit + the Brāhmī script + Indian linguistic science. Thonmi returned with the design for the [[alphabet-tibetan-tantric|Tibetan abugida]] — explicitly modeled on the Gupta-Brāhmī of contemporary northern India — and the foundational grammatical treatises *Sum-cu-pa* (the Thirty Verses) + *rTags-kyi 'jug-pa* (Treatise on Signs), both still in use.

The literary language has two principal phases:

- **Old Tibetan (snga-rabs)** — 7th-10th c.; the Dunhuang manuscripts + the early imperial inscriptions; less standardized; preserves archaic morphological + phonological features.
- **Classical Tibetan (yig rnying / chos skad)** — 11th c. onward; the highly-standardized canonical norm of the *phyi dar* ("later diffusion") of Buddhism; the language of the Kangyur + Tengyur + all subsequent scholastic + ritual + historical literature; remains essentially unchanged to the present as the operating language of Tibetan Buddhist scholarship + liturgy.

The two phases are mutually intelligible — Old Tibetan is to Classical Tibetan roughly as Old English is to Middle English.

## The Sanskrit-translation engineering

Classical Tibetan is one of the most striking examples in history of a *language deliberately engineered for translation work* (Verhagen 1994-2001). The Tibetan-language reforms of the 9th-century Tibetan emperors Khri-srong-lde'u-btsan, Khri-lde-srong-btsan ("Ral-pa-can"), and Khri-gtsug-lde-btsan produced:

- **The *Mahāvyutpatti*** — a Sanskrit-Tibetan lexicon of standardized translation equivalents for c. 9,500 Buddhist technical terms.
- **The *sGra sbyor bam po gnyis pa*** — a manual of translation principles + style.
- **A royal decree** standardizing Tibetan Buddhist terminology + prohibiting unauthorized re-translations.
- **The Lo-tsā-ba (Sanskrit *lokācārya*) institution** — the formally-trained translator-scholar working with an Indian *paṇḍita* on each translation.

The result is that Tibetan Buddhist texts are *calques* of their Sanskrit originals at the level of word + clause structure — a literate reader of Classical Tibetan can often reconstruct the precise Sanskrit syntax + technical vocabulary behind the Tibetan rendering. This makes the Tibetan canon the *principal surviving witness* to large regions of Indian Buddhist literature — tantric, Madhyamaka, Yogācāra, Pramāṇa, Vinaya — whose Sanskrit originals are mostly lost.

## The Kangyur + Tengyur

The Tibetan Buddhist canon is conventionally divided into two collections:

- **bKa' 'gyur (Kangyur)** — "the Translated Word." ~108 volumes (depending on recension). Texts attributed to the Buddha — Vinaya, Sūtras (Prajñāpāramitā, Ratnakūṭa, Avataṃsaka, Mahāparinirvāṇa), Tantras.
- **bsTan 'gyur (Tengyur)** — "the Translated Treatises." ~225 volumes. Indian commentarial literature — Nāgārjuna's Mūlamadhyamakakārikā + commentaries, Asaṅga + Vasubandhu's Yogācāra corpus, Dignāga + Dharmakīrti's Pramāṇa logic, Candrakīrti, Śāntideva, Atiśa, the tantric commentarial cycles.

Together this is the largest single Buddhist canon — substantially larger than the Pāli Tipiṭaka (~12,800 pages in PTS edition) or the Chinese Taishō Tripiṭaka (~85 vols.). The Tibetan canon's distinctive value: it preserves the Indian *commentarial* tradition (Tengyur) much more completely than Chinese, which translated relatively few Indian commentaries.

## The trans-regional Buddhist literary language

Classical Tibetan spreads as the operating language of Vajrayāna Buddhism across the Inner Asian + Himalayan Buddhist world: Tibet proper, Bhutan, Ladakh + Zanskar, Sikkim, Mongolia (after the 13th-c. Sa-skya patron-priest alliance with Kubilai Khan), Inner Mongolia, the Buryat republic, Tuva, Kalmykia (Volga delta). Mongolian + Manchu Buddhists studied Tibetan as their canonical language even when texts were also translated into their own languages — a structural parallel to Latin in medieval Europe, or Classical Chinese in the Sinosphere.
