# Classical Tibetan

The Sino-Tibetan literary + canonical language of [[tradition-tibetan-buddhism|Tibetan Buddhism]] from c. 650 CE to the present — orthographically frozen at its 7th-9th-c. spoken state, perpetually copied + memorized + recited as the active scholastic + liturgical register of the Nyingma + Kagyu + Sakya + Gelug schools plus the parallel [[tradition-bon|Bon]] tradition. Classical Tibetan is the most extensively-attested Sino-Tibetan literary language, the canonical-translation vehicle for the Indian Mahāyāna sutra + tantra + scholastic corpus, and the active community language of one of the largest continuous Buddhist textual traditions in the world.

## What Classical Tibetan is

Classical Tibetan emerges with the Tibetan script's invention or codification c. 632 CE under King Songtsen Gampo (r. 618-649), traditionally attributed to his minister Thonmi Sambhota — sent to India to learn Brāhmī-script principles and devise a Tibetan equivalent. The script is a Brāhmī-route adaptation: 30 consonants + 4 vowel-signs + a complex consonant-cluster system. By the 9th c. CE the script is firmly established and the literary register crystallized.

The orthographic conservatism is remarkable: written Tibetan today preserves the 7th-9th-c. spoken Tibetan phonology, so the written word for "horse" *rta* (now pronounced [ta]) reveals its Old Tibetan *rta* pronunciation with the *r-* prefix still audible. Reading classical Tibetan is therefore a kind of cumulative philological reconstruction of 7th-c. spoken Tibetan from a 21st-c. orthography.

## The canonical translation project

The defining achievement of Classical Tibetan is the systematic translation of the Indian Buddhist canon into Tibetan over the 7th-13th centuries:

- **Old Translation period (7th-9th c.)** — Thonmi Sambhota, Vairocana, Padmasambhava-era translators. The translation style is variable; technical terminology not yet standardized.
- **Mahāvyutpatti standardization (c. 820 CE)** — under King Khri-lde-srong-btsan (Sad-na-legs), a translation commission convenes at Bsam-yas + Skar-cung to standardize Buddhist technical vocabulary. The *Mahāvyutpatti* (the resulting Sanskrit-Tibetan dictionary, ~9,500 entries) and the *Sgra-sbyor bam-po gnyis-pa* (the methodological treatise) jointly establish the canonical-translation register that all subsequent translators are expected to follow.
- **New Translation period (10th-13th c.)** — Rinchen Zangpo (958-1055), Marpa (1012-1097), Atiśa's collaborators, the Sakya school translators. The standardized register is applied to a vast corpus of tantric + scholastic material that becomes the bulk of the Tengyur.

The result: the Tibetan Kangyur (~108 vols. of Buddha-attributed sutras + tantras + vinaya) + Tengyur (~225 vols. of Indian Buddhist scholastic treatises) constitute one of the most extensive translation corpora in world history. Modern scholars often consult the Tibetan to reconstruct lost Sanskrit originals — Tibetan's word-by-word fidelity to the source makes back-translation possible in ways that are not possible from the more idiomatic Chinese translations.

## The Bsam-yas debate

The 792-794 CE debate at Bsam-yas between Indian Buddhist representatives (Kamalaśīla, defending a gradualist Mahāyāna path) and Chinese Buddhist representatives (Hwa-shang Mahāyāna, defending a subitist Chan-style path) is the foundational moment in Tibetan Buddhism's choice of the Indian over the Chinese inheritance (Snellgrove 1987 ch. 7). The Tibetan canonical-translation project's lopsided orientation toward Indian Sanskrit sources rather than Chinese-Buddhist sources is one consequence of this decision.

## Living tradition

Classical Tibetan is not a dead language. The major Tibetan Buddhist monasteries (Sera, Drepung, Ganden — re-established in exile in Karnataka; Tashilhunpo in Tibet + Bylakuppe; the Kagyu + Nyingma + Sakya monastic networks worldwide) conduct full scholastic curricula in Classical Tibetan. The Bardo Thödol + the Lam-rim + the Great Stages of the Path of Tsongkhapa are read + memorized + studied + commented on in the same Classical-Tibetan register that crystallized in the 9th c. CE. This makes Classical Tibetan one of the more extensively-living ancient literary languages in the world, alongside Classical Arabic + Classical Chinese + Sanskrit + Pali + Ge'ez + Coptic + (in restricted liturgical use) Old Church Slavonic + Latin.
