# Sabaic (the language of Saba / Sheba)

The principal language of ancient South Arabia — of the Sabaean kingdom at Marib, its great dam, the Almaqah temple later folk-named "Mahram Bilqis" after the Queen of Sheba, and finally of Himyar, whose 4th-6th c. CE inscriptions document an official monotheism ("Raḥmānān, Lord of Heaven") in the generations just before Islam. Sabaic is the epigraphic reality beneath three scriptural traditions' Sheba legends — and a corpus that conspicuously does not contain the queen the legends remember.

## The corpus

Old South Arabian survives in ~10,000+ inscriptions in the **musnad** monumental script ([[alphabet-south-arabian]]) plus everyday cursive (*zabūr*) on palm-rib sticks. Sabaic is the best-attested of its four languages. The religious center of gravity:

- **The Almaqah dedications** — thousands of texts from the federation's national cult of the god [[almaqah|Almaqah]] at Marib's Awwam temple ([[tradition-sabaean-religion]]).
- **The royal res-gestae** — Karib'il Watar's Sirwah inscriptions (c. -685), the wars and waterworks of early Saba.
- **The dam texts** — Marib's irrigation civilization documented in stone down to Abraha's 554 CE repair account (CIH 541), the longest Sabaic inscription; the dam's final ruin enters the Quran (34:15-17) as the parable of Saba's ingratitude.
- **The Rahmanist corpus** — from the 4th c. CE the polytheist formulary vanishes from official texts, replaced by Raḥmānān ("the Merciful"); under Jewish-aligned kings (Robin 2004) and then the Christian-Aksumite period, South Arabia runs a documented official monotheism for two centuries before Muhammad ([[tradition-himyarites]]).

## Cross-tradition findings

- **The Ge'ez correction.** The textbook story — Ethiopian civilization founded by Sabaean colonists whose language became [[language-geez|Ge'ez]] — is rejected by current Semitics: Ethio-Semitic is a *sister*, not a daughter. What demonstrably crossed the Red Sea is the **culture package**: the musnad script (ancestor of [[alphabet-geez-ethiopic]]), Sabaic royal-temple forms, and actual Sabaic-language inscriptions of the D'MT polity in Ethiopia (c. -800 to -400). Script and prestige travel; the language stays — the distinction this lens exists to keep (Stein 2011; Weninger 2011).
- **Raḥmānān before al-Raḥmān.** The Himyarite inscriptions' monotheist divine name Raḥmānān anticipates — and per most scholarship feeds — the Quranic al-Raḥmān ("the Merciful," sūra 55:1), giving epigraphic ground to the Quran's South Arabian monotheist horizon (Hoyland 2001; Robin 2004; [[classical-arabic]]).
- **Sheba's three scriptural afterlives.** Saba is remembered in 1 Kings 10 (the queen before Solomon, [[hebrew-biblical]]), Quran 27 (Bilqīs and the hoopoe) and Quran 34 (the dam), and the Ethiopian [[phase-8-008-kebra-nagast|Kebra Nagast]] (where the queen — [[makeda-queen-of-sheba|Makeda]] — founds the Solomonic dynasty). The Sabaic corpus itself records no such queen: the distance between the legend's reach and the epigraphy's silence measures how scripture remembers a trading power at the edge of its world.
