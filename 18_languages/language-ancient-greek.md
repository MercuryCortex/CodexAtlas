# Ancient Greek (Archaic + Classical)

The language of [[phase-2-008-homeric-epics|Homer]], [[phase-2-009-hesiod-theogony-works-and-days|Hesiod]], the Presocratics, the tragedians, and [[phase-3-022-plato-timaeus-critias-atlantis|Plato]] — the Greek of the alphabetic Archaic and Classical periods (c. -800 to -323), distinct by Tier-1 convention from its daughter [[koine-greek]], the post-Alexander common dialect of the Septuagint and the New Testament. Nearly every Greek text in the Atlas older than Alexander is in this language.

## Why a separate node from Koine

The standard history of the language (Horrocks 2010) divides Greek into stages — Mycenaean, Archaic/Classical, Koine, Medieval, Modern — and the division is load-bearing for this Atlas: the religious corpus splits across the Ancient/Koine boundary. Homer's epics, Hesiod's *Theogony*, the [[document-orphic-gold-tablets|Orphic gold tablets]], the [[document-derveni-papyrus|Derveni papyrus]], [[document-heraclitus-fragments|Heraclitus]], [[document-parmenides-poem|Parmenides]], and Plato's *Timaeus* are Ancient Greek; the Septuagint, the New Testament, Philo, and the Corpus Hermeticum are Koine. Widening the [[koine-greek]] node to absorb Homer would misdate the corpus by half a millennium and erase the dialect landscape (epic Ionic-Aeolic, Attic prose, Doric choral lyric) that Koine specifically leveled.

## The shape of the language

Ancient Greek was not one uniform language but a dialect continuum — Attic, Ionic, Doric, Aeolic, Arcado-Cypriot — held together by a shared literature. The epic language of Homer is itself an artificial *Kunstsprache*, an Ionic base shot through with Aeolic and older forms, never spoken by anyone but learned by every literate Greek. This dialectal texture is religiously meaningful: oracle and hymn keep epic coloring, Doric marks choral-lyric cult song, and Attic — the dialect of a single city's empire and its philosophers — becomes the prestige base from which Koine later levels everything.

Greek is also attested four centuries before the alphabet: **Mycenaean Greek**, written in the Linear B syllabary (c. -1400 to -1200), already names Zeus, Hera, Poseidon, and Dionysus in Bronze-Age palace tablets — the earliest documentary evidence for the Olympian cult. Linear B was adapted from the still-undeciphered Minoan [[alphabet-linear-a|Linear A]]; with the palaces' fall, Greece lost writing entirely until the Phoenician adaptation.

## The alphabet event

Around -800, Greek speakers adopted the [[alphabet-phoenician|Phoenician abjad]] and repurposed consonant signs their language did not need as vowels — the innovation treated at [[alphabet-greek-vowel-revolution]]. The earliest surviving alphabetic Greek (the Dipylon oinochoe and "[[nestor|Nestor]]'s cup," c. -775 to -720) is already hexameter verse, suggesting the alphabet was bound up with recording poetry nearly from the start (Jeffery 1990).

## Cross-tradition findings

- **Inherited Indo-European poetics.** Homeric Greek and [[sanskrit-vedic|Vedic Sanskrit]] preserve identical fixed formulae — *kléos áphthiton* = *śrávas ákṣitam* ("imperishable fame") — and the same serpent-slaying narrative grammar (Apollo-Python ~ Indra-Vṛtra). This is transmission by common descent from Proto-Indo-European poetic craft, not contact (Watkins 1995).
- **The divine-name cognate set.** Zeus (gen. *Dios*) < PIE *Dyḗus = Vedic [[dyaus-pita|Dyauṣ Pitṛ́]] = Latin Iuppiter — the flagship etymological transmission of the sky-father across the Indo-European world (Fortson 2010).
- **The Pre-Greek substrate.** A large stratum of Greek vocabulary — including divine and cultic names (Athena, Apollo *per* some analyses, Hyacinthus, labyrinthos) — is not Indo-European and is attributed to the pre-Greek Aegean language(s) (Beekes 2010). The Olympian religion is, at the level of its names, already a fusion of Indo-European and Aegean-substrate inheritances.
- **The stage handoff to scripture.** Ancient Greek's daughter [[koine-greek]] became the language of Jewish scripture in translation (Septuagint) and of Christian scripture in composition (New Testament) — the clearest case in the Atlas of one civilization's literary-philosophical language becoming another's scriptural vehicle.
