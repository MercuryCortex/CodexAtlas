# Galilean Aramaic (Jewish Palestinian Aramaic)

The Western Late Aramaic register of Jewish Palestine from c. 100-700 CE — the language of the [[phase-4-032-jerusalem-talmud|Jerusalem Talmud]] (Talmud Yerushalmi), the Palestinian Targums (Neofiti + Pseudo-Jonathan + Fragment-Targums), the Palestinian Amoraic Midrashim, and the synagogue inscriptions of Roman-Byzantine Palestine. Galilean Aramaic is one of the two great rabbinic commentary-languages of late antiquity, alongside its eastern sister [[language-babylonian-aramaic|Babylonian Aramaic]].

## Position in the Aramaic family

Galilean Aramaic descends from [[language-aramaic-imperial|Imperial Aramaic]] via Middle Aramaic, then splits at c. 200 CE into the Western Late Aramaic branch (Galilean Aramaic + Christian Palestinian Aramaic + Samaritan Aramaic) alongside the Eastern Late Aramaic branch ([[language-syriac|Syriac]] + Jewish Babylonian Aramaic + Mandaic). The west-east split reflects the Roman-Byzantine vs Sasanian-Persian division of the Aramaic-speaking world.

## The Jerusalem Talmud

The defining text of Galilean Aramaic is the [[phase-4-032-jerusalem-talmud|Jerusalem Talmud]] (Talmud Yerushalmi), redacted in the rabbinic academies of Tiberias, Sepphoris, Caesarea, and Lod c. 350-400 CE. It is the Palestinian-Amoraic commentary on the Mishnah, organized by Mishnaic tractate — about 39 of the 63 Mishnaic tractates have surviving Jerusalem Talmud, with significant lacunae especially in the order Kodashim.

The Jerusalem Talmud is distinct from the [[phase-4-033-babylonian-talmud|Babylonian Talmud]] in language (Galilean vs Babylonian Aramaic), in content (different agendas + different Amoraic generations represented), and in subsequent reception (the Babylonian became dominant in medieval halachic transmission while the Jerusalem remained the minority authority). For Atlas purposes the most striking material is Berakhot 9:5's *yetzer ha-tov / yetzer ha-ra* (good-inclination / evil-inclination) doctrine + the extensive Palestinian-Amoraic discussion of the Land of Israel + the priestly courses + the Temple liturgy from Palestinian memory.

## The Palestinian Targums

The Palestinian Targums (Targum Neofiti, Targum Pseudo-Jonathan, the Fragment-Targums recovered from the Cairo Geniza, and the older Targum-substrate that underlies them all) are Aramaic translations + paraphrases of the Pentateuch. They preserve a more expansive, midrashic-elaborated, aggadic-rich translation tradition than the more literal Babylonian Targums (Onkelos). The Palestinian-Targum register includes:

- Extensive aggadic expansion (filling narrative gaps, inserting interpretive material)
- Anti-anthropomorphic paraphrasing of God-language
- Halachic harmonization (reconciling contradictory pentateuchal statements)
- Theological updating (introducing concepts like Memra "Word", Shekhinah "Presence", and others)

The Targums are one of the principal vehicles by which post-Biblical Jewish theological vocabulary develops, and Galilean Aramaic is the substrate. The "Memra" concept in particular — God's *Word* as a quasi-hypostatized agent — is recurringly compared to the Johannine Logos and has been one of the principal points of cross-tradition discussion in Jewish-Christian comparative theology.

## Galilean Aramaic and Jesus

The Aramaic linguistic substrate of Jesus's documented sayings (preserved in transliteration in the Greek Gospels — *Talitha qum*, *Eli Eli lema sabachthani*, *Effatha*, *Abba*) is typically identified as a 1st-c. CE Galilean Aramaic — the immediate predecessor + earliest substrate of the documented Galilean Aramaic corpus. The famous *Eli Eli lema sabachthani* of Mark 15:34 / Matthew 27:46 is grammatically Galilean Aramaic, citing Psalm 22:1's Hebrew Eli but completing the citation in spoken Galilean Aramaic.

## Decline

Galilean Aramaic continues in declining use after the Arab conquest (635-640 CE) until c. 700-800, when Arabic largely displaces it as the Jewish-Palestinian vernacular + scholarly language. The Karaite + Rabbanite Jewish communities of Palestine, the Cairo Geniza materials of 9th-12th c. CE, and the Judeo-Arabic medieval Jewish literary tradition (Saadia Gaon, etc.) all reflect the post-Galilean-Aramaic Arabic-dominant phase.
