# Cherokee (Tsalagi)

The Southern Iroquoian language of the Cherokee people — and the only language in recorded history to receive its writing system from a single demonstrably non-literate individual. Sequoyah (ᏍᏏᏉᏯ, c. 1770-1843), who could not read or write any language, analyzed Cherokee into 85 syllables and built a character for each (completed 1821). Within a few years the Cherokee Nation had achieved near-universal literacy — and its ritual specialists were writing down, for the first time anywhere in North America, an Indigenous religion's esoteric corpus in its own language and script.

## The language

Cherokee is the sole Southern Iroquoian language, separated from its northern relatives (Mohawk, Seneca, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, Tuscarora) by the family's deepest split — millennia of independent development in the southern Appalachians. It is polysynthetic: a single Cherokee verb carries pronoun, aspect, and classificatory information that English spreads across a sentence, including the famous shape-classes (a verb of giving changes form according to whether the thing given is alive, flexible, long, liquid, or compact).

## The syllabary event

Sequoyah's creation ([[alphabet-cherokee]]) is treated on the script node; what belongs to the language is the literacy explosion and what the Nation did with it: a constitutional government printing its laws (1827), the bilingual *Cherokee Phoenix* (1828) — the first Native American newspaper — and the parallel religious corpora below. Missionaries arrived with romanization schemes and found the Nation already literate in its own system; they capitulated and cast syllabary type.

## Two written religions at once

- **The sacred formulas (idi:gawé:sdi).** Cherokee healers and ritual specialists used the syllabary to write personal manuscript books of incantation — hunting, medicine, love, war, protection — previously transmitted orally under initiation. James Mooney's edition of the Swimmer manuscript (1891) brought the corpus to scholarship: a written esoteric literature of a Native North American religion, in the people's own script, made for insiders rather than ethnographers ([[tradition-cherokee]]).
- **Cherokee Christianity.** The Worcester-Boudinot circle completed the New Testament in 1860; the Cherokee hymnbook (1829) carried "Amazing Grace" into Cherokee, where — sung on the Trail of Tears — it became a de facto national anthem. The same script, the same decades, two scriptural streams.

## Cross-tradition findings

- **Deliberate script-creation, twice in history.** Cherokee and [[language-middle-korean|Middle Korean]] are the two fully-documented cases of a writing system *engineered* for a language (Sequoyah 1821; King Sejong 1443-46), each followed by rapid mass literacy and immediate religious text-production. The pairing is a controlled comparison in how scripts begin — court academy versus solitary inventor — independently converging on the same outcome.
- **Writing as ritual power.** The formula-books were efficacious objects: guarded, personal, powerful because written. This recapitulates — independently — the script-sacralization the Atlas tracks from [[alphabet-medu-netjer|Egyptian]] divine writing to the [[classical-arabic|Arabic]] of the Quran: a writing system acquiring religious charge beyond its linguistic function.
- **Scripture translation as preservation vector.** As across the Old World, Bible translation fixed an enormous documented corpus of the language (1860 NT) — which modern revitalization programs now mine alongside the formulas: missionary and traditionalist corpora jointly sustaining the language they once contested.
