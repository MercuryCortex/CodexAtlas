# Middle Korean

The language for which [[alphabet-hangul|Hangul]] was created — the only major writing system in history whose inventor, design principles, promulgation date, and stated purpose are all documented. Late Middle Korean (1443-1600) is consequently the rare case of a language stepping out of another language's script into full phonological visibility in a single generation — and the first thing it did with its new alphabet was print Buddhist scripture in the vernacular.

## Before the alphabet: total diglossia

For its first fifteen documented centuries, Korean was a spoken language wearing Chinese writing. Official, historical, and canonical life was conducted in [[language-classical-chinese|Classical Chinese]] outright — the Tripitaka Koreana (the 81,258 woodblocks of the Buddhist canon carved under the Goryeo) is a *Chinese-language* monument of Korean Buddhism, and Korea's print revolution (the 1377 [[event-jikji-printed-1377|Jikji]], the world's oldest extant movable-metal-type book) printed Chinese text. Korean itself leaked into writing only through strained character-conventions (*idu*, *hyangchal*, *gugyeol*) that bent Chinese graphs to Korean grammar — leaving Old and Early Middle Korean among the most poorly-attested major languages relative to their cultural weight.

## 1443-46: the engineered alphabet

[[king-sejong-of-joseon|King Sejong]]'s Hunminjeongeum ("Correct Sounds for the Instruction of the People," promulgated 1446) created a featural alphabet whose letter-shapes diagram the articulating mouth — treated in full at [[alphabet-hangul]]. The promulgation document itself (*Haerye*) survives, grounding the design in Neo-Confucian cosmology (yin-yang and Five-Phase assignments for vowels and consonants) while the literati objected that civilization meant writing Chinese. The court's answer was a publication program — and its content is the religious payload:

- the dynastic hymn cycle *Yongbieocheonga* (legitimating the new Joseon house under Heaven's mandate),
- then a cascade of **Buddhist vernacular literature**: Sejong's own hymn cycle *Worin cheongang ji gok*, the Buddha-biography *Seokbo sangjeol*, and the *eonhae* sutra translations of the royal translation bureau (Lotus, Diamond, Śūraṅgama) — the first time Korean Buddhists could read their canon in their own language ([[tradition-korean-buddhism]], [[tradition-seon-buddhism]]).

The Confucian canon followed in the same bilingual *eonhae* format ([[tradition-joseon-confucianism]]).

## Cross-tradition findings

- **Buddhism as the vernacular-literacy engine.** The first substantial prose ever written in Korean is Buddhist devotional-canonical literature produced by the court translation bureaus — a clean instance of the cross-tradition pattern in which scripture-translation drives a language's literization (parallels: Gothic, [[language-old-church-slavonic|Old Church Slavonic]], [[language-geez|Ge'ez]]; structural cousin to [[language-pali|Pali]]'s canon-driven writing-down).
- **The diglossia that did not end.** Hangul did not replace Classical Chinese; Korea ran a two-script, two-language scriptural economy into the 19th c. — Chinese for the canon's authority, Korean for its accessibility, with the *eonhae* page physically interleaving both. Compare the Latin-vernacular economy of medieval Europe ([[language-latin-ecclesiastical]]).
- **Documented invention as a control case.** Because Hangul's creation is fully documented (inventor, date, design memo), Middle Korean serves as the control case against which legend-wrapped script origins elsewhere (Thoth, Mashtots's vision, Sequoyah's solitary feat) can be measured: it shows what a script-creation actually looks like when the historical record survives complete.
