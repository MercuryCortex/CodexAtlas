# Classical Chinese (Literary Sinitic)

The pan-East Asian literary + administrative language from the Spring and Autumn period (c. -500) to the early 20th century — used as the *written* lingua franca across China, Korea, Japan, and Vietnam (the Sinographic cultural sphere), even when none of those communities spoke Chinese vernacularly. The vehicle of the Confucian canon, the Daoist canon, the entire Chinese Buddhist Tripiṭaka, the dynastic histories, and the administrative apparatus of every East Asian centralized state until the modern vernacularization movements. One of the most extensively-attested and longest-running literary registers in human history.

## What Classical Chinese is

Classical Chinese is a literary register, not a spoken language. Its grammar + vocabulary crystallized in the Spring and Autumn + Warring States period (-770 to -221) — the language of Confucius, Mencius, Laozi, Zhuangzi — and was then preserved orthographically across two millennia of Sinitic spoken-language change. By the Tang dynasty (618-907) the spoken language had drifted far from Classical Chinese; by the Song (960-1279) it was unintelligible to anyone without scholastic training. But the canonical-literary register continued unchanged: writers in the Ming + Qing dynasties composed in essentially the same Classical Chinese as the Han + Tang writers, even though their spoken languages were entirely different.

The defining linguistic features:

- **Monosyllabic** — almost every classical Chinese morpheme is a single syllable, written with a single character.
- **No grammatical inflection** — no person/number/tense/case marking. Grammatical relations are conveyed by word order, particles, and context.
- **Highly compressed** — classical-Chinese sentences are often a third the length of their modern Chinese paraphrases. The famous opening of the Daodejing — *dào kĕ dào, fēi cháng dào* — is six syllables / six characters / a complete philosophical proposition.
- **Logographic script** — the same characters can be read in any of the spoken-Sinitic descendant languages with that language's pronunciation, allowing trans-temporal + trans-linguistic readability.

## The four canonical traditions

Classical Chinese is the medium of four major canonical literatures, each one of world-historical significance:

1. **The Confucian canon** — the Four Books + Five Classics (*Shijing*, *Shujing*, *Liji*, *Yijing*, *Chunqiu* + *Lunyu*, *Mencius*, *Daxue*, *Zhongyong*). Codified as the imperial-examination curriculum from the Song dynasty onward; the basis of two millennia of East Asian official-scholastic culture.
2. **The Daoist canon** — Daodejing, Zhuangzi, Liezi; the immense Daozang (1,500 texts in the Ming Zhengtong edition) including the Inner-Alchemy + Shangqing + Lingbao + Tianshi ritual literatures.
3. **The Chinese Buddhist canon (Taishō Tripiṭaka)** — translated from Sanskrit + [[language-buddhist-hybrid-sanskrit|Buddhist Hybrid Sanskrit]] by successive generations of translators (Kumārajīva, Xuanzang, etc.). Thousands of texts; the canonical source for all Korean + Japanese + Vietnamese Buddhism.
4. **The dynastic histories** — Sima Qian's *Shiji* (c. -100), the *Hanshu*, the *Hou Hanshu*, etc., comprising the Twenty-Four Histories (二十四史) compiled across two millennia.

## The Sinographic cosmopolis

For most of pre-modern history, *literate East Asia operated in Classical Chinese*, even when its speech was Korean / Japanese / Vietnamese. Korean *hanmun*, Japanese *kanbun*, Vietnamese *Hán văn* are local reading-traditions of the same Classical Chinese text-corpus, with elaborate diacritic + glossing conventions allowing a Korean / Japanese / Vietnamese reader to read out a Classical Chinese text in his own language while preserving the text. This is one of the most extensive examples in world history of a *cosmopolitan literary language* used across multiple unrelated spoken-language communities — a parallel to [[language-sanskrit-classical|Classical Sanskrit]] in pre-modern South + Southeast Asia (the Pollock-Cosmopolis dynamic), to [[language-latin-classical|Classical Latin]] in pre-modern Europe, and to [[classical-arabic|Classical Arabic]] in the Islamic world.

## The Bsam-yas debate and the Indian-vs-Chinese Buddhist choice

The 792-794 CE debate at Bsam-yas (Tibet) adjudicated between Indian + Chinese Buddhist representatives for the Tibetan court's authoritative tradition. Tibetan Buddhism subsequently developed on the Indian-Sanskrit inheritance via Tibetan translation; East Asian Buddhism developed on the Indian-Sanskrit inheritance via Chinese translation. Translation policies diverged sharply: Tibetan optimized for word-by-word Sanskrit fidelity (modern reconstruction-friendly); Chinese optimized for native idiomatic readability (vernacular-friendly, harder for back-translation).

## Vernacularization and the modern register

The 1919-1920 May Fourth Movement promoted *báihuà* (written Mandarin vernacular) as the new standard written register, displacing Classical Chinese from most contexts. Modern Standard Written Chinese (the language of contemporary novels + newspapers + textbooks) is a partial-vernacularization that still retains a substantial classical substratum — modern educated Chinese readers can navigate Classical Chinese with effort. Classical Chinese remains in active partial-use for ceremonial inscriptions, classical literature scholarship, religious texts in Buddhist + Daoist + Confucian contexts, and high-register literary effect.
