# Ecclesiastical Latin

The form of Latin that emerges as the operating language of Latin Christianity from the 2nd century CE — a *translation register* shaped by the Vetus Latina + Vulgate Bible translations, by the Greek-Christian theological vocabulary it inherits, by the Hebrew/Aramaic Semitic-religious vocabulary it absorbs through the Septuagint chain, and by its accommodation to spoken Vulgar Latin. The medium in which Western European Christianity — and through it the Latin philosophical, scientific, legal, and bureaucratic tradition of medieval Europe — was conducted for roughly fifteen centuries.

## What Ecclesiastical Latin is

Ecclesiastical Latin is not a different language from [[language-latin-classical|Classical Latin]] but a different *register* — one that absorbs material Classical Latin would have refused. Christine Mohrmann's foundational four-volume *Études sur le latin des chrétiens* (1958-1977) shows that the Christian Latin lexicon is built from three substantial substrates:

- **Vulgar-Latin substrate.** The everyday spoken Latin of the late-imperial period, with its phonological and morphological simplifications (loss of vowel quantity contrasts, collapse of the case system in the spoken language, the periphrastic future, the analytic perfect). The Vulgate's prose is often deliberately more accessible than Cicero or Tacitus; Augustine famously preferred to be *intelligible* over *Latinate*.
- **Greek borrowings + calques.** The Christian theological + ecclesiastical vocabulary is overwhelmingly Greek in origin — *ecclesia* (church), *baptisma* (baptism), *eucharistia* (eucharist), *episcopus* (bishop), *presbyter* (priest), *diaconus* (deacon), *evangelium* (gospel), *apostolus* (apostle), *martyr*, *angelus*, *diabolus*, *psalmus*, *hymnus*, *paradisus*. Most of these are Greek words *transliterated* into Latin rather than translated.
- **Hebrew + Aramaic borrowings.** Through the Septuagint → Vulgate pipeline, Semitic religious vocabulary enters Latin: *amen*, *alleluia*, *Sabbatum*, *cherubim*, *seraphim*, *messias*, *Pascha*, *Gehenna*, *Sabaoth*. The Vulgate carries Hebrew place-names, theonyms, ritual terms into the Latin Christian thought-world.

## The Vulgate and its consequences

The translation that anchors Ecclesiastical Latin is the Vulgate of [[jerome]] (Old Testament from Hebrew + a small amount of Aramaic; New Testament from Greek; completed 382-405). The Council of Trent (1546) declared the Vulgate the *authentic* Bible of the Latin Church. For the next four centuries the Vulgate was the *operative* Bible of Western Catholicism — every theological treatise, every sermon, every catechism, every popular vernacular Bible (Wycliffite, early Luther, Douay-Rheims, Authorized Version even in places) sits on top of the Vulgate's lexical and theological choices.

A short list of consequential Vulgate renderings:

- *salus* for *sōtēria* — "salvation" with a stronger this-worldly + medical connotation than Greek *sōtēria*.
- *gratia* for *charis* — "grace" loaded with Augustinian + later Reformation theological freight.
- *justificatio* for *dikaiōsis* — the linchpin of the 16th-c. Reformation controversy.
- *poenitentia* for *metanoia* — "penance" / "repentance"; the Reformation again hangs on this rendering.
- *spiritus* for *pneuma* / *ruaḥ* — "spirit" / "breath" / "wind"; an enormous semantic field collapsed into one word.

## The medieval + scholastic continuation

After Jerome, Ecclesiastical Latin merges seamlessly into Medieval Latin. The same language carries:

- The Carolingian theological + liturgical reforms (Alcuin, c. 800).
- The early-medieval encyclopedists (Isidore of Seville's *Etymologiae*, c. 625).
- Bede's *Ecclesiastical History of the English People* (731).
- Anselm's *Proslogion* (1078) and the development of scholastic philosophy.
- The 12th-13th c. translation movements from Arabic + Greek (Gerard of Cremona, Michael Scot, William of Moerbeke) bringing Aristotle + Avicenna + Averroes into Latin scholasticism.
- Thomas Aquinas's *Summa Theologiae* (c. 1265-1274).
- The Latin of the medieval universities (Paris, Oxford, Bologna), of canon law (the *Decretum* of Gratian, c. 1140), of European diplomacy + bureaucracy.
- The Renaissance Humanist Latin of Petrarch, Erasmus, and the printing-press recovery of classical authors.

There is no clean break between Ecclesiastical Latin, Medieval Latin, and Neo-Latin. The same language flows in unbroken continuity from Jerome through Aquinas through Erasmus into modern Catholic theological + canonical use.
