# Old Georgian

The literary language of Christian Kartli (eastern Georgia) — a member of the small, isolate Kartvelian family, written from the 5th century in its own purpose-made script, and the documented hinge through which the life of the Buddha became a Christian saint's legend. Like its Armenian twin, Old Georgian is a conversion-literacy package: alphabet, Bible translation, and original martyr-literature arriving within a few generations of the kingdom's baptism.

## A literature that begins abroad

Georgia's conversion is dated by tradition to 326/337 (King Mirian III through the captive woman Saint Nino). The earliest *dated* Georgian, strikingly, is not in Georgia: the Bir el Qutt mosaic inscriptions (c. 430) come from a Georgian monastery near Bethlehem — the pilgrimage-monastic network was the literature's cradle. The Khanmeti palimpsests carry the earliest Bible stratum (translated from Armenian exemplars with Syriac contact, later realigned on Greek); the first original work, the *Martyrdom of Shushanik* (c. 476-483), dramatizes the Christian-Zoroastrian frontier — an Armenian princess martyred by a husband converting to the Sasanian religion for political advancement.

After Georgia accepted Chalcedon (c. 607, breaking with miaphysite Armenia), its canon was systematically re-translated from Greek, culminating in the Athonite school at Iviron ("of the Iberians" — i.e., Georgians) on Mount Athos itself ([[tradition-georgian-orthodox]], [[tradition-byzantine-christianity]]).

## Cross-tradition findings

- **The Buddha becomes a Christian saint — through Georgian.** The *Balavariani* is the key documented link in the strangest transmission chain in hagiography: the Buddha's legend travelled Indic → Manichaean Middle Persian → Arabic (*Bilawhar wa-Yūdāsaf*) → **Old Georgian** (*Balavariani*) → Greek (*Barlaam and Ioasaph*) → Latin and every European vernacular. "Josaphat" is *bodhisattva* worn down by five languages; he received a feast day in the Roman Martyrology (Nov. 27). Old Georgian is where the tale crossed from the Islamic to the Byzantine world and was fully Christianized (Lang 1957; Lopez & McCracken 2014).
- **Twin alphabets, divergent canons.** Georgian and [[language-classical-armenian|Armenian]] are sibling conversion-literacy projects (the Armenian sources even claim one inventor for both alphabets — a claim Georgia rejects); their post-Chalcedon split (607) is legible in the manuscripts, as Georgian scriptural texts pivot from Armenian to Greek exemplars. The pair is a controlled experiment in how doctrinal schism redirects translation streams.
- **Frontier martyrdoms as linguistic documents.** Shushanik (Zoroastrian frontier, 5th c.) and Abo of Tbilisi (Islamic frontier, 8th c.) bracket Old Georgian's first centuries: the language's original literature is a running record of the Caucasus as the meeting-line of Christianity, Zoroastrianism, and Islam.
