# Coptic

The final attested phase of the Egyptian language — written in a Greek-derived alphabet (with six supplementary signs inherited from the Demotic script), used as the medium of Egyptian Christianity from the 3rd century CE to the present, and the language of the [[event-nag-hammadi-discovery|Nag Hammadi Library]], of the foundational documents of monasticism, and of one of the most consequential late-antique Christological + theological traditions.

## What Coptic is

Coptic is the same language as the Egyptian of the [[alphabet-medu-netjer|hieroglyphs]] — the *continuous linguistic descendant* of the language of the Pyramid Texts (c. -2400), the Middle Egyptian classical norm (c. -2000), and Demotic (the late + Greco-Roman cursive register, c. -650 to +500). The transition to Coptic is fundamentally a script reform: rather than continue writing Egyptian in the increasingly cumbersome Demotic script (whose graphic conventions could not unambiguously represent vowels), Christian Egyptian intellectuals + scribes adopted the Greek alphabet — making Egyptian's spoken sound-shape explicitly written for the first time — and added six supplementary letters derived from Demotic to handle the Egyptian-specific consonants. The result is a transparent alphabetic writing system for what is, structurally, still the Egyptian language.

The principal Coptic dialects are:

- **Sahidic** (sa, southern) — the supraregional literary norm of the 4th-9th centuries; the dialect of Shenoute, of most Nag Hammadi codices, of the bulk of classical Coptic Christian literature.
- **Bohairic** (bo, northern / Delta) — the dialect of the Nitrian + Wadi Natrun monastic communities; becomes the standard Coptic Orthodox liturgical norm from c. 1100 onward + remains so to the present.
- **Akhmimic** (ak) + **Lycopolitan / Subakhmimic** (ly) + **Fayyumic** (fa) — regional Middle-Egyptian dialects; Lycopolitan is the dialect of the Manichaean Medinet Madi + Kellis codices and several Nag Hammadi tractates.

## The Greek lexical density

Coptic is famous for the extraordinary density of [[koine-greek|Greek]] loanwords in its lexicon — by some calculations, up to 20-25% of the productive vocabulary of literary Sahidic Coptic is Greek-origin. This is the highest non-genetic loanword saturation of any major liturgical language. The Greek loans cover the Christian theological + ecclesiastical + scientific + administrative + everyday-lexical fields. Coptic Christian writers code-switch between Egyptian and Greek with extreme freedom; the language is structurally an Afroasiatic-Egyptian skeleton wearing a heavily-Greek lexicon.

## The Nag Hammadi Library

The single most consequential surviving Coptic corpus is the [[event-nag-hammadi-discovery|Nag Hammadi Library]] — thirteen leather-bound papyrus codices discovered in 1945 in Upper Egypt, containing approximately 52 Coptic-language tractates, almost all of them translations of earlier Greek originals. The corpus preserves nearly all the principal surviving texts of:

- **Sethian Gnosticism** (the *Apocryphon of John*, *Gospel of the Egyptians*, *Trimorphic Protennoia*, *Zostrianos*, *Allogenes*, *Three Steles of Seth*).
- **Valentinian Gnosticism** (the *Gospel of Truth*, *Gospel of Philip*, *Treatise on the Resurrection*, *Tripartite Tractate*).
- **Thomasine + Jewish-Christian literature** (the *Gospel of Thomas*, the *Book of Thomas the Contender*).
- **Hermetic literature** (excerpts from Hermetic discourses, the *Discourse on the Eighth and Ninth*, *Asclepius*).

Before Nag Hammadi, our knowledge of Gnostic + Hermetic Egypt depended on heresiologists (Irenaeus, Hippolytus, Epiphanius). After Nag Hammadi we have the Gnostic + Hermetic texts in their own voice — in Coptic translation, presupposing Greek originals largely lost.

## Monasticism

Coptic-speaking Egypt is the birthplace of cenobitic + anchoritic Christian monasticism. The foundational monastic figures — Anthony the Great (251-356), Pachomius (c. 292-348), Shenoute of Atripe (c. 348-465), Macarius the Egyptian (c. 300-391), the Desert Fathers + Mothers of the *Apophthegmata Patrum* — wrote, taught, and prayed in Coptic. Pachomian + Anthonian Egyptian monasticism is the direct ancestor of all subsequent Christian monastic traditions: Greek + Cappadocian (Basil of Caesarea explicitly modelled on Pachomius), Syrian, Latin (John Cassian transmits Egyptian models into Gaul; Benedict of Nursia's Rule descends from these chains), and Slavonic.

## After the Arab conquest

After the 7th-c. Arab-Islamic conquest of Egypt (641), Coptic gradually loses ground to Arabic as the everyday language. By c. 1300 the language is shrinking; by c. 1600-1700 vernacular Coptic is functionally extinct. Coptic continues, however, as the *liturgical* language of the Coptic Orthodox Church to the present — and the Coptic Orthodox liturgy is one of the most ancient continuously-celebrated Christian rites in the world.
