# Old Irish (Goídelc)

The language of early-medieval Ireland — first attested in the [[alphabet-ogham|ogham]] memorial stones (the **Primitive Irish** stage, c. 350-600), then reborn as Europe's earliest large vernacular literature in the margins of Latin scripture. Old Irish carries the largest body of early-medieval vernacular law in Europe, the Ulster Cycle sagas, and the synthetic origin-myth of the *Lebor Gabála* — a pre-Christian world preserved entirely by the Christian monasticism that succeeded it.

## From stones to margins

- **The ogham stage.** ~400 standing stones in Ireland and western Britain carry Primitive Irish name-formulas ("of X son of Y") in the 20-stroke ogham alphabet — the language still wearing its archaic endings, before the sound-collapse that produced Old Irish. The script story lives at [[alphabet-ogham]]; the stones are the only contemporary record of the pre-literate language.
- **The gloss stage.** Contemporary Old Irish proper survives chiefly as **glosses** — thousands of vernacular notes written between the lines of Latin Bibles and grammars (Würzburg, Milan, St Gall, 8th-9th c.), preserved on the Continent because Irish missionary monasticism carried its books there. Europe's most precisely-datable early vernacular is interlinear commentary on scripture.
- **The saga and law stage.** The Táin, the Ulster Cycle, the Brehon law tracts, and the *Lebor Gabála* are linguistically rooted in this period but survive in later manuscripts — oral-derived literature passing through monastic scriptoria, the Irish instance of the gap that also defines the Eddas and the Vedas.

## Cross-tradition findings

- **Pagan myth in monastic custody.** Ireland and Iceland ([[language-old-norse]]) are the twin cases of northern-European pre-Christian mythology surviving *only* through Christian vernacular manuscript culture — with the same interpretive consequence: the Túatha Dé Danann reach us already euhemerized into invasion-races and synchronized to biblical chronology by the *Lebor Gabála*'s monastic compilers (McCone 1990). What looks like the pagan source is already the church's edition of it.
- **The fili and the Brahmin.** The Irish poet-jurist caste (*filid*) — a hereditary learned order with a ~12-year memorization curriculum guarding law, genealogy, and praise-poetry — has been compared since the 19th c. to the Vedic-Brahminical institution as the two conservative extremes of the Indo-European world ([[sanskrit-vedic]]). The disciplined form of the comparison (inherited poetic-legal formulae: Watkins 1995) is sound; the maximal "Celts and Aryans" version is dated, and is carried here as a flagged scholarly parallel, not a transmission claim.
- **Literacy's two-way trade.** Latin Christianity gave Irish its manuscript literacy ([[language-latin-ecclesiastical]]); within two centuries Irish monasticism was re-exporting Latin learning to a post-Roman Continent (Columbanus's foundations; the St Gall library where Old Irish glosses now sit). The periphery that received the book trade returned it with interest.
- **Ogham as letter-mysticism.** The medieval Irish tradition (Auraicept na n-Éces, the scholars' primer) wrapped ogham's letters in tree-names and origin-legend — the Irish member of the cross-tradition pattern of sacralized alphabets treated at [[alphabet-ogham]] and beside [[alphabet-elder-futhark|the runes]].
