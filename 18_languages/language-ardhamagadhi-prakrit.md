# Ardhamāgadhī Prakrit

The Middle-Indo-Aryan canonical language of the Śvetāmbara Jain tradition — traditionally identified as the language in which Mahāvīra (24th and last Tīrthaṅkara, c. -540 to -468) delivered his teachings, the linguistic medium of the 45-text Śvetāmbara Āgama canon, and the Jain counterpart to [[language-pali|Pāli]] in the parallel śramaṇa-movement of Buddhism. One of the three principal classical-canonical Prakrits (alongside Pāli + Śaurasenī).

## What Ardhamāgadhī is

Ardhamāgadhī ("half-Māgadhī") is a Middle-Indo-Aryan vernacular of the eastern Gangetic plain — the geographical home of Magadha (modern Bihar), the region where Mahāvīra (and the historical Buddha) taught. The "half-Māgadhī" label reflects the language's intermediate position: it preserves some eastern (Māgadhī) features (most notably the *-e* nominative masculine singular, the *l* > *r* shift, the merger of three Old-Indo-Aryan sibilants into one) but lacks the full set of eastern features that characterize pure Māgadhī, suggesting a koiné-like literary register that mediates between regional eastern speech + a more supraregional Middle-Indo-Aryan norm.

The Jain tradition's identification of Ardhamāgadhī as Mahāvīra's teaching-language is structurally similar to (though linguistically more accurate than) the Theravāda tradition's identification of Pāli as the Buddha's language. The actual situation is most likely: Mahāvīra taught in an Eastern Middle-Indo-Aryan vernacular of the 5th c. BCE, and the canonical Ardhamāgadhī preserves a stylized literary version of that vernacular — moderated over centuries of oral transmission by speakers from more western Prakrit areas and finally committed to writing at Valabhī (Gujarat) in the 5th c. CE.

## The Śvetāmbara canon

The Jain Āgamas — the canonical scriptures of the Śvetāmbara branch — are the principal Ardhamāgadhī corpus. The tradition counts 45 or 46 canonical texts:

- **The eleven Aṅgas** ("limbs") — Ācārāṅga (rules of conduct), Sūtrakṛtāṅga (refutation of heretical doctrines), Sthānāṅga + Samavāyāṅga (numerical doctrine-catalogues), Vyākhyāprajñapti / Bhagavatī (the largest Aṅga, encyclopedic dialogue), Jñātādharmakathā (illustrative narratives), Upāsakadaśāḥ + Antakṛddaśāḥ + Anuttaraupapātikadaśāḥ (decade-collections of laymen-saints + final-liberation stories), Praśnavyākaraṇa (Q-and-A), Vipākasūtra (karmic-consequence narratives). The traditional twelfth Aṅga, the Dṛṣṭivāda, is lost.
- **The twelve Upāṅgas** ("sub-limbs") — cosmological + biographical + doctrinal supplements.
- **The ten Prakīrṇakas** ("miscellaneous") — devotional + ethical + ritual texts.
- **The six Chedasūtras** — monastic discipline.
- **The four Mūlasūtras** ("root scriptures") — *Uttarādhyayana* (the most archaic linguistic stratum), *Āvaśyaka* (essential daily ritual), *Daśavaikālika* (foundational monastic training), *Piṇḍaniryukti* (alms-collection rules).

Outside the Aṅga + Upāṅga + Prakīrṇaka + Cheda + Mūla structure stand the foundational paracanonical texts: the *Kalpasūtra* (traditionally Bhadrabāhu, c. -300; recited annually at the Paryushana festival; contains the Tīrthaṅkara hagiography), the *Tattvārtha Sūtra* of Umāsvāti (2nd-5th c., the only Jain text accepted by BOTH Śvetāmbara + Digambara branches, but composed in Sanskrit), and the *Bhadrabāhu Niryuktis* (verse commentaries on the canonical texts).

## The Jain śramaṇa choice of vernacular

Like the Buddhist movement's choice of Middle-Indo-Aryan Prakrits (Pāli, Gāndhārī, the various Buddhist Hybrid Sanskrits) over Vedic-Brahmanical Sanskrit, the Jain choice of Ardhamāgadhī is *not* a linguistic accident — it is a deliberate theological + political statement. Mahāvīra is reported in the tradition to have explicitly preferred the vernacular over Sanskrit, on the principle that the dharma should be accessible to ordinary people regardless of caste or Sanskrit education. This is part of the broader Iron-Age Gangetic śramaṇa rejection of Vedic-Brahmanical hegemony, in which the choice of language is itself an ideological commitment. Jain Mahārāṣṭrī (a slightly later canonical Prakrit register) + Śaurasenī (the Digambara canonical Prakrit) continue the same vernacular-preference into the 1st-millennium-CE Jain literary tradition.

## The Aśokan-era parallel

The Aśokan inscriptions (mid-3rd c. BCE) — the earliest substantial dated Middle-Indo-Aryan textual corpus — show regional dialect variation that closely parallels the variation in the Jain + Buddhist canonical languages. The eastern Pillar Edicts (Delhi-Topra, Allahabad-Kosam, Lauriya-Araraj, etc.) are in a Middle-Indo-Aryan very close to Ardhamāgadhī in many phonological features; the western Rock Edicts (Girnar) are in a more western variety closer to Pāli; the northwestern Rock Edicts (Shahbazgarhi, Mansehra) are in a Gāndhārī variety with characteristic northwestern features. Aśoka's empire-scale chancery used regional Prakrits adapted to the local linguistic milieu — an early documented example of the Middle-Indo-Aryan dialect-continuum.
