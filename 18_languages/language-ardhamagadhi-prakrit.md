# Ardha-Māgadhī Prakrit

The canonical Middle Indo-Aryan language of [[tradition-jainism|Śvetāmbara Jainism]] — traditionally identified as the language of [[mahavira|Mahāvīra]]'s teaching (c. -500) and the immediate-disciple transmission. Ardha-Māgadhī ("half-Māgadhī") is the language of the Jain Āgama corpus — 45+ canonical texts whose final redaction is the Council of Valabhī (453 CE) under Devarddhi Gaṇi but whose compositional layers extend back to the late centuries BCE.

## Position in the Middle-Indo-Aryan family

Ardha-Māgadhī is a sister-Prakrit to [[language-pali|Pali]] — both Middle Indo-Aryan literary registers, both descended from vernacular sisters of Vedic Sanskrit (not from Classical Sanskrit), both associated with the eastern-Indian "Greater Magadha" cultural zone (Bronkhorst 2007), both deployed by founders of new śramaṇa-renunciate traditions (Mahāvīra and the historical Buddha Siddhārtha Gautama, near-contemporaries c. -500) who consciously chose vernacular Middle Indo-Aryan over Sanskrit as their canonical language. The choice parallels the early Christian decision to preserve scripture in Koine Greek rather than literary Atticist Greek + Hebrew.

The traditional name "Ardha-Māgadhī" (literally "half-Māgadhī") reflects the Jain grammatical tradition's view that the language is partway between the spoken Māgadhī of the Magadha region and a more cosmopolitan literary Middle Indo-Aryan register. Linguistically the language shows characteristic Middle-Indo-Aryan features: simplification of Old-Indo-Aryan consonant clusters (Skt. *dharma* → AM *dhamma*; Skt. *karma* → AM *kamma*); loss of the dual number; vowel-system simplification; verbal-morphology simplification.

## The Jain Āgama

The canonical corpus comprises:

- **11 Aṅgas** ("limbs") — the core canonical texts, including the foundational [[phase-2-038-acharanga-sutra|Ācārāṅga]] (treating monastic conduct + the ascetic life), the [[phase-2-039-sutrakritanga|Sūtrakṛtāṅga]] (doctrinal-polemical, with the locus-classicus ahimsa formulation at I.11.33), and the lengthy Vyākhyāprajñapti / Bhagavatī Sūtra (dialogues of Mahāvīra).
- **12 Upāṅgas** ("sub-limbs") — including Jambūdvīpa-Prajñapti + Sūrya-Prajñapti, the Jain cosmographic + cosmological corpus.
- **Mūla-sūtras + Cheda-sūtras + Cūlikā-sūtras + Prakīrṇaka-sūtras** — the additional canonical layers.

Together the Śvetāmbara Āgama is one of the major Indian religious canons of antiquity, alongside the Vedic Saṃhitā-Brāhmaṇa corpus + the Buddhist Tipiṭaka.

## The Digambara split

The Digambara branch of Jainism (the "sky-clad" naked-monk tradition, separated from the Śvetāmbara branch in or near the early common era) does not accept the Śvetāmbara Āgama as canonical. Digambara tradition holds that the original Mahāvīran teaching was substantially lost during the famine-era exodus to the Deccan (~ -300 BCE under Bhadrabāhu), and that the surviving canonical material is preserved instead in the Digambara canonical scriptures (Ṣaṭkhaṇḍāgama, Kaṣāya-prābhṛta, c. 100-300 CE) composed in Jain Śaurasenī Prakrit, not Ardha-Māgadhī. The two branches' canonical-language traditions therefore split: Śvetāmbara = Ardha-Māgadhī; Digambara = Jain Śaurasenī Prakrit.

## The ahimsa formulation

Sūtrakṛtāṅga I.11.33's formulation is the locus classicus of the Jain absolute-nonviolence doctrine: *savve pāṇā piyāuyā suhasāyā dukkhapaḍikūlā appiyavahā piyajīviṇo jīviukāmā savvesiṃ jīviyaṃ piyaṃ* — "all beings love life; suffering is repugnant to all; killing is detestable to all; all beings love their own life and desire to live; life is dear to all." This is the most-cited single canonical articulation of the principle that flowers into the broader Indian + global nonviolence tradition: Aśoka's edicts, the Buddhist + Vaiṣṇava + much-later Gandhian inheritance.

## Transmission and modern scholarship

The Jain canonical-textual tradition was transmitted orally for centuries before being committed to writing at Valabhī (453 CE); modern manuscript evidence dates from the medieval period (12th c. CE onward). 19th-century European Indology (Hermann Jacobi, Heinrich Lüders, Richard Pischel) established the linguistic + philological framework; the Pāli Text Society + the Prakrit-Text-Society have produced the standard editions. Modern Jain communities in India + the diaspora maintain a continuous tradition of Āgama study in the original Ardha-Māgadhī alongside vernacular Indian-language translations.
