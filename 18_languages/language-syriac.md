# Syriac

The Eastern Aramaic literary language of [[tradition-syriac-christianity|Syriac Christianity]] — emerging at Edessa (modern Şanlıurfa, southeastern Turkey) in the early centuries CE, becoming by the 4th century the principal literary medium of Christian Aramaic culture from the Mediterranean to the Persian Gulf, and serving as the intermediary language between Greek philosophy + science and the Islamic civilization. Roughly the third great Christian literary language after Greek + Latin.

## What Syriac is

Syriac is the literary form of the Eastern Aramaic vernacular of Edessa, standardized in the 2nd-3rd centuries CE as a Christian literary language. It is a descendant of [[language-aramaic-imperial|Imperial Aramaic]] (and hence cousin to Jewish Babylonian Aramaic, Mandaic, and Christian Palestinian Aramaic), but it is the variety whose script + literature + theology achieved the deepest cultural reach.

The Syriac literary norm is exceptionally stable — Ephrem's 4th-century hymns and Bar Hebraeus's 13th-century encyclopedia are essentially in the same language. As with Classical Arabic + Classical Sanskrit + Classical Latin, the codified Syriac literary register persisted as a frozen high register across a millennium while spoken Aramaic varieties continued to evolve underneath it.

## The three load-bearing roles

### 1. The Peshitta — the Syriac Bible

The [[phase-4-108-peshitta|Peshitta]] (literally "the simple [version]") is the standard Bible of all Syriac-tradition Churches. The Peshitta Old Testament is translated *directly from the Hebrew* (Weitzman 1999) — not, like the Latin Vulgate, mediated through the Greek Septuagint — giving Syriac Christianity an independent and frequently-conservative textual tradition of the Hebrew Bible. The Peshitta New Testament is translated from Greek (revised to its standard form in the early 5th c.). Through the Peshitta, Syriac Christianity is the only ancient Christian tradition with direct Semitic-language access to the Hebrew Bible.

### 2. The Greek-to-Arabic translation movement runs through Syriac

The single most consequential cross-tradition role of Syriac is as the *intermediary language* in the systematic translation of Greek philosophy, mathematics, medicine, and astronomy into Arabic (Gutas 1998; Hugonnard-Roche 2004). The pipeline:

- **Stage 1 (6th-8th c.):** Greek → Syriac, by East Syrian + West Syrian scholars at Nisibis, Reshaina, Edessa, Gondishapur. Sergius of Reshaina translates Aristotelian + Galenic corpora into Syriac; the East-Syrian scholastic tradition develops a rigorous Syriac philosophical vocabulary.
- **Stage 2 (9th-10th c.):** Syriac → Arabic, in Abbasid Baghdad at the *Bayt al-Ḥikma* + adjacent institutions. The principal translator [[ephrem-the-syrian|Hunayn ibn Ishaq]] (d. 873; not yet in vault under that ID) and his Christian-Syriac school produced Arabic translations of nearly all the surviving Aristotelian corpus, Plato's dialogues, Hippocrates + Galen, Euclid + Archimedes, Ptolemy's *Almagest*. Many translations first went *Greek → Syriac (draft)* and then *Syriac → Arabic (final)*.

Without Syriac as the intermediary linguistic medium, the Islamic-civilization reception of Greek thought — and consequently the 12th-13th c. Latin Christian reception of Greek-via-Arabic — would have been structurally different or impossible. The technical philosophical + medical vocabulary of [[classical-arabic|Classical Arabic]] preserves heavy Syriac calque-loaning.

### 3. The eastward Christian expansion

Syriac-language Christianity ([[tradition-nestorian-christianity|the Church of the East]]) carried the gospel + monasticism + Aristotelian logic + medicine eastward along the Silk Road — into the Sasanian Persian empire (after the 5th-c. Christological splits drove the East Syrians into Sasanian territory), to Central Asia (Sogdiana, the Turfan oases), to Tang-dynasty China (the 781 Xi'an "Nestorian Stele" of Sogdian-Syriac Christianity), to South India (the Saint Thomas Christians of Kerala, whose liturgy is to this day in Syriac), and even to Mongol-empire-era Khanbaliq (Beijing). At its 13th-century maximum extent the Syriac-language Church was the most geographically extended single Christian tradition in the world.
