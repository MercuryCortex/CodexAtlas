# The Entheogen Hypothesis

The **comparative-theory hub** for the claim that **psychoactive plants and fungi were a wellspring of religious experience** — that behind many traditions' visionary, ecstatic, and mystical practices lie identifiable psychoactive substances. This node sits *above* the specific substance nodes ([[substance-soma]], [[substance-kykeon]], [[substance-fly-agaric]], and the batch of sacred psychoactives) as the place where the general argument — and its serious problems — are stated once.

It is a **live, contested hypothesis, not settled fact.** The honest framing runs on a spectrum: some single cases are strongly evidenced, some are plausible-but-unproven, and the grand "psychedelics explain the origin of religion" claim is speculative.

## The word

"Entheogen" was coined in **1979** by a committee of five scholars (Ruck, Bigwood, Staples, Ott, and Wasson) from Greek *éntheos* ("god-within", "inspired") + *-genés* ("generating") — literally *that which generates the divine within*. It was proposed to replace **"hallucinogen"** (which implies illusion/deception) and **"psychedelic"** (by then culturally loaded with the 1960s counterculture) with a neutral term for the *sacramental, ritual* use of psychoactives. The word's adoption is itself part of the story: it reframed these substances as religious rather than recreational or pathological.

## The strong cases and the weak cases

**Strong / well-evidenced.** Some psychoactive-sacrament traditions are simply *documented*, not hypothetical: Siberian fly-agaric shamanism, Mesoamerican [[substance-peyote]] and psilocybin-mushroom (*teonanácatl*) and [[substance-ololiuqui]] use, Amazonian [[substance-ayahuasca]], Andean [[substance-tobacco-amerindian]]. Here the "hypothesis" is not really in question — the sacramental psychoactive use is historically and ethnographically attested.

**Contested single cases.** The identity of [[substance-soma]] (ephedra vs. fly-agaric vs. Syrian rue) and the ergot-*kykeon* thesis for Eleusis are genuine scholarly debates with strong arguments on multiple sides and no consensus.

**Weak / speculative.** The grand claim — that psychoactives are *the* origin of religion as such (McKenna's "stoned ape"; sweeping "all gods were mushrooms" readings) — outruns its evidence and is not accepted by mainstream anthropology or history of religion.

## Why it stays contested

- **The pharmacology is real; the history is inferential.** Modern peer-reviewed work (Griffiths et al. 2006) shows psilocybin *reliably* occasions experiences subjects rate as genuinely mystical — so the *mechanism* is not in doubt. What is disputed is whether any *particular* ancient rite used a psychoactive, since the physical evidence (residues, botanical remains) is usually absent or ambiguous.
- **Advocacy vs. evidence.** Several key proponents (Wasson, Ott, McKenna) argued with a program to advance; critics note the temptation to read psychoactives *into* thin evidence.
- **It is not all-or-nothing.** The correct posture is case-by-case: accept the documented sacraments, weigh the contested identifications on their merits, and hold the grand origin-of-religion claim as unproven.
