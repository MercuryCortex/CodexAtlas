# Peyote

A small, slow-growing, spineless cactus of the Chihuahuan Desert whose grey-green dried "buttons" (the above-ground crown) contain mescaline and over thirty other phenethylamine and isoquinoline alkaloids. The oldest direct chemical evidence of psychoactive plant use in the Americas: radiocarbon-dated buttons from Shumla Cave (Texas) returned dates of 5700 BP with mescaline still preserved in the specimens (El-Seedi et al. 2005).

## Aztec and pre-Columbian use

Sahagún's *Florentine Codex* (Book 11, mid-16th century, recording earlier Nahua knowledge) describes *peyotl* under the chichimeca and toltec — a substance used to endure long marches, to see visions of war, to communicate with the dead. The Spanish Inquisition formally prohibited peyote in 1620 (Edict of the Holy Inquisition, Mexico City), treating it as a diabolic medium of communication. Indigenous use continued in the northern frontier zones outside effective colonial reach.

## Wixárika (Huichol) annual pilgrimage

The most-studied continuous tradition: each year a group of Wixárika initiates, led by a *mara'akame* (singer-shaman), walks the ~400 km from the western Sierra Madre to Wirikuta — the high desert in San Luis Potosí identified as the cosmic birthplace of the sun. The pilgrimage is a re-enactment of the primordial Deer-Hunt: the peyote IS the deer IS the corn — the three are one in the foundational mythology (Myerhoff 1974). The collected buttons are brought back to be consumed at the *fiesta del peyote* during the dry season.

## Native American Church

Plains-tribe peyote ritual coalesced in the late 19th century from earlier southern-plains practice (Comanche, Kiowa, Apache) into a pan-tribal "Peyote Way" that fused with Christian elements (Christ as peyote, the half-moon and cross altars, baptismal language). Formally incorporated 1918 in Oklahoma to defend the religion from prohibition under the Indian Religious Crimes Code; the [[tradition-native-american-church]] now has ~300,000 members across Canada, the US, and Mexico. The American Indian Religious Freedom Act Amendments of 1994 made federal protection explicit (*Employment Division v. Smith* 1990 had previously denied constitutional protection).

## Pharmacology and ritual structure

Mescaline (typically 200-400 mg per session, 4-8 large buttons) produces a long (10-12 hour) state of visionary experience characterized by intense visual phenomena, emotional openness, and (in tradition-specific framings) communication with the peyote spirit. NAC ritual is overnight: sundown to sunrise, around a crescent-moon earthen altar, with water drum, gourd rattle, sacred fire, and the cycle of fixed and personal songs. The ritual frame is explicitly therapeutic — many participants come for healing of alcohol dependence or grief.

## Cross-tradition connections

- **[[substance-ayahuasca]]**: the other major continuously-practiced American entheogen complex; analogous in social function (community ritual healing) but botanically and pharmacologically unrelated.
- **[[substance-fly-agaric]]**: the Siberian-shamanic vertical-axis entheogen; the Bering crossing question makes the comparison structurally interesting.
- **[[substance-psilocybin-mushroom]]**: the Mesoamerican parallel (*teonanácatl*, "flesh of the gods") used by Mazatec, Nahua, and Zapotec — another Inquisition-suppressed pre-Columbian tradition.
