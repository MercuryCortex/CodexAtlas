# Fly agaric (Amanita muscaria)

The red-capped white-spotted mushroom of the boreal forest — the iconic fairytale mushroom of European storybooks — is one of the oldest documented entheogens in continuous use. Siberian peoples (Koryak, Chukchi, Khanty, Mansi, Itelmen) have used dried *mukhomor* in shamanic practice into the modern ethnographic period. The pharmacology is atypical: the muscimol and ibotenic acid in the mushroom act on GABA and glutamate receptors rather than the serotonin system, producing a state phenomenologically distinct from psilocybin or DMT — often sedative, sometimes dissociative, with motor effects and reports of size-distortion (the Alice-in-Wonderland symptom complex).

## The urine recycling

One of the most pharmacologically distinctive features of fly-agaric use: muscimol passes through the renal system essentially unmetabolized. Russian colonial observers from the 17th century onward repeatedly recorded Siberian groups consuming the urine of someone who had taken the mushroom — a way to extend the supply of a substance that grew in only short seasonal flushes. The practice is well-documented in primary sources (Krasheninnikov 1755 on Kamchatka; Steller's accounts) and survives in modern ethnographic confirmation.

## The Wasson Soma hypothesis

R. Gordon Wasson's 1968 *Soma: Divine Mushroom of Immortality* argued that the Vedic [[substance-soma]] was fly agaric. The argument rested on (a) the Rigveda's described preparation by pressing + filtration (which Wasson read as mushroom-pressing), (b) Rigvedic phrases Wasson read as mushroom-imagery (the "single eye" / cap), (c) the urine-recycling practice (Wasson read Rigveda 9.74 references to "urine" as describing the recycled mushroom). The hypothesis was widely discussed in the 1970s but has been progressively displaced by Harry Falk's ephedra hypothesis (1989) as the institutional standard for soma identity. Wasson's argument remains historically important as the founding work of Western ethnomycology.
