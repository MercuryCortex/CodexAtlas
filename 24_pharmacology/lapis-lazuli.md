# Lapis Lazuli

## What it is
Lapis lazuli is a deep-blue metamorphic rock prized since the Neolithic as a **gemstone and pigment**. Its colour comes chiefly from the mineral lazurite; the finest grades are flecked with golden pyrite (read in antiquity as stars in a night sky). Ground and purified, it yields **ultramarine**, the most precious blue pigment of the pre-modern world. Almost all ancient lapis came from a single source region: the **Sar-i Sang mines of Badakhshan** in present-day northeastern Afghanistan.

## Earliest attestation
Lapis appears in elite burials and temple deposits from the 4th millennium BCE onward — at Mesopotamian sites (the Royal Cemetery of Ur is full of it), in Egypt (inlay in jewellery, the funerary mask of Tutankhamun, amulets), and across the Indus and Iranian worlds. Its movement from Badakhshan to Sumer, Egypt, and the Indus is one of the earliest documented long-distance luxury trades.

## Significance
- **Sacred colour.** In Egypt lapis (and its imitation in blue faience/Egyptian blue) signified the heavens and the hair/beards of the gods; it was associated with the night sky and with deities of the sky and the dead.
- **Pigment.** Powdered to ultramarine, lapis painted the robe of the Virgin in medieval and Renaissance Christian art — so costly it was reserved for the holiest figures.
- **Materia medica.** It enters several pharmacopeias as a mineral remedy (and, ground, as a purgative in some traditions).

## Cross-tradition findings
- **A single-source commodity binding three civilizations.** Because virtually all ancient lapis derived from Badakhshan, the stone is a clean tracer of the **earliest long-distance exchange networks**: the same material reached Sumerian, Egyptian, Iranian, and Indus elites, carried along corridors that prefigure the later Silk Road. The Atlas treats lapis as a commodity whose distribution maps a transmission of materials (and, with them, prestige aesthetics) across the Bronze Age Near East.
