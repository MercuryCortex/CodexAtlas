# Psilocybin mushrooms

The genus *Psilocybe* contains roughly 200 species, of which a few dozen produce psychoactive doses of psilocybin and psilocin — tryptamine alkaloids that act on serotonin 5-HT2A receptors. In Mesoamerican ritual practice the mushrooms have been continuously used in oracle and healing contexts for at least the late-Aztec period (Sahagún records *teonanácatl* — "flesh of the gods" — under the Mexica) and probably much longer; the Guatemalan "mushroom stones" (c. -1000 to -500) appear to depict the same practice in stone.

## The Mazatec velada

The best-documented contemporary tradition is the Mazatec *velada* — an all-night vigil-ceremony of communal mushroom-eating presided over by a *curandero* or *curandera*. The participants eat pairs of mushrooms (in even numbers, by tradition) and pass the night in song-led visionary practice; the curandera's chant-songs (preserved in the published Sabina material) are the principal ritual technology. The mushroom is understood as a divine person — *la santa María*, *los niños santos* — whose voice the curandera ventriloquizes. The Mazatec frame is therapeutic and diagnostic: the velada is convened to address sickness, find missing persons, or resolve disputes.

## The 1955 Wasson velada

R. Gordon Wasson — a Wall Street banker and amateur ethnomycologist — and his photographer Allan Richardson were the first outsiders documented to participate in a Mazatec velada. María Sabina admitted them on the misinformation that they were searching for a missing son. Wasson's 1957 *Life Magazine* article ("Seeking the Magic Mushroom", 13 May 1957) opened the entire Mesoamerican-entheogen field to Western attention; the consequences for the Mazatec community — particularly for María Sabina, who was blamed by her village for the resulting influx of outsiders — were destructive. The episode is the foundational ethical case study for ethnobotanical research.

## Pre-Columbian iconography

The "mushroom stones" of highland Guatemala — small carved stelae depicting a figure under a mushroom-shaped cap — are the most-cited archaeological evidence for deep pre-Columbian Mesoamerican use. Roughly 200 examples are catalogued, dating to the Preclassic period (c. -1000 to -500). Interpretation remains contested (a minority view reads them as stools or grave-markers), but the dominant view in mesoamerican archaeology accepts them as mushroom-cult artifacts.
