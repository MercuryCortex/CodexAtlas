# Bulguksa Temple and Seokguram Grotto (Gyeongju)

## What it is

**Bulguksa** (佛國寺 — "Temple of the Buddha Land") is the **principal Buddhist temple of Silla-era Korea** and the masterpiece of Korean Buddhist architecture. Together with the **Seokguram Grotto** (石窟庵 — "Stone Cave Hermitage") ~3 km uphill on the slopes of Mt. Toham, it forms a single integrated 8th-c. Silla-Korean Buddhist programme — temple at the foot of the mountain, grotto-Buddha at the summit — commissioned by the Silla prime minister **[[kim-daeseong|Kim Daeseong]]** (700–774) and completed ~751–774 CE under Kings Gyeongdeok and Hyegong.

The grotto contains a **~3.5 m granite seated Buddha** — the **most celebrated single sculpture of Korean Buddhism** and one of the finest realisations of the Tang-derived Vairocana / cosmic-Buddha iconographic programme in any tradition. The Bulguksa lower terrace contains two of the most architecturally significant Korean stone pagodas: **Seokgatap** (Pagoda of Shakyamuni) and **Dabotap** (Pagoda of Many Treasures) — each an architectural-iconographic monument in its own right.

Bulguksa was Korea's **first UNESCO World Heritage inscription** (jointly with Seokguram, 1995, File 736).

## History

- **528 CE** — traditional founding under King Beopheung of Silla (the king under whom Silla adopted Buddhism as state religion in 527 CE). The original temple was small.
- **751–774 CE** — **[[kim-daeseong|Kim Daeseong]]**, Silla prime minister, undertakes the great reconstruction. Per the [[samguk-yusa|*Samguk Yusa*]], Kim built **Bulguksa for his parents in this life** and **Seokguram for his parents in his previous life** — a Mahayana doctrinal-filial gesture combining present-life and previous-life parental devotion. The temple programme is fully Buddhist-cosmological: the architecture instantiates the doctrine of the *Buddha-kṣetra* (Buddha-land) of the *[[phase-4-102-avatamsaka-sutra|Avatamsaka Sutra]]*.
- **9th–14th c.** — Bulguksa remains the principal Buddhist temple of Korea through the Unified Silla (668–935) and Goryeo (918–1392) periods, with continuous patronage and several rebuilds.
- **1593** — the **Imjin War (Japanese invasion of Korea under Toyotomi Hideyoshi)** — Bulguksa is **burned to the ground**; many of the wooden structures destroyed. Stone elements (the two pagodas, the staircases, the platforms) survive.
- **1604–1805** — gradual reconstruction during the late Joseon period at progressively smaller scales.
- **1907** — **Seokguram is rediscovered** — having been largely lost to historical memory. A Japanese postal worker (per the standard account) stumbled on the grotto while sheltering from a storm. The Japanese colonial administration (1910–1945) undertook major restorations, including some controversial interventions.
- **1969–1973** — major South Korean government restoration of Bulguksa, returning the complex to a closer approximation of the 8th-c. Silla form. The standing Bulguksa today is principally this 1973 reconstruction on the surviving 8th-c. stone foundations.
- **1995** — UNESCO World Heritage inscription.

## Bulguksa — architectural form

Bulguksa is built on **two raised stone terraces** on the lower slopes of Mt. Toham. The terraces are reached by the famous **paired flights of stone stairs** — the **Cheongun-gyo (Blue Cloud Bridge)** and **Baegun-gyo (White Cloud Bridge)** to the upper precinct, and the **Yeonhwa-gyo (Lotus Flower Bridge)** and **Chilbo-gyo (Seven Treasures Bridge)** to the lower precinct. These stairways are themselves treated as **architectural-symbolic bridges from the mundane realm to the Buddha-land** — the names mark the threshold from terrestrial to Pure-Land territory.

The principal buildings (in the 1973 reconstruction following 8th-c. plans):

- **Daeungjeon** (Great Hero Hall) — the principal Shakyamuni hall on the upper terrace. The principal worship hall of Bulguksa.
- **Geungnakjeon** (Hall of Ultimate Bliss) — the Amitabha (Pure-Land) hall on the lower terrace. Houses a famous gilt-bronze seated Amitabha Buddha.
- **Birojeon** (Vairocana Hall) — houses a gilt-bronze seated Vairocana Buddha; one of the principal surviving 8th-c. Silla bronze Buddhas.
- **Gwaneumjeon** (Avalokiteshvara Hall).
- **Mujeong-seol-jeon** (lecture hall).

The two stone pagodas in the front courtyard — **Seokgatap** (Pagoda of Shakyamuni) and **Dabotap** (Pagoda of Many Treasures) — are placed paired on either side of the central axis. They are **architecturally and iconographically of contrasting type**:

- **Seokgatap** — a classical **Silla three-storey stone pagoda**, restrained, severe geometric form. The canonical exemplar of the Silla pagoda type that becomes the principal Korean pagoda vocabulary.
- **Dabotap** — an **elaborately ornamented stone pagoda** of unique design, with a square base supporting an octagonal upper section, decorated with stone lions, carved railings, and ornate brackets. The most distinctive single pagoda in Korean Buddhism.

The pairing of restrained-Shakyamuni and ornate-Many-Treasures pagoda mirrors the iconographic programme of the *[[phase-4-101-lotus-sutra|Lotus Sutra]]*'s Chapter 11, in which the Buddha of Many Treasures (Prabhutaratna) appears in his jewelled stupa beside Shakyamuni.

In 1966, repair work on Seokgatap recovered an extraordinary cache of dedication-objects, including the **Pure Light Dharani Sutra** — a Buddhist printed scroll dated ~706–751 CE that is one of the **oldest surviving printed documents in the world**, possibly predating the [[diamond-sutra-868|Dunhuang Diamond Sutra]] (868 CE) by ~150 years.

## Seokguram — the grotto Buddha

**Seokguram** is a man-made grotto, built ~742–774 CE, ~3 km uphill from Bulguksa on the slopes of **Mt. Toham**. It is a **stone-built grotto** (not a true rock-cut cave like [[mogao-caves-dunhuang|Mogao]] or [[ajanta-caves|Ajanta]]) — granite blocks assembled to form a chamber and then covered with earth, simulating the rock-cut cave-temple form. This is the **principal East Asian engagement with the Indian rock-cut Buddhist cave tradition in a Korean context**, executed by **assembly of stone blocks rather than excavation** because the local Mt. Toham granite was unsuitable for rock-carving.

The plan:

- A **rectangular antechamber** with **wall-relief carvings of guardian figures** (the Four Heavenly Kings, the Vajrapani, the Eight Devas) and bodhisattvas.
- A **circular domed inner chamber**, ~6.85 m in diameter, with a **single seated Buddha at the centre** (~3.5 m tall, granite, on a lotus pedestal).
- Around the wall of the circular chamber: **ten standing relief figures** — the Ten Great Disciples + the Eleven-Headed Avalokiteshvara behind the Buddha.
- A **domed roof** of carefully fitted granite, with a single circular keystone at the centre.

The **Seokguram Buddha** is the central sculptural masterpiece. It is a seated Buddha in *bhumisparsa-mudra* (earth-touching gesture — the moment of enlightenment), in the meditative posture, with extraordinarily refined facial modelling and drapery. Stylistically it is the **Korean Silla cognate of the Tang Vairocana sculpture tradition** — closely related to the [[longmen-grottoes|Longmen Fengxian Si]] Vairocana programme (~672–675) and the [[todaiji-daibutsuden|Todaiji]] Daibutsu (~752), all three deploying the same Tang-derived monumental-Buddha iconographic vocabulary.

The Buddha's position is **carefully oriented**: it faces **east-south-east**, looking out from the mountain toward the Sea of Japan and the **sunrise over the East Sea**. The orientation was likely calculated for the **winter-solstice sunrise** to penetrate the antechamber and illuminate the Buddha's face — though modern conservation alterations have partially obscured this original effect.

## The Pure Light Dharani Sutra — earliest printed text

The 1966 discovery in Seokgatap of the **Pure Light Dharani Sutra (*Mugu jeonggwang dae darani-gyŏng*)** — a Buddhist xylograph printed scroll dated by its dedication-context to ~706–751 CE — is one of the most consequential single discoveries in the history of printing. If the early date is correct, it predates the **[[diamond-sutra-868|Diamond Sutra]] of Dunhuang (868 CE)** as the oldest surviving printed document by ~150 years. The Korean claim is internationally contested (with Chinese scholars favouring Tang priority), but the Pure Light Dharani Sutra is **at minimum among the very oldest surviving printed documents** and demonstrates the existence of woodblock printing technology in 8th-c. Silla Korea.

## MASSIVE WIN — the East Asian Buddhist transmission chain at its 8th-c. peak

Bulguksa / Seokguram are the **Korean monumental architectural realisation of the same East Asian Mahayana Buddhist iconographic programme** that produced [[todaiji-daibutsuden|Todaiji]] in Japan ~22 years earlier and that descends from [[longmen-grottoes|Longmen]] / Tang China ~75 years earlier.

The transmission chain:

> **India** → **Central Asia / Silk Road / [[mogao-caves-dunhuang|Mogao]]** → **Tang China ([[longmen-grottoes|Longmen Fengxian Si]] Vairocana, ~672–675)** → **Silla Korea (Bulguksa / Seokguram, ~751–774) and Nara Japan ([[todaiji-daibutsuden|Todaiji]], ~745–752)**

Three observations on this chain:

1. **It is multi-directional, not single-track**. Buddhism transmitted Tang → Silla AND Tang → Nara, in parallel — not just Tang → Korea → Japan. Bulguksa and Todaiji are **siblings**, not parent and child.
2. **Korea is older as a recipient of Buddhism, younger as a producer of surviving monumental architecture**. Korea received Buddhism in 372 CE (Goguryeo) and 528 CE (Silla); Japan received Buddhism via Korea in 538 CE. But the principal **surviving** Korean monumental Buddhist architecture is **all post-668 Silla (Bulguksa / Seokguram) or later (Goryeo / Joseon)**. Japan preserves older monumental Buddhist architecture ([[horyuji-temple|Horyuji]], ~607) than Korea does. The reason: **Baekje's destruction (660 CE) and the long warfare on the peninsula destroyed the earlier Korean monumental Buddhist architecture**, while Japan's relative geographic insulation preserved its Asuka-period buildings.
3. **The doctrinal substrate is shared**. All three sites — Longmen Fengxian Si, Bulguksa / Seokguram, Todaiji — deploy the *Avatamsaka Sutra* Vairocana programme, all three are imperial / state-Buddhist commissions, all three use the cosmic-illuminator Buddha as the doctrinal centre.

Bulguksa and Seokguram are therefore the **Korean anchor of the late-8th-century East Asian state-Buddhist architectural moment**, and Seokguram's Buddha is the principal Korean sculptural realisation of the Tang Vairocana iconographic tradition.

## Kim Daeseong's commission and the *Samguk Yusa* narrative

The *Samguk Yusa* preserves the story of **[[kim-daeseong|Kim Daeseong]]**'s commission. The story is doctrinally rich:

- Kim Daeseong, in his **previous life**, was a poor boy who gave his only field to a Buddhist temple. In gratitude the temple deity arranged his **reincarnation as the son of Silla prime minister Kim Munyang**.
- In his current life, Kim Daeseong, having risen to be prime minister himself, fulfilled his **dual-life debt**:
  - **Bulguksa** built for his **present-life parents**.
  - **Seokguram** built for his **previous-life parents**.

Whether or not the narrative is historically accurate, it is the principal classical doctrinal articulation of the two-site programme: **two parents in two lives, two commissions in two locations, one integrated Mahayana religious-architectural programme**. The doctrinal use of cross-life karma to articulate a paired temple-and-grotto commission is one of the most distinctive features of the Korean Silla Buddhist programme.
