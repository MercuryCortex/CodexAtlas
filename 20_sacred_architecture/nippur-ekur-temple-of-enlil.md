# Nippur — Ekur, Temple of Enlil

## What it is

The **Ekur** ("Mountain House") at **Nippur** is the temple of **Enlil**, the supreme god of the Sumerian pantheon — and Nippur is the **religious capital of Sumer**. Unlike [[ziggurat-of-ur|Ur]] or [[uruk-white-temple-eanna|Uruk]], Nippur was never a major political capital; its standing was entirely religious. **Every Sumerian king from the Early Dynastic period onwards sought legitimation from Enlil at Nippur** — kingship was, in the Sumerian theological understanding, "lowered from Heaven at Eridu," but ratified at Nippur.

If Eridu is where civilisation began and Uruk is where it became monumental, **Nippur is where it became politically theological** — the place at which the pact between Sumerian kings and the divine order was sealed.

## History

Nippur (modern Nuffar) sits in central-southern Iraq, roughly equidistant between Babylon to the north and Ur to the south. Its central position on the Sumerian map is part of its religious significance — Nippur is **the centre of the Sumerian sacred geography**, the panhellenic-style centre to which all the city-state cults paid deference.

**The Ekur temple sequence:**

- **Early Dynastic (~2900–2334 BCE)** — The Ekur is fully active; the cult-platform develops on accumulated earlier construction.
- **Akkadian (~2334–2154 BCE)** — Sargon and his successors patronise the Ekur. Naram-Sin's later sack of Nippur is described in [[phase-1-022-lament-for-ur|the Curse of Agade]] as the sin that destroys his dynasty.
- **Third Dynasty of Ur (~2100 BCE)** — [[ur-nammu]] builds a formal stepped ziggurat at Nippur, paralleling his ziggurats at Ur, Uruk, and Eridu. The Ekur is now an Ur III standardised three-terrace ziggurat in the same form as [[ziggurat-of-ur]].
- **Old Babylonian (~1800–1600 BCE)** — Nippur is the centre of the Sumerian-language scribal tradition. The Old Babylonian scribal schools at Nippur produce the canonical written versions of much of the Sumerian literary corpus — the temple hymns, the laments, the [[phase-1-015-sumerian-king-list|King List]], the Sumerian Gilgamesh poems.
- **Kassite (~1500–1155 BCE)** — Major rebuilding of the Ekur under Kassite kings; Nippur becomes the Kassite religious capital. The temple is repeatedly rebuilt over the next several centuries.
- **Middle and Neo-Assyrian (~1300–600 BCE)** — Continued cult; the Ekur is rebuilt several times.
- **Neo-Babylonian and Achaemenid (~600–330 BCE)** — Continued use; Nippur's religious significance gradually declines as Babylonian cult-centres dominate.
- **Seleucid and Parthian (~330 BCE – 200 CE)** — Late temple-construction at Nippur; the site is eventually abandoned in late antiquity.

**The first major American Near Eastern excavation** was the University of Pennsylvania campaign at Nippur (1889–1900), led by John Punnett Peters and (controversially) Hermann Hilprecht, recovering tens of thousands of cuneiform tablets — the "Nippur Tablet Collection" at the University Museum, Philadelphia, is the principal corpus of Old Babylonian Sumerian literary texts in existence. The Oriental Institute (Chicago) resumed excavation 1948–1990 under various directors, with McGuire Gibson's long-running campaign (1972–1990) producing much of the modern understanding.

## Architectural form

The Nippur Ekur is **the religious centrepiece of a much larger sacred precinct** that includes:

- **The ziggurat** (Ur-Nammu form, ~2100 BCE) — three-terrace stepped platform, ~58 × 39 m at base, paralleling [[ziggurat-of-ur]].
- **The temple proper (Enlil's cella)** — a tripartite cella-and-courtyard complex adjacent to the ziggurat.
- **The Inanna temple** at Nippur (a separate substantial structure with its own long stratigraphic sequence — one of the best-recovered temple stratigraphies in Mesopotamia, excavated principally by Donald Hansen).
- **Subsidiary chapels** for the divine family — Enlil's consort Ninlil, their sons Ninurta and Nanna, and other associated deities.
- **The Tablet Hill scribal quarter** — where the scribal schools produced and preserved the literary corpus.

The Ekur is **not a single building** but a multi-building precinct — Mesopotamian sacred architecture is increasingly precinct-architecture by this period, not single-building architecture. This precinct-form is the architectural model that recurs at the **temenos** of later Greek sanctuaries, at the **harem ha-bayit** of [[solomons-temple-first|Solomon's Temple]], and in the great enclosed Roman temple-courts of the East ([[temple-of-jupiter-baalbek|Baalbek]]).

## Theological / cosmological function — the *Duranki*

The Ekur's most consequential theological epithet is **Duranki** — Sumerian for "**Bond of Heaven and Earth**". This is the explicit architectural-theological proposition that **the temple itself is the cosmic axis** — the architectural feature that holds the universe's two halves (heaven, earth) in mutual relation.

- **Ekur** = "Mountain House" → the temple **is** the cosmic mountain.
- **Duranki** = "Bond Heaven-Earth" → the temple **is** the axis between the two cosmic realms.

These are not metaphors. In Sumerian temple theology, the temple **is**, ontologically, the cosmic mountain and the cosmic axis. The architectural object is identified with the cosmic-theological function.

This identification is the **headwater** of the **[[theme-ziggurat-as-stairway-to-heaven|stairway-to-heaven motif]]** and the **[[axis-mundi|axis mundi]]** concept that Mircea Eliade later universalises. Nippur's Ekur is the source-text of the doctrine.

**Enlil's role in the pantheon:** Enlil is *the* king of the gods in the older Sumerian pantheon (later partially displaced by Marduk in the Babylonian re-organisation that produces [[phase-1-008-enuma-elish|Enuma Elish]]). Enlil controls the *me* of kingship — the divine ordinances of rule. **No Sumerian king is legitimate without Enlil's blessing**, and Enlil's blessing is administered at the Ekur. The political function of the temple is therefore **kingship-ratification** — the architectural staging of the divine charter of rule.

## MASSIVE WIN — Nippur as the textual fountainhead of Sumerian literature

Nippur is one of the most consequential single sites in the history of literature: **the Old Babylonian scribal schools at Nippur (~1800 BCE) produced the canonical written versions of most of the Sumerian literary corpus that survives today**.

This includes:

- **[[phase-1-015-sumerian-king-list|The Sumerian King List]]** — the foundational document of Sumerian historiography.
- **[[phase-1-001-kesh-temple-hymn|The Kesh Temple Hymn]] and the broader Sumerian temple-hymn corpus** — the genre that names and praises every major Sumerian temple.
- **The Sumerian Gilgamesh poems** — the precursors of [[phase-1-004-gilgamesh-old-babylonian|the Akkadian Epic of Gilgamesh]].
- **[[phase-1-017-descent-of-inanna|Descent of Inanna]] and the broader Inanna-Dumuzi cycle**.
- **The lament-for-cities genre** — including [[phase-1-022-lament-for-ur|the Lament for Ur]].

These texts were produced **in the scribal quarter adjacent to the Ekur**, by scribes who were also temple personnel. **Sumerian literature is, in its preserved form, a Nippur production**.

When the Old Babylonian Sumerian school dies in the 17th c. BCE (the language becomes a learned-religious language only, not vernacular), the texts they preserved enter the Akkadian and later Mesopotamian tradition. From there: into the Hebrew imagination via captivity-period contact with Babylonian learning (the Flood narrative, the genealogies, the cosmological architecture), and ultimately into the cultural inheritance of the West.

**Every flood narrative, every list of antediluvian patriarchs, every god-king-pact theology in the broader West Asian tradition has, somewhere in its background, the Nippur scribal schools writing and preserving Sumerian texts in the shadow of the Ekur.**
