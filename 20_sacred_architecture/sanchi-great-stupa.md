# Great Stupa at Sanchi

## What it is

The **Great Stupa at Sanchi** (Stupa No. 1) is the **earliest surviving monumental Buddhist building in India** and the **type-specimen of the classical stupa form**. Built originally by the Mauryan emperor **[[ashoka|Ashoka]]** (~260 BCE) and enlarged by successive Shunga and Satavahana dynasties (~150 BCE – 50 CE), it is the architectural-and-iconographic anchor for the early-Buddhist tradition.

It is what the **Buddhist stupa-form is**: a solid hemispherical dome (*anda*, "the egg") raised on a low cylindrical drum, capped by a square railing (*harmika*) and a tiered umbrella (*chattravali*), encircled by a stone railing (*vedika*) with four monumental gateways (*toranas*) at the cardinal directions. Every later stupa in the world — Borobudur, Bodhnath, the Shwedagon, the Chinese pagoda's earliest forms — descends architecturally from this type.

## The architectural form

- **The Dome (*anda*)** — solid brick-and-rubble hemispherical mound, ~36 m in diameter at the base, ~16 m tall. The interior contains relics (in Sanchi's case the relics of [[sariputta|Sariputta]] and [[mahamoggallana|Mahamoggallana]], the Buddha's two principal disciples; recovered from the site by Alexander Cunningham 1851 and now restored at Sanchi after a long detour through the Victoria and Albert Museum). Solid: there is no interior space; the stupa is a built mound, not a building one enters.
- **The Railing (*vedika*)** — stone railing (~3 m tall) surrounding the dome, with four gates oriented to the cardinal directions. The railing is carved with **lotus medallions** at top and bottom and with abundant narrative reliefs.
- **The Four Toranas** — monumental stone gateways at the four cardinal directions, each ~10 m tall, carved with **jataka tales** (Buddha's past lives), narrative scenes from the Buddha's life, and abundant iconographic detail. These are the principal sculptural achievement of the site and the **earliest extensive surviving Buddhist narrative-relief programme**.
- **The Harmika** — square stone railing atop the dome, marking the symbolic location of the relic-chamber within (though the relics at Sanchi are deposited within the dome's mass, not at the harmika).
- **The Chattravali** — three-tiered stone umbrella atop the harmika, symbolising the cosmic axis. (Sanchi's surviving chattravali is partially restored.)

The stupa's external surface was originally **plastered white** and probably **painted** — Sanchi as we see it today, with the brown-and-tan stone exposed, is a post-plaster condition. The visual effect at construction would have been a brilliant white hemispherical dome rising above the green Madhya Pradesh landscape, visible across the Vidisha plain.

## The toranas — the iconographic programme

The four monumental gateways (added ~50 BCE – 50 CE under the Satavahana dynasty) are the **principal sculptural document of early-Indian Buddhist iconography**. The reliefs are remarkable for several reasons:

- **Pre-anthropomorphic-Buddha period.** The Sanchi reliefs date to the era *before* Buddhist art began depicting the Buddha in human form. The Buddha's **presence in narrative scenes is signified by aniconic symbols** — the empty throne, the Bodhi tree, the wheel of dharma, the footprints, the parasol. The viewer is invited to read the Buddha into spaces where he is **architecturally absent**. This is one of the most theologically loaded **iconographic absences** in religious art.
- **Jataka tales** — narrative panels of the Buddha's previous lives (the Vessantara Jataka, the Shaddanta Jataka, others) on the toranas' architraves.
- **Life-of-the-Buddha scenes** — the great miracles (Sravasti), the descent from Tavatimsa, the First Sermon, the relic-distribution after the parinirvana.
- **The Conversion of the Kashyapa brothers** scene (East Torana) is one of the most-studied narrative panels in Indian art.
- **Yakshas and yakshis** (nature spirits) on the gateway pillars — including the celebrated [[salabhanjika-yakshi|Salabhanjika Yakshi]] of the East Torana (a tree-spirit grasping a tree branch; the iconographic ancestor of the *tribhanga* female pose that runs through subsequent Indian sculpture).

## Ashoka's stupas — the foundation

The Buddhist textual tradition (the *Asokavadana*, ~200 CE) records that [[ashoka|Ashoka]] commissioned **84,000 stupas** across his empire — a symbolic number meaning "many beyond counting." Modern scholarship (Strong 1983; Thapar 1997) treats this as **iconographic exaggeration** with a **real underlying core**: Ashoka did commission widespread stupa-building, of which a handful (Sanchi, Bharhut — now mostly transferred to the Indian Museum Kolkata, the Vaishali stupa, and the original cores of various other monuments) survive in identifiable form.

Sanchi is the **most architecturally complete surviving Ashokan-era Buddhist monument**, and the most accessible single site for studying the architectural origin-conditions of the stupa form.

## The afterlife — abandonment and rediscovery

Sanchi was an active Buddhist monastic centre through the **3rd century CE** (the toranas' iconographic programme reflects this period's continued patronage) and remained occupied through the **Gupta period** (4th–6th c.) with some additional construction. Buddhism in India gradually declined from the 8th c. onwards; Sanchi was **abandoned by ~1100 CE**.

The site was **rediscovered by British officer-archaeologist Alexander Cunningham in 1851** during the Indian survey expeditions. Initial excavations damaged the stupa (some treasure-hunters' shafts were sunk through the dome to search for relics) before [[john-marshall|John Marshall]] (Director-General of the Archaeological Survey of India 1902–1928) led the principal early-20th-c. restoration that consolidated the site to roughly its current form.
