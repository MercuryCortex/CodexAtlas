# Maison Carrée (Nîmes)

## What it is

The **Maison Carrée** ("the Square House" — *quadrate* = rectangular in 17th-c. French usage) at Nîmes (Roman *Nemausus*), in southern France, is **the most architecturally complete surviving Roman temple in the world**. Built ~16 BCE – 2 CE as an imperial-cult temple dedicated to **Gaius and Lucius Caesar** (Augustus's two grandsons and designated heirs), it stands today essentially in its original form: complete cella, complete pseudoperipteral colonnade, complete entablature, complete pediment, complete frontal staircase.

In a discipline (Roman temple architecture) where most evidence comes from ruined buildings whose original form must be reconstructed from foundations and fragments, the Maison Carrée is the **principal three-dimensional reference object** — the building that lets us see what an Augustan-period Roman temple actually looked like, in essentially complete form, more than two thousand years later.

The temple is paired in importance with the [[temple-of-bacchus-baalbek|Temple of Bacchus at Baalbek]] (Antonine, ~150 CE) as the **two best-preserved major Roman temples in the empire**, west and east. Together they bracket roughly the central two centuries of Roman imperial temple architecture's existence.

## History

**Construction context:**

The temple was built in the early Augustan period as part of the **broader Augustan-Agrippan provincial architectural programme** in Gaul. Augustus's general and son-in-law **Marcus Vipsanius Agrippa** served as governor of Gaul; the architectural programme that gives Nîmes its **monumental Augustan urban infrastructure** — the city walls (Augustan), the **Pont du Gard aqueduct** (Augustan), the amphitheatre (later, but in the same Augustan-derived architectural language), the **Tour Magne** (originally a Gaulish tower, Augustan-rebuilt), and the **Maison Carrée** — dates to this period.

The temple's date is fixed by its dedication: it was dedicated to **Gaius Caesar** (born 20 BCE, died 4 CE) and **Lucius Caesar** (born 17 BCE, died 2 CE), Augustus's grandsons by his daughter Julia and Agrippa. They were adopted by Augustus and designated *principes iuventutis*, "leaders of the youth," signalling them as imperial heirs. The temple was dedicated to them, likely in the period 2 BCE – 4 CE (during their lifetimes, when their imperial destiny was politically affirmed). The dedication inscription, **lost as a physical object but reconstructed by the 18th-c. epigrapher Jean-François Séguier** from the pattern of holes for bronze letter-pins still visible on the temple's frieze, reads:

> **C·CAESARI·AVGVSTI·F·COS / L·CAESARI·AVGVSTI·F / COS·DESIGNATO / PRINCIPIBVS·IVVENTVTIS**

— "To Gaius Caesar, son of Augustus, consul; to Lucius Caesar, son of Augustus, consul-designate; leaders of the youth."

The fact that the inscription **survives** only in the pattern of pin-holes on the building, and that an 18th-century epigrapher could **reconstruct the original text from those pin-holes**, is itself one of the more remarkable archaeological-epigraphic accomplishments of European antiquarianism — Séguier's 1758 reconstruction is the basis for our knowledge of the dedication.

**The deaths of Gaius and Lucius — and the imperial-succession irony:**

Both princes died young (Lucius in 2 CE, Gaius in 4 CE), upending Augustus's succession plans and forcing the eventual succession of his stepson Tiberius. **The Maison Carrée was therefore dedicated to two princes who never inherited.** It is one of the most poignant Augustan-imperial-cult buildings: an architectural enactment of a succession-plan that the future falsified.

**Late antiquity, medieval, and modern history:**

- **4th–5th c. CE** — Pagan cult progressively ceases.
- **~6th–7th c. CE** — The building becomes a Christian church, dedicated to **Saint Stephen** (and known variously under different patrons over the medieval centuries).
- **Various medieval uses** — at different periods the building serves as a council chamber, a private house, a stable, an archive, a monastery, an art gallery.
- **1670** — Formally consecrated as the church of the Augustine community.
- **1789 onward** — Revolutionary period: the building is requisitioned for various civic uses.
- **1816** — Restored to architectural-historical use; becomes a museum.
- **18th–19th c.** — Intense European architectural interest. Thomas Jefferson visits in 1786 (calling it "one of the most beautiful monuments of architecture left us by antiquity") and uses it as the model for the **Virginia State Capitol** at Richmond (1785–1788), which transmits the Maison Carrée's plan into the American neoclassical tradition.
- **2023** — Inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage list.

## Architectural form

The Maison Carrée is the **type-specimen of the Augustan-Roman pseudoperipteral temple**.

**Plan and dimensions:**

- **Pseudoperipteral hexastyle** — six columns across the front, with the side columns engaged in (attached to) the cella walls rather than freestanding. This is **the principal Roman variant of the Greek peripteral temple form**: the side and rear columns become wall-attached engaged columns, freeing up the cella's interior space while preserving the visual rhythm of the colonnade.
- **Frontal staircase** with no rear stairs — characteristic **Italic-Roman directional temple** form.
- **High podium** (~2.85 m above the surrounding pavement).
- **Plan dimensions**: 26.4 × 13.5 m at platform level.
- **Column height**: ~9.0 m, Corinthian order.

**The Corinthian capitals** are among the finest Augustan-period Corinthian capitals in the empire, with crisp acanthus-leaf carving in classical proportions.

**The pediment + entablature** survive complete — frontal pediment, frieze, architrave. The frieze's pin-holes preserve the dedication inscription.

**The cella** is complete and accessible.

**Significance of the pseudoperipteral form:**

The pseudoperipteral type is the **principal Roman architectural compromise** between Greek peripteral form (columns surround the cella on all sides as a freestanding colonnade — a structural and visual ideal) and Etruscan-Italic frontal form (a deep porch with columns only at the front — practical and directional). The Roman pseudoperipteral type preserves the **visual** appearance of the Greek peripteral colonnade (by means of engaged side-columns) while preserving the **directional, podium-raised, frontal-staircase** Italic temple's practical-ritual organisation. This synthesis is **characteristic of Roman architectural-cultural creativity**: not pure imitation of Greek precedent, not pure preservation of Italic tradition, but **active synthesis** producing forms unique to the Roman synthesis.

The Maison Carrée is the **canonical surviving example** of this Roman synthesis at its high Augustan moment.

## Theological / cosmological function

**Imperial cult and dynastic succession.** The Maison Carrée's dedication to Gaius and Lucius Caesar makes it one of the principal architectural documents of the **Roman imperial cult's dynastic-succession mechanism**. The imperial cult was not only Augustus-worship; it was a religious-political infrastructure for **ratifying imperial succession through architectural-religious acts**. By dedicating temples to the *principes iuventutis* (the heirs), the imperial cult **architecturally pre-affirmed their future imperial legitimacy**. The temple at Nîmes is a public visible monumental statement that **these are the next emperors, divinely ratified, monumentally housed in stone**.

When Gaius and Lucius both died young, the temple's dedication outlived its political purpose — but the building remained as an architectural document of how the imperial-cult succession mechanism had been organised. **In the architectural record, Augustus's failed succession plan still stands.**

Beyond the specific dynastic-succession function, the temple serves as a **public state-religious focus for the city of Nemausus**:
- The city's principal religious-ceremonial sacrifices were conducted at the temple.
- The temple was integrated into the **annual cycle of imperial-cult observances** that maintained the city's loyalty to Rome.
- The temple was the architectural-religious counterpart to the city's **civic-administrative** infrastructure — the forum, the basilica, the curia.

## MASSIVE WIN — Maison Carrée is the principal surviving Augustan-Roman temple, and through Jefferson the ancestor of American neoclassicism

The Maison Carrée's principal finding is its **architectural-historical role as the most-completely-surviving Augustan Roman temple**, making it the **definitive three-dimensional reference object** for Augustan-period Roman temple architecture. **No other building survives so intact from this period.**

This is consequential because:

**1. It documents what Augustan Rome's religious architecture actually looked like.** The first Pantheon (Agrippan, ~25 BCE) does not survive; the second Pantheon (Hadrianic, ~125 CE) survives but is a rotunda, not a peristyle temple. The Temple of Mars Ultor (Augustan, ~2 BCE) survives only in foundations and reconstructed elevation. **The Maison Carrée is the principal surviving witness for what Augustan-period mainstream Roman temple architecture looked like at full scale.**

**2. It is the source of American neoclassical religious-architectural inspiration.** Thomas Jefferson's 1786 visit to Nîmes, his enthusiastic correspondence about the Maison Carrée, and his use of its plan as the basis for the **Virginia State Capitol** (1785–1788) brought the Maison Carrée's architectural form into the **founding American neoclassical tradition**. The Virginia State Capitol — the first major American building deliberately modelled on a Roman temple — initiates the **American neoclassical tradition** that produces the architectural language of Washington D.C. (the U.S. Capitol, the White House, the Lincoln Memorial, the Jefferson Memorial), of state capitols across the United States, and through them of much of the broader Western neoclassical revival. **The Maison Carrée, through Jefferson, is one of the architectural-historical ancestors of the American state's monumental architecture.**

**3. The pseudoperipteral type-specimen.** As the canonical surviving example of the pseudoperipteral form, the Maison Carrée is the **principal architectural-historical reference** for the Roman synthesis of Greek and Italic temple traditions. Any architectural-historical understanding of the Roman pseudoperipteral type depends on this single building.

**A second finding: the Maison Carrée is the cleanest documentary witness for the architectural-religious mechanism of imperial-cult dynastic succession.** The temple's dedication to Gaius and Lucius Caesar — both of whom died before inheriting — preserves an **architectural enactment of a succession-plan that history falsified**. This is one of the most poignant single Augustan-imperial-cult survivals: the political logic of the imperial cult's succession-affirmation mechanism, frozen in stone at the moment when the affirmation failed. The building is therefore an architectural document not only for what imperial-cult temples looked like, but for what the imperial cult was *doing* — using architecture to manufacture dynastic legitimacy in advance of dynastic facts.
