# Longmen Grottoes

## What it is

**Longmen** (龍門 — "Dragon's Gate") is a monumental **Buddhist cliff-temple complex** on the **Yi River cliffs south of Luoyang**, Henan Province. It contains **~2345 caves and niches, ~100,000 Buddhist statues, ~2800 dedicatory inscriptions, and ~40 pagodas** carved into both the eastern and western cliffs over a continuous ~600-year period of imperial patronage (~493–1127 CE). Together with [[yungang-grottoes|Yungang]] (Datong) and [[mogao-caves-dunhuang|Mogao]] (Dunhuang), it is one of the **three principal monumental Buddhist cave-temple complexes of imperial China**.

Longmen's defining feature is the **colossal Vairocana Buddha of Fengxian Si** (奉先寺) — at **~17.4 m seated**, the largest Buddha at the site and one of the masterpieces of Tang sculpture — commissioned by [[empress-wu-zetian|Empress Wu Zetian]] (China's only female emperor) c. 672–675 CE.

UNESCO World Heritage (2000, File 1003).

## History

- **493 CE** — [[emperor-xiaowen-of-northern-wei|Emperor Xiaowen]] of Northern Wei relocates the dynasty's capital from Datong (in the north) to Luoyang (in the central plains). The imperial Buddhist cave-carving programme migrates with him: from [[yungang-grottoes|Yungang]] (near Datong) to Longmen (near Luoyang).
- **493–534** — Northern Wei caves: Guyang Cave, Binyang Three Caves, Lianhua (Lotus) Cave, Weizi Cave, Yaofang (Medical Prescriptions) Cave. Stylistically continuous with the latest Yungang work — slim attenuated bodhisattva figures, elaborately patterned drapery, archaic smiles.
- **534–589** — Eastern Wei + Northern Qi caves, much less extensive.
- **589–618** — Sui-era continuity.
- **618–907** — **Tang dynasty: the peak of Longmen patronage**. ~60% of the surviving caves are Tang. The Tang style develops the fully volumetric Buddha-body, naturalistic drapery, and dynamic guardian-figure compositions that become the canonical Tang Buddhist sculptural style.
- **~672–675** — **Fengxian Si** (奉先寺) excavated and the colossal **Vairocana Buddha** sculpted, patronised by [[empress-wu-zetian|Wu Zetian]] (then Empress-Dowager regent). The inscription names the donor (Wu) and the principal craftsmen (Wei Jing among others).
- **8th–9th c.** — Tang-period activity wanes after the An Lushan rebellion (755–763); some continuing patronage through Late Tang.
- **907–1127** — Northern Song-era restorations + some additional niches; activity declines.
- **after 1127** — Jin / Mongol period; the site falls into desuetude.
- **1900s** — partial damage from Republican-era looting; significant Buddha heads removed and sold to Western collectors (now scattered through Met, Penn Museum, Nelson-Atkins, and others — a contentious patrimony question).
- **1949–** — Chinese state conservation; UNESCO inscription 2000.

## The principal monuments

### Fengxian Si (奉先寺) — the Tang masterpiece

The **Fengxian Si ensemble** (~672–675 CE), commissioned by Empress Wu, is the largest and most architecturally monumental complex at Longmen. It consists of:

- The **central Vairocana Buddha** — ~17.4 m seated; broad-shouldered, full-faced, with the slight smile and downcast meditative gaze characteristic of high-Tang sculpture. Tradition (not securely established) holds the face was modelled on Wu herself.
- Flanking **disciples Ananda and Kasyapa**.
- Flanking **bodhisattvas** (~12 m).
- Flanking **dvarapala** (guardian-king) and **vajrapani** figures at the outer ends.

The whole ensemble was originally housed under a great wooden timber-frame canopy that has long since perished — the figures now stand in the open air against the cliff.

The dedicatory inscription (the *Fengxian Si Bei*) is one of the most important Tang Buddhist epigraphic monuments — naming Wu's patronage, the architects, the date, and the iconographic programme.

### Binyang Caves (Northern Wei)

Three adjacent caves (Central, Northern, Southern Binyang) commissioned ~500–523 CE by Emperor Xuanwu (r. 499–515) for his deceased father Xiaowen. Each contains a central seated Buddha flanked by disciples and bodhisattvas. The **Central Binyang** is the principal Northern Wei monument at Longmen and shows the mature Northern Wei sculptural style — slender bodies, elongated faces, drapery in linear cascades.

### Guyang Cave (Northern Wei, the earliest)

The oldest cave (begun ~493 CE), containing some of the earliest Buddhist sculpture at Luoyang. The interior walls bear hundreds of small donor-niches with inscriptions — the **Twenty Inscriptions from Guyang** (*Longmen Ershi Pin*) are among the most-studied pieces of Chinese epigraphic-aesthetic record, used by Qing-era epigraphers and modern calligraphic historians as exemplars of Northern Wei stele-style calligraphy.

### Wanfo Cave (Tang, "Ten Thousand Buddhas")

A Tang cave commissioned in 680 by an imperial consort, with **~15,000 small Buddha figures** carved into the walls — a tour-de-force of the small-Buddha repetition motif that characterises medieval Chinese popular Buddhist devotion.

## Architectural form — cliff and cave

Longmen's form is the same Indian-derived **rock-cut Buddhist cave** lineage as Yungang and Mogao, but adapted to the **limestone cliffs of the Yi River** (rather than the conglomerate sandstone of Mogao or the volcanic tuff of Yungang). The harder stone allowed:

- More **high-relief carving** directly into the cliff (rather than separately sculpted clay figures, as at Mogao).
- Larger **monumental Buddhas** (the Fengxian Si Vairocana is the principal example; impossible in Mogao's softer matrix).
- **Crisper sculptural detail** that has survived weathering.

The classic Longmen cave has a square or rectangular plan, **a central seated principal Buddha on the back wall flanked by disciples + bodhisattvas + guardian figures**, with the side walls covered in smaller donor niches and inscriptions.

## The inscription corpus — Longmen Ershi Pin

Longmen's ~2800 dedicatory inscriptions, especially the **Twenty Inscriptions from Guyang Cave** (*Longmen Ershi Pin*), are one of the **principal corpora of medieval Chinese Buddhist epigraphy and calligraphy**. They document:

- The **donors** — emperors, empresses, court eunuchs, monks, lay families.
- The **occasions** — death of a parent, healing of an illness, prayer for safe travel, gratitude after military victory.
- The **iconography** — what was carved, which sutra it illustrates, why.
- The **dates** — anchoring Northern Wei + Tang Buddhist sculptural history to a precise chronology.

Amy McNair's *Donors of Longmen* (2007) is the principal recent study of this epigraphic corpus and the political-religious networks it documents.

## MASSIVE WIN — imperial patronage and the Vairocana of Wu Zetian

Longmen is the **principal architectural monument of imperial patronage of Buddhism** in medieval China. Two pulses dominate:

1. **Northern Wei imperial Buddhism (493–534)** — the non-Han Tuoba-Wei dynasty used monumental Buddhist sculpture as its principal mode of legitimation in the central plains, having migrated south from Datong + Yungang.
2. **Tang imperial Buddhism (618–~750)** — the apex, with Empress Wu Zetian's Fengxian Si Vairocana as the single greatest monument.

The **Vairocana Buddha of Fengxian Si** is the **single greatest sculptural monument of female imperial patronage in Chinese history**. [[empress-wu-zetian|Wu Zetian]] — the only woman ever to rule China as emperor in her own right — used Buddhist patronage extensively to legitimate her unprecedented rule, drawing on the *Mahamegha Sutra*'s prophecy that a future female cakravartin would rule the world. The Fengxian Si commission, with its colossal Vairocana (the universal-illuminator Buddha of the *Avatamsaka Sutra*, the cosmic principle of buddhahood itself), positioned Wu as the chief patron and architectural sponsor of a cosmic-Buddhist universal order. Tradition holds the Buddha's face was modelled on her. Whether or not the resemblance is intentional, the **scale and programme of the commission are unmistakably about asserting cosmic-Buddhist patronage as the basis of Wu's legitimacy**.

This is in turn part of the **East Asian transmission chain**:

> **Indian rock-cut Buddhist cave → Bamiyan / Kucha → [[mogao-caves-dunhuang|Mogao]] → [[yungang-grottoes|Yungang]] → Longmen → Tang temple form → Korea ([[bulguksa-temple-and-seokguram-grotto|Bulguksa]]) → Japan ([[horyuji-temple|Horyuji]], [[todaiji-daibutsuden|Todaiji]])**

Longmen's Tang sculptural style is the **direct precedent** for the Buddhist sculpture of Korean Silla (the Seokguram Grotto's Buddha is its closest Korean cognate) and Japanese Nara-period sculpture (the Todaiji Daibutsu is its closest Japanese cognate, though larger in scale and bronze rather than stone).
