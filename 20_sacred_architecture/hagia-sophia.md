# Hagia Sophia

## What it is

**Hagia Sophia** — "Holy Wisdom" — was the **Great Church of Constantinople** and for nearly a thousand years (537–1453) the architectural and liturgical heart of Eastern Christendom. Built in **532–537 CE** under [[justinian-i|Emperor Justinian I]] by the architect-mathematicians [[isidore-of-miletus|Isidore of Miletus]] and [[anthemius-of-tralles|Anthemius of Tralles]], it was at its consecration **the largest church in the world** and remained the largest cathedral interior in Christendom for nearly 1000 years (until Seville Cathedral, completed 1507).

It has been:
- **Byzantine Orthodox cathedral** (537–1204)
- **Latin Catholic cathedral** (1204–1261, under Latin Empire after the Fourth Crusade)
- **Byzantine Orthodox cathedral again** (1261–1453)
- **Ottoman imperial mosque** (1453–1934 — added minarets, mihrab, calligraphic medallions; the Christian mosaics were plastered over, not destroyed)
- **Republican-Turkish museum** (1934–2020 — Atatürk's deconfessionalisation)
- **Mosque again** (2020–present — re-converted by Erdoğan's government; the mosaics are now covered during prayer but visible at other times)

Five religious-political phases. Few buildings have so many.

## Form — the architectural breakthrough

Hagia Sophia's architectural breakthrough is the **pendentive-supported dome over a rectangular basilical plan**:

- The earlier monumental-dome solution ([[pantheon-rome|Pantheon]], ~125 CE) put a hemispherical dome directly atop a cylindrical drum. This works structurally but limits the floor-plan to a circle.
- Hagia Sophia's architects solved the harder problem of putting a **dome on a square plan**. The solution was the **pendentive** — a triangular curved surface, with its apex at a corner of the square and its upper edge forming a quarter of the dome's base. Four pendentives transfer the circular dome's load onto four piers at the corners of a square bay. **The pendentive is one of the most important architectural inventions in history**, and Hagia Sophia is its first monumental application.

The central dome is **31 m in diameter** (smaller than the [[pantheon-rome|Pantheon]]'s 43.3 m, but on a rectangular plan and over a vastly larger interior space). It rises 55 m above the floor. The dome floats over a 40-window ring at its base — Procopius famously described it as "suspended from heaven by a golden chain."

The dome partially **collapsed in an earthquake in 558**; rebuilt by Isidore the Younger (nephew of Isidore of Miletus) by 562 with a slightly more pointed profile that has been structurally stable since. Additional partial collapses and reinforcements followed in 989 and 1346, with [[mimar-sinan|Sinan]]'s major Ottoman-era structural reinforcement in the 1570s (he added the four external buttresses that visually dominate the exterior).

## Mosaics and iconography

The Christian-era mosaic programme is the **most important surviving Byzantine mosaic ensemble**. Highlights:

- **The Apse Theotokos** (~867 CE) — the Virgin and Child in the apse, post-iconoclast restoration; Patriarch Photios's dedicatory homily survives.
- **The Imperial Door mosaic** (~9th–10th c.) — a Byzantine emperor (probably Leo VI) prostrate before Christ.
- **The Empress Zoe mosaic** (~11th c.) — Christ flanked by Emperor Constantine IX Monomachos and Empress Zoe; the heads of both emperor-figures were re-cut to fit successive husbands.
- **The Deesis mosaic** (~13th c., possibly Latin-period) — Christ flanked by Mary and John the Baptist; one of the most-celebrated Byzantine portraits.
- **Comnenus mosaic** (~12th c.) — Emperor John II Comnenus + Empress Eirene flanking the Theotokos.

During the Ottoman mosque period (1453–1934), the mosaics were **plastered over but not destroyed** — this is why they are recoverable today. The 1934 museum-conversion enabled their restoration; 1990s–2010s restoration is the most extensive single mosaic-conservation project of the modern era.

## The 1054 schism on its sanctuary floor

On **16 July 1054**, the papal legate Cardinal Humbert of Silva Candida placed a bull of excommunication against the Patriarch of Constantinople ([[michael-i-cerularius]]) on the **altar of Hagia Sophia**. Cerularius responded in kind. This is the canonical date of the **Great Schism** ([[event-great-schism-1054]]) between Catholic and Orthodox Christianity, and the **architectural setting of the schism is the sanctuary of Hagia Sophia**.

## The Ottoman conversion (1453)

On **29 May 1453**, [[mehmed-ii|Mehmed II]]'s forces took Constantinople. Mehmed entered the cathedral, ordered a Muslim cleric to lead Friday prayer the next day, and the building became the **imperial mosque of the Ottoman Empire**. Successive sultans added:

- **Four minarets** (one Conquest-era; three Ottoman-classical period, including by Sinan).
- **Mihrab** in the apse, slightly off-axis from the Christian-era central axis (to face Mecca).
- **Minbar** (pulpit).
- **Eight enormous calligraphic medallions** (1849, Kazasker Mustafa İzzet Efendi) bearing the names of God, Muhammad, the four Rightly-Guided Caliphs, and Hasan and Husayn — the largest Islamic calligraphic disks ever produced (~7.5 m diameter, gold on green).

The Ottoman conversion did **not** destroy the Byzantine architectural and mosaic programme. It **plastered over** the Christian images (Islamic figurative-image prohibition) and overlaid the Christian-Byzantine programme with an Islamic-Ottoman one. This is why the building today contains **both layers**, fully visible.

## The afterlife — Sinan and Ottoman classical mosque

When [[mimar-sinan|Mimar Sinan]] (~1490–1588) designed the great imperial Ottoman mosques — Süleymaniye (1557), Selimiye (1575), and his earlier and later works — he was in **direct architectural dialogue with Hagia Sophia**. Sinan's mosques are not copies; they are **solutions** to the architectural problem Hagia Sophia poses (large central dome flanked by half-domes over rectangular nave) using Ottoman classical structural and material vocabulary.

The Ottoman classical mosque form (Sinan and afterward) is, recognisably, the great **Islamic re-engineering** of the Byzantine architectural vocabulary embodied in Hagia Sophia. It is one of the densest cross-tradition architectural-transmissions in religious history: Byzantine Christian dome-and-half-dome → Ottoman Islamic dome-and-half-dome.

## The 2020 reconversion

In **July 2020**, the Turkish Council of State annulled the 1934 conversion to museum, and the Erdoğan government re-opened the building as a mosque. The mosaics are now **covered during the five daily prayers and Friday congregational prayer** but are visible at other times. The reconversion was contested internationally (Russian Orthodox Patriarch, UNESCO, Greek and Cypriot governments) and is in itself one of the most consequential single religious-architectural status-changes of the 21st century.
