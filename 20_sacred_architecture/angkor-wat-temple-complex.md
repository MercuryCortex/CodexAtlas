# Angkor Wat Temple Complex

## What it is

**Angkor Wat** ("the City-Temple" in Khmer) is the **largest religious building in the world by area** — the principal monument of the [[event-angkor-wat-construction-c-1113-1150|Angkor Wat construction project]] under [[suryavarman-ii|Suryavarman II]] (r. 1113–c. 1150) of the [[khmer-empire|Khmer Empire]]. The complex covers ~162 hectares within a moated enclosure ~1.5 × 1.3 km, with a central temple rising in three concentric galleries crowned by a quincunx of five towers — an architectural model of [[mount-meru|Mount Meru]] surrounded by the cosmic ocean.

Originally built as a **Vaishnava Hindu temple** dedicated to [[vishnu]], Angkor Wat was progressively reconfigured as a **Buddhist site** from the late 13th c. onwards. It is the **only major Angkor monument never to have been entirely abandoned** — a continuous religious site for ~900 years. Currently a Theravada Buddhist temple.

It is the principal monument of UNESCO's Angkor inscription (1992, File 668).

## History

**~802 CE** — Jayavarman II's foundational establishment of the Khmer state cult of *kamratĕṅ jagat ta rāja* (the lord-of-the-universe-who-is-king) at Mahendraparvata. The Khmer royal religion is initially Shaiva.

**~889–910** — Yasovarman I founds Yasodharapura at the site of modern Angkor. Subsequent Khmer kings build a series of state temples (Bakheng, Pre Rup, East Mebon, etc.) at the site.

**1113–c. 1150** — **Reign of Suryavarman II**. Angkor Wat is built as Suryavarman II's state temple. Uniquely among major Angkor monuments, the temple is dedicated to **Vishnu** (not Shiva) and is oriented **west** (not east, the standard direction).

**~1150 CE** — Suryavarman II dies; his posthumous name is **Paramaviṣṇuloka** ("[he who has gone to] the supreme world of Vishnu"), supporting the reading of Angkor Wat as simultaneously a state temple and the king's funerary monument.

**1177** — A Cham (proto-Vietnamese) raiding force sacks Angkor. The Khmer state is briefly in disorder.

**~1181–1218** — **Jayavarman VII** reconquers Angkor and definitively shifts the Khmer state cult to **Mahayana Buddhism**. His foundations (Bayon, Ta Prohm, Preah Khan, Neak Pean, etc.) are predominantly Buddhist. Angkor Wat is *not* destroyed but is progressively re-functioned for Buddhist use during the 13th–14th centuries.

**~14th–15th c.** — Khmer state shifts from Mahayana to **Theravada Buddhism** under Sinhalese Buddhist influence. Angkor Wat's sanctums and galleries are progressively re-dedicated to Theravada use; standing Buddha images (many later defaced) are installed in the four cardinal galleries.

**1431** — Sack of Angkor by the Ayutthaya Siamese. The Khmer capital relocates to Phnom Penh; the broader Angkor temple-network is largely abandoned. **Angkor Wat is the major exception** — it continues to function as a Theravada Buddhist site through the abandonment of the surrounding city.

**16th–19th c.** — Angkor Wat remains active. Portuguese (1586) and Japanese (1632, Ukondaiyu Kazufusa's pilgrimage diagram) visitors leave records.

**1860** — French naturalist [[henri-mouhot|Henri Mouhot]] visits Angkor; his posthumously published account (1864) brings Angkor to European attention.

**1907** — The École française d'Extrême-Orient (EFEO) begins systematic study, conservation, and partial restoration of the Angkor complex.

**1992** — UNESCO World Heritage inscription. Listed simultaneously on the *List of World Heritage in Danger* due to ongoing looting and the political instability of post-Khmer-Rouge Cambodia. Removed from the danger list 2004.

## Architectural form — the cosmic-mountain temple-city

Angkor Wat is conceived as an architectural-cosmological diagram on three concentric scales:

**The outer moat (1.5 × 1.3 km)** — the cosmic ocean encircling the world. The moat is ~200 m wide; only the western causeway (the principal entry) and a smaller eastern bridge cross it.

**The outer enclosure wall** (~1024 × 802 m, ~4.5 km perimeter) — the cosmic boundary; pierced by the western *gopura* (entrance gateway) and corresponding outer pavilions on the other three cardinal directions.

**The central temple** rises in three concentric rectangular galleries:
- **Outer gallery** (~187 × 215 m): one of the most extensive narrative-bas-relief programmes in world art, ~600 m of continuous relief carving. The principal scenes:
  - **West Gallery, north half**: *Battle of Kurukshetra* (Mahabharata)
  - **West Gallery, south half**: *Battle of Lanka* (Ramayana)
  - **South Gallery, west half**: *Procession of Suryavarman II* (the king reviewing his army)
  - **South Gallery, east half**: *Heavens and Hells* (32 hells of Hindu cosmology)
  - **East Gallery, south half**: *Churning of the Ocean of Milk* (the longest single relief, ~50 m; ~88 asuras and ~92 devas churning Mount Mandara on the turtle Kūrma)
  - **East Gallery, north half**: *Battle of Vishnu and the Asuras*
  - **North Gallery, east half**: *Battle of Krishna and Bana*
  - **North Gallery, west half**: *Battle of the Devas and Asuras*
- **Middle gallery** (~100 × 115 m): the upper-cloister level, more restricted access in the original Vaishnava programme.
- **Inner sanctuary** (~60 × 60 m): the central tower-quincunx — four corner towers and one central tower, all rising in stepped-pyramidal form. The central tower is ~65 m above ground level. The five towers map the five peaks of Meru.

The temple is built almost entirely of **sandstone**, with laterite for the foundations and concealed structural elements. The principal sandstone source is the Kulen Hills ~50 km north of Angkor; tens of thousands of stones were quarried, dressed, transported (probably by canal raft during the wet season), and assembled in the ~37-year construction window.

## The Vaishnava-Buddhist transition

Angkor Wat is one of the clearest cases in world religious-architectural history of a single monument undergoing a **smooth typological transition** between two distinct religious traditions:

1. **Vaishnava phase (~1113–c. 1180)**: Built and inaugurated as a state temple to Vishnu under Suryavarman II. Bas-relief programme is overwhelmingly Vaishnava (Vishnu's avatars, the Churning of the Ocean of Milk, the Mahabharata + Ramayana cycles).

2. **Mahayana Buddhist phase (~1180–c. 1300)**: Following Jayavarman VII's state-cult reorientation, the monument is re-functioned without major destruction. Mahayana Buddhist iconography is added in subsidiary spaces; the central programme remains intact.

3. **Theravada Buddhist phase (~1300–present)**: Following the broader Khmer turn to Theravada, the monument's principal sanctums are re-dedicated; standing Buddha images are installed; the monument operates continuously as a Theravada site through to the present.

The Vaishnava bas-reliefs remain visible and intact through all phases. **The iconographic programme is reframed rather than destroyed.** This is one of the most striking cases of religious-architectural continuity through a tradition-shift in world history.

## Theological / cosmological function

Angkor Wat is the **architectural body of Mount Meru** in the Vaishnava cosmographic vocabulary:
- **Central tower (axis)** = the peak of Meru, the cosmic axis (*axis mundi*).
- **Four corner towers** = the four secondary peaks of Meru.
- **Three concentric galleries** = the three cosmic continents (Jambudvīpa and the surrounding ring-continents in Vaishnava cosmography).
- **Outer enclosure walls** = the *cakravala*, the iron-mountain rim of the cosmos.
- **Outer moat** = the cosmic ocean.

The temple's **west orientation** is the principal interpretive puzzle. Most Khmer temples face east (the standard direction in Hindu cosmography, the direction of the rising sun and life). Angkor Wat faces west — the direction of the setting sun and the realm of the dead. Two principal readings:
1. **Funerary**: Angkor Wat was simultaneously a state temple and the funerary monument of Suryavarman II. His posthumous name (*Paramaviṣṇuloka*) supports this.
2. **Vaishnava-specific**: Vishnu's traditional direction is west in some textual traditions; Angkor Wat's orientation is theologically appropriate to its specific Vaishnava dedication.

Mainstream Cambodianist scholarship (Coedès, Jacques, Mannikka) treats both readings as compatible: the monument is *both* state temple *and* funerary monument *and* Vaishnava-specifically oriented.

[[eleanor-mannikka|Mannikka]] (1996) argues additionally that the temple's precise measurements encode specific cosmological numbers: the *yuga* calendar (the 432,000-year *mahayuga*), specific solar and lunar alignments, the proportional ratios of the cosmic *kalpa*. The specific numerical claims are contested but the general cosmological intent is universally accepted.

## MASSIVE WIN — Vaishnava → Buddhist architectural continuity

Angkor Wat is the **canonical case in world religious-architectural history** of a major monument undergoing a smooth typological transition between two religious traditions:

> **Vaishnava Hindu state temple (Suryavarman II, ~1113–1150)**
> → **Mahayana Buddhist re-functioning (Jayavarman VII, ~1180s)**
> → **Theravada Buddhist site (Khmer turn, ~14th c. onwards)**
> → **Continuous Theravada use to the present**

The pattern reverses two common stereotypes:
- **The "one tradition, one monument" stereotype**: most historians assume that when a tradition changes, monuments are either destroyed or abandoned. Angkor Wat shows that **the same monument can serve three distinct traditions in sequence** with iconographic reframing rather than physical destruction.
- **The Hindu-Buddhist-rivalry stereotype**: in modern political-religious framings, Hindu and Buddhist traditions are often presented as competing. Angkor Wat shows that the Khmer state — and many SE Asian polities — treated Hindu and Buddhist traditions as part of a single Indic religious continuum, with state cults shifting between them across reigns without monument-destruction.

The architectural-iconographic vocabulary is sufficiently shared between Hindu and Buddhist traditions that the **cosmic-mountain temple form is religiously neutral** at the structural level — what changes is the specific deity at the centre and the specific narrative programme on the walls. The form-as-cosmic-mountain transcends sectarian boundaries.

Cross-tradition cousins in this **shared-form-different-deity** mode:
- The Pantheon, Rome — converted from Roman polytheist temple to Christian church (~609 CE), continuously used since.
- The Hagia Sophia, Istanbul — Christian basilica (537 CE) → mosque (1453) → museum (1934) → mosque (2020).
- The Dome of the Rock, Jerusalem — Islamic shrine on the site of the destroyed Jewish Second Temple, deploying Byzantine-Christian architectural vocabulary.
- **Angkor Wat** — Vaishnava temple → Mahayana Buddhist → Theravada Buddhist, continuous religious use.

What Angkor Wat uniquely offers: **the smoothest typological transition in the cluster**. Where the other examples involved iconoclastic destruction or major iconographic overwriting, Angkor Wat's Hindu bas-reliefs were preserved through the Buddhist re-functioning. The traditions did not war over the form.
