# Izumo Taisha

## What it is

**Izumo Taisha** (出雲大社 — "Great Shrine of Izumo"; in older usage *Izumo Ōyashiro*) is **one of the two principal shrines of [[tradition-shinto|Shintō]]**, paired with [[ise-grand-shrine|Ise]]. It is dedicated to **[[okuninushi|Ōkuninushi-no-Mikoto]]** — the great earth-deity and ancestor of the terrestrial pantheon of Japanese mythology, central figure of the [[phase-8-019-kojiki|*Kojiki*]]'s 'Izumo cycle' and the *kuni-yuzuri* ('transfer of the land') narrative.

The shrine's distinctive architectural form is *taisha-zukuri* (大社造) — the **most ancient Shintō shrine architectural style**, possibly antedating even Ise's *shinmei-zukuri*. Per medieval texts (notably the *Kuchizusami* of 970 CE), the original Izumo Taisha was **48 jō** (~48 m) tall — making it one of the tallest pre-modern wooden buildings on earth, taller than [[todaiji-daibutsuden|Todaiji]]'s 49 m Daibutsuden. Modern archaeology partially confirms this: in 2000, excavation under the shrine precinct uncovered **three massive cedar pillar-bundles** (each 1.35 m in diameter, bound together to make a ~3 m composite column), dated to the early Kamakura period (~13th c.), consistent with a Heian–Kamakura era structure of dramatic height. The present shrine — rebuilt 1744 — is "only" ~24 m tall, but is still by far the **tallest *taisha-zukuri* shrine in Japan**.

Izumo Taisha is one of the few principal Shintō shrines administered **independently** of the *Jinja Honchō* (Association of Shinto Shrines) network — its priesthood is the **Izumo-no-Kuni-no-Miyatsuko** lineage, an institutionally autonomous priestly family unbroken from antiquity (the present incumbent, Senge Takatoshi, is the 84th generation).

## History

- **mythological founding** — per the [[phase-8-019-kojiki|*Kojiki*]], the shrine was founded by **Ōkuninushi himself** as the place of his eternal worship, after he yielded the visible land to the heavenly grandson Ninigi in the *kuni-yuzuri* episode. The actual antiquity of the site is impossible to securely establish but is certainly substantial — pre-Buddhist (i.e. pre-538 CE).
- **659 CE** — the *Nihon Shoki* records construction of "the great shrine of Izumo" under Empress Saimei; this is the earliest textually-attested rebuild.
- **8th c.** — the [[izumo-no-kuni-fudoki|*Izumo no Kuni Fudoki*]] (733 CE) provides the most detailed early account of the shrine and the Izumo theological programme.
- **~10th–13th c.** — multiple rebuilds at what may have been the maximum architectural height (~48 m per medieval texts; if accurate, this would have made Izumo one of the tallest pre-modern wooden buildings on earth).
- **1248, 1346, 1500, 1667** — successive rebuilds at progressively reduced heights.
- **1744** — the present shrine completed under Tokugawa patronage. ~24 m tall — half the legendary medieval height, but still the tallest *taisha-zukuri* shrine extant.
- **1868** — Meiji Restoration; Izumo is reclassified within the state-Shintō framework but the Izumo-no-Kuni-no-Miyatsuko priesthood retains substantial autonomy.
- **2000** — excavation discovers the massive Kamakura-era pillar-bundles, partially confirming the medieval texts on the ~48 m original height.
- **2008–2013** — major restoration of the *Honden* (main sanctuary), the first major restoration in 60 years; the *kami* was ritually moved to a temporary sanctuary during the work.

## The *kuni-yuzuri* — transfer of the land

The mythological foundation of Izumo Taisha is the [[phase-8-019-kojiki|*Kojiki*]]'s *kuni-yuzuri* ("transfer of the land", 国譲り) narrative — one of the most important episodes in classical Japanese mythography.

The story (Kojiki Book 1, sections 33–37):

1. **Amaterasu**, observing from heaven, decides that the central country of Japan (which has been "made" by Ōkuninushi and his fellow earth-deities) should be ruled by her descendants.
2. After failed missions, the heavenly deities send **Takemikazuchi** (the deity of thunder) and **Amenotorifune** to demand Ōkuninushi's submission.
3. Ōkuninushi defers to his sons: **Kotoshironushi** consents to the transfer; **Takeminakata** resists, is defeated by Takemikazuchi (in the prototype-sumo-wrestling-match of Japanese myth), and flees to Suwa.
4. **Ōkuninushi agrees to yield the visible land** to the heavenly grandson Ninigi, **on condition** that:
   - **A great shrine be built for him at Izumo** ("the place where pillars reach to the heavens"), where he will be eternally worshipped.
   - **He retains rule of the invisible realm** (*kakuriyo*) — the unseen world, the realm of death, the spiritual world.
5. **Ninigi descends to earth** with the regalia (the mirror, the sword, the jewel) and his line founds the Japanese imperial dynasty.

The narrative is the **mythological architectural foundation of Izumo Taisha**: the shrine exists because Ōkuninushi demanded it as the price of his yielding. The shrine and the *kuni-yuzuri* narrative are theologically inseparable.

The doctrinal consequence is the **two-pole structure of classical Japanese Shintō**:

| Pole | Realm | Shrine | Deity |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Visible / imperial** | *Utsushiyo* — visible world | [[ise-grand-shrine|Ise]] | [[amaterasu|Amaterasu]] |
| **Invisible / spiritual** | *Kakuriyo* — invisible world | **Izumo Taisha** | [[okuninushi|Ōkuninushi]] |

The two shrines together exhaust the highest level of the Shintō pantheon and ritual hierarchy. **Ise is the imperial-political-visible cult-centre; Izumo is the spiritual-invisible cult-centre**. The architectural division of labour is one of the most articulate cases of **paired-shrine cosmology** in world religion.

## Architectural form — *taisha-zukuri*

The Izumo *Honden* (main sanctuary) is the **principal exemplar of *taisha-zukuri*** (大社造 — "great-shrine style") — the most ancient Shintō shrine architectural vocabulary. Key features:

- **Square plan** (compared to Ise's rectangular *shinmei-zukuri*).
- **Massive single central pillar (*shin-no-mihashira*)** rising the full height inside the building.
- **Raised floor** on stilts (the descent of the building-form from the granary).
- **Gable-end entrance** (compared to Ise's long-side entrance) — the entrance is at the gable-end of the building, beneath the gable peak, requiring a steep flight of stairs to enter (the building's interior floor is well above ground level).
- **Plain unpainted Japanese cypress** (*hinoki*), like Ise.
- **Thatched roof**, with *chigi* (forked rafter-finials) and *katsuogi* (cylindrical roof-ridge logs) as in *shinmei-zukuri* — but the *chigi* at Izumo are **vertically chamfered** (indicating a male deity, by Shintō convention; horizontal chamfer = female deity).

The cult image faces **westward** (toward the Sea of Japan) — unusual for Shintō shrines (most face south). This westward orientation reflects Izumo's geographical position on the Sea-of-Japan coast and the historical role of the western coast as the corridor for cultural and migratory contact with the continent.

## The Kamiarizuki — month of the gods

In the traditional Japanese lunar calendar, the 10th month is called **Kannazuki** (神無月 — "month without gods") **everywhere in Japan except Izumo**, where it is called **Kamiarizuki** (神在月 — "month with gods"). The traditional explanation: in the 10th month, **all the *kami* of Japan travel to Izumo** to hold their annual conference at the Great Shrine, where Ōkuninushi presides. They discuss the affairs of the coming year, especially the matchmaking of new couples (one of Ōkuninushi's principal cultic functions is **deity of marriage**).

The annual **Kamiari Festival** (~mid-November) is one of the principal festivals at Izumo and one of the most distinctive single Shintō calendrical observances. The gods are said to arrive at the Inasa-no-Hama beach (~1 km west of the shrine), be received by Izumo priests, and proceed to the shrine.

## Ōkuninushi as deity of *en-musubi* (marriage)

Ōkuninushi is the **principal Shintō deity of *en-musubi*** (縁結び — "tying of the karmic bonds of relationship") — especially marriage. Izumo Taisha is consequently one of the most popular shrines in Japan for engagement and marriage rituals. The annual conference at the Kamiari Festival is held to involve the divine arrangement of marriages for the coming year.

The cultic role of Ōkuninushi as deity of marriage is connected to his mythological role in the *Kojiki*: he is repeatedly married (variously and dramatically) over the course of the Izumo cycle, ending with multiple wives and the largest divine progeny in the Japanese pantheon.

## MASSIVE WIN — paired-shrine cosmology and the *kuni-yuzuri* architectural articulation

Izumo Taisha and [[ise-grand-shrine|Ise]] form the **most explicit case in world religious architecture of a society's two-pole theological cosmology directly articulated in paired monumental architecture**.

The structure:
- **Two shrines**, both of the highest *jingū / taisha* class.
- **Geographically separated** (Ise in the east-central; Izumo in the western coast).
- **Dedicated to the two principal deities of the two poles of the pantheon** (heavenly/imperial Amaterasu; terrestrial/spiritual Ōkuninushi).
- **Architecturally distinct** (Ise's *shinmei-zukuri* vs Izumo's *taisha-zukuri*) — the architectural differences emphasising the doctrinal differences.
- **Ritually linked** via the *kuni-yuzuri* narrative that founds both.

Closest world parallels:
- **Mecca and Medina** in Islam (two sacred cities of the *Haramayn*) — but the doctrinal structure is different (both are linked to Muhammad; there is no two-pole pantheon).
- **Jerusalem and Mecca** in early Islamic Qibla-orientation — but these are sequential rather than paired.
- **Hindu temple-pairings** of Shiva and Vishnu at major pilgrimage sites — but the doctrinal-cosmological articulation is less explicit.

The Ise-Izumo pairing is **architecturally and doctrinally one of the cleanest cases of paired-shrine cosmology in world religion**. The two shrines + their architectural-doctrinal distinctness + the *kuni-yuzuri* myth that articulates the relationship constitute a complete **architectural-mythological diagram of the Shintō pantheon-structure**.

It is one of the principal candidates for the project's motif anchor [[theme-paired-shrine-cosmology]].
