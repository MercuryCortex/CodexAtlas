# Tikal Temple I — Temple of the Great Jaguar

## What it is

**Temple I**, the *Temple of the Great Jaguar*, is the iconic 47-metre stepped funerary pyramid that anchors the east side of the Great Plaza at [[tikal]], the largest Classic-period Maya city. Built c. 700–740 CE as the funerary monument for [[jasaw-chan-kawiil-i]] (r. 682–734 CE), the 26th ruler of the Tikal dynasty, it is one of the most-studied royal-tomb pyramids in the Americas and the archetypal image of Classic Maya monumental sacred architecture.

The pyramid is paired across the plaza with [[tikal-temple-ii]] (the slightly smaller, slightly earlier pyramid dedicated to Jasaw's queen Lady Lachan Une' Mo'). Together with the surrounding [[north-acropolis-tikal]] necropolis, the Great Plaza forms one of the densest Classic-period royal ritual landscapes in the Maya area.

## History

### The political moment of construction

Jasaw Chan K'awiil I came to power in 682 CE at the end of a long Tikal eclipse during which the rival city of [[calakmul]] had defeated and humiliated Tikal's previous king (Nuun Ujol Chaak) and severed its alliances. In **695 CE Jasaw defeated Calakmul's king Yich'aak K'ahk' ('Fiery Claw')** ([[event-defeat-of-calakmul-695-ce]]), reversing Tikal's fortunes and inaugurating the Late-Classic Tikal renaissance.

Temple I is the architectural commemoration of that renaissance. The visible monumental restatement of Tikal's divine-king-as-cosmic-axis ideology after 130 years of subordination. Schele & Miller (1986) read the whole Great Plaza programme as Jasaw's deliberate restaging of the Tikal cosmos.

### Construction sequence

The pyramid was likely begun late in Jasaw's reign (after 720 CE) and completed by his son [[yikin-chan-kawiil]] following Jasaw's death in 734 CE. Burial 116 — Jasaw's tomb chamber — sits at the heart of the pyramid in a vaulted stone chamber beneath the structure, accessed only during the funerary deposition and then permanently sealed. The pyramid is built *over* the tomb (unlike Egyptian pyramids, where shafts retained access).

### Modern excavation

The tomb was excavated in **1962** by [[william-coe]] of the University of Pennsylvania's Tikal Project (1956–1969 — one of the largest archaeological projects in the Americas, surveying the entire city). Coe found Jasaw lying on a stone bench, accompanied by jadeite mosaic regalia, carved bones, painted ceramics, and the famous **incised bone carvings** depicting the canoe of the dead bearing the deceased king and supernatural paddlers — one of the most iconic Classic Maya texts on royal death and cosmic transit.

## Architectural form

### Dimensions and structure

- **Height:** 47 m (the temple proper plus the *roof-comb* superstructure that rises an additional ~7 m above the building).
- **Base:** ~50 m square at the lowest terrace.
- **Nine terraces** stepping upward to the temple shrine at the summit.
- **Single steep stairway** on the west face (facing the plaza), with a slope of ~70° — the climb is treacherous; modern visitors are now barred from ascending.
- **Three-room shrine** at the summit (the *Templo*), reached through corbel-vaulted doorways.
- **Roof comb** — a hollow openwork superstructure above the shrine that originally bore an enormous sculpted and painted image of Jasaw in royal regalia, now mostly destroyed but partially reconstructable from photographs.

The **nine terraces** are widely read as a deliberate architectural reference to the **nine levels of Xibalba** — the Maya underworld of the *[[phase-8-001-popol-vuh|Popol Vuh]]* — through which the deceased ruler must journey (Schele & Miller 1986; Freidel, Schele & Parker 1993). The pyramid is *literally* the Maya underworld in architectural form; the deceased king is buried at its base, and the temple at the summit is his apotheotic emergence as a transfigured ancestor-god.

### The lintels

Above the three doorways of the summit shrine are three carved **sapodilla-wood lintels** (Lintels 2, 3, and 4 of Temple I). **Lintel 3** is the most-studied: it depicts the enthroned Jasaw beneath a protective *jaguar way* (his animal-companion spirit), the supernatural framing of his rulership immortalized. The lintel inscription names Jasaw, names the dedication date (734 CE), and is the foundational text for the dating of the temple. The lintels were removed in the 19th century — Lintel 3 is now in the Museum für Völkerkunde, Basel.

### Decoration

The original pyramid was painted **bright red** — traces of red stucco survive in protected interior chambers. The lintels, roof comb, and decorative friezes were polychrome (red, blue, white). The blinding-white-stone Maya ruins of postcards are post-collapse, post-vegetation, post-erosion. In Jasaw's day, Temple I would have been a **massive red mountain against the green jungle** — visible for miles, lit by the sun, the architectural reification of K'inich solar-kingship.

## Theological / cosmological function

### The pyramid as Witz (sacred mountain)

The Maya word for both *mountain* and *temple-pyramid* is ***witz***. The Classic Maya read their stepped pyramids as architectural mountains — specifically, the cosmic mountains where the gods reside, where the maize god is reborn from the underworld, and where the deified ancestor-kings dwell after death. The carved *Witz Monsters* (zoomorphic mountain-personifications) on the corners of many Maya buildings make the equivalence visually explicit.

Temple I is the **Witz of Jasaw Chan K'awiil** — the cosmic mountain that *is* his transfigured body, his divine kingship encoded in stone. The funerary chamber at its base is the **cave** within the mountain (Maya cosmology: caves = openings to the underworld; mountains contain caves; temples = built mountains containing the cave of royal entombment).

### Divine kingship and the Maize God

Maya kings identified themselves with the **Maize God** (*Hun Hunahpu* in the *Popol Vuh* tradition, *Hun Ixim* in Classic-period inscriptions), who is killed in the underworld and reborn through the agricultural cycle. The royal funeral and the maize cycle are theologically identical: the king dies, descends into the underworld-pyramid, and is reborn as a transfigured ancestor. The pyramid is the architectural device that enacts this transformation — the [[theme-pyramid-as-resurrection-machine|pyramid-as-resurrection-machine]] motif in its Maya instantiation.

### Astronomical alignment

Temple I and Temple II form an **east-west pair across the Great Plaza** — Aveni (2001) reads this as a sunrise-sunset axis. The east-facing Temple I receives the morning sun on its painted west face; Temple II faces it from the west. The orientation may also encode equinox or solstice alignments (debate continues), but the **east-west solar polarity** at minimum is consensus.

Tikal's later **Twin-Pyramid Complexes** (Group 4D-6, 4E-4, etc., built every k'atun = 20-year cycle) are explicitly astronomical-calendrical monuments. Temple I is the dynastic-funerary ancestor of that tradition.

## MASSIVE WIN — independent emergence of the royal-tomb-pyramid

**Cross-civilization parallel:** Tikal Temple I (c. 730 CE) is a Maya structural exact-analogue of Khufu's Great Pyramid (c. 2580 BCE) — **across 3300 years and without any possible Old-World contact**.

Both are:
- **Monumental stepped or geometric pyramids** built as funerary monuments for a single named ruler.
- **Erected over the tomb** of that ruler — the body is the sacred axis of the structure.
- **Cosmic-mountain analogues** — Witz in Maya, Akhet in Egyptian (both: 'horizon-mountain where the sun rises and the king ascends').
- **Solar-kingship encoded in architecture** — the king is identified with the sun-god (K'inich-Ajaw in Maya; Re in Egyptian), and the pyramid is the device of his daily/eternal resurrection.
- **Nine-fold underworld architecture** — Maya: nine terraces = nine Xibalba levels. Egyptian: twelve hours of the night-Duat, but with nine-fold cosmological substructures in the Coffin Texts and Book of Caverns.

The independent emergence proves the **cosmic-mountain-as-royal-resurrection-device** is not a culturally-specific motif but a **convergent solution** to the problem of architecturalising divine kingship + mortality + cosmic order. Same problem, same answer, no contact. Parallel emergence of the same type-class with [[ziggurat-of-ur]] (Sumerian, ~2100 BCE), [[borobudur-stupa-mandala]] (Buddhist, ~800 CE), and [[pyramid-of-the-sun-teotihuacan]] (Mesoamerican, ~200 CE).

**Mesoamerican-internal lineage:** Temple I is part of a 1500-year unbroken Mesoamerican stepped-pyramid tradition stretching from the Olmec ([[la-venta-pyramid]], ~1000 BCE) through Teotihuacan (Pyramid of the Sun, ~200 CE) and Classic Maya (Tikal, Palenque, Calakmul, Copán) to the late Postclassic Aztec ([[templo-mayor-tenochtitlan]], ~1500 CE). One continuous architectural-theological tradition.
