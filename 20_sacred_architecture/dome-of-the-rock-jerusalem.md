# Dome of the Rock

## What it is

The **Dome of the Rock** (Arabic *Qubbat al-Sakhra*) is the **earliest surviving monumental Islamic building** and one of the most ideologically loaded structures ever built. Commissioned by the Umayyad caliph **[[abd-al-malik]]** and completed in **691–692 CE**, it stands at the centre of the Temple Mount / Haram al-Sharif platform in Jerusalem, directly above the **Foundation Stone** (*al-Sakhra*, *Even haShetiyya*) over which the Holy of Holies of [[solomons-temple-first|Solomon's First Temple]] traditionally stood.

It is, simultaneously:

- The earliest surviving monumental Islamic building (and the earliest surviving Umayyad monument).
- A direct architectural response in the visual language of late-antique sacred-architecture to the [[church-of-the-holy-sepulchre-jerusalem|Anastasis Rotunda]] across the city.
- The headquarters and namesake of the [[tradition-knights-templar|Knights Templar]] (1119–1187), who called it *Templum Domini*.
- The site Islamic tradition associates with the **Miʿraj** — the Prophet's Night Journey ascent from "the farthest mosque" (al-masjid al-aqṣā, Qurʾān 17:1).
- Over the **Foundation Stone** sacred to Judaism — the cosmic axis from which creation began and over which the Ark of the Covenant once stood.

Three Abrahamic traditions, one rock, one octagonal building, one mosaic-encrusted dome.

## Form — what it is architecturally

The Dome is a **double-shelled timber dome** of ~20 m diameter set on a high circular drum, supported by an inner ring of 4 piers and 12 columns, surrounded by an octagonal ambulatory with a second ring of 8 piers and 16 columns, all wrapped in an octagonal outer wall faced (post-1545) in the iconic Iznik tiles commissioned by Suleiman the Magnificent.

Inside, the unbroken inscription band running around the octagon is the **earliest extant Qurʾanic monumental inscription** — 240 m of Kufic text, including the earliest dated public quotation of verses on the singleness of God, with passages that some scholars (Robinson 2005; Necipoğlu 2008) read as polemically directed against Christian Trinitarian theology, given the building's visual dialogue with the Anastasis next door.

## The Anastasis dialogue

Grabar's foundational 1959 essay (*Ars Orientalis* 3) established the still-canonical reading: the Dome is the **first Islamic statement in stone** that Islam owns the Holy City's vertical skyline. Its dome **matches the height** of the dome of the [[church-of-the-holy-sepulchre-jerusalem|Holy Sepulchre]] (then the city's tallest sacred building) and **its form responds to it**: a rotunda + ambulatory + dome over a sacred locus.

This is the same architectural type as the Anastasis — but applied to a different sacred locus (the Foundation Stone, not the empty tomb), under a different theological vocabulary (Qurʾanic inscriptions, not Christian iconography), in the same architectural genre (centralised plan + dome).

Krautheimer's *Iconography of Mediaeval Architecture* (1942) frames this kind of architectural quotation: medieval / late-antique builders did not aim at literal geometric copy. They built **iconographic relatives** — same type, same locus-class, polemical or homage relationship. The Dome of the Rock is the most consequential iconographic-relative in religious-architectural history.

## Templar occupation (1119–1187)

After the [[event-first-crusade-1095-1099|First Crusade]] took Jerusalem in 1099, the Crusaders converted the Dome of the Rock to a Christian church, placed a cross on the dome, and renamed it **Templum Domini** ("Temple of the Lord"). The al-Aqsa Mosque adjacent became **Templum Salomonis** ("Temple of Solomon").

In **1119**, [[hugues-de-payens]] and his eight companions founded the [[tradition-knights-templar|Order of the Poor Fellow-Soldiers of Christ and of the Temple of Solomon]] — quartered in al-Aqsa (Templum Salomonis), with the Dome of the Rock as their devotional centre. The name **"Templars"** is literally *Knights of the Temple* — referring to this complex, here, on this rock.

When the Templars returned to Europe and built churches, they built **rotundas** — modeled on what they had occupied. The [[convent-of-christ-tomar|Charola at Tomar]] (~1160) and [[temple-church-london|Temple Church London]] (1185) are the foremost survivors of a Europe-wide Templar round-church programme.

The transmission line:

> [[church-of-the-holy-sepulchre-jerusalem|Anastasis Rotunda]] (Jerusalem, 4th c.)
> → **Dome of the Rock** (Jerusalem, 691 — Umayyad-Islamic interpretation of the rotunda type)
> → Templar Europe (12th c. — Templars build rotundas in Europe modeled on what they had occupied)

The Templars are the relay. The Dome is the catalyst.

## Restoration to Islamic use

In **1187**, after [[saladin]]'s recapture of Jerusalem, the cross was removed, the bell-tower struck, and Islamic ritual restored. Suleiman the Magnificent rebuilt the exterior with the iconic Iznik tile programme in 1545. Subsequent major restorations were carried out under the Hashemite Jordanian custodianship (1958–1964; major gold-leaf re-gilding 1993).

## MASSIVE WIN — the three-tradition palimpsest

The Foundation Stone is the densest sacred-multivalent locus on earth:

| Tradition | The rock means |
|---|---|
| **[[tradition-judaism|Judaism]]** | *Even haShetiyya* — the foundation-stone of creation. Talmud (Yoma 54b): "The world was created from Zion." Possibly the site of the Akedah ([[abraham-patriarch]]'s binding of [[isaac-patriarch]]). The Holy of Holies of [[solomons-temple-first|Solomon's First Temple]] stood here; the Ark of the Covenant rested on it. |
| **[[tradition-islam|Islam]]** | The rock from which [[muhammad-prophet]] ascended to heaven on the Miʿraj (Qurʾān 17:1; *al-masjid al-aqṣā*, "the farthest mosque"). |
| **[[tradition-medieval-christianity|Christianity]]** (Crusader-era) | *Templum Domini* — taken to be the site of Christ's Presentation, sometimes conflated with Solomon's Temple. The Templars built atop their occupation here. |

One stone, three traditions, one building, twelve centuries of contested custody. No other site in religious-architectural history packs this density of cross-tradition reading.
