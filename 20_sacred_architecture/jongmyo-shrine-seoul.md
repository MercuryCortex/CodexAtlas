# Jongmyo Shrine (Seoul)

## What it is

**Jongmyo** (宗廟 — "Ancestral Shrine") is the **royal Confucian ancestral shrine of the [[tradition-joseon-confucianism|Joseon dynasty]] of Korea** (1392–1910). It is the **best-preserved royal ancestral shrine in East Asia** and one of the most rigorously executed examples of state-Confucian ritual architecture anywhere. Built **1395** by **[[king-taejo-of-joseon|King Taejo]]**, the dynastic founder, as one of his first acts of state-founding; expanded through the Joseon centuries; rebuilt **1608** after burning during the Imjin War (Japanese invasion 1592–1598); standing in its present form since the 1726 and 1834 expansions.

The shrine houses the **spirit tablets (*sinju*)** of the **27 Joseon kings and 28 queens** in two principal halls:

- **Jeongjeon** (정전, 正殿 — "Main Hall") — the central hall containing the spirit tablets of the kings considered to have made the principal contributions to the dynasty (19 spirit tablets). The Jeongjeon at ~109 m is the **longest single wooden building in East Asia**.
- **Yeongnyeongjeon** (영녕전, 永寧殿 — "Hall of Eternal Peace") — the secondary hall containing the spirit tablets of kings and royal relatives whose contributions were considered lesser (34 spirit tablets).

The shrine continues to function: descendants of the Yi royal family (the Jeonju Yi clan) perform the **Jongmyo Jerye** (royal ancestral rite) annually on the first Sunday of May, with the accompanying **Jongmyo Jeryeak** (royal ancestral ritual music) — both inscribed on the **UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage** list (2001, in the original cohort).

UNESCO World Heritage (1995, File 738).

## History

- **1392 CE** — Yi Songgye founds the **Joseon dynasty**, ending Goryeo. He is enthroned as King Taejo.
- **1394 CE** — capital relocated from Gaegyeong (modern Kaesong) to Hanyang (modern Seoul).
- **1395 CE** — Jongmyo founded along with **Gyeongbokgung Palace** and the Sajikdan altar, as part of the integrated Joseon Confucian-state-architectural programme of the new capital. The location of Jongmyo to the east of the palace + Sajikdan to the west reflects the classical Chinese principle of *zuomu youshe* (左廟右社 — "ancestral shrine to the left, earth altar to the right") from the *[[zhou-li|Zhou Li]]*.
- **1408 CE** — King Taejo dies; his spirit tablet enshrined at Jongmyo. This establishes the institutional practice that defines the shrine's function for the next 500 years.
- **1421 CE** — Yeongnyeongjeon (secondary hall) added under King Sejong.
- **1546** — major Jeongjeon expansion.
- **1592–1598** — the **Imjin War**. Toyotomi Hideyoshi's invasion devastates Joseon Korea; Jongmyo is **burned to the ground** in 1592. The spirit tablets are evacuated and preserved.
- **1608** — Jongmyo rebuilt under King Seonjo following the war. This is the present principal structure.
- **1726, 1834** — further expansions of the Jeongjeon to its present 19-chamber length.
- **1910** — Japanese annexation of Korea ends the Joseon dynasty. Some Japanese plans called for demolition or repurposing of Jongmyo; resistance kept the shrine intact. The Jongmyo Jerye rite was suppressed during the colonial period (1910–1945).
- **1945** — liberation. Jongmyo Jerye eventually resumed under the auspices of the Jeonju Yi Royal Family Association.
- **1995** — UNESCO World Heritage inscription.
- **2001** — Jongmyo Jerye + Jeryeak inscribed on the UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage list (original cohort).

## Architectural form — Confucian severity

Jongmyo's defining architectural feature is **architectural severity in the service of ritual function**. The shrine's design is governed by Confucian doctrine of **the ancestral cult as the centre of state-religious life**, with **maximum dignity through restraint** rather than monumental ornamentation. The principal architectural features:

- **The Jeongjeon's extreme horizontality** — at ~109 m long × ~17 m deep, the building is one of the most extreme **horizontal proportions** of any pre-modern monumental structure. The form is generated by the **need to enshrine spirit tablets of successive kings side-by-side in chronological order**, with each king's tablet in its own chamber.
- **A continuous 19-bay front colonnade** with a single hipped-and-gabled roof spanning the entire length — emphasising the **continuity of the dynastic line** as architectural form.
- **Stone-paved entrance court** (*woldae*) in front of the Jeongjeon, where the ritual is performed — extending ~70 m before the shrine.
- **Restrained dark-wood + plain plaster + grey-tile roof** — no painted vermilion, no polychromy, no gold-leaf gilding. The most architecturally restrained palette of any major East Asian ritual building.
- **Strict N–S cardinal orientation** — the Jeongjeon faces south, the worshippers approaching from the south, the spirit tablets oriented to face south (the position of the ruler in classical-Chinese cosmological doctrine).
- **No iconographic images** — only spirit tablets. The Confucian ancestral cult prohibits iconographic representation; the cult focus is the abstract spirit-tablet bearing the deceased's posthumous name.

The aesthetic effect is **monumental simplicity** — the structural geometry of severe horizontal volumes generates an architectural-religious impact more austere than the ornate Buddhist temples ([[bulguksa-temple-and-seokguram-grotto|Bulguksa]]) or palace complexes ([[gyeongbokgung-palace|Gyeongbokgung]]) of the same period.

The grounds are landscaped as **a wooded park** with stone-paved processional paths leading from the southern entrance through groves of pines to the central hall — emphasising the contemplative pre-approach to the shrine.

## The Jongmyo Jerye — the rite itself

The **Jongmyo Jerye** (royal ancestral rite) is the **principal Confucian ancestral ritual of the Joseon dynasty**. It was traditionally performed **five times a year** (the four seasonal sacrifices + the year-end winter sacrifice); under modern administration it is performed once annually on the first Sunday of May. The rite involves:

- **Procession** of the royal descendants and ritual officials from the southern gate to the Jeongjeon, in chronological-precedence order, in court costume.
- **Setting out of the offering tables** — ritual food, wine, silk offerings before each spirit tablet.
- **Eulogy reading** addressed to each enshrined king individually.
- **Triple libation** (initial, second, terminal).
- **Ritual music (*Jeryeak*)** — performed throughout, with the *jongmyo jeryeak* orchestra of ~40 musicians playing classical Korean court instruments. The music itself is one of the oldest continuously-performed pieces of court music in the world, dating in its present form to the early Joseon period.
- **Ritual dance (*Ilmu*)** — performed by 64 dancers (8 × 8 rows for the highest civil-and-military ranks) in two complementary sequences (*munmu* civil-dance + *mumu* martial-dance).
- **Concluding rituals** — burning of offerings, departure.

The full rite takes ~5 hours. It is one of the **most architecturally + ritually integrated programmes** of any surviving East Asian state-religious tradition.

## MASSIVE WIN — Confucian ancestral cult architecture in its most articulate form

Jongmyo is the **most architecturally rigorous surviving example of state-Confucian ancestral-cult architecture anywhere**.

The Confucian ancestral cult — the worship of the royal ancestors as the foundational ritual of the state, with the king as the principal performer — is the **central state-religious programme of Confucian-Sinophone East Asia**, from the *Zhou Li*'s articulation in the 1st millennium BCE through the Han-Tang-Ming-Qing dynastic period in China and through Joseon Korea + Vietnamese imperial Vietnam.

But:

- **In China**, the equivalent Ming-Qing Imperial Ancestral Temple (*Taimiao*) survives, but in much-altered form as part of the Beijing Forbidden City complex. The Confucian ritual lapsed in 1912 with the fall of the Qing and was never restored.
- **In Vietnam**, the Nguyễn-era royal ancestral architecture at Huế survives but at smaller scale and with significant 20th-c. wartime damage.
- **In Joseon Korea**, the institutional discipline was the most thoroughgoing — the Joseon dynasty was the **deepest Confucian state in East Asian history**, with no rival in the rigour of its commitment to Confucian-orthodox ritual programmes — and the architectural and ritual programme at Jongmyo was correspondingly the most articulately executed.

Jongmyo therefore preserves the state-Confucian ancestral-cult tradition in **its most fully articulated architectural-and-ritual form**. The combination of:

1. **The largest single wooden building in East Asia** (Jeongjeon, ~109 m).
2. **The most architecturally restrained monumental ritual building in East Asia** (no ornament, no polychromy, no iconographic images).
3. **The annual rite still performed** (Jongmyo Jerye) — by the descendants of the royal family, in the original setting.
4. **The associated ritual music and dance still performed** (Jongmyo Jeryeak + Ilmu) — UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage.
5. **The full ritual code preserved** (*[[gukjo-orye-ui|Gukjo Orye Ui]]*) — the 1474 ritual codification specifying every detail.

makes Jongmyo the **single most complete surviving Confucian ancestral-cult complex**. It is the architectural and ritual cognate of the Chinese [[temple-of-heaven-beijing|Temple of Heaven]] — Heaven's altar there, royal-ancestral shrine here — and together they document the state-Confucian ritual programme at its East-Asian peak.

## State-Confucian ritual architecture cluster

Jongmyo, the [[temple-of-heaven-beijing|Temple of Heaven]] (Beijing), and the [[temple-of-confucius-qufu|Temple of Confucius at Qufu]] (China) form a **tight regional cluster of state-Confucian ritual architecture** across the Ming-Qing-Joseon period:

| Site | Date | Country | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| Confucius Temple, Qufu | continuous since c. -478, monumental Ming-Qing form | China | Ancestral temple of Confucius himself + family lineage |
| Temple of Heaven, Beijing | 1406–1420 (Ming) | China | Emperor's altar to Heaven |
| **Jongmyo, Seoul** | **1395 (Joseon)** | **Korea** | **Royal ancestral shrine** |

All three: cardinal-aligned, axial-symmetric, restrained-palette (relative to Buddhist temples of their respective traditions), site of annual state ritual. Together they document state-Confucian architectural practice in early-modern East Asia at the level of the highest state-cult.
