# Girsu — Eninnu, Temple of Ningirsu

## What it is

The **Eninnu** ("House of Fifty") at **Girsu** (modern Tello, in southern Iraq) is the temple of the warrior-thunder-god **Ningirsu**, tutelary deity of the Sumerian state of **Lagash**. The surviving form of the Eninnu was built ~2125 BCE by Lagash's ruler **[[gudea-of-lagash|Gudea]]** — and the temple is documented in extraordinary detail by **[[phase-1-018-gudea-cylinders|Gudea's two great clay cylinders]]**, the longest Sumerian literary composition known and the most explicit ancient temple-building text in any tradition.

The Eninnu is therefore **the best-documented temple-build of antiquity** — we know Gudea's dream-vision in which Ningirsu commissioned the work, his ritual preparations, his sourcing of foreign timber and stone, his year-by-year construction sequence, the architectural plan, the votive deposits, the dedication ceremony. This is **not a temple known from rubble alone**; this is a temple whose theological-architectural rationale was preserved by its own builder in 1400 lines of detailed Sumerian poetic prose.

For the comparative study of ancient sacred architecture, the Eninnu is the **canonical illustration of how a Sumerian temple was theologically, ritually, and architecturally constructed** — and, through Hurowitz's foundational comparative work (1992), it is **the single closest pre-Israelite parallel to the biblical narrative of Solomon's Temple**.

## History

**Tello** — the modern site — was identified in 1877 by **Ernest de Sarzec**, French vice-consul at Basra, who began the excavation that became **the first major Sumerian dig in archaeology**, predating the Uruk and Ur campaigns by decades. The Sarzec excavations (1877–1900) recovered the Gudea statues (now distributed across the Louvre, the British Museum, and Iraqi museums) and the Gudea Cylinders — the founding evidence-base for the very concept of a *Sumerian* civilisation distinct from Akkadian/Babylonian. **Tello is where Sumerian archaeology begins.**

**The Lagash state** flourished as a regional power 2500–2100 BCE in the marshland between Ur and the modern site of An-Nasiriyah. Its three major settlements were:
- **Lagash itself** (al-Hiba — administrative capital)
- **Girsu** (Tello — religious capital, where the Eninnu is)
- **Nina-Sirara** (Surghul — secondary religious centre)

Lagash had earlier ziggurat-building rulers — **Ur-Nanshe** (c. 2500 BCE), **Eannatum** (the *Stele of the Vultures* king, c. 2400 BCE), and the broader Early Dynastic IIIb dynasty. The Eninnu existed in earlier forms throughout this period.

**Gudea's reign** (c. 2144–2124 BCE) falls in the **interregnum between the collapse of the Akkadian empire and the rise of Ur-Nammu's Third Dynasty of Ur** (~2112 BCE). With Akkad gone and Ur not yet ascendant, the small but pious Lagash state under Gudea is briefly the dominant cultural-religious patron of southern Mesopotamia. **Gudea uses this window to build the Eninnu in its definitive form**, and to commission an extraordinary body of statues and inscriptions documenting the build.

**Subsequent history:** After Ur-Nammu's empire absorbs Lagash, the Eninnu continues as a regional cult-centre but loses its earlier political prominence. The site declines through the second millennium and is gradually abandoned in the first.

**Modern excavation:** the French at Tello continue intermittently (André Parrot leads in the mid-20th c.); the **British Museum's current project under Sébastien Rey** (2016–present) is recovering the temple's outer enclosure (including the **Gudea Mirror Pond**), confirming many details of the Gudea Cylinders' description.

## Architectural form

The Eninnu was a **temple complex** rather than a single building, comprising:

- **A central terraced platform** (the *gigunu*) — stepped, in the Sumerian temple-platform tradition descended from Eridu and Uruk.
- **A high temple on the summit** with a tripartite cella plan — the cult-image of Ningirsu in the inner sanctum.
- **The "twin courtyards"** described in Gudea Cylinder B — the *ki-lugal-bana* and *ki-amgalla* — flanking spaces for the divine assemblies.
- **Subsidiary chapels** for the Lagash divine family — Bau (Ningirsu's consort), Igalim and Shulshagana (their sons), and the seven "daughters of Bau."
- **The Gudea Mirror Pond** — a large reflective water-pool, recently confirmed in excavation, used in the Sumerian *pirig-mush* river-mirror ritual.
- **Outer enclosure walls** of mud-brick.

The materials, per the Cylinders, were:
- Cedar timber from the **Amanus mountains** in northern Syria (the same cedar source that, 1100 years later, supplies [[solomons-temple-first|Solomon's Temple]] via [[hiram-of-tyre-king|Hiram of Tyre]]).
- Diorite from the **Magan** stone-quarries (modern Oman) — the stone of the famous Gudea statues.
- Copper, gold, silver, gypsum, asphalt — all imported from the periphery of the Sumerian world.

The Eninnu is therefore the architectural focus of an entire **west-Asian raw-material network**, sourcing timber and stone from distances of 1000 km and more. The temple-build is the city-state's pretext for organising long-distance trade.

## Theological / cosmological function

Ningirsu is the **warrior-thunder-god**, often identified or syncretised with the larger figure of **Ninurta** (Enlil's son at Nippur). The Eninnu is therefore a **martial temple** — Ningirsu is the patron of the Lagash state's military success, and the temple's iconography (the famous *Stele of the Vultures* under Eannatum, the *Imdugud* lion-headed-eagle on Ningirsu's standard) is heavy with warfare and victory imagery.

The Gudea Cylinders' theological framing of the build is canonical:

- **The god commissions the build via dream-vision.** Gudea sleeps in the temple's predecessor and dreams that Ningirsu appears and reveals the temple's plan. The plan is divinely *given*, not humanly *designed*.
- **The king's role is to execute, not invent.** Gudea repeatedly insists in the Cylinders that he is Ningirsu's servant, not the temple's true builder; the temple **already exists** in the divine mind, and Gudea is its material executor.
- **The build is itself a ritual.** Each architectural action (laying the foundation deposits, sourcing materials, raising the walls, fixing the doors, installing the cult-image) is accompanied by elaborate prayer, sacrifice, and purification ritual. **The temple is consecrated *in the building of it*, not only at the dedication ceremony.**
- **The completed temple is the god's literal dwelling.** Ningirsu enters the finished temple in the closing tablets of Cylinder B, and the building is **alive** with his presence.

This theological grammar — **divine commission → ritualised construction → divine indwelling** — is exactly the grammar of [[solomons-temple-first|1 Kings 5–8]], a thousand years later in the Levant.

## MASSIVE WIN — Eninnu and Solomon's Temple share a single West Asian literary genre

The single most important comparative-architectural insight produced by twentieth-century Assyriology is **Victor Hurowitz's** 1992 demonstration that **the biblical narrative of Solomon building the Temple (1 Kings 5–8 / 2 Chronicles 2–7) belongs to the same West Asian literary genre as the Gudea Cylinders**.

The parallel structure is striking:

| Stage | Gudea Cylinders (~2125 BCE) | 1 Kings 5–8 (~10th c. BCE / written ~6th c. BCE) |
|---|---|---|
| **Divine commission via dream** | Gudea dreams Ningirsu in the predecessor-temple; Ningirsu reveals the plan | Solomon dreams YHWH at Gibeon (1 Kings 3:5–14); David's plans are divinely given (1 Chr 28:19) |
| **Foreign-cedar sourcing** | Cedar from the Amanus mountains | Cedar from Lebanon via [[hiram-of-tyre-king|Hiram of Tyre]] |
| **Foreign-craftsman engagement** | Foreign artisans for specialised work | [[hiram-abi|Hiram-Abi]] from Tyre |
| **Ritualised foundation** | Foundation-deposits, purification of the site, elaborate ritual | Sacrifices, prayers, sanctification rituals |
| **Architectural specifics with cosmological meaning** | Tripartite cella; orientation; cosmic-mountain symbolism | Tripartite plan (*ulam-hekhal-devir*); cherubim; cosmic symbolism |
| **Dedication ceremony with kingly speech** | Gudea's dedication speech in Cylinder B | Solomon's dedication prayer (1 Kings 8) |
| **Divine indwelling** | Ningirsu enters and occupies the temple | YHWH's glory fills the Temple (1 Kings 8:10–11) |

**Either the Israelite narrative is drawing from a shared West Asian temple-building genre of which Gudea is the principal extant ancestor, or both descend from a common deeper source.** Hurowitz's monograph documents this point-by-point with sufficient evidential weight that the parallel is now **mainstream consensus in biblical studies**.

The key finding here is that **the Solomon narrative — one of the foundational texts of Western religious identity — is not a unique theological invention but the Israelite instance of an architectural-theological literary genre that is well-attested across the West Asian temple-building tradition for over a thousand years before Solomon**. The Eninnu is the genre's best-preserved earlier exemplar.

This places the Solomon temple-build narrative in the same family as Gudea's Eninnu, Tukulti-Ninurta's Assur temple, Esarhaddon's restoration of Babylon, Nebuchadnezzar II's Etemenanki rebuild, and the broader Mesopotamian-and-Levantine **royal-builder-of-the-god's-house** tradition. The shared West Asian theology of kingship-as-temple-building is one of the most consequential cross-tradition continuities in the religious-architectural record.
