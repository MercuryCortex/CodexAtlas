# Çatalhöyük

## What it is

**Çatalhöyük** is a Neolithic proto-urban settlement on the Konya Plain in south-central Turkey, occupied **c. 7400–6200 BCE**. At its peak it housed 3,000–8,000 inhabitants in densely-packed mud-brick houses, with no streets between them — residents moved across rooftops and entered houses through ceiling-hatches. UNESCO World Heritage 2012 (File 1405).

For the lens of sacred architecture, Çatalhöyük is the **canonical example of fully-integrated sacred-and-domestic space**. There is no separate temple. There is no plaza with a public altar. The sacred is **woven into every house** — through wall paintings, bull bucrania (cattle skulls with horns mounted on walls), figurines in storage bins, and burials beneath floors. The hypothesis Çatalhöyük makes available is: **before there was a temple-as-building-type, sacred space was the house itself**.

## The architectural-and-ritual repertoire

Çatalhöyük houses are remarkably standardised across the site's ~1200 years of occupation:

- **Single-room mud-brick houses**, ~25 m² floor area, accessed from the roof via a ladder through a ceiling-hatch.
- **Hearth and oven** near the south wall (below the entrance — so smoke rises out through the same hatch).
- **Raised platforms** along the north and east walls — used for sleeping, working, and **burying the dead**. Çatalhöyük inhabitants buried their dead **beneath the platforms of their own houses**, often after a period of excarnation (defleshing).
- **Bull bucrania** mounted on the walls — bovid skulls plastered into the wall, sometimes with the horns embedded, painted red, and in some cases part of larger wall reliefs.
- **Wall paintings** in many houses: red ochre + black pigment, depicting hunting scenes (deer-and-leopard, vultures-and-headless-humans), geometric panels, and what Mellaart called "**the world's oldest landscape painting**" — a possible volcanic-eruption scene with Hasan Dağ in the background (still debated — Meece 2006).
- **Figurines** — small clay female figurines, the most famous being the **"Seated Woman of Çatalhöyük"** (a heavyset seated female flanked by two felines, found in a grain bin — Mellaart 1967). The "Mother Goddess" reading (Gimbutas) is built on this figurine class.

## The Mother-Goddess hypothesis and its critique

[[james-mellaart|Mellaart]] (1967) read Çatalhöyük as the proto-evidence of a **unified Neolithic Mother-Goddess religion** stretching across the Near East and Old Europe. [[marija-gimbutas|Gimbutas]] (1974, 1989) elaborated the thesis into a **Goddess-religion-pre-Indo-European** archaeology.

Post-2000 critique (Meskell, Meece, Hodder) substantially complicates the hypothesis:

- The figurine assemblage at Çatalhöyük is **not predominantly female**. Many figurines are zoomorphic, abstract, or ambiguous. The female ones are a subset.
- Figurine **find contexts** are predominantly **storage bins and refuse middens**, not altar-like settings — arguing against a cultic-veneration reading.
- The "Seated Woman with Felines" is a striking object but **statistically anomalous** within the figurine corpus.
- The pan-Neolithic-Mother-Goddess hypothesis (Gimbutas) does not survive contact with the Çatalhöyük data once that data is read at scale rather than through the iconic individual figurines.

The contemporary scholarly position (Hodder 2014): figurines + bull bucrania + house burials + wall paintings are **real and abundant evidence of a coherent symbolic system**. But the system was **diverse, locally inflected, and not reducible to "Mother-Goddess worship"**. The vault carries both layers — the empirical assemblage is real T1 data; the Mother-Goddess interpretive frame is T1 within the Gimbutas tradition but T2-contested in the wider field.

## Why the lens entry matters

Çatalhöyük matters for sacred architecture because it **shows the form sacred space can take when temples-as-separate-buildings don't yet exist**. Every house at Çatalhöyük performs the functions a later civilisation would distribute across **temple + house + cemetery + workshop**. The compression is the point.

When sacred architecture differentiates — when the first temples emerge (Ubaid Mesopotamia ~5500 BCE, Predynastic Egypt ~4000 BCE) — they emerge **out of this kind of integrated matrix**. Çatalhöyük is the deep ancestor of the typological move "the sacred has its own building."
