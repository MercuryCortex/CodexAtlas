# Eridu — Temple of Enki

## What it is

**Eridu** is the southernmost Sumerian city, and at its centre is the **oldest known temple sequence in Mesopotamia** — eighteen successive temples to the god [[enki-ea|Enki]] (Akkadian *Ea*), built one atop the rubble of the last across approximately **3,500 years of continuous cult**, from the Ubaid 1/Hajji Muhammad horizon (~5400 BCE) to the Third Dynasty of Ur and beyond (~2000 BCE).

This is the **architectural deep root of every Mesopotamian temple platform**, and through that the deep root of the entire ziggurat-and-stepped-pyramid family of monumental sacred structures across the Old World.

In the [[phase-1-015-sumerian-king-list|Sumerian King List]], Eridu is the **first city of the world**: "When kingship was lowered from heaven, kingship was in Eridu." In Mesopotamian theology, Eridu is where civilisation begins — and the architectural evidence at Tell Abu Shahrain (the modern name for the site) is the closest thing we have to the empirical residue of that theology.

## History

Tell Abu Shahrain rises out of the alluvial plain ~24 km SW of [[ziggurat-of-ur|Ur]], in what was, during the period of Eridu's prosperity, the shore zone of a brackish marsh feeding the Persian Gulf — the perfect setting for a god of *freshwater rising into salty marsh* (the Apsu, Enki's cosmological domain). The site was identified in the 19th c. and dug repeatedly:

- **1854** — John George Taylor (British vice-consul at Basra) trenches the mound, recovers Ur-Nammu inscribed bricks; identifies the ziggurat-top building.
- **1918–1919** — Reginald Campbell Thompson + H. R. Hall (British Museum) — limited soundings during the British post-WWI archaeological window in Iraq.
- **1946–1949** — Fuad Safar, Mohammed Ali Mustafa, and Seton Lloyd (Iraqi-led campaign): the **definitive excavation**, recovering the 18-temple stratigraphy and publishing the report in 1981. Safar et al. established that the Eridu mound is not a single building but a **stratified architectural palimpsest** of successive temples occupying the same sacred ground for three millennia.

The 18-temple sequence (numbered XVIII at the base, I at the top) maps onto the standard Mesopotamian chronology:

| Stratum | Period | Date | Description |
|---|---|---|---|
| XVIII–XVII | Ubaid 1 / Hajji Muhammad | ~5400–5000 BCE | Small mud-brick chapel, ~3×4 m; the earliest temple |
| XVI–XII | Ubaid 2 | ~5000–4500 BCE | Expansion; tripartite plan emerges |
| XI–VII | Ubaid 3 | ~4500–4000 BCE | Formal tripartite cella with offerings table + niche-shrine; rises on accumulated platform |
| VI–IV | Ubaid 4 / Late Ubaid | ~4000–3800 BCE | Major buildings; the platform now elevates the temple significantly |
| III–II | Uruk period | ~3800–3100 BCE | Stratigraphic gap; reuse and partial abandonment |
| I (Ur-Nammu ziggurat) | Third Dynasty of Ur | ~2100 BCE | Ur-Nammu builds a formal ziggurat (the **E-unir**) atop the millennial sacred mound, paralleling [[ziggurat-of-ur]] |

After Ur-Nammu's ziggurat, Eridu declines but never quite dies — the cult of Enki is referenced in 1st-millennium ritual texts; the city is named in Neo-Babylonian theology as a foundational site, paired with Babylon (and the [[etemenanki]] ziggurat is rhetorically described as the cosmic centre that re-encompasses Eridu's prerogative).

## Architectural form

The Eridu temple sequence is **the empirical record of how the Mesopotamian temple platform came into being** — not designed top-down but accreted bottom-up.

**The mechanism:**
- Each generation's temple is built of mud-brick on a low platform.
- When that temple ages, its walls are knocked down to platform-height and a new, slightly larger temple is built on the levelled core.
- Over centuries, the platform rises — first by a metre, then by several, then by ten — purely as an artefact of cumulative reconstruction-on-sacred-ground.
- By the Late Ubaid (~4000 BCE), the platform is monumental in itself; the temple atop it is visibly elevated above the city.

**The tripartite cella plan** is already in place by Ubaid 3 (Temple VII, ~4500 BCE):
- Long central nave aligned to a cardinal direction.
- Offering-table or altar near one end.
- Cult-niche / image-niche at the opposite end.
- Side-rooms / antechambers flanking the cella.

This **tripartite plan persists for 3,000 years** and is the architectural ancestor of the Mesopotamian *cella* type — and, far downstream, of the tripartite cella ([[solomons-temple-first|Ulam-Hekhal-Devir]]) at Solomon's Temple, of [[ain-dara-temple-syria|Ain Dara]], and of the broader Levantine longhouse-temple family.

**Ur-Nammu's ziggurat (Temple I, ~2100 BCE)** is a formal stepped ziggurat in the [[ziggurat-of-ur]] mode — Ur-Nammu's construction programme erected near-identical ziggurats at Ur, Uruk, Nippur, and Eridu, the **first time a unified architectural type was imposed across the Sumerian heartland**. At Eridu, the ziggurat was named the **E-unir** ("House of the High Platform") — by Ur-Nammu's time, the *idea* of the high platform has been fully theologised.

## Theological / cosmological function

Eridu is the **city of [[enki-ea|Enki]]** (Akkadian *Ea*) — the god of:

- **The Apsu** (Sumerian *abzu*) — the cosmic freshwater ocean that lies *beneath* the earth and from which springs, marshes, and the Tigris-Euphrates rise. Enki's temple is named the **E-abzu**, "House of the Apsu."
- **Wisdom, magic, craft, and the *me*** — the sacred ordinances of civilisation. In Sumerian myth (esp. [[inanna-sumerian|Inanna]]'s descent and the *me*-stealing tale), Eridu is where the *me* — the divine archetypes of cities, kingships, crafts, sexual practices, musical instruments — are kept in Enki's keeping.
- **Creation and the human craft** — Enki's clay-shaping of humanity in [[phase-1-006-atrahasis|Atrahasis]] and the broader Mesopotamian creation tradition; the parallel of the Genesis [[phase-1-016-eridu-genesis-flood|Eridu-Genesis flood]] tradition is named after this city.

The Apsu cosmology shapes the temple's spatial programme: Enki's temple is positioned **at the boundary between dry land and marsh** — the architectural enactment of Enki's position at the boundary between solid earth and freshwater abyss. Some Eridu shrines preserve installations interpretable as **freshwater basins / ritual water-bringing features** that materialise the Apsu inside the building.

Crucially, in the Sumerian king-list ideology, **the temple precedes the city**. Eridu is not a city that built a temple; Eridu is a temple that grew a city around it. This inversion is the Mesopotamian theological self-understanding of *what civilisation is*: civilisation is what happens around a place where the gods have descended.

## MASSIVE WIN — Eridu is the deep root of the entire stepped-monumental-sacred-architecture family

The continuous architectural lineage looks like this:

> **Eridu Temple XVII (~5400 BCE)** — first small mud-brick shrine on charged ground
> → **Eridu Temple VII (~4500 BCE)** — formal tripartite cella on an accumulated platform
> → **[[uruk-white-temple-eanna|Uruk White Temple]] (~3200 BCE)** — the platform-supported-temple now monumentally explicit (12 m platform)
> → **Sumerian Early Dynastic temple platforms (~2900–2334 BCE)** — increasingly tall and formal
> → **[[ziggurat-of-ur|Ur-Nammu's Ziggurat of Ur]] (~2100 BCE)** — the standardised multi-terrace + monumental staircase form
> → **[[etemenanki|Etemenanki]] / Babylon (~600 BCE)** — seven tiers, the [[tower-of-babel]] referent
> → polemic inversion into Hebrew scripture → into Christian and Islamic theological imagination

**This is ~5,000 years of continuous architectural transmission.** Every later stepped sacred structure in the Old World — every ziggurat, every step-pyramid that emerged in conversation with Mesopotamian forms, the architectural language behind the Babel narrative — sits at the end of this chain. The chain begins at Eridu.

And the chain has a **distinct origin-event**: it began with a small Ubaid-period community deciding that **this particular patch of ground was where the freshwater god lived**, and that when their shrine fell into ruin, the next shrine had to be built **on the same patch of ground, on top of the previous one**. The architectural principle of *accumulation as sanctification* — that the depth of accumulated sacred history is itself a quality of the place — is invented at Eridu in the 6th millennium BCE.

This principle (sacred-ground-accreted-by-reconstruction) is exactly the principle later instantiated at the Temple Mount in Jerusalem ([[solomons-temple-first]] → [[solomons-temple-second]] → Herod's expansion → [[dome-of-the-rock-jerusalem|Dome of the Rock]]), at the Kaaba (multiple rebuildings on the same patch), at the Pantheon (Agrippa → Hadrian → Christian church), and at countless other long-continuity sacred sites. **Eridu is where the principle is first attested architecturally.**
