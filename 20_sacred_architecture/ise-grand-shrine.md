# Ise Grand Shrine (Ise Jingū)

## What it is

**Ise Grand Shrine** (伊勢神宮, *Ise Jingū*, also *Jingū* without qualifier — the name needs no prefix in classical Japanese ritual usage) is the **principal shrine of Shintō** and the **single most important religious site in Japan**. It is dedicated to **[[amaterasu|Amaterasu Ōmikami]]** — the sun-goddess, principal *kami* of the Japanese pantheon, and **ancestress of the imperial line**.

The shrine consists of two principal precincts ~6 km apart:

- **Inner Shrine (*Naikū*, 内宮)** — dedicated to [[amaterasu|Amaterasu]]; houses (in its inner sanctum) one of the **Three Sacred Treasures** (*Sanshu no Jingi*) of the Japanese imperial regalia, the **Yata-no-Kagami** sacred mirror.
- **Outer Shrine (*Gekū*, 外宮)** — dedicated to [[toyouke|Toyouke Ōmikami]], the deity of grain and sustenance, enshrined here in 478 CE per the *Engishiki* — said to have been brought to Ise to "serve Amaterasu's food."

Together with **125 ancillary shrines** in the surrounding region, the complex is administered as a single integrated ritual site.

The shrine's defining feature — and the trait that makes it unique among major world religious buildings — is the **shikinen sengū** (式年遷宮): the **entire shrine is dismantled and rebuilt on an adjacent site every 20 years**, using fresh cypress timber, joinery construction (no nails), and the inherited tradition of the original 7th-century form. The current cycle (the 62nd in unbroken sequence) was completed in **2013**; the next rebuild is scheduled for **2033**.

## History

- **traditional founding** — per the *Nihon Shoki* (720 CE) and *Kojiki* (712 CE), the site was founded by **Princess [[yamato-hime-no-mikoto|Yamato-hime-no-Mikoto]]**, daughter of the legendary 11th emperor [[suinin-tenno|Suinin Tennō]] (trad. -29 to 70 CE). She is said to have wandered from province to province with Amaterasu's sacred mirror seeking the goddess's preferred resting-place, finally settling at Ise where the deity revealed her desire to dwell.
- **historical founding** — modern archaeology dates the original Ise sanctum to roughly the **3rd–5th centuries CE**. The site predates the **Buddhist arrival in Japan** (~538 CE) and is a key witness to **pre-Buddhist native Japanese religious architecture**.
- **685 CE** — Emperor [[temmu-tenno|Temmu]] formally **establishes the 20-year rebuild institution (*shikinen sengū*)**. The first completed cycle is 690 CE under Empress Jitō.
- **690–present** — **62 completed rebuild cycles in unbroken sequence** (with brief disruptions during the late Sengoku-era 16th c. when civil war interrupted patronage for ~120 years; the cycle was restored 1585).
- **927 CE** — the *[[engishiki|Engishiki]]* Heian ritual code formally codifies the Ise priesthood and the *shikinen sengū* protocol.
- **Kamakura–Muromachi periods** — the Ise priesthood develops the **Watarai-Shintō** school of theology (centred at the Outer Shrine), which articulates a sophisticated philosophical-Shintō doctrine integrating Daoist + Buddhist + native elements.
- **Edo period** — the **Okage Mairi** ("thankful pilgrimage") movement makes Ise the principal pilgrimage destination of common Japanese; ~2 million pilgrims at the 1830 peak.
- **1868–1945** — Meiji Restoration; Ise becomes the **principal architectural monument of state-Shintō (*kokka shintō*)**. The first Meiji emperor's pilgrimage to Ise in 1869 marks the formal re-centring of the imperial-Shintō religious system on the shrine.
- **1945–1947** — postwar disestablishment of state-Shintō; Ise transitions to administration under the *Jinja Honchō* (Association of Shinto Shrines).
- **2013** — 62nd *shikinen sengū* completed.

## Architectural form — *shinmei-zukuri*

The Ise architectural style is *shinmei-zukuri* (神明造) — the **earliest and most distinctively native Japanese sacred-architectural vocabulary**, antedating the arrival of continental Buddhist architecture. Key features:

- **Plain hinoki cypress (*Chamaecyparis obtusa*) wood**, unpainted, untreated, allowed to weather naturally.
- **Raised-floor structure** on stilts (descendant of the Yayoi-era raised-floor granary).
- **Thatched roof of *kaya* miscanthus** with the distinctive **chigi** (forked rafter-finials extending above the roof-ridge) and **katsuogi** (cylindrical roof-ridge logs perpendicular to the ridge).
- **Joinery only — no nails or metal fasteners**.
- **Surrounded by four concentric fenced enclosures** of increasing sanctity, with the inner sanctum (*shōden*) at the centre.
- **A vacant adjacent rebuild site (*kodenchi*)**, the same size and orientation, where each cycle's new shrine is built before the old one is dismantled.

The form is **simple, archaic, and intentionally so**. It deliberately rejects the continental Buddhist architectural vocabulary that arrived in the 6th century: no painted vermilion columns, no bracket-system *kumimono* roof support, no Chinese tile, no stone foundations. Ise's architecture is the **negative image of [[todaiji-daibutsuden|Todaiji]]** — the latter being the most monumental Buddhist Chinese-derived hall in Japan.

## The *shikinen sengū* — the 20-year rebuild

Every 20 years, the entire Ise shrine complex is **dismantled and rebuilt on the adjacent reserve site**. The protocol:

1. **Year 1 (8 years before rebuild)** — sacred wood-cutting (*Yamaguchi-sai*) ritual in the dedicated *Kiso* mountain forests of Nagano.
2. **Year 2–8** — timber preparation, ritual purification of materials, training of new craftsmen in the inherited joinery techniques.
3. **Year 8 (rebuild year)** — the new shrine is fully constructed on the *kodenchi* (adjacent site).
4. **Sengū-sai ceremony** — the *kami* is ritually transferred from the old shrine to the new in a midnight torchlight procession.
5. **Old shrine dismantled** — its timber is redistributed: principal ridgepoles become ridgepoles of provincial shrines; minor components become repair material; small fragments become souvenirs / amulets.

The protocol has been **carried out 62 times** since 690 CE, including ~125 ancillary shrines, ~1500 ceremonial items, the *torii* gates, the bridges (most famously the Uji Bridge over the Isuzu River), and all interior treasures.

The cost is borne by donations from across Japan; the 2013 cycle cost ~55 billion yen (~$550 million USD).

## Theological function — purity and renewal

The *shikinen sengū* is not understood by the Ise priesthood as a **maintenance** practice — it is a **ritual practice**. Old wood is impure; new wood is pure. Continuity is achieved **through controlled renewal**, not through preservation of physical fabric. The doctrinal logic is the central Shintō logic of **purity (*kegare* / *misogi*)** — old, weathered, decayed = impure; new, fresh, unpainted = pure.

The form passes from one cycle to the next with **maximum continuity**: the joinery techniques, the proportions, the rituals are transmitted via **a master-apprentice craft lineage** (the *miyadaiku* shrine-carpenters) trained from generation to generation. The result is a **paradoxical institution**: the building is materially never older than 20 years, but the form is structurally ~1300 years old. Continuity-through-renewal as an explicit institutional doctrine.

## The Yata-no-Kagami sacred mirror

Within the innermost sanctum of the Naikū is housed the **Yata-no-Kagami** — one of the **Three Sacred Treasures** of the Japanese imperial regalia. Tradition holds it to be the same mirror in which Amaterasu saw her own reflection when the other deities lured her from the cave (the central episode of the [[amaterasu-cave-myth|Ama-no-Iwato]] narrative in the *Kojiki* and *Nihon Shoki*). The mirror has not been viewed by anyone except the highest Ise priesthood for centuries; its physical form is institutionally undocumented and the position of the Shrine is that it is **not to be viewed**.

The other two regalia — the *Kusanagi-no-Tsurugi* sword (housed at Atsuta Shrine, Nagoya) and the *Yasakani-no-Magatama* jewel (housed at the imperial palace, Tokyo) — complete the regalia set, but the **mirror is the principal cult-object** of the Ise Naikū.

## MASSIVE WIN — architectural renewal as ritual rebirth

The *shikinen sengū* is **unique in world religious architecture**. No other major religious tradition combines:

1. **Cyclical full-scale rebuild** of the principal sanctuary on a fixed schedule.
2. **~1300 years of continuous unbroken practice** (62 cycles).
3. **Deliberate institutional commitment to formal continuity through material discontinuity** — the building is structurally ~1300 years old as a *type* but materially never older than 20 years.
4. **Theological grounding** — the rebuild is doctrinally articulated as **purity-renewal**, not maintenance.
5. **Craft-lineage transmission** — the *miyadaiku* carpenter lineages have transmitted the joinery techniques unbroken from the 7th c. onward.

This makes Ise the anchor of a potential **cross-tradition motif**: **architectural renewal as ritual rebirth**. Parallel cases (none of them at Ise's institutional rigor):

- The **Mesoamerican calendar-cycle architectural-encasement** practice — Maya temples encased and rebuilt every 52-year *calendar round* (Templo Mayor Tenochtitlan, Tikal North Acropolis).
- **Hindu temple consecration cycles** (*kumbhabhishekam*) — re-consecration of temple deities every 12 years, but the building is not rebuilt.
- **Vedic Aśvamedha and Agnicayana fire-altars** — built, used once, then ritually abandoned. The Vedic logic is *opposite* to Ise (single-use rather than cyclical-renewal).
- **Some Tibetan-Buddhist sand-mandala practices** — created and ritually destroyed within days, embodying impermanence at the micro-scale.

Ise's combination of **monumental scale + cyclical institutional continuity + explicit theological articulation of renewal-as-purification** is **without close parallel**. It is one of the project's clearest candidate cases for the proposed motif anchor [[theme-architectural-renewal-as-ritual-rebirth]].

The contrast with [[hagia-sophia]] / [[forbidden-city-beijing]] / [[borobudur-stupa-mandala]] is illuminating: those traditions invest in **permanence as the architectural form of religious truth**; Ise invests in **controlled impermanence as the architectural form of religious truth**. Same architectural problem; opposite doctrinal answer.

## Ise and the imperial cult

Ise is the **principal architectural anchor of the Japanese imperial cult**. The shrine is dedicated to Amaterasu, who is — in the canonical [[kojiki|*Kojiki*]] and [[nihon-shoki|*Nihon Shoki*]] mythography — the **direct ancestress of the imperial line** (via her grandson Ninigi, who descended to earth and whose great-grandson Jimmu became the first human emperor).

From the Meiji Restoration (1868) through the 1945 disestablishment, Ise was the architectural centre of **state-Shintō** — the imperial-Shintō ideology that anchored Meiji-Showa Japan. The Meiji Emperor's 1869 pilgrimage to Ise was the first imperial pilgrimage to the shrine in ~600 years and re-centred the imperial-religious programme on Ise. Postwar disestablishment ended the state-cult, but Ise retains its symbolic centrality: imperial accession rituals continue to involve Ise; the emperor's *daijōsai* (great food-offering enthronement rite) explicitly invokes Amaterasu via Ise.
