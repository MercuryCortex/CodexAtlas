# Notre-Dame de Paris

## What it is

**Notre-Dame de Paris** ("Our Lady of Paris") is the **cathedral of the Archdiocese of Paris** and one of the most universally recognised symbols of French Gothic architecture. Built ~1163–1345 on the Île de la Cité (the historic island in the Seine at the heart of medieval Paris), it stands at the geographic and symbolic centre of France — *kilometre zero* of the French national road system is marked on the parvis directly in front of the cathedral's west façade.

The cathedral is one of the principal monuments of the **French Early Gothic style** — older than [[chartres-cathedral|Chartres]] and the major **four-storey-elevation early Gothic experiment** that Chartres' three-storey High Gothic subsequently superseded. Notre-Dame de Paris remains, however, the canonical popular icon of "the Gothic cathedral" in global cultural memory.

On **15 April 2019** a catastrophic fire destroyed the wooden spire (Viollet-le-Duc's 1859 addition) and most of the wooden roof above the stone vaults. Restoration began immediately and is ongoing — the cathedral reopened to the public in **December 2024** following ~5 years of intensive reconstruction.

It is part of the UNESCO Paris, Banks of the Seine World Heritage Site (1991, File 600).

## History

**~360 CE** — Early Christian basilica on or near the site of the future Notre-Dame.

**~6th c. CE** — Saint-Étienne basilica + Notre-Dame chapel founded by King Childebert I; serve as the principal Parisian churches through the early medieval period.

**1163** — **Foundation stone laid** by [[pope-alexander-iii|Pope Alexander III]] during a state visit to Paris, in the presence of [[bishop-maurice-de-sully|Bishop Maurice de Sully]] and [[king-louis-vii-of-france|King Louis VII]]. The new cathedral is conceived as the principal church of the growing Capetian royal capital.

**1163–1182** — Choir and apse built; consecrated 1182. The high altar is in use.

**1182–1225** — Nave built; the principal four-storey-elevation interior is completed.

**~1225–1250** — The west front (façade) is built. Its rose window is added ~1225. The two western towers (~69 m tall) are completed mid-13th c.

**~1250s** — Transept arms extended by Jean de Chelles and Pierre de Montreuil. The North Rose (~1250) and the South Rose (~1260) are the two great transept-rose windows; both are largely original medieval glass and are among the largest medieval rose windows surviving.

**~1220–1230** — Major flying-buttress campaign added around the chevet to enable larger clerestory windows. Notre-Dame de Paris's flying buttresses become one of the principal European examples of the mature flying-buttress structural type.

**~1345** — Final completion of the cathedral as built in the medieval period. The construction had spanned roughly 180 years.

**~1430** — Henry VI of England is crowned King of France here (in the Hundred Years' War context).

**1572** — Marriage of the future Henri IV of France to Margaret of Valois, prelude to the St. Bartholomew's Day Massacre.

**1572–1789** — Various Baroque-period interior modifications (most undone by Viollet-le-Duc in the 19th c.).

**1793–1795** — **French Revolution iconoclasm**. The cathedral is sacked: the 28 statues of the Kings of Judah on the west façade are decapitated (the heads were rediscovered buried near the Hôtel Moreau in 1977 and are now displayed at the Musée de Cluny). The cathedral is rededicated as the **Temple of Reason** (1793) and then the **Temple of the Supreme Being** (1794). The Crown of Thorns and some other relics are saved.

**1804** — Napoleon I is crowned Emperor of the French here, in an extraordinary ceremony in which he placed the crown on his own head (David's painting of the event is in the Louvre).

**1831** — [[victor-hugo|Victor Hugo]] publishes *Notre-Dame de Paris* (English title: *The Hunchback of Notre-Dame*). The novel is a deliberate intervention in the French heritage debate: Hugo argues that the cathedral is in disgraceful disrepair and demands national restoration.

**1844–1864** — **The Viollet-le-Duc restoration**. [[eugene-emmanuel-viollet-le-duc|Eugène Viollet-le-Duc]] (with Jean-Baptiste-Antoine Lassus until Lassus' death 1857) leads the major restoration. The work is extensive: rebuilding the central spire (1859, ~96 m above the floor), restoring sculptures, replacing damaged stained glass, repairing flying buttresses, reconstructing the chimaeras and gargoyles. Some 20th-c. scholars criticised Viollet-le-Duc for creating idealised medieval forms rather than restoring authentic ones; the consensus has shifted to recognising the restoration itself as a major monument of 19th-c. architectural-historical thought.

**1909** — Joan of Arc beatified at Notre-Dame; canonised 1920.

**1944** — Liberation of Paris; the cathedral is the site of the Te Deum led by General de Gaulle.

**1991** — UNESCO World Heritage inscription (as part of Paris/Seine listing).

**15 April 2019** — **The Great Fire**. The cathedral catches fire during ongoing minor restoration work. Over ~12 hours, the wooden roof above the stone vaults, the lead roofing, the Viollet-le-Duc spire (1859), and significant portions of the wooden interior framework burn. The stone vaults largely survive (with one major collapse where the spire fell through). The principal relics (including the Crown of Thorns) are saved by firefighters and clergy. The west towers, the structural envelope, the major stained-glass windows, and the principal sculpture survive.

**2019–2024** — Reconstruction. President Macron pledges five-year reconstruction; ~340,000 donors contribute over €840 million internationally. [[philippe-villeneuve|Philippe Villeneuve]] (Architecte en chef des monuments historiques) leads the project. The spire is rebuilt to Viollet-le-Duc's 1859 design (after public debate about whether to replace it with a contemporary design; the traditional reconstruction prevails).

**December 2024** — Cathedral reopened to the public after the reconstruction.

## Architectural form — the canonical Early Gothic four-storey elevation

Notre-Dame de Paris is one of the principal examples of the **French Early Gothic four-storey elevation**:

**Plan**:
- **Length**: ~127 m
- **Width at transepts**: ~48 m
- **Vault height**: ~33 m (originally; some sources suggest the original was ~35 m)
- **West towers**: ~69 m
- **Spire (Viollet-le-Duc, 1859, destroyed 2019, rebuilt 2024)**: ~96 m
- **Cross plan**: a Latin cross with five-aisle nave (double aisles), single-bay crossing, and a single-aisle ambulatory around the chevet

**The four-storey elevation** (Notre-Dame de Paris's distinctive feature):
1. **Arcade**: pointed-arch arches on compound piers
2. **Gallery (tribune)**: a full-height storey above the aisles, with arched openings into the nave — the storey that Chartres (1194+) subsequently omitted
3. **Triforium**: a low blind arcade above the gallery
4. **Clerestory**: round-window-and-lancet stained-glass windows

The four-storey elevation makes the clerestory **smaller** than in later High Gothic cathedrals (because the gallery-storey takes up vertical space that could otherwise be devoted to clerestory windows). Notre-Dame is therefore **less luminous** than Chartres or Reims — a structurally older Gothic form.

**The flying buttresses**:
- The earliest flying buttresses in Western architecture are at Saint-Denis (Suger, ~1140) and a few other early Gothic sites
- Notre-Dame de Paris's chevet flying buttresses, expanded ~1220–1230, are among the most architecturally legible Gothic flying-buttress arrays surviving from the early period
- The flying buttress externalises the structural load that earlier (Romanesque + early Gothic) interior walls had had to bear, freeing the clerestory for stained glass

**The west façade (1200–1250)**:
- Three deeply-recessed portals
- The **Gallery of Kings** — 28 statues of the Kings of Judah (decapitated 1793, restored by Viollet-le-Duc)
- The **West Rose Window** (~1225) — 9.6 m diameter
- The two western towers (69 m)

**The transept roses (1250s)**:
- **North Rose** (Jean de Chelles, ~1250) — 13.1 m diameter, largely original medieval glass; centred on the Virgin Mary surrounded by Old Testament kings, judges, prophets
- **South Rose** (Pierre de Montreuil, ~1260) — 13.0 m diameter; centred on Christ surrounded by the four evangelists, the twenty-four elders of the Apocalypse

## Theological / cosmological function

Notre-Dame de Paris is dedicated to **Mary**:
- The principal Marian foundation in medieval Paris
- The north transept rose centres on Mary
- The annual **Marian devotions** at the cathedral were major medieval public-religious events
- The west front sculpture features Mary, the Coronation of the Virgin in the central tympanum

The cathedral additionally functioned as the **principal site of French royal religious-political life**:
- Royal weddings, coronations, Te Deums for military victories
- Funeral masses for major French figures
- Joan of Arc's beatification (1909) and canonisation (1920) Te Deums
- De Gaulle's Liberation Te Deum (1944)

The cathedral is the architectural body of **Marian devotion + French national-religious identity** — a fusion that has made Notre-Dame the symbolic centre of French religious-cultural life across nine centuries.

## MASSIVE WIN — the early Gothic moment + the 2019 fire as global heritage event

Notre-Dame de Paris occupies a uniquely visible position in the French Gothic transmission chain (cf. [[chartres-cathedral]] for the full chain):

> **[[saint-denis-abbey-paris|Saint-Denis Abbey]] (1135–1144)** → birth of Gothic
> → **Notre-Dame de Paris (begun 1163)** → **canonical Early Gothic four-storey experiment**
> → **[[chartres-cathedral|Chartres]] (1194–1220)** → High Gothic three-storey supersession
> → Reims, Amiens, Beauvais, Cologne, Strasbourg, Salisbury, Milan, Seville

What Notre-Dame de Paris uniquely contributes:
1. **The mature flying buttress** — Notre-Dame's chevet flying buttresses are the principal early monumental examples
2. **The cathedral-as-French-national-symbol** — no other Gothic cathedral has accumulated equivalent national-identity charge over nine centuries
3. **The Viollet-le-Duc restoration** — the foundational 19th-c. example of "restoration as conceptual project," shaping the modern global discipline of heritage conservation
4. **The 2019 fire** — the most globally visible heritage disaster of the 21st century; the worldwide response (real-time television coverage, mass donations, cross-tradition prayer and mourning) marks the cathedral as a **trans-confessional global heritage monument** in a way unmatched by any other Christian building

The fire raises broader questions about the cathedral's **religious vs. cultural** identity: many global mourners were not Catholic; many were not Christian; the cathedral evidently carries cultural-heritage significance that exceeds its specific Catholic religious identity. Notre-Dame de Paris is one of the cleanest contemporary cases of a religious building whose **symbolic charge has exceeded its specific religious tradition** — comparable in this respect to Hagia Sophia (which carries Eastern Orthodox + Islamic + secular cultural significance) and to the Bamiyan Buddhas (whose destruction was internationally mourned across many traditions and none).
