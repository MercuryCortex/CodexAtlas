# Forbidden City (Zijincheng, Beijing)

## What it is

The **Forbidden City** (*Zijincheng*, 紫禁城 — "Purple Forbidden City") is the **imperial palace of the Ming and Qing dynasties** and the principal **architectural embodiment of the Chinese imperial cult** for nearly 500 years (1420–1912). It is **the largest preserved wooden palace complex in the world** — ~72 ha, ~980 surviving buildings, ~8700 rooms — laid out on a strict N–S axis at the **cosmological centre of Beijing**.

Built **1406–1420** under the [[yongle-emperor|Yongle Emperor]] when he relocated the Ming capital from Nanjing to Beijing, the complex is **simultaneously**:

- The **imperial residence** of 24 emperors (14 Ming + 10 Qing).
- The **principal ritual stage** of the imperial Confucian state-cult, with great court audiences and ceremonies in the three Outer Court halls.
- The **architectural centre of Beijing-as-cosmogram** — the [[mandate-of-heaven|Son of Heaven]]'s palace at the precise diagrammatic centre of a four-altar cardinal ritual ring ([[temple-of-heaven-beijing|Temple of Heaven]] south, Temple of Earth north, Sun east, Moon west).
- The **continuation of the deep tradition of Chinese palace-planning** going back to the *Zhou Li* (*Rites of Zhou*) ideal city-plan that informed every Chinese imperial capital from Han Chang'an onwards.

UNESCO World Heritage (1987, File 439). The Palace Museum (*Gugong*) since 1925.

## History

- **1406–1420** — Yongle-era construction. Crew of ~1 million labourers + 100,000 craftsmen. Timber from Sichuan, marble from quarries west of Beijing, "golden bricks" (*jinzhuan*) from kilns near Suzhou.
- **1421** — capital officially relocated; Yongle moves the court from Nanjing to Beijing.
- **1644** — Ming falls. The Manchu Qing dynasty takes the palace **without destroying it** — they continue using the entire Ming complex as their own imperial seat. The structural continuity Ming-to-Qing is one of the most consequential **non-destructive dynastic transitions** in world architectural history.
- **17th–18th c.** — Qing-era rebuilds following fires (1647, 1679, 1797). Qianlong-era expansion of the inner court and addition of Tibetan-Buddhist sanctums (Yuhuage, Fanhualou, etc.) reflecting Qing-Manchu engagement with [[tradition-tibetan-buddhism|Tibetan Buddhism]].
- **1912** — Republic founded; Qing falls. Last emperor [[puyi|Puyi]] retains residence in the Inner Court under the *Articles of Favourable Treatment*.
- **1924** — Puyi expelled. The complex becomes a public museum (1925).
- **1949–** — restored progressively as the Palace Museum.

## Architectural form — the axial-cosmological programme

The complex is organised on a single **N–S central axis** that extends from the southern Yongding Gate of outer Beijing, through Tiananmen, Duanmen, the **Meridian Gate (Wumen)** entrance to the Forbidden City proper, north through the three **Outer Court** great halls, through the **Inner Court** residential halls, exiting at the northern Shenwumen gate, continuing up Jingshan ("Coal Hill"), and onward to the northern walls.

The principal axial sequence inside the Forbidden City:

| Position | Structure | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Southern entrance | **Meridian Gate (*Wumen*)** | The principal ceremonial entrance; the only one the emperor uses on the central path |
| Outer Court | **Hall of Supreme Harmony (*Taihedian*)** | The largest hall; the emperor's throne for the most important ceremonies (enthronement, New Year, winter solstice, imperial wedding, dispatch of generals) |
| Outer Court | **Hall of Central Harmony (*Zhonghedian*)** | The emperor's preparation hall before ceremonies in Taihedian |
| Outer Court | **Hall of Preserving Harmony (*Baohedian*)** | The hall for state banquets and the highest civil-service examination (*dianshi*) |
| Inner Court | **Palace of Heavenly Purity (*Qianqinggong*)** | The Ming-era and early-Qing imperial residence and audience-hall for routine business |
| Inner Court | **Hall of Union (*Jiaotaidian*)** | Where the 25 imperial seals (*xishi*) of the Qing dynasty are stored |
| Inner Court | **Palace of Earthly Tranquility (*Kunninggong*)** | The empress's principal residence (Ming); Qing-era Shamanic ritual hall |

The geometry is **square** plan, **cardinal alignment**, **axial symmetry**, **gold-yellow tile roofs** (the imperial colour), **red walls and columns**, **white-marble triple-terraced platforms** for the principal halls. Court audiences are conducted with the emperor on the north (facing south — the cosmological position of the ruler in Confucian tradition: **"the south-facing one"** — *nan mian zhe* — is a classical term for emperor).

**Round-Heaven, square-Earth** at the building scale: the Forbidden City's plan is **square / rectangular** (Earth), counterpointed to the round Heaven altar at the southern Temple of Heaven. The two together — palace and altar — diagram the cosmological order.

## The Outer Court / Inner Court division

The complex divides into:

- **Outer Court** (*Waichao*) — the southern half — principal ceremonial halls (the three "Harmony" halls on the central axis + flanking pavilions). Here the emperor performs the **public ritual programme**: enthronements, New Year audiences, winter-solstice court, imperial weddings.
- **Inner Court** (*Neiting*) — the northern half — imperial residence, empress and consorts' apartments, the imperial garden at the far north. Here the emperor **lives**, conducts day-to-day governance with confidential advisors, performs private ritual.

The dividing line is the **Gate of Heavenly Purity** (*Qianqingmen*) at the centre of the complex. **No commoner crossed this line**; few officials did. The Outer / Inner division is the architectural diagram of **public-cult-vs-private-residence**, and it survives intact in the present complex.

## The materials — gold-and-red

The Forbidden City's distinctive palette is:

- **Imperial yellow (*huangse*)** roof tiles — reserved for the emperor's buildings. The yellow is from a glaze containing iron + lead.
- **Cinnabar red** columns, walls, doors — the colour of yang, life-force, the south, the south-facing emperor.
- **White marble** triple-terraced platforms (*sanchen*) under the great Outer Court halls.
- **"Golden bricks" (*jinzhuan*)** — the floor tiles of the principal halls. Not actually gold, but a special low-fired clay from Suzhou kilns near Lake Tai, polished to a near-metallic black surface. Each tile took ~2 years to produce.
- **Phoebe nanmu** (*nanmu*, *Phoebe zhennan*) — the principal hardwood for the structural timbers, shipped from Sichuan over months of river transport.

## MASSIVE WIN — palace-as-cosmogram

The Forbidden City is the **most fully articulated case in world architecture of a single building-complex as the architectural diagram of an entire imperial-cosmological doctrine**.

The doctrine: **the emperor (Son of Heaven) mediates between Heaven and the realm. He sits at the centre. North-south is the cosmological axis. East-west is the secondary axis. Heaven is round; Earth is square. The yang-colours (red, yellow) belong to the emperor; the yin-colours belong to the consorts and ancestors. The palace is the centre; the Heaven-altar is south; the Earth-altar is north; Sun is east; Moon is west.**

The architectural translation:
- **Plan**: square / rectangular precinct (Earth) on a strict N–S axis (cosmological axis).
- **Position**: at the **cosmological centre of Beijing**, with the four cardinal altars around it.
- **Sequence**: south-to-north processional through three Outer Court halls (public-cult) to Inner Court residence (emperor-as-private-cosmic-centre).
- **Materials and colour**: yellow (emperor) + red (yang) + white marble (purity) — none of which is reserved for any non-imperial building in the empire.
- **Surrounding context**: the four altars ([[temple-of-heaven-beijing|Heaven]], Earth, Sun, Moon) at the four cardinal positions, the **whole capital as cosmological diagram**.

The result is **Beijing-as-mandala**, with the palace at the centre. In comparative terms this is structurally analogous to the way [[borobudur-stupa-mandala|Borobudur]] uses stepped-mandala form to diagram the Mahayana *Trailokya* — except the Forbidden City's diagram is **at the city-scale rather than the building-scale** and is **political-cosmological** rather than soteriological.

## Influence on East Asian palace architecture

The Forbidden City is the **principal architectural model** for state-imperial / royal architecture across the Confucian Sinosphere:

- **Korea — Gyeongbokgung Palace (Seoul, Joseon dynasty)** + [[jongmyo-shrine-seoul|Jongmyo Shrine]] take direct cues from the Ming-Beijing model.
- **Vietnam — Imperial City of Huế (Nguyễn dynasty)** is even more explicitly modeled on the Forbidden City, on a smaller scale.
- **Japan — Kyoto Imperial Palace** is a much more austere variant on the same East-Asian palace-plan tradition (though Japanese imperial architecture diverged sharply from the Chinese model from the Heian period onward).

The Ming–Qing Beijing capital-plan is the **canonical reference** for "imperial East Asian palace" as an architectural type-class.
