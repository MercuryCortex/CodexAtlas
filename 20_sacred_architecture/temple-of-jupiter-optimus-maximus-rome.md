# Temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus (Capitoline)

## What it is

The **Temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus** ("the Best and Greatest"), on the **Capitoline Hill** at Rome, was the **single most important temple of the Roman state religion** from the foundation of the Republic in 509 BCE through the Christianisation of the Empire in the 4th c. CE — a span of approximately **nine centuries** as Rome's principal state-religious institution.

It was the temple of the **Capitoline Triad**: Jupiter (Roman supreme god) in the central cella, Juno (his consort) in the left cella, Minerva (the goddess of wisdom and statecraft) in the right cella — the **Roman political-religious pantheon's central architectural expression**.

Every Roman state event of religious significance, from the **annual consular oath** at the start of each year to the **culmination of every triumphal procession**, ended at the Temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus. The temple was, in functional terms, **the architectural Constitution of the Roman state-religious order** — the building at which the political-religious legitimacy of every Roman institution was renewed.

The temple was destroyed and rebuilt **four times** across its nine centuries — 83 BCE, 69 CE, 80 CE, 191 CE — each rebuild a state-funded re-enactment of Roman religious-political identity. The original building no longer stands; portions of the substructure survive within the medieval Palazzo Caffarelli and other Capitoline buildings.

## History

**Foundation tradition (late 6th c. BCE):**

Roman tradition (preserved principally by Livy, *Ab Urbe Condita* I.55) holds that:
- **Tarquinius Priscus** (5th king of Rome, late 7th c. BCE) initiated planning.
- **Tarquinius Superbus** (7th and last king, late 6th c. BCE) substantially completed construction.
- The temple was **dedicated in 509 BCE** by the consul **Marcus Horatius Pulvillus** — **the foundational year of the Roman Republic**, after the expulsion of Tarquinius Superbus.

The dedication ceremony at the moment of the Republic's foundation is one of the most consequential single religious-political acts in Roman tradition: the Republic's first religious act was to dedicate the supreme state temple to Jupiter. **The Republic and the temple are foundationally co-dedicated.**

**Architectural origin — Etruscan construction:**

The original temple was constructed in **Etruscan architectural technique** — a high podium of stone (the Etruscan-Italic frontal-temple type), a mud-brick superstructure, a wooden roof structure, a triple-cella interior, and a terracotta sculptural programme. The cult-statue of Jupiter (in terracotta) was commissioned from the Etruscan sculptor **Vulca of Veii**. This documents the **deep Etruscan substrate of Roman religion** at the moment of the Republic's foundation: the supreme temple of the new Roman Republic was built by Etruscan kings using Etruscan technique and Etruscan sculptural traditions.

The original dimensions (per Stamper's reconstruction):
- Platform: ~62 × 53 m — one of the largest podium-temples of its period in the Mediterranean.
- Hexastyle Etruscan-Tuscan order columns across the front.
- Three cellae side-by-side.
- Triple porch with widely-spaced columns.

**Multiple rebuilds:**

| Date | Event | Rebuilder | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **83 BCE** | Fire destroys the original temple | Quintus Lutatius Catulus | First rebuild begins; uses some Hellenistic Greek elements (marble columns from Athens) |
| 75 BCE | Rebuild dedicated | Catulus | Largely faithful to the original plan but in updated Hellenistic-Roman style |
| **69 CE** | Destroyed in the Vitellian civil war | (Tacitus narrates) | The temple is set on fire during the conflict between Vitellians and the troops of Vespasian's allies |
| **75 CE** | Rebuild completed | Vespasian | The Flavian rebuild largely follows the Augustan stylistic programme |
| **80 CE** | Destroyed again by fire | | Another fire, possibly during the same conflagration that damages the Theatre of Pompey |
| **82 CE** | Rebuild completed | Domitian | The Domitianic rebuild is the most lavish — gold-plated bronze roof tiles, marble columns, the most ornate of all the Capitoline temples |
| **191 CE** | Damaged by fire under Commodus | | Restored under Septimius Severus |

The **multiple-rebuild pattern** is itself significant: the Roman state-religious-political order treated the Capitoline Temple as the architectural object whose continuity *must* be maintained, even at vast cost, across each catastrophe. Every rebuild was a state-funded affirmation that **the Republic-and-Empire continues, and its central religious institution continues with it**.

## Architectural form

The Republican-Augustan-Flavian Capitoline Temple was a **triple-cella podium temple** in the Etruscan-Italic tradition, gradually Hellenised across successive rebuilds:

- **High podium** (~3.5–4 m above the surrounding pavement) — the **Etruscan-Italic high-podium tradition** that distinguishes Roman temples from Greek temples. Greek temples sit on a low *stylobate*; Roman temples sit on a **high podium with a single frontal staircase**.
- **Frontal emphasis** — only one staircase (the central staircase on the front), in contrast to Greek temples' peripteral all-sides accessibility. The Roman temple is **directional**: it faces a forecourt and is approached from one side only.
- **Triple cella** — three side-by-side rectangular rooms for the Capitoline Triad, accessed from a deep front porch.
- **Wide-spaced front columns** with a deep porch (the **deep front porch** is characteristic of Italic temples, in contrast to the shallower Greek temple porch).
- **Terracotta sculptural programme on the original** (later replaced with stone and marble in successive rebuilds).

These features make the Capitoline Temple the **type-specimen of the Etruscan-Italic-Roman temple tradition**, distinct from the Greek peripteral temple. The Roman temple is **frontal, podium-raised, deep-porched, single-cella-or-triple-cella** — and this is the architectural type that Roman temple-architecture develops and spreads across the empire.

The **architectural-theological proposition** is also distinctive: where the Greek peripteral temple is an *exterior object* designed to be seen from outside (the cult was largely conducted at the altar in front of the temple, not in the building), **the Roman temple is approached and entered** — the high podium raises the temple above its forecourt; the deep porch invites entry; the cult-statue inside is the architectural-religious focus. This **interior-oriented architectural-religious logic** is later developed by Roman concrete architecture into the [[pantheon-rome|Pantheon's]] vast monumental interior — the architectural-historical hinge from Greek exterior-temple to Roman interior-temple.

## Theological / cosmological function

**The Capitoline Triad** — Jupiter, Juno, Minerva — is the **architectural-religious centre of the Roman state**. Each god's role:

- **Jupiter Optimus Maximus** — "the best and greatest" Jupiter — the supreme god of the Roman state, the sky-god, the divine guarantor of Roman sovereignty. The cult of Jupiter Optimus Maximus is **the principal cult of the Roman state**; the supreme priest of Jupiter (the *Flamen Dialis*) is one of the most religiously powerful figures of the Republic.
- **Juno** — Jupiter's consort, the goddess of marriage, women, and the state's matrimonial-genealogical continuity.
- **Minerva** — the goddess of wisdom, statecraft, and skilled craft, integrated into the central triad as the divine guarantor of Roman political-administrative competence.

**The architectural-political function:**

- **Triumphal procession terminus.** Every Roman triumphal procession (the *triumphus* — the ceremonial victory parade granted to a successful general by Senate vote) ended at the Temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus, where the general formally sacrificed white oxen to Jupiter and presented his spoils. **The temple is the architectural ratification of every Roman military victory.**
- **Consular inauguration site.** Every Roman consul began his year of office with sacrifices at the temple, formally inaugurating his magistracy.
- **Senate religious headquarters.** While the Senate met in the Curia (the senate-house in the Forum below), many religious deliberations and ceremonial state-religious acts were conducted at or in connection with the Capitoline Temple.
- **The Sibylline Books.** The temple housed the **Sibylline Books** — Rome's secret prophetic oracular books, consulted by the Senate at moments of state crisis. The Sibylline Books were destroyed in the 83 BCE fire; replacement Sibylline oracles were reassembled and re-deposited in the rebuilt temple.

The **Capitoline Triad** spreads across the Empire as the **principal cult of Romanisation**: in every Roman colony, the central forum is dominated by a *capitolium* — a triple-cella temple of the Capitoline Triad, in imitation of the Roman home temple. **The architectural pattern is the principal religious-architectural mechanism of Roman imperial cultural projection.**

## MASSIVE WIN — the Capitoline Temple is the architectural Constitution of the Roman state

The Temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus is the **architectural-religious foundation of the Roman political order** — the building that is the institutional centre of the Roman state-religion-and-state-politics fusion that characterises Roman civilisation from its Republican origin through its imperial maturity.

This is consequential because:

**1. Rome's political-religious unity is architecturally enacted.** Roman religion was not a separate sphere from politics; it was the **religious dimension of the political order itself**. Magistrates were also priests; political deliberation was preceded by religious ritual; military victory was ratified by religious sacrifice. The Capitoline Temple is the **architectural object that holds this unity together**. The building is **Rome's political-religious cosmology made concrete**.

**2. The architectural pattern is exported across the empire.** Every Roman colony has its capitolium — a triple-cella temple modelled on the Roman original. This means the **architectural type of the Capitoline Temple is the principal religious-architectural mechanism of Roman imperial integration**. From Iberia (capitolia at Italica, Sagunto) to North Africa (Sufetula, Dougga) to Britain (London's capitolium, lost) to Asia Minor (Antioch, Aphrodisias), the **same architectural pattern declares "this is now a Roman city, integrated into the Roman religious-political order"**. The Capitoline Temple is therefore the **architectural ancestor of every capitolium in every Roman colony** — hundreds of buildings across the empire.

**3. The temple's continuity-through-catastrophe is the architectural enactment of Roman identity.** Four destructions, four rebuilds. Each rebuild was a state-funded affirmation that **the Republic-and-Empire continues**. The architectural object is the visible-and-tangible focus of Roman continuity-through-change. The pattern recurs at the **Hagia Sophia** (rebuilt by Justinian after destruction), at **St. Peter's** (rebuilt by the Renaissance papacy after Constantine's original was demolished), at the **Pantheon** (Agrippa → Hadrian rebuild). The Capitoline Temple is the **prototypical example of the architectural-political logic of rebuilding-as-continuity**.

**4. The pattern of provincial outdoing-the-centre.** A particularly striking architectural-imperial pattern is that the **provincial monumental projections of Jupiter Optimus Maximus exceed the Roman home temple in scale**. [[temple-of-jupiter-baalbek|Baalbek's Temple of Jupiter Heliopolitanus]] is larger and architecturally more ambitious than the Capitoline Temple ever was; **the provincial syncretic temple eclipses its imperial centre**. This is characteristic of Roman imperial architectural-religious practice: the empire's reach outward produced architectural objects whose monumentality at the periphery exceeded the more modest forms maintained at the centre. The Capitoline Temple is the **architectural-religious original of which Baalbek is the eastern monumental reflection** — and the contrast between them documents the structural pattern of Roman imperial architectural-religious projection.

**A second, smaller finding: the temple's loss in Christian Late Antiquity is one of the largest architectural-religious losses in Western history.** When the Theodosian edict of 391 CE shuttered pagan cult, the Capitoline Temple — the principal architectural-religious object of the Roman state for nine centuries — was abandoned and progressively dismantled for building materials. **The building that was the architectural centre of Roman religion does not survive.** Where [[pantheon-rome|the Pantheon]] survived through Christian conversion and [[hagia-sophia|Hagia Sophia]] survived through Islamic conversion, **the Capitoline Temple was simply demolished** because there was no Christian use for the form. This makes the Capitoline Temple a documentary-historical object more than an extant architectural object, accessible to modern study mainly through literary description (Livy, Tacitus), inscriptions, surviving substructure fragments, and the architectural-typological evidence of provincial capitolia that copied its form.
