# Hazor — Canaanite Temples

## What it is

**Hazor** (modern Tel Hazor, in Upper Galilee) was **the largest Canaanite city of the Late Bronze Age** — at its 14th–13th c. BCE peak, a city of perhaps 20,000 inhabitants covering ~80 hectares (the Late Bronze Age tells of Megiddo, Lachish, and Jerusalem are an order of magnitude smaller). The biblical text describes Hazor as "head of all these kingdoms" (Joshua 11:10), and the archaeological record confirms: Hazor was the **regional hegemon of southern Canaan** in the Late Bronze Age and one of the most extensively-temple-furnished cities of the broader Bronze Age Levant.

The site preserves **at least four major Canaanite temples** in distinct phases and locations — the **Long Temple** in Area H, the **Orthostat Temple** also in Area H (a later phase atop the Long Temple), the **Stelae Temple** in Area C, and the **Southern Temple** in Area F — making Hazor the **most temple-rich Late Bronze Age Canaanite site known**. Together with Ugarit at the northern pole of the Canaanite cultural sphere, **Hazor is the principal evidence-base for the architectural-theological tradition that Solomon's First Temple inherits**.

## History

**Hazor's stratigraphy:**

- **Early Bronze (~3000–2000 BCE)** — Significant Early Bronze settlement; precursors of later temple sites in operation.
- **Middle Bronze (~2000–1550 BCE)** — Hazor reaches its first major peak; the city expands onto the lower plateau. Hazor is named in the **Mari letters** (~18th c. BCE) as a major polity exchanging diplomatic correspondence with the Mesopotamian kingdoms — the first Levantine city named in Mari archives.
- **Late Bronze (~1550–1200 BCE)** — Hazor's **apogee**. The major temples in Area H, Area C, and Area F are all built or extensively rebuilt during this period. Hazor is named in the **[[phase-1-012-amarna-letters|Amarna Letters]]** (~14th c. BCE) as one of the major Canaanite city-states corresponding with Egypt. The Long Temple in Area H reaches its definitive form.
- **~1230 BCE** — Hazor is **destroyed in a massive conflagration** — the temples are burned, the palace is destroyed, the city is abandoned. This destruction is one of the **principal candidates for the historical referent of the biblical conquest narrative** (Joshua 11:10-13: "Joshua at that time turned back and took Hazor and struck its king with the sword, for Hazor formerly was the head of all those kingdoms. And they put every soul in it to the sword, devoting them to destruction; there was none left that breathed. And he burned Hazor with fire."). Whether the destruction is Israelite, Sea Peoples, or internal-Canaanite remains debated; the archaeological evidence is **intentional destruction by burning**, with **deliberate damage to the cult statues** consistent with **iconoclastic religious motivation** (Ben-Tor argues for Israelite agency on this basis).
- **Iron Age I (~1200–1000 BCE)** — Small, impoverished resettlement.
- **Iron Age II (~10th–8th c. BCE)** — **Solomon refortifies Hazor** (1 Kings 9:15) as one of his three northern border-cities (with Megiddo and Gezer); later rebuilds under the Omride dynasty (Ahab) make Hazor a major Israelite fortress-city.
- **732 BCE** — Hazor is destroyed by the Assyrian king **Tiglath-Pileser III**; never recovers as a major settlement.

**Excavation:**
- **1928** — John Garstang conducts limited soundings.
- **1955–1958, 1968** — **Yigael Yadin** leads the major Israeli excavations that establish Hazor's stratigraphy and recover the temples.
- **1990–present** — **Amnon Ben-Tor's** long-running Hebrew University campaign, ongoing as of 2026.

## Architectural form — the temples

**1. The Long Temple (Area H, Late Bronze Age phases):**

The Long Temple in Area H is **the principal Hazor temple** and the most architecturally important. Across several phases (Stratum 2 → 1A), it is rebuilt as a:

- **Tripartite plan** — porch + main hall + raised inner sanctum (the *holy of holies*). **This is the same tripartite plan-grammar as [[solomons-temple-first|Solomon's First Temple]]**: *ulam-hekhal-devir*.
- **Orthostat-faced walls** — the inner walls were lined with carved basalt orthostats (large stone panels set vertically into the wall), some carved with reliefs of lions and other figures. **The orthostat technique** is the same architectural technique 1 Kings 6 describes for Solomon's Temple (*gazit*, dressed stone).
- **Elevated podium setting** — the temple sits on a raised platform, paralleling the elevated-temple form of the broader Bronze Age Levantine architectural tradition.
- **Cella altar and basalt cult-objects** — a basalt offering-table, basin, and other cult-furniture recovered in situ.

Dimensions: ~25 × 15 m at base, walls ~3 m thick.

**2. The Orthostat Temple (Area H, latest Late Bronze phase):**

A later, smaller temple built atop the destroyed Long Temple in the same Area H — even more dramatically orthostat-faced, with massive basalt orthostats lining a small inner cella. This is the **most ornamentally-articulated Canaanite temple known**.

**3. The Stelae Temple (Area C):**

A small **stelae temple** — a small open shrine containing a row of upright stone stelae (one with a relief of two upraised hands beneath a crescent moon — a striking Bronze Age iconographic object now in the Israel Museum) and an associated **seated male cult-figure**. The "two-handed-stele" iconography is **strongly suggestive of moon-god / Sin worship**.

**4. The Southern Temple (Area F):**

A separate temple precinct on the southern slope, less fully excavated but architecturally similar to the Long Temple.

The pattern is: **Hazor is a multi-temple precinct city**, not a single-temple city. Different gods (Baal, El, Sin) had their own temples; major temples were rebuilt over centuries on the same sacred ground. **This multi-temple architectural pattern is the standard Late Bronze Age Levantine model** — and it is the model Solomon partially inherits (Solomon's Temple is one of several Jerusalem high-places before the Deuteronomic centralisation of cult; the bāmôt — "high places" — of the broader Israelite landscape are the Iron Age survival of this multi-temple Canaanite pattern).

## Theological / cosmological function

Hazor's religion is the **broader Canaanite religion** — the **same** Late Bronze Age Levantine pantheon attested at [[ugarit-temple-of-baal|Ugarit]]: a high god **El**; a storm-god **[[baal-hadad|Baal]]** (the rising king of the gods over the course of the Late Bronze Age); a mother-goddess **[[asherah|Athirat/Asherah]]**; the chief consort-goddess **[[astarte-canaanite|Astarte]]**; the moon-god **Yarikh** (cognate with the Mesopotamian **Sin**); and a range of secondary deities.

**The multi-temple precinct architecturally enacts the multi-deity pantheon.** Different gods get different houses; each house is sized and ornamented per the god's relative status. This is the **polytheistic architectural-theological logic** that Iron Age Yahwism (especially in its Deuteronomic centralising-and-monotheising form) explicitly rejects: **one god, one temple, one altar**, in *one* city.

The Hazor evidence shows the architectural-theological tradition Iron Age Yahwism was rejecting **in its monumental Bronze Age form** — and shows that the *plan* of the Israelite temple-form (tripartite, orthostat-faced, podium-raised) survives the reformation even when the *theology* changes.

## MASSIVE WIN — Hazor + Ugarit + Ain Dara are the architectural trinity behind Solomon's Temple

The architectural-theological lineage from Canaanite Bronze Age temple to Solomon's First Temple has, in the modern archaeological record, **three principal evidentiary anchors**:

1. **[[ugarit-temple-of-baal|Ugarit (Temple of Baal + Temple of Dagan, ~14th c. BCE)]]** — northern-Syrian-coast pole; the textual centre; preserves the Bronze Age Levantine theology in pure form (the Baal Cycle).
2. **Hazor (Long Temple + Orthostat Temple + Stelae Temple, Late Bronze Age)** — southern-Canaanite pole; the multi-temple precinct city; preserves the architectural-iconographic pattern in pure form.
3. **[[ain-dara-temple-syria|Ain Dara (Iron Age, ~10th–8th c. BCE)]]** — Iron Age N Syrian survival of the tradition; preserves the closest plan-parallel to Solomon's Temple (cherub orthostats, tripartite plan, basalt-orthostat construction).

[[solomons-temple-first|Solomon's First Temple]] (~960 BCE) sits at the southern end of this three-pole architectural triangle:
- Same tripartite plan (Hazor Long Temple → Solomon's Temple → Ain Dara).
- Same orthostat-faced ashlar masonry (Hazor → [[hiram-of-tyre-king|Phoenician]] continuators → Solomon's Temple → Ain Dara).
- Same elevated-podium presentation.
- Same iconographic vocabulary (cherubim → at Solomon, ancestrally winged-sphinxes at Hazor/Ugarit).
- Different theology: monotheised, centralised, polemically anti-Baalist.

**The architectural form survives the theological reformation**. The Israelite tradition rejects the polytheistic Canaanite cult; it does not reject the Canaanite **architectural-theological grammar** of the temple itself. **Solomon's Temple is, architecturally, a Canaanite temple repurposed for YHWH-only worship.** This is one of the most consequential and least-acknowledged facts about the origins of biblical religion — and Hazor is the principal southern-Levantine evidence for it.

Hazor's other massive contribution is the **archaeological enactment of the biblical Joshua-conquest narrative**. The 13th c. BCE destruction-by-burning at Hazor — with deliberately defaced cult statues — is the **single best archaeological candidate** for an event behind the Joshua 11 narrative. Whether the destroyers were proto-Israelites, Sea Peoples, or internal Canaanite rivals is debated; **but the destruction itself, in its religious-iconoclastic profile, is the archaeological echo of the biblical conquest tradition**. Hazor is one of the few biblical events for which the archaeological record provides a plausible event-correlate.
