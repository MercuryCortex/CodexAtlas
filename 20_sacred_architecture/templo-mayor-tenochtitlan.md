# Templo Mayor

## What it is

The **Templo Mayor** (Great Temple) was the central religious monument of [[tenochtitlan]], the imperial capital of the Mexica (Aztec) empire, from the city's founding in 1325 CE until its destruction by [[hernan-cortes]]'s forces in 1521. It was a **dual-shrined stepped pyramid** — atypical in Mesoamerica, where pyramids usually carry a single shrine — bearing the temples of **[[huitzilopochtli]]** (Mexica tribal-solar war god) on the south side and **[[tlaloc]]** (rain god, with deep roots back to Teotihuacan and earlier) on the north.

The temple was at the geographical, religious, and political centre of Tenochtitlan and, by extension, of the Mexica empire. It was the **single most important sacred structure in pre-conquest Mesoamerica at the moment of European contact**. It was systematically destroyed by Cortés (its stones reused in the colonial Cathedral and viceregal palace), and its location was lost beneath the colonial Mexico City for 350 years.

In 1978 the chance discovery of the **Coyolxauhqui Stone** by electrical workers behind the Cathedral led to the **Proyecto Templo Mayor** (1978–82, [[eduardo-matos-moctezuma]] directing) — the most important Mesoamerican archaeological project of the 20th century. The temple's lowest levels were recovered, the cult-objects in 200+ ritual deposits were excavated, and the Templo Mayor Museum was built directly over the site.

## History

### Foundation myth and the founding of Tenochtitlan

The Mexica were a relatively-late wandering Nahua-speaking group who arrived in the Basin of Mexico c. 1250 CE, after the collapse of the earlier Tepanec and Toltec states. Mexica origin-history (preserved in the Codex Boturini, *Tira de la Peregrinación*, and the [[duran-historia-de-las-indias-de-nueva-espana|Durán *Historia*]]) records that they were led southward by **Huitzilopochtli** himself, who had instructed them to settle where they saw an **eagle perched on a cactus eating a serpent** — a sign they witnessed on a small island in the marshes of Lake Texcoco in **1325 CE**.

That island became the foundation of [[tenochtitlan]]. The Templo Mayor was erected at the exact spot of the eagle-on-cactus sign — the **cosmic centre of the Mexica universe**. The image is on the modern Mexican flag.

### Construction phases

The Templo Mayor was rebuilt at least **seven times** between 1325 and 1487 CE, each new phase entirely **enveloping** the previous structure (rather than demolishing it). This is **standard Mesoamerican practice** (the Pyramid of the Sun and El Castillo show the same pattern). The temple grew progressively larger, and each enlargement was a major imperial-religious event.

The seven phases identified by the Proyecto Templo Mayor:
- **Phase I (~1325)**: lost (foundations submerged by water table; never recovered).
- **Phase II (~1390)**: the earliest preserved structure; visible at the bottom of the site today. Twin shrines already present.
- **Phases III–VI (~1400–1469)**: progressive enlargements under emperors Itzcoatl, Moctezuma I, and Axayacatl.
- **Phase VII (1486–1502 dedicated 1487)**: the great Ahuitzotl enlargement; the visible upper Templo Mayor at the time of Cortés.

### The 1487 dedication

The 1487 dedication of Ahuitzotl's enlarged Templo Mayor is the **single largest religious ceremony attested in Mesoamerican history**. According to multiple Mexica and Spanish-recorded sources, the ceremony lasted **four days** and involved the sacrifice of between **20,000 and 80,000 captives** (the higher numbers are usually treated as Mexica imperial-propaganda exaggeration; the lower numbers are still extraordinary — Matos Moctezuma 1988 considers ~20,000 plausible). The line of victims extended from the Templo Mayor's summit down the steps, across the plaza, and outside the sacred precinct.

The political-religious purpose: to **terrify subject peoples**, demonstrate Mexica imperial-cosmic power, and consecrate the enlarged temple as the cosmic axis. By 1487 the Mexica empire was at its territorial peak, and the temple is its architectural-religious self-statement.

### Cortés's destruction

In **August 1521** Cortés took Tenochtitlan after an 80-day siege. He systematically demolished the Templo Mayor — using its stones to build the Spanish viceregal palace and (later) the Mexico City Cathedral directly adjacent. By 1525 the temple's superstructure was gone; by 1550 its plaza had been built over. The deliberate annihilation of the temple was both an architectural-strategic act (consuming the Mexica's most important building) and a religious one (eradicating the cult and replacing it visibly with Christianity).

### Rediscovery — 1978

Sporadic discoveries through the colonial period (sculptures occasionally turned up during building works — the Coatlicue statue 1790, the Sun Stone 1790 — both now in the Museo Nacional de Antropología) hinted at the location. But it was not until **21 February 1978** that electrical-utility workers digging behind the Metropolitan Cathedral struck a colossal carved stone of a dismembered female figure — the **Coyolxauhqui Stone**, a 3.2-m circular relief of the moon-goddess dismembered by Huitzilopochtli. This identified the location of the Templo Mayor base. The subsequent excavation has continued in phases to the present.

## Architectural form

### Dimensions (final Phase VII)

- **Height:** ~45 m (estimated from Spanish accounts and reconstructions; the surviving Phase II core is much smaller).
- **Base:** ~80 m × 100 m.
- **Stepped pyramidal platform** with **two staircases on the west face**, one ascending to each summit shrine.
- **Two shrines on the summit:**
  - **Huitzilopochtli's shrine (south)**: associated with the dry season, war, sun, blood, the south, the colour red.
  - **Tlaloc's shrine (north)**: associated with the rainy season, agriculture, rain, water, the north, the colour blue.
- The **two-stairway double-temple summit** is unusual in Mesoamerica and is the signature architectural form of the Templo Mayor. Only one other Mexica double-temple is known (at Tlatelolco, the twin-city of Tenochtitlan).

### The Coyolxauhqui Stone

At the **foot of the south (Huitzilopochtli) staircase**, embedded into the pavement of the platform, lies the colossal **Coyolxauhqui Stone** — a circular carved relief (3.25m diameter, 11 tonnes) showing the **dismembered moon-goddess Coyolxauhqui**, severed head, severed limbs, blood-streams. This is the architectural counterpart of the Huitzilopochtli birth-myth (see below).

### Iconographic + ritual deposits

The Proyecto Templo Mayor has excavated **over 200 ritual offering deposits** (caches of objects buried within the temple's construction phases as foundation/dedication offerings). Contents include: imported jade and obsidian, sacrificed jaguars and pumas with collars, marine shells from both Atlantic and Pacific coasts, sacrificed children dressed as miniature Tlaloc figures, masks, knives, copal incense — the **inventory of the Mexica's tribute-empire and cosmological universe** deposited inside the temple.

## Theological / cosmological function

### Coatepec — the Snake Mountain re-enactment

The Templo Mayor was the **architectural re-enactment of the foundational myth of Huitzilopochtli's birth on Coatepec** ("Snake Mountain") (Sahagún, *Florentine Codex* Book 3).

The myth (compressed): The mother-goddess Coatlicue, sweeping the temple on Mt. Coatepec, becomes mysteriously pregnant (impregnated by a ball of feathers falling from heaven). Her existing children — her daughter the moon-goddess **Coyolxauhqui** and her 400 sons the **Centzonhuitznahua** (the southern stars) — take this as dishonour and resolve to kill her. As they ascend the mountain to attack, Coatlicue's unborn son **Huitzilopochtli** springs fully-armed from the womb, **kills and dismembers Coyolxauhqui**, throws her body down the side of the mountain, and routs the 400 southern stars. The cosmic order is established: the sun (Huitzilopochtli) rises and triumphs over the moon (Coyolxauhqui) and the stars; the dawn destroys the night.

The Templo Mayor IS Coatepec, architecturally. Huitzilopochtli's shrine at the summit is the **summit of Snake Mountain**. The Coyolxauhqui Stone at the foot of the stairway is the **dismembered moon-goddess at the base of the mountain**. The serpent-head balustrades on the staircase are the snakes of Snake Mountain.

Every **sacrificial victim** killed at the summit had their **head severed and rolled down the staircase past the Coyolxauhqui Stone** — re-enacting Huitzilopochtli's defeat of the moon-goddess on every ritual occasion. The architecture and the ritual together perform the cosmic-foundational myth in continuous repetition (López Austin & López Luján 2009).

### The dual cult — Huitzilopochtli + Tlaloc

The two shrines encode **Mexica cosmological dualism**:
- **Huitzilopochtli (south)**: Mexica tribal god, solar, war, dry season (~Nov–Apr), blood, the *colors red*.
- **Tlaloc (north)**: pan-Mesoamerican rain god (the older deity by far — direct continuity from Teotihuacan), wet season (~May–Oct), water, agricultural, blue.

The two shrines together encode the **complete agricultural-religious year**. The empire required *both* the rains (Tlaloc) and the military supremacy + blood-sacrifice that maintained Huitzilopochtli's sun. Tlaloc was *not* a Mexica-tribal invention but the **single oldest continuously-worshipped deity in Mesoamerica** (back to Teotihuacan c. 200 CE and earlier); the Mexica placed his shrine alongside their own tribal god to **claim continuity with the entire Mesoamerican religious past**.

### Axis mundi

The Templo Mayor stood at the **cosmographic centre** of the Mexica universe: the four cardinal directions of the world radiated from it (the [[four-causeways-of-tenochtitlan|four ceremonial causeways]] of the city — east to Tepeyacac, west to Tacuba, south to Iztapalapa). It was simultaneously the **horizontal centre** and the **vertical axis** (with Huitzilopochtli's shrine at the summit pointing skyward and the offering-caches buried in its foundations pointing into the earth).

## MASSIVE WIN — the architectural reification of a foundational myth

**The Templo Mayor is one of the clearest known cases of monumental architecture as the explicit physical embodiment of a religious narrative.** The building IS the Coatepec myth: the mountain (the pyramid), the dismembered moon-goddess at its base (the Coyolxauhqui Stone), the sun-god triumphant at its summit (the Huitzilopochtli shrine), and the perpetual re-enactment of the cosmic battle (each sacrifice).

Parallel cases:
- **Maya**: [[temple-of-the-inscriptions-palenque|Pakal's pyramid]] embodies the underworld-descent / world-tree-ascent of the deceased king (his sarcophagus lid is the iconographic explanation of the architectural programme).
- **Egyptian**: [[pyramid-of-khufu|Khufu's pyramid]] enacts the pharaoh's solar resurrection (the Pyramid Texts at Unas are the textual explanation of the architectural programme).
- **Christian**: [[church-of-the-holy-sepulchre-jerusalem|Holy Sepulchre]] enacts the Passion-Resurrection narrative — the building IS the empty tomb + Calvary embedded in a single structure.
- **Hindu**: [[brihadeshwara-temple]] (Chola, ~1010 CE) embodies the cosmic mountain Meru with the linga of Shiva at the summit.

The Templo Mayor is one of the cleanest and most-systematically-documented cases of this pattern. The architecture + iconography + ritual + myth form a **single integrated system** documented in Mexica + Spanish primary sources + recovered archaeologically.

**Cross-civilization parallel (state-imperial-temple-as-cosmic-axis):** Templo Mayor ↔ [[ziggurat-of-ur]] (Sumerian state temple) ↔ [[solomons-temple-first]] (Israelite state temple) ↔ [[pyramid-of-khufu]] (Egyptian state monument) ↔ [[karnak-temple-complex]] (New Kingdom imperial cult). All: imperial-state temples at the centre of an imperial capital, encoding the state's foundational divine narrative, periodically expanded by successive rulers. Same imperial-religious-architectural pattern across multiple mutually-uncontacting civilizations.

**The Spanish destruction parallel:** The Templo Mayor + Solomon's First Temple share the unusual distinction of being **state temples deliberately and systematically destroyed by a foreign conquering power as a religious-political act** (the Babylonians in 586 BCE; the Spanish in 1521 CE). In both cases, the destruction symbolizes the end of the polity and inaugurates a long period of religious-political subjugation. The Templo Mayor was not just demolished but actively buried under the cathedral of the conquering religion — a deliberate architectural-religious displacement parallel to the construction of mosques on the Temple Mount.
