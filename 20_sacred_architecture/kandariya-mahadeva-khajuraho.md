# Kandariya Mahadeva Temple, Khajuraho

## What it is

**Kandariya Mahadeva** (Sanskrit: *Kandariya Mahādeva*, "the Great God of the Cave") is the **largest and most architecturally accomplished of the Khajuraho temples** — the apex of the **Nagara-style North-Indian temple architecture**. Built ~1025–1050 CE under the [[chandela-dynasty|Chandela dynasty]] (probably during the reign of [[vidyadhara-chandela|Vidyadhara]], c. 1003–1035), it is dedicated to [[shiva]] as *Mahādeva* — "the Great God".

It is part of the **Khajuraho Group of Monuments** — a complex of ~25 surviving sandstone temples (of an original ~85) built c. 950–1050 CE under successive Chandela kings. Inscribed on UNESCO World Heritage List 1986 (File 240).

Kandariya Mahadeva stands ~31 m tall and ~30 m long. Its principal *shikhara* (the curvilinear-tower spire) clusters with **84 subsidiary peaklets** (*urushringas*) — making the silhouette the most explicit architectural model in surviving Hindu temple architecture of the **clustered-peak form of Mount Meru**.

## History

**~900 CE** — The Chandelas emerge as a major Central Indian Rajput dynasty.

**~950 CE** — Chandela temple-building activity at Khajuraho begins under King Yashovarman (Lakshmana Temple, ~954 CE — the earliest of the Western Group).

**~1003–1035** — Reign of [[vidyadhara-chandela|Vidyadhara]], the Chandela king who repelled the invasions of [[mahmud-of-ghazni|Mahmud of Ghazni]] (1019, 1022) and during whose reign Khajuraho temple-building reached its peak. Kandariya Mahadeva is most plausibly his royal foundation, c. 1025–1050 CE.

**~1100–1200** — Chandela power declines after the Delhi Sultanate's establishment; temple-building at Khajuraho ends.

**~13th–18th centuries** — Khajuraho is largely abandoned. The temples survive remarkably intact (no major iconoclastic destruction) because the site fell off the major trade routes and remained covered by jungle.

**1838** — Captain T. S. Burt, a British engineer of the Bengal Engineers, "rediscovers" the temples after hearing local reports. The temples become known to Western scholarship.

**1986** — UNESCO inscription.

## Architectural form — the Nagara vimana

Kandariya Mahadeva is the most accomplished surviving example of the **Nagara** (North-Indian) temple style, characterised by the curvilinear **shikhara** (spire/tower) over the sanctum:

- **Plan**: A single axis. The temple is a longitudinal sequence: *ardha-mandapa* (entry porch) → *mandapa* (assembly hall) → *maha-mandapa* (great hall) → *antarala* (vestibule) → *garbhagriha* (sanctum). Each successive bay is taller and the *shikhara* over the *garbhagriha* is the highest element.
- **Section**: A stepped plinth (*jagati*) raises the temple ~5 m above ground level. The pilgrim ascends to enter, performing a physical analogue of the cosmological ascent.
- **The *shikhara***: 31 m tall, curvilinear, clustered with 84 subsidiary peaklets (*urushringas*) that step down and outward, creating the silhouette of a clustered mountain peak. The peak is crowned with an *amalaka* (ribbed stone disc) and *kalasha* (finial pot). This is the **clustered-peak form** that [[adam-hardy|Adam Hardy]] (2007) has decoded as the *anekandaka* type — the most architecturally sophisticated implementation of the *shikhara* form.
- **External wall iconography**: Three superimposed registers of figural sculpture wrap the temple — ~870 figures in total — depicting *surasundaris* (celestial nymphs), *mithunas* (amorous couples), deities of the *dikpalas* (directional guardians), and erotically explicit panels (the source of Khajuraho's modern fame).

The **erotic panels** — concentrated on the north and south *jangha* (wall) of the *mandapa* — have been variously interpreted as (a) tantric-Shaiva iconographic programme, (b) celebration of *kama* as one of the four *purusharthas* (life-aims) of Hindu thought, (c) protective-talismanic figures averting the evil eye, or (d) representations of the union of the practitioner with the divine. [[devangana-desai|Desai]] (1996) defends the tantric reading: the explicit panels are concentrated at the *mandapa* junctions where, in tantric ritual geometry, the practitioner crosses cosmic thresholds.

## Theological / cosmological function

Kandariya Mahadeva is one of the most explicit architectural realisations of the **temple-as-cosmic-mountain** principle that [[stella-kramrisch|Stella Kramrisch]] (1946) articulated as the structural theology of the Hindu temple:

- The *garbhagriha* (literally "womb-house") at the temple's centre houses the *linga* of Shiva — the focal point of the cosmos.
- The *shikhara* rising above the *garbhagriha* is the architectural body of **Mount Meru**, the cosmic mountain at the centre of the universe.
- The 84 subsidiary peaklets clustering around the principal *shikhara* model Meru's multi-peaked silhouette.
- The pilgrim's progression — ascending the *jagati*, traversing the *mandapas* toward the *garbhagriha* — physically enacts the journey toward the cosmic centre.
- The external iconographic programme — *surasundaris*, *mithunas*, *dikpalas* — populates the temple-mountain's flanks with the celestial beings who inhabit Meru's slopes.

The Hindu temple is **not a meeting-house for worshippers** (as a synagogue, mosque, or many Protestant churches are) but a **physical residence for the deity and architectural model of the cosmos**. The pilgrim's *darshan* (seeing the deity) is an encounter at the cosmic centre — the temple itself is the vehicle that makes the encounter possible.

## MASSIVE WIN — the Indic temple-mountain convergence

Kandariya Mahadeva is one of the canonical exemplars of the **Indic temple-as-cosmic-mountain motif** ([[theme-cosmic-mountain-as-temple-form]]) — a structural convergence with cross-civilisational architectural cousins:

| Tradition | Building | Style | Date | Cosmic-mountain reading |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sumerian | [[ziggurat-of-ur|Ziggurat of Ur]] | Stepped platform | ~2100 BCE | Temple-as-stairway/mountain |
| Egyptian | [[pyramid-of-khufu|Great Pyramid]] | True pyramid | ~2570 BCE | Solar-mountain-of-resurrection |
| Mesoamerican | [[temple-of-the-feathered-serpent-teotihuacan]] | Stepped pyramid | ~200 CE | Cosmic-mountain-temple |
| Mahayana Buddhist | [[borobudur-stupa-mandala|Borobudur]] | Stepped stupa-mandala | ~800 CE | Trailokya cosmic mountain |
| **Hindu Nagara** | **Kandariya Mahadeva** | **Clustered-peak shikhara** | **~1030 CE** | **Mount Meru as architectural form** |
| Hindu Dravida | [[brihadeeswarar-temple-thanjavur|Brihadeeswarar]] | Stepped-pyramidal vimana | ~1010 CE | Mount Meru — Dravida style |
| Khmer Hindu→Buddhist | [[angkor-wat-temple-complex|Angkor Wat]] | Stepped temple-mountain | ~1120 CE | Meru-with-cosmic-ocean-moat |

**No documented architectural-form transmission at the deep level** between Ur, Khufu, Teotihuacan, and the Indic / South-east-Asian cluster. **The same theological problem produces convergent architectural solutions.** Within the Indic+SE-Asian cluster, by contrast, transmission is dense and documented: Sanchi → Borobudur → Angkor → Khajuraho is a continuous tradition with shared scriptural and iconographic vocabulary.

What Kandariya Mahadeva adds to the convergence-cluster: the **highest-density implementation of the multi-peaked Meru silhouette** in surviving stone. The 84 *urushringas* clustering around the central *shikhara* are without architectural parallel — no other Hindu temple makes the clustered-peak form so explicit.
