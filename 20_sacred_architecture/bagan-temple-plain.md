# Bagan Temple Plain

## What it is

The **Bagan temple plain** (modern Burmese: *Bagan*; older English: *Pagan*) is one of the **densest sacred-architectural landscapes on Earth** — an alluvial plain ~104 km² on the east bank of the Irrawaddy River in central Myanmar, containing over **2,200 surviving Buddhist temples and stupas** (from an original estimated ~10,000+ built during the 11th–13th centuries CE).

The structures were built during the **Bagan Kingdom** (1044–1287 CE) — the unified Burmese state founded by [[anawrahta|King Anawrahta]] in 1044. The Bagan kings progressively conquered the surrounding Mon, Pyu, and other polities, and concentrated unprecedented royal and lay resources into Buddhist temple-building. By the late 12th century, Bagan had eclipsed all earlier Southeast Asian Buddhist landscapes in monument density.

Bagan is inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List 2019 (File 1588) — a long-delayed inscription, previously blocked by the Myanmar military government.

## History

**~9th c.** — Bagan exists as a small Pyu-Burman polity on the Irrawaddy. Limited monument-building.

**1044** — [[anawrahta|Anawrahta]] (r. 1044–1077) becomes king of Bagan; begins unifying the surrounding kingdoms.

**1057** — Anawrahta conquers the **Mon kingdom of Thaton**, capturing the Mon king Manuha and bringing him to Bagan along with ~30,000 Mon artisans, monks, scribes, and craftsmen. With them comes the **Pali Tipitaka** (Theravada Buddhist canonical scriptures) in 30 elephant-loads. This is the moment of the **Theravada turn** in Bagan religious history: the Mahayana / Tantric / nat-worship synthesis previously dominant gives way to a Theravada orientation under Mon influence.

**~1057–1100** — Anawrahta and his successors begin the major temple-building programme. Principal monuments: Shwezigon Pagoda (begun under Anawrahta ~1059, completed under Kyansittha), the Ananda Temple (Kyansittha, 1090–1105), the Thatbyinnyu (Alaungsithu, ~1144).

**1084–1112** — **Reign of Kyansittha**, the second great Bagan king. The Ananda Temple (~1090–1105) is the apex of his patronage.

**1174–1211** — **Reign of Narapatisithu**. The Sulamani (1183) and Dhammayazika (1196) are the major foundations.

**~1100–1287** — The temple-building peak. Over the 11th–13th centuries, more than 10,000 temples, stupas, and monasteries are built across the plain. The density of monument-construction has no clear parallel in world architectural history.

**1277–1287** — **Mongol invasions under Kublai Khan**. Repeated raids destabilise Bagan. The final fall of the unified Bagan Kingdom is conventionally dated 1287. Many monuments are damaged; the kingdom fragments into successor polities (Pinya, Sagaing, Ava).

**14th–18th c.** — Bagan continues as a regional Buddhist pilgrimage centre but the imperial monument-building era ends. Many monuments deteriorate; flood, earthquake, and lightning damage progressively reduce the standing inventory.

**1975** — Major earthquake damages many monuments.

**1990s–2010s** — Controversial Myanmar military-junta-era restoration: hundreds of temples are restored using modern materials (cement, painted stucco, reconstructed superstructures of disputed authenticity). UNESCO and ICOMOS objected to the methods; the restoration is widely considered to compromise the archaeological integrity of much of the plain.

**2016** — Major earthquake damages ~400 monuments.

**2019** — UNESCO World Heritage inscription, finally — with management plans for more rigorous conservation methods.

## Architectural form — the temple-stupa landscape

Bagan's architectural inventory comprises three principal types:

- **Stupas (*zedi* in Burmese)** — solid bell-shaped monuments containing relics; the principal Theravada form. The **Shwezigon Pagoda** (begun 1059) is the canonical Bagan stupa, with its distinctive **bell-shaped *hti* (umbrella)** finial that becomes the template for later Burmese stupa architecture across the country.
- **Temples (*pahto* in Burmese)** — hollow structures containing a central sanctum with Buddha images. The **Ananda Temple** (~1090–1105) is the masterpiece, with its Greek-cross plan (an unusual feature, possibly indicating Mahayana / Indian Vimalakirti influence), four standing Buddhas at the cardinal directions, and ~1,500 small terracotta plaques narrating Jataka tales.
- **Monasteries (*kyaung* in Burmese)** — residential structures for the *sangha*. These were predominantly wooden and have not survived; the brick monastic foundations indicate scale.

The principal building material is **brick** — locally fired, often unglazed but sometimes carrying **terracotta plaques** with Jataka scenes (the Ananda has ~1,500; the Mingalazedi has ~561). Bagan is one of the principal world centres of **brick monumental architecture**.

The temple-form combines:
- **Square plinth + concentric ambulatory** (Indic temple cosmology — Meru-form)
- **Stepped-pyramidal upper terraces** (an architectural cousin of the Borobudur-form and the Khmer temple-mountain)
- **Crowning *sikhara* + *hti*** (the *sikhara* is an Indic North-Indian-Nagara loan; the *hti* is the bell-shaped umbrella-finial that becomes the signature Burmese stupa-crown)

The **temple-stupa landscape principle** — distinct from a single monumental temple — is Bagan's signature contribution to sacred architecture: rather than concentrating religious-architectural energy in one or two great monuments, the Bagan polity distributed its merit-making across thousands of foundations, each individually significant, collectively forming a **landscape-as-cosmological-field**.

## Theological / cosmological function

Bagan's monuments are predominantly **merit-making foundations** (Pali *puñña-kamma*): each temple or stupa is sponsored by a king, queen, minister, merchant, or lay community as an act generating *merit* (good karma) for the donor's future rebirths. The donor's inscription is often preserved on the foundation stone, naming the patron and the spiritual purpose.

The **architectural form** combines Theravada and earlier Mahayana / Pyu elements:
- **The stupa as cosmic-mountain** ([[theme-stupa-as-cosmic-mountain]]) — the bell-shape models the cosmic egg / Meru-form
- **The temple as Meru-model** — the central tower above the sanctum models the cosmic axis
- **The Jataka programme** — the terracotta plaques on temple exteriors retell the Buddha's previous lives, transforming the temple into a 3-D Buddhist curriculum

The **landscape as cosmographic field** is Bagan's distinctive theological move: the plain itself becomes a **mandala-at-landscape-scale**, with the major temple-stupas arranged in cosmologically-meaningful positions relative to the Irrawaddy River (the cosmic Ganges), Mount Popa (the volcanic peak ~50 km SE, sacred to the indigenous *nat* spirits), and the cardinal directions.

## MASSIVE WIN — landscape-scale Buddhist architectural density

Bagan is the **canonical example in world sacred architecture of landscape-scale architectural density** — a single religious tradition concentrating tens of thousands of monuments in a single ~100 km² alluvial plain over ~250 years.

Comparative cases:

| Tradition | Site | Density | Period |
|---|---|---|---|
| Buddhist | **Bagan** | ~10,000 monuments / 100 km² / 250 years | ~1044–1287 CE |
| Buddhist | Anuradhapura, Sri Lanka | hundreds of monuments / 40 km² / ~1000 years | ~3rd c. BCE–11th c. CE |
| Buddhist | Bodh Gaya circuit, Bihar | hundreds / smaller area / ~1500 years | ~3rd c. BCE–13th c. CE |
| Hindu | Khajuraho temples cluster | ~85 originally, ~25 surviving / small area / 100 years | ~950–1050 CE |
| Egyptian | Theban necropolis (Valley of the Kings + Karnak + Luxor) | hundreds of tombs + temples / ~10 km² / ~1500 years | ~2050–1077 BCE |
| Christian | Lalibela (Ethiopia) | 11 rock-cut churches / very small area / ~50 years | ~12th–13th c. CE |

**Bagan is unique in the cluster for the *density-per-time* ratio** — most temples are built within a single 250-year imperial window. No other religious tradition in world history concentrated comparable architectural energy in such a brief period across so dense an area. The closest cousin is the **medieval European cathedral-building boom** (~1100–1300 CE) — but the European cathedrals are far fewer in number (~100 great cathedrals across all of Europe) and far more dispersed.

Bagan's landscape-scale density is partially a function of the Theravada merit-making theology: where Hindu and Christian temple-patronage tended to concentrate on a few great monuments per polity, Theravada merit-economics rewarded ANY sized foundation. The result is the most extensive surviving brick-architectural landscape from any single religious tradition.

## The Mongol invasions and the end of Bagan

The fall of Bagan (~1277–1287 CE) is one of the principal cases of **monumental religious-architectural civilisation collapse** in world history:
- Bagan was the dominant SE-Asian Buddhist polity ~1100–1250.
- The Mongol invasions under Kublai Khan (1277, 1283, 1287) collapsed the unified Bagan state in ~10 years.
- The temple-building stops abruptly. Later Burmese polities (Pinya, Sagaing, Ava, Toungoo, Konbaung) build at Mandalay, Ava, etc. but never reach Bagan's density.

The architectural landscape survives — the monuments are not destroyed in the invasion, just abandoned at the imperial-patronage level. This makes Bagan one of the best-preserved cases of a *complete* monumental religious landscape from a single ~250-year window in world history.
