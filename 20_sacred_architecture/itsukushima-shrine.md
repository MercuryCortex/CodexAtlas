# Itsukushima Shrine (Miyajima)

## What it is

**Itsukushima Shrine** (厳島神社, *Itsukushima Jinja*) is a [[tradition-shinto|Shinto]] shrine on the island of **Itsukushima** (commonly called **Miyajima** — "Shrine Island") in the Seto Inland Sea off Hiroshima. It is dedicated to the **three Munakata goddesses** — [[ichikishima-hime|Ichikishima-hime]], [[tagori-hime|Tagori-hime]], and [[tagitsu-hime|Tagitsu-hime]] — the patron deities of seafarers and the principal *kami* of the maritime Seto Inland passage.

Its defining feature is that the **entire shrine complex is built on stilts over the tidal flat**, so that at high tide the buildings appear to **float on the sea**. The famous **giant vermilion *torii* gate** stands ~200 m offshore, also on stilts, **anchored only by its own weight and gravity** — at high tide it stands in the water; at low tide visitors can walk out to its base.

The complex is the principal architectural monument of **Heian-period aristocratic Shinto** — built in its present form by [[taira-no-kiyomori|Taira no Kiyomori]] in 1168 as the personal devotional centre of the Taira clan. The architecture deploys the **Heian aristocratic *shinden-zukuri* (palace-style)** vocabulary — long covered corridors, vermilion lacquered columns, hip-and-gable roofs — applied to a Shinto sacred-island programme.

UNESCO World Heritage (1996, File 776).

## History

- **593 CE** — traditional founding by the local Saeki clan. The first shrine is dedicated to the three Munakata goddesses, in obedience to a divine command from Amaterasu (per the *Nihon Shoki* the three sisters were assigned to islands in the Seto Inland Sea).
- **806 CE** — [[kūkai|Kūkai]] (founder of [[tradition-shingon-buddhism|Shingon Buddhism]]) traditionally established Buddhist worship on **Mt. Misen** (the principal mountain of the island) — beginning the island's long Shinbutsu-shūgō (Shinto-Buddhist fusion) tradition.
- **1168** — [[taira-no-kiyomori|Taira no Kiyomori]], having risen to be the most powerful military aristocrat of late-Heian Japan, **rebuilds Itsukushima in its present form**, applying Heian aristocratic *shinden-zukuri* architectural vocabulary to the shrine. The Taira adopt Itsukushima as their clan-deity (*ujigami*) shrine.
- **1180s** — the **Genpei War** (1180–1185). The Taira are defeated by the Minamoto; Kiyomori dies in 1181; the Taira are exterminated at the Battle of Dan-no-ura (1185). Their patronage of Itsukushima ends, but the architectural programme survives.
- **1207, 1223, 1325** — fires + storms repeatedly damage the shrine; each rebuild preserves the Kiyomori-era form.
- **1555** — the **Battle of Miyajima** between Mōri Motonari and Sue Harukata fought on the island; Motonari, victorious, becomes the new patron and undertakes major restorations.
- **Edo period** — peaceful continuity; Itsukushima becomes a popular pilgrimage destination alongside Ise.
- **1868** — the Meiji-era *shinbutsu bunri* (forced separation of Shinto and Buddhism) divides the island's Shinto shrines from the Buddhist complexes (Daiganji, Daishōin, Misen); the Shinto and Buddhist components are administered separately from this point.
- **1875** — major restoration of the *torii* (the present gate is the **8th in the line**, completed 1875).
- **1996** — UNESCO World Heritage inscription.
- **2019–2022** — most recent major restoration of the great *torii*.

## Architectural form — the tidal shrine

The principal architectural innovation is **construction on stilts over the tidal flat** of Itsukushima Bay. The complex includes:

- **Main shrine (*Honden*)** — the principal sanctum, on the inner platform.
- **Worship hall (*Haiden*)** — adjacent.
- **Marodo Shrine (*Marōdo Jinja*)** — secondary shrine for visiting deities (the "guest" *kami*).
- **Noh stage (*Nōbutai*)** — a freestanding stage for *kagura* and noh performance; one of the few medieval noh stages over water.
- **Covered corridors (*kairō*)** — ~280 m of covered colonnade connecting the buildings, painted vermilion, with hip-and-gable roofs.
- **Five-storey pagoda (*Gojū-no-tō*)** — added 1407; Buddhist component of the medieval Shinbutsu-shūgō ensemble.
- **Senjōkaku (*Toyokuni Jinja*)** — a great 1587 hall commissioned by Toyotomi Hideyoshi but never completed; one of the largest wooden Heian-style halls in Japan.
- **The Great *Torii*** (大鳥居, *Ōtorii*) — the iconic offshore gate; ~16.6 m high, ~24 m wide, ~60 t weight; **anchored only by its own gravity** (the four legs penetrate the tidal flat without foundation). The gate is the 8th in the line; principal columns are **single camphor trees** (*Cinnamomum camphora*) of ~600-year age.

The buildings are all painted **vermilion** (the colour of yang and exorcism) and decorated with **white lattices, black trim, and gold lacquerwork**. The result is unlike any other major Shinto shrine — the Ise-Izumo austerity is wholly replaced by Heian-aristocratic colour and ornament.

## Theological function — three sea-goddesses and tidal architecture

The shrine's patron deities are the **three Munakata goddesses**, born from the breath of [[amaterasu|Amaterasu]] when she contested ownership of the *Yasakani-no-Magatama* jewel with her brother [[susanoo|Susanoo]] (per the *Kojiki* and *Nihon Shoki*). They are the patron deities of:

- **Maritime safe-passage** through the Seto Inland Sea — the principal sea-route between western and eastern Japan.
- **Travelers** generally.
- **The Taira clan** (their *ujigami*) and through the Taira, the warrior aristocracy of Heian and post-Heian Japan.

The architectural placement on stilts over the tidal flat is theologically meaningful: the shrine **does not stand on the sacred island itself** (which historically could not be defiled by birth, death, or contact with the impure) but is **suspended between the island and the sea**, a literal liminal position. The high-tide flooding under the shrine is part of the design — the sea itself participates in the shrine's spatial logic.

## MASSIVE WIN — sacred island and tidal architecture

Itsukushima's combination of features makes it one of the most distinctive sacred-architectural conceits in world religion:

1. **The whole island is sacred** — historically no births, deaths, or burials were permitted on Miyajima; pregnant women and the sick were ferried to the mainland; deaths and burials happened off-island. The architectural programme exists **in dialogue with the island's purity**, not as a building on it.
2. **The shrine is over water** — built on stilts over the tidal flat, so it floats at high tide and is approachable on foot at low tide. The architectural logic is **tidal** in a way no other major world religious building is.
3. **The great *torii* stands in the sea** — the principal sacred gate is offshore, in the water itself. The pilgrim arriving by boat **passes through the *torii* to approach the shrine**, the sea-route forming the principal axis of approach.
4. **Architectural-ornamental Heian-aristocratic vocabulary** applied to Shinto — Itsukushima is the principal site where the *shinden-zukuri* (aristocratic palace) architectural language is deployed for sacred-Shinto programme. The result is more ornamentally and chromatically rich than any other major Shinto shrine.

The combination of **sacred island + sea-stilt construction + offshore *torii* + Heian-aristocratic ornamental programme** is **unique among world religious sites**. Comparative gestures (sacred islands in Christian / Hindu / Polynesian traditions, sacred-water shrines elsewhere) exist, but none combines all four features in a single integrated programme.

The shrine is also one of the **principal surviving Shinbutsu-shūgō architectural ensembles** — the historical Shinto-Buddhist fusion that prevailed in Japan from the Heian through the Edo period and was forcibly separated by Meiji legislation. The five-storey pagoda, the Daiganji Buddhist temple at the shrine's foot, and the Daishōin / Misen Buddhist complexes on the mountain above are all administered separately today but **architecturally remain in dialogue** as components of the once-unified Shinbutsu site.
