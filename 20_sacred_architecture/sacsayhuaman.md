# Sacsayhuamán

## What it is

**Sacsayhuamán** is a vast Inca ceremonial complex on the hill immediately north of Cusco. Built between c. 1440 and 1532 CE under [[pachacuti-inca-yupanqui|Pachacuti]] and his successors, it is the **most famous example of Inca cyclopean stonework** anywhere in the empire. Its three monumental zigzagging defensive walls, built of polygonal stones up to **150–200 tonnes**, were the largest building project in pre-Columbian South America after the Inca empire's own creation.

The Spanish, encountering it in 1533, called it a "fortress" (*fortaleza*) — and the name has stuck in popular imagination. **But it is not, primarily, a fortress.** It is a major ceremonial complex with the heart of Inca state religion: a series of plazas, sacred precincts, temples, and observatories overlooking Cusco from above. It also had defensive function (it sheltered the [[manco-inca-yupanqui|Manco Inca]] rebellion against the Spanish in 1536), but defense was secondary to ritual purpose.

Most of Sacsayhuamán is **gone** — the Spanish used it as the principal stone quarry for colonial Cusco for ~200 years after the conquest. **What survives** (~20% of the original mass) is mostly the cyclopean lowest course of the three terraced walls, which were too large to move. The walls visible today are the **bedrock** of what was a vastly larger sacred complex.

## History

### Pre-Inca history

The Sacsayhuamán hill (Quechua name *Saqsaywaman* — "satiated falcon" or "speckled falcon") was a sacred huaca long before the Incas. The hill is the **northern apex of Cusco**, which itself is the central point of [[tawantinsuyu]] (the four-quartered Inca empire). Cusco was conceived as a **puma-shaped city**, with Sacsayhuamán as the **puma's head** (the zigzag walls representing the puma's teeth) — though this iconographic reading is debated and may be a post-conquest interpretation by Garcilaso.

### Inca construction

Construction began under [[pachacuti-inca-yupanqui|Pachacuti]] (r. 1438–1471) as part of his comprehensive rebuilding of Cusco into an imperial capital. Construction continued under his successors [[topa-inca-yupanqui]] (1471–1493) and [[huayna-capac]] (1493–1527). It was **never fully completed** — work was still in progress when the Spanish arrived in 1533.

Cieza de León (1547 eyewitness, writing 1553–54) reports that "more than 20,000 men" worked simultaneously on Sacsayhuamán at its construction peak — drawn from across the empire through the **mita** corvée labour system. The construction took ~70+ years and would have continued without the conquest.

### The 1536 siege

In **April 1536** [[manco-inca-yupanqui]] (puppet Inca emperor installed by the Spanish but turned rebel) launched a general Inca uprising against the Spanish occupation of Cusco. He used Sacsayhuamán as the rebellion's main base — the still-intact ceremonial complex served as a natural high-ground fortress overlooking the Spanish-held city below. The siege lasted ~10 months, with the Inca briefly capturing most of Cusco except for a small Spanish redoubt around the main plaza.

In **May 1536** the Spanish under [[juan-pizarro]] (brother of Francisco) launched a costly assault on the complex. Juan Pizarro was killed by a thrown stone. The Spanish eventually took Sacsayhuamán; many of its Inca defenders threw themselves from the towers rather than be captured. The Andean condor (legend says) was named the bird of the soul because of the souls released at this moment — the Inca defenders becoming birds in the sky.

This event gave Sacsayhuamán its enduring (mis)identification as a fortress.

### Quarrying

After the 1536 siege, the Spanish authorities of colonial Cusco used Sacsayhuamán as the principal stone source for building the new colonial capital — most of the colonial-period stonework in Cusco's churches, viceregal palace, and houses comes from Sacsayhuamán. The smaller stones were taken first; the cyclopean blocks (~50+ tonnes) were left because they could not be moved. By ~1700 the visible upper levels were essentially gone.

The Spanish authorities issued multiple decrees against further destruction (interestingly — even the colonial administrators recognized the loss); these decrees were ignored. The site's current state of ruination is principally colonial-quarrying damage, not earthquake or weathering.

## Architectural form

### The three terraced walls

The signature feature: **three terraced cyclopean walls** running ~360 m along the north edge of the central plaza, ascending the hillside in three levels. Each wall **zigzags** with sharp angles (offering defensive flanking sightlines and ritual cosmographic shape; the puma's-teeth interpretation).

Stone sizes:
- Largest single stones: **150–200 tonnes** (the corner-blocks of the lowest course).
- Typical stones: 5–20 tonnes.
- All stones are **polygonal** (not rectangular) and **fitted without mortar**, with joints so precise the surfaces appear seamless.

The cyclopean stone-cutting and fitting technique is the **type-specimen of Inca high-imperial stonework**. Protzen (1993) demonstrated experimentally that the technique uses repeated **iterative refitting**: the master stone is cut to approximate shape, lifted into place against the previous course, then the underside is **traced from below**, removed, recut, refitted, and the cycle repeats until the joint is perfect. This is incredibly labour-intensive but achievable without metal tools.

The largest stones were **quarried at sites ~22–30 km away** and transported overland by an estimated 1500–2000 labourers per stone, on rolling logs and earth ramps. The transport is documented in the chronicles.

### The central plaza and upper structures

Above the cyclopean walls (now mostly gone) stood:
- **Three large towers** — Muyuq Marka (round, central, possibly the temple of the sun), Sallaq Marka, Paukar Marka. Foundations partially preserved.
- A great **trapezoidal plaza** (~300 × 100 m) on top — the largest open ceremonial space in the empire.
- **Stone reservoirs** and **carved-bedrock features** (the Inca Throne / Cusilluchayuq), some of which survive on the bedrock outcrop opposite the walls.

### Astronomical features

The complex contains alignments to multiple solar phenomena:
- **Solstice sightlines** from the great plaza.
- **Carved bedrock seats and platforms** likely for astronomical observation.
- Connection to the [[ceque-system-cusco|ceque system]] (the 41 radial sacred lines from the Coricancha) — multiple ceques originated or passed through Sacsayhuamán.

## Theological / cosmological function

### Imperial-religious centre

Sacsayhuamán's primary function was **imperial-religious-ceremonial**. The major Inca calendrical festivals — particularly **Inti Raymi** (the June-solstice festival of the sun) — culminated at Sacsayhuamán's great plaza, with the emperor presiding. The complex contained temples to **Inti** (the sun), the moon, the stars, and Wiraqocha (the creator god). It was the **northern-cardinal complement** to the Coricancha (the sun temple in central Cusco): Coricancha was the centre, Sacsayhuamán was the high-ceremonial counterpart above.

The modern **Inti Raymi reconstruction** (held annually on 24 June, the southern-hemisphere winter solstice / Inca new year) is performed at Sacsayhuamán's restored plaza — drawing on the Garcilaso (1609) eyewitness description for its choreography. The contemporary festival is part historical-reconstruction, part political-cultural assertion of Andean indigenous identity; ~100,000 attendees annually.

### Puma symbolism

Garcilaso (Book VII, *Comentarios Reales*) interpreted Cusco as a **puma-shaped city** with Sacsayhuamán as its head. The interpretation: **the city is a stone animal** — a sacred landscape, in literal animal form, lived in by humans. The puma was associated with imperial-Inca kingship.

The interpretation has been debated. Critics note that aerial views of Cusco don't strongly resemble a puma except in a stylized way; supporters note that **the city was not meant to be viewed from above but inhabited from within** — and that the puma is a conceptual, not visual, identification. Bauer (2004) treats the puma identification as authentic Inca-period but possibly post-Pachacuti elaboration.

Either way, the conceptual point — **city as animate body, sacred geographically organized** — is genuine Andean.

### Ceque system

Sacsayhuamán was the originating point of **multiple ceques** (radial sacred lines from the [[coricancha-cusco|Coricancha]]) extending outward across the imperial landscape. Each ceque was a sightline studded with **huacas** (shrines, springs, peaks, marked stones). The ceque system is one of the most distinctive and conceptually-sophisticated **sacred-geographic systems** in world religion — and Sacsayhuamán sat at its physical-symbolic centre alongside the Coricancha.

## MASSIVE WIN — the ceque system as cross-tradition sacred-geography axis programme

**The Cusco ceque system** (Bauer 1998) is a network of **41 radial sacred lines** extending out from the [[coricancha-cusco|Coricancha]] (the central sun temple of Cusco) across the imperial landscape. Each ceque was a sightline studded with **huacas** (~328 ritual sites documented in [[cobo-historia-del-nuevo-mundo|Cobo's *Historia*]]) — peaks, springs, marked stones, carved boulders. Different social groups (ayllus) in Cusco maintained different ceques + their huacas in an integrated ritual calendar. The ceques were associated with calendrical periods, water sources, and astronomical directions.

This is a **sacred-geographic axis programme** with strong cross-tradition parallels:
- **Indic vāstu-puruṣa-maṇḍala** — the cosmic-grid sacred-geometry that organizes Hindu temple placement and city plans ([[vastu-purusha-mandala]]).
- **Egyptian temple-axis programmes** — the Nile-aligned procession of Karnak-Luxor-Theban-necropolis, the cardinal alignments of the Giza pyramids ([[theme-sacred-geometry-giza]]).
- **Mesoamerican causeway systems** — the four cardinal causeways radiating from Tenochtitlan to the lake shores; the sacbé network of Yucatec Maya cities.
- **Chinese fengshui + ley-line geomancy** — the *qi*-energy axis programmes organizing imperial Chinese sacred geography.
- **British ley-line tradition** (modern; speculative): Alfred Watkins's 1925 *The Old Straight Track*; not Tier-1, but the conceptual analogue.

The Andean ceque system is the **best-documented pre-modern non-Eurasian example of a sacred-geographic radial-axis programme** organizing an empire's religious-spatial life. Bauer (1998) is the standard treatment.

**Cyclopean-masonry cross-tradition parallel:** Inca cyclopean walls (15th c. CE) ↔ Mycenaean cyclopean walls (~1300 BCE — Tiryns, Mycenae) ↔ Hattusa Hittite cyclopean walls (~1400 BCE) ↔ Malta megalithic temples ([[malta-temples-c-3600-2500-bce]], ~3500 BCE) ↔ Pre-pottery Neolithic [[gobekli-tepe|Göbekli Tepe]] (~9500 BCE) ↔ Sardinian nuraghi (~1500 BCE) ↔ Easter Island moai platforms (~1300 CE). Multiple mutually-uncontacting civilizations independently developed **megalithic / cyclopean construction** for sacred-monumental purposes. The architectural-cultural class is genuinely universal.

**Imperial-state-temple destruction:** The systematic Spanish destruction of Sacsayhuamán as quarry-fodder parallels the systematic Babylonian destruction of [[solomons-temple-first|Solomon's First Temple]] (586 BCE), the Roman destruction of the [[solomons-temple-second|Second Temple]] (70 CE), and the Spanish destruction of [[templo-mayor-tenochtitlan|Templo Mayor]] (1521). Same imperial pattern: conquering empire dismantles the previous polity's central religious-architectural symbol both as resource extraction and as religious displacement.
