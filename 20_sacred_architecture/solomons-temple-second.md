# Second Temple

## What it is

The **Second Temple** was the central Yahwistic cult-temple in Jerusalem from **~516 BCE to 70 CE** — a span of nearly 600 years across three major political eras (Persian, Hellenistic-Hasmonean, Roman). It was built on the site of [[solomons-temple-first|Solomon's First Temple]] after the Babylonian Exile, dramatically expanded by [[herod-the-great|Herod the Great]] from ~20 BCE, and destroyed by the Romans under [[titus|Titus]] in **70 CE** during the First Jewish-Roman War.

It is the architectural setting of:

- **The Hebrew Bible's post-exilic books** (Ezra, Nehemiah, Haggai, Zechariah, Malachi).
- **Second-Temple Judaism** (the matrix of Pharisees, Sadducees, Essenes, Apocalypticists, Hellenizers, Zealots).
- **The Gospels** — Jesus + the Apostolic generation teach and worship here.
- **The destruction-event** whose architectural absence is the negative space around which **rabbinic Judaism** organises.

The **Western Wall** (HaKotel) is what physically survives — a portion of the western retaining-wall of Herod's expanded platform, not of the Temple itself.

## The two phases — Zerubbabel and Herod

### Phase 1: Zerubbabel's Temple (~520–516 BCE → ~19 BCE)

After Cyrus the Great's [[event-cyrus-conquest-of-babylon-539-bce|conquest of Babylon (539 BCE)]] permitted the return of exiled Judahites (Ezra 1, the "Cyrus Decree"), [[zerubbabel|Zerubbabel]] led a returnees' party back to Jerusalem and began rebuilding the Temple in ~520 BCE. The work was completed in **516 BCE** (Ezra 6:15).

This Zerubbabel Temple was a **modest building** — Ezra 3:12 records that elders who had seen the First Temple wept at the foundation of the new, because the new was so much smaller. It nonetheless served as the operative sacrificial-cult temple for ~500 years, with successive Persian, Hellenistic, and Hasmonean modifications.

In **167 BCE**, the Seleucid [[antiochus-iv-epiphanes|Antiochus IV Epiphanes]] desecrated the Temple by installing an altar to Olympian Zeus — the "abomination of desolation" of Daniel 11:31. The **Maccabean Revolt** (167–164 BCE; [[event-maccabean-revolt-167-164-bce]]) followed, and the rededication of the purified altar on 25 Kislev 164 BCE is the origin of **Hanukkah** (the Festival of Lights).

### Phase 2: Herod's Temple (~19 BCE → 70 CE)

Beginning in ~19 BCE (Antiquities 15.380), [[herod-the-great|Herod the Great]] launched the most ambitious religious-architectural project of the Eastern Mediterranean of his era. He **did not demolish the Zerubbabel Temple**; he disassembled and rebuilt it in place over decades, ensuring continuous cult-operation. He also **massively expanded the Temple Mount platform** — doubling its area to ~144,000 m² by adding immense retaining-wall enclosures (of which the **Western Wall** is one).

The Herodian Temple was famous for its scale and finish. Josephus (*Jewish War* 5.5.6, §222–224):

> "The exterior of the building wanted nothing that could astound either mind or eye. For, being covered on all sides with massive plates of gold, the sun was no sooner up than it radiated so fiery a flash that persons straining to look at it were compelled to turn away, as from solar rays. To approaching strangers it appeared from a distance like a snow-clad mountain; for, where it was not gilt, it was exceeding white."

Detailed architecture: Mishnah Tractate *Middot* (~200 CE), compiled from priestly memory of the destroyed building — the **most detailed surviving architectural description of any First-Century building in the Eastern Mediterranean**.

## Theological-architectural detail

The plan replicates the First Temple's tripartite structure: **Ulam → Hekal → Devir**, with concentric courts of increasing accessibility-restriction beyond:

| Court | Accessible to |
|---|---|
| Court of Gentiles (outer) | All visitors |
| Court of Women | Jewish men + women |
| Court of Israel | Jewish men in ritual purity |
| Court of Priests | Levitical priests |
| The Temple proper (Hekal) | Priests in service |
| The Holy of Holies (Devir) | High Priest, Yom Kippur only |

A surviving **Soreg-balustrade inscription** (now in the Istanbul Archaeology Museum) warned non-Jews not to pass beyond the Court of Gentiles on pain of death — confirming the spatial-purity programme that Josephus and the New Testament both describe.

The Holy of Holies of the Second Temple was **empty** — the Ark of the Covenant had been lost in or before the First Temple's destruction. The empty *Devir* is one of the most theologically charged architectural absences in religious history; later Rabbinic theology read it as the place where the Shekhinah dwells without physical sign.

## Destruction — 9 Av 70 CE

In the **First Jewish-Roman War** (66–73 CE), Roman forces under [[titus|Titus]] besieged Jerusalem in spring 70 CE. The Temple was burned on the **9th of Av** (per Talmud Taanit 29a; Josephus dates the burning to the 10th of Av in *Jewish War* 6.4.5, §250) — by traditional rabbinic memory the **same date as the destruction of the First Temple in 586 BCE**, making the 9th of Av (Tisha B'Av) the principal day of mourning in the Jewish liturgical calendar.

The Roman Empire celebrated the destruction on the **Arch of Titus** in Rome (built 81 CE), whose interior relief panel depicts the **Menorah, the Table of Showbread, and the Silver Trumpets** being carried off as spoils. The Arch of Titus is the **only surviving contemporary iconographic depiction of the Temple's cult-implements**.

## The rabbinic-Judaic turn

The destruction of the Temple terminated the sacrificial cult and produced **the deepest reorganisation in the history of Judaism**. The Pharisaic-rabbinic response — formulated by [[yohanan-ben-zakkai|Yohanan ben Zakkai]] in the post-70 Yavneh academy, codified in the Mishnah (~200 CE) and Talmud (~500 CE) — replaces:

- **Sacrifice** with **prayer** (the Amidah's structure mirrors the Temple's tamid daily offerings).
- **Priesthood** with **rabbinic teacher**.
- **Temple-as-architectural-locus** with **synagogue + study-house** (and ultimately with the home, on Shabbat and Passover).

Avot d'Rabbi Nathan 4 records Yohanan ben Zakkai's foundational ruling: "Acts of loving-kindness replace sacrifice." Modern Judaism — Orthodox, Conservative, Reform, all — is in a recognisable sense **a religion organised around the absence of this building**.

## MASSIVE WIN — the architecture's afterlife in Christianity and Islam

- **Christianity**: the Temple's destruction is **prophesied by Jesus** in the Synoptic Apocalypse (Mark 13, Matt 24, Luke 21) before the fact, and is **central to Christian self-definition**: 1 Corinthians 3:16 ("you are God's temple"), John 2:19–21 ("destroy this temple…"), Hebrews 9–10 (Christ as high priest in the heavenly temple), Revelation 21:22 (the New Jerusalem **has no temple**, because the Lamb is the temple). Christianity is **architecturally born from the building's destruction**.
- **Islam**: the [[dome-of-the-rock-jerusalem|Dome of the Rock]] (691 CE) is built on the Temple Mount over the Foundation Stone — making the **same physical sacred locus** the heart of Islamic Jerusalem (and the Templars' Crusader HQ, who occupied al-Aqsa as *Templum Salomonis* 1119–1187).
- **[[tradition-knights-templar|The Knights Templar]]** (1119) take their name from the Temple of Solomon — by way of their Jerusalem HQ at al-Aqsa, popularly identified with the Solomonic site.

No single building has had a more consequential afterlife in religious architecture and theology.
