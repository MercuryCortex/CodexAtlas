# Hōryū-ji (Temple of the Flourishing Law)

## What it is

**Hōryū-ji** (法隆寺 — "Temple of the Flourishing Law") is a Buddhist temple complex in **Ikaruga, Nara Prefecture, Japan**, traditionally founded c. **607 CE** by **[[prince-shotoku|Prince Shōtoku]]** (574–622), the Asuka-era prince-regent who established Japanese Buddhism as a state religion. Its principal Western Precinct buildings — the **five-storey pagoda, the *Kondō* (golden hall), the central gate, and the surrounding cloister** — are **the oldest surviving wooden buildings in the world**. Dendrochronological dating places the principal timbers at:

- **Central Gate (*Chūmon*)** — principal timbers ~594 CE.
- **Pagoda** — principal timbers ~670–680 CE (rebuild after the 670 fire; the pagoda's central pillar is older — ~594 CE, possibly reused from the original 607 structure).
- **Kondō (Golden Hall)** — ~700 CE.
- **Cloister** — ~711 CE.

The dendrochronology confirms what the *Nihon Shoki* records: that the original 607 Hōryū-ji **burned in 670** and was **rebuilt by 711**. Even the rebuild structures are now over **1300 years old** — older than any other surviving wooden building anywhere.

Hōryū-ji was Japan's **first UNESCO World Heritage site** (1993, File 660).

The temple is the **principal architectural and cultic monument of Prince Shōtoku** — whose semi-legendary biography founds Japanese imperial-Buddhist statehood — and the **earliest principal surviving exemplar of Asuka-period East Asian Buddhist temple architecture**, transmitted via **Baekje Korea**.

## History

- **538 CE** — Buddhism formally transmitted to Japan when **King Sŏng of [[baekje-kingdom|Baekje]]** sends a Buddhist mission to Emperor Kinmei, including a gilt-bronze Shakyamuni statue, sutras, and (the traditional account holds) some texts on Buddhist temple architecture. This is the **canonical date of the Baekje-to-Japan Buddhist transmission**.
- **587 CE** — battle of Mt. Shigi-san. The pro-Buddhist Soga clan, led by [[soga-no-umako|Soga no Umako]] and the young Prince Shōtoku, defeats the anti-Buddhist Mononobe clan. **Buddhism becomes state-supported** in Japan.
- **593 CE** — [[empress-suiko|Empress Suiko]] enthroned; appoints her nephew Shōtoku as prince-regent.
- **607 CE** — Hōryū-ji founded by Prince Shōtoku, in fulfillment of his father Emperor Yōmei's deathbed vow per the *Nihon Shoki*. Shōtoku resides nearby at the **Ikaruga-no-Miya** (since destroyed).
- **622 CE** — Shōtoku dies. The cult of his sanctity begins to develop.
- **623 CE** — **Tori Busshi (Kuratsukuri-no-Tori)** completes the **Shaka Triad** altarpiece of the *Kondō*, in memory of Shōtoku. The inscription survives.
- **670 CE** — Hōryū-ji **burns** in a great fire (per the *Nihon Shoki*'s entry of Tenchi Tennō Year 9). Reconstruction begins immediately.
- **711 CE** — reconstruction complete. This is the standing temple today.
- **739 CE** — **Eastern Precinct (*Tōin*)** founded by the monk Gyōshin, with the *Yumedono* (Hall of Dreams) on the site of the former Ikaruga-no-Miya. The *Yumedono* houses the **Guze Kannon** statue — traditionally held to be a portrait-statue of Prince Shōtoku in the form of Kannon.
- **Kamakura–Edo** — continuous cultic activity; the Shōtoku-cult develops into one of the major personal-cults of Japanese Buddhism.
- **1949** — a fire damages the Kondō murals; this leads to the establishment of Japan's national Cultural Properties Protection Law (1950).
- **1993** — UNESCO World Heritage inscription (Japan's first).

## Architectural form — Asuka-period transmitted Baekje vocabulary

The Hōryū-ji Western Precinct preserves the **Asuka-period architectural vocabulary** — transmitted from Baekje Korea, ultimately derived from Northern Wei + Liang-Chen China. Key features:

- **Asymmetric pairing**: the **pagoda** (west of axis) and the **Kondō** (east of axis) face each other across a central courtyard, with the *Chūmon* (central gate) at the south and the *Daikōdō* (lecture hall) at the north. The asymmetric east-west pairing of pagoda and golden hall is **the canonical Asuka layout**, contrasted with later Japanese layouts (Tōdaiji uses a single axial Kondō with twin pagodas flanking; Yakushiji uses paired pagodas flanking a single Kondō).
- **Entasis columns** — the principal wooden columns have a subtle **bulge in the middle**, identical to Greek-classical *entasis*. This is one of the most discussed cross-cultural transmissions in architectural history: Greek architectural elements travelled east via Hellenistic Bactria + Gandhara to Tang China, and from Tang to Japan via Baekje. The Hōryū-ji entasis is the easternmost surviving example.
- **Cloud-pattern brackets (*kumo-hijiki*)** — the distinctive Asuka bracket-arms are carved in cloud-shaped curves rather than the angular linear forms of later periods. These cloud-brackets are the visual signature of Asuka-period architecture.
- **Tile roof, plastered walls, painted vermilion columns** — the standard Buddhist temple palette transmitted from Korea/China.
- **Hipped-and-gabled roofs** with deep eaves.

The **five-storey pagoda** is the principal vertical element. It is ~32.5 m tall, with a single central pillar (*shinbashira*) penetrating all five storeys and grounded on a foundation stone below the pagoda. The pagoda houses **clay-sculpted scenes of the Parinirvana** of the Buddha at its lower level — masterpieces of Asuka-Hakuhō sculpture (~711 CE).

## The Kondō Shaka Triad and the Asuka aesthetic

The principal altarpiece of the *Kondō* is the **Shaka Triad** — a gilt-bronze seated Shakyamuni Buddha flanked by two standing bodhisattvas — completed in **623 CE by Kuratsukuri-no-Tori (Tori Busshi)** in memory of Prince Shōtoku. The inscription on the back of the altarpiece survives and is the principal contemporary text confirming both the artist and the date.

The Shaka Triad is the **canonical exemplar of Asuka-period Japanese Buddhist sculpture** — stylistically continuous with Northern Wei-derived Korean Baekje sculpture, with characteristic **frontal pose, archaic smile, schematic drapery in cascading folds, and elongated almond-eye facial type**. It is one of the most-studied pieces of East Asian Buddhist sculpture and the **single most important Asuka-period sculptural commission** to survive.

The *Kondō* also houses the **Yakushi Nyorai** (gilt-bronze; the Medicine Buddha) and the **Amida Triad** plus other principal images, all of various Asuka–Hakuhō dates.

## The Yumedono and the Guze Kannon

The **Yumedono** (夢殿 — "Hall of Dreams") is the principal hall of the Eastern Precinct, octagonal, built ~739 CE on the site of Prince Shōtoku's former residence. It houses the **Guze Kannon (*Guze Kannon Bosatsu*)** — a ~1.8 m wooden statue of Avalokiteshvara, traditionally held to be a portrait-statue of Prince Shōtoku himself in the form of the bodhisattva.

The Guze Kannon was a **hibutsu** (秘仏 — "hidden Buddha") for centuries, viewed by no one. **It was first viewed by Western eyes in 1884 by Ernest Fenollosa and Okakura Kakuzō**, who unwrapped the cloth-wrapped statue against the protests of the monks. Their viewing is one of the foundational episodes of modern East Asian art history. The statue was wrapped in ~500 m of cloth covering it head to foot. It is one of the masterpieces of Asuka-period wooden sculpture.

## MASSIVE WIN — the Baekje-to-Japan Buddhist transmission preserved

Hōryū-ji is the **principal architectural archive of the Buddhist transmission from Korea to Japan in the 6th–7th centuries**.

The transmission narrative:

1. **538 CE** — King Sŏng of [[baekje-kingdom|Baekje]] sends a Buddhist mission to Emperor Kinmei of Japan: Buddhist statues, sutras, monks.
2. **587 CE** — the pro-Buddhist Soga faction defeats the anti-Buddhist Mononobe at the Battle of Mt. Shigi-san; Buddhism becomes state-supported.
3. **~590s–600s** — Baekje craftsmen arrive in Japan to build the first Buddhist temples. Asukadera (~588) is the first; Hōryū-ji (~607) the second.
4. **660 CE** — **Baekje falls** to a Tang-Silla alliance. Many Baekje refugees flee to Japan, bringing additional architectural and artistic knowledge.

The crucial fact: **Baekje itself, the transmitting Korean state, was destroyed in 660 CE**. The architectural tradition Baekje transmitted to Japan — the cloud-bracket Asuka vocabulary, the entasis columns, the asymmetric pagoda-and-Kondō layout, the Northern-Wei-derived sculptural style — **does not survive in any standing Korean building**. The surviving Korean monumental Buddhist temples are all later (Silla post-668, Goryeo post-918, etc.) and deploy a different architectural vocabulary.

**Hōryū-ji therefore preserves, as the only place on earth, the standing architectural vocabulary of late-Baekje Korean Buddhism**. This is one of the most consequential archives of religious-architectural transmission in world history: the building tradition that produced it has no surviving Korean exemplars, and Hōryū-ji is the principal evidence for what late-Baekje monumental Buddhist architecture looked like.

This connects to the **East Asian Buddhist transmission chain**:

> **India → Central Asia → Northern Wei / Liang-Chen China → Baekje Korea (~538) → Asuka Japan (Hōryū-ji, ~607)**

And then, ~150 years later:

> **Tang China → Silla Korea ([[bulguksa-temple-and-seokguram-grotto|Bulguksa]], ~774) and Nara Japan ([[todaiji-daibutsuden|Todaiji]], ~752)**

The two waves are architecturally distinct. The Asuka wave (Hōryū-ji vocabulary) and the Nara wave (Tōdaiji vocabulary) document **two separate phases of East Asian Buddhist architectural transmission**, ~150 years apart. The earlier wave survives only at Hōryū-ji.

## The cult of Prince Shōtoku

The cult of **Prince Shōtoku as a Buddhist saint** develops at Hōryū-ji from the 8th century onward and becomes one of the major personal-cults of Japanese Buddhism. Shōtoku is variously identified as:

- A manifestation of **Avalokiteshvara (Kannon)** — hence the identification of the Yumedono *Guze Kannon* as his portrait-statue.
- A previous incarnation of the great Chinese Buddhist patriarch **Huisi (515–577)** — a doctrinal claim made by the Tendai school.
- The founder of Japanese imperial-Buddhist statehood — a political-historical claim that anchors Japanese state Buddhism through the medieval period.

Shōtoku's traditional **Seventeen-Article Constitution** (604 CE) — a set of Buddhist-Confucian-Daoist administrative principles — is the foundational document of Japanese Buddhist state-religious life. Whether Shōtoku himself authored it is contested by modern scholarship (many treat it as a later composition retroactively attributed to him); the cultic-historical reality is that **Shōtoku's name anchors the doctrine of Buddhist-imperial Japan**, and Hōryū-ji is its architectural centre.
