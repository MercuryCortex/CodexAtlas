# Machu Picchu

## What it is

**Machu Picchu** is a 15th-century Inca site occupying a saddle between two Andean peaks (Machu Picchu = "Old Peak" + Huayna Picchu = "Young Peak") at 2,430m elevation, ~75 km northwest of Cusco. Built c. 1450 CE under the Inca emperor [[pachacuti-inca-yupanqui|Pachacuti]], it is almost certainly his **royal estate** — a private imperial retreat with attached agricultural terraces, residences for nobles, ceremonial precincts, and three exceptional ritual structures (the Torreón, the Temple of the Three Windows, and the Intihuatana).

Machu Picchu was occupied for ~80–100 years, then **abandoned around the time of the Spanish conquest** (~1532–1540). Crucially, **the Spanish never reached it** — the site was unknown to the colonial authorities and survived the systematic Spanish destruction of Inca sacred monuments (Coricancha was dismantled, Sacsayhuamán was used as a quarry, intihuatana stones across the empire were broken). Local farmers always knew the site existed; international scholarly attention came with [[hiram-bingham]]'s 1911 expedition.

Machu Picchu is now the **iconic image of Inca civilization** and the most-visited archaeological site in the Americas (~1.5M visitors/year pre-pandemic), named a New Seven Wonders of the World (2007).

## History

### Pachacuti and the construction

[[pachacuti-inca-yupanqui|Pachacuti]] (r. 1438–1471) is the emperor who transformed the Inca from a small Cusco-centred polity into the largest pre-Columbian empire of the Americas (Tawantinsuyu, "the four quarters"). Modern Inca-historical consensus (D'Altroy 2014; Bauer 2004) treats Pachacuti as the founder of the imperial period and the patron of the major Cusco-region monumental construction (Sacsayhuamán, Ollantaytambo, Coricancha enlargement, Machu Picchu).

Pachacuti's reign saw the **construction of multiple royal estates** along the Sacred Valley (Urubamba River drainage), each with its own farms, residences, and ceremonial structures, supporting the emperor and his court on circulation through his realm. Machu Picchu is the **most spectacular and best-preserved** of these estates.

### Abandonment

The site was occupied from ~1450 to ~1540. The abandonment date is contested:
- Some scholars argue abandonment shortly after the **1532 Spanish conquest** of Cusco, when the Inca state collapsed and the network of royal estates lost their administrative function.
- Others argue abandonment up to a generation later, possibly c. 1572 (the Spanish capture of Vilcabamba and the execution of the last Inca emperor Tupac Amaru I).

Either way: the site was empty by the end of the 16th c. and remained so for ~350 years. Cusqueño farmers in the early 20th c. were aware of the ruins; a Peruvian businessman, [[agustin-lizarraga]], visited and **inscribed his name on a wall** in 1902 — predating Bingham by nine years.

### Bingham's 1911 expedition

[[hiram-bingham]] was a Yale historian seeking the "lost city of Vilcabamba" — the last Inca refuge. Guided by the local farmer [[melchor-arteaga]] (his name is in Bingham's own diaries), he reached Machu Picchu on **24 July 1911**. He believed for the rest of his life that Machu Picchu *was* Vilcabamba (it isn't — Vilcabamba is at Espíritu Pampa, much further west; correctly identified by Gene Savoy in the 1960s).

Bingham led three expeditions (1911, 1912, 1914–15) with National Geographic + Yale funding. The expeditions removed thousands of artifacts — bones, ceramics, metalwork, ritual objects — to Yale's Peabody Museum. The dispute over their return became a century-long Peru-Yale legal-diplomatic affair, finally resolved with the return of the collections to Cusco in **2011–2012**.

### "Lost City" myth

Bingham's 1911 reports popularized the **"Lost City of the Incas"** framing. The framing is partly false: Machu Picchu was never "lost" to the people living near it (the Quichua-speaking farmers of the area), only to the Spanish administration and to international scholarship. The romance of "lost city" rediscovery is a colonial framing imposed on indigenous geography that never really lost the site at all.

## Architectural form

### Site layout

The site occupies a saddle between Machu Picchu peak (south) and Huayna Picchu peak (north), with steep drops to the Urubamba River ~400m below on both east and west. The layout is divided into:

- **Agricultural sector (south + east):** ~700 terraces stepping down the mountain, irrigated by an engineered water system (16 fountains fed by a single spring). Wright & Valencia (2000) calculate the system could support ~750 people year-round.
- **Urban / ceremonial sector (north + west):** ~200 stone buildings arranged around plazas and around the three principal ceremonial structures (Torreón, Temple of the Three Windows, Intihuatana).
- **Royal residence quarter:** finest stonework; presumed Pachacuti's lodgings.
- **Princess's quarter, priests' quarter, common-people's quarter** — Bingham's nomenclature, still in use, though the actual social-functional divisions are partly speculative.

### Stonework

Machu Picchu's ashlar masonry is the **type-specimen of Inca high-quality construction**. The finest walls — particularly in the Torreón and the surrounding sacred precinct — show:
- **Polygonal precision-fit ashlars** (irregular interlocking blocks with no mortar, polished to fits so tight a knife blade cannot pass between them — though this oft-repeated trope is partially exaggerated).
- **Slightly-leaning walls** (3–5° inward) — earthquake-resistant; survives ground motion that brings rectilinear buildings down.
- **Trapezoidal doorways and windows** — wider at the base than the top; further earthquake resistance.
- **Stone-cutting without metal tools** — Inca masonry used stone hammers and hardened bronze chisels; the precision was achieved through extensive abrasion-and-refit cycles (Protzen 1993).

### The three principal ceremonial structures

**The Torreón ("the Tower"):** the only curved building at Machu Picchu — a D-shaped semicircular structure with the city's finest stonework. Built around a single huge boulder (incorporated into the wall) on which an altar is carved. Below the Torreón is a small carved cave (the "Royal Tomb" of Bingham's nomenclature — never had remains; ceremonial use). The Torreón has a **window precisely aligned to the June-solstice sunrise** (Dearborn & White 1983; Bauer & Dearborn 1995) — the **Inti Raymi** festival of the Inca solar year.

**Temple of the Three Windows:** a single-walled rectangular building on the Sacred Plaza, with three trapezoidal east-facing windows. The largest stones in the site outside the Torreón; the three windows likely associated with the Inca three-world cosmology (Hanan / Kay / Uku Pacha — upper / middle / lower worlds).

**The Intihuatana:** a carved natural-rock outcrop at the highest point of the urban sector — a carefully shaped stone column rising from a multi-tiered plinth. The name *Intihuatana* means "**hitching post of the sun**" or "the place where the sun is tied" in Quechua. Its function is debated but most likely:
- A **sundial / solar observatory** marking the solstices and the zenith passages.
- A **ritual centre** where, at the equinox or solstice, priests symbolically "tied" the sun to prevent it from disappearing northward (Bauer & Dearborn 1995).

The Machu Picchu Intihuatana is **one of very few intact such stones in any Inca site** — the Spanish *extirpación de idolatrías* campaigns of the 17th c. systematically broke intihuatana stones across the empire as instruments of "demonic" astronomy. The Machu Picchu stone survived because the Spanish never came here.

### Astronomical alignments

Beyond the Torreón solstice-window alignment, the entire site shows alignments to:
- The **summer solstice sunrise** behind the peak of Huayna Picchu (December solstice; opposite end of the year).
- The **zenith passages** (when the sun passes overhead, twice a year at this latitude).
- The **southern celestial pole** and the **Milky Way's dark constellations** (Llama, Tinamou, Fox, etc.) — the Inca read the dark patches in the Milky Way as animal forms; these were as much part of Inca astronomy as the bright stars (Urton 1981).

## Theological / cosmological function

### Sacred landscape — apus and huacas

Inca religion was fundamentally about the **sanctity of place** (*huaca*). Every distinctive feature of the landscape — a peak, a spring, a rock, a cave — could be a *huaca*. The **apus** were the great mountain-spirits; the major Andean peaks were animate, named, and propitiated.

Machu Picchu sits at the intersection of multiple major apus: **Salkantay** (6,271m, visible to the south), **Veronica** (5,893m, east), **Pumasillo** (5,991m, west). The site's positioning is **deliberate astronomical-geographical**: the layout of the city aligns with specific sightlines to these apus on specific calendar days.

The Reinhard (1991) interpretation: **Machu Picchu is the architectural heart of a sacred landscape extending many kilometres in every direction.** The terraces, the buildings, the alignments — all are calibrated to the surrounding mountains and the celestial events visible above them.

### Royal estate as ritual centre

Like many Inca royal estates (Pisac, Ollantaytambo, Yucay), Machu Picchu combined agricultural production, residential function, and ritual purposes in a single integrated complex. The emperor and his court spent time here; the agricultural terraces fed the estate; the ceremonial structures hosted the major calendrical festivals (Inti Raymi, Capacocha, etc.) for the imperial elite.

The Inca empire had **no separate priestly class fully distinct from the imperial administration** — the emperor himself was the chief ritual officiant (he was the son of the sun-god [[inti]]). Royal estates were therefore *simultaneously* political-administrative and religious centres.

## MASSIVE WIN — integrated astronomical-mountain-temple architecture in stone

**Machu Picchu is one of the most fully-integrated examples in any tradition of architecture × astronomy × sacred mountain × terraced agriculture × imperial residence as a single coherent religious-ceremonial system.** Every element of the site connects to every other:
- The terraces follow the mountain's topography and the water flow.
- The buildings align with the surrounding apus and the celestial events.
- The Intihuatana, the Torreón, and the Temple of the Three Windows encode the **solar year** in stone.
- The whole occupies a saddle between two named sacred peaks.

**Cross-tradition astronomical-alignment parallel:** Machu Picchu's June-solstice Torreón alignment ↔ [[stonehenge-sacred-site|Stonehenge]]'s summer-solstice sunrise alignment ↔ [[newgrange-passage-tomb|Newgrange]]'s winter-solstice dawn illumination ↔ [[el-castillo-chichen-itza|El Castillo]]'s equinox serpent-shadow ↔ [[karnak-temple-complex|Karnak]]'s solstice axis ↔ [[pueblo-bonito-chaco-canyon|Pueblo Bonito]]'s solstice alignments ↔ [[bighorn-medicine-wheel|Bighorn Medicine Wheel]]'s solstice + stellar alignments. **Six+ mutually-uncontacting civilizations independently encoding solstice / equinox / stellar events in monumental architecture.** Convergent religious-architectural reasoning under astronomy-as-cosmic-order.

**The Andean sacred-mountain (apu) tradition** ↔ **the Indic sacred-mountain ([[mount-meru]]/[[mount-kailash]]) tradition** ↔ **the Greek Olympus tradition** ↔ **the Japanese Mt. Fuji tradition** ↔ **the Mesopotamian cosmic-mountain ziggurat tradition**. Mountain-as-axis-mundi is one of the most widely-attested religious universals; Machu Picchu is the type-specimen of sacred-monumental-architecture **on** the mountain (as opposed to **as** the mountain — the ziggurat case).

**Inca + Spanish destruction:** Machu Picchu is *the* case where Spanish destruction did NOT reach — preserving an Inca sacred site that would otherwise have shared the fate of Coricancha, Pisac, Sacsayhuamán (used as quarry), Ollantaytambo (partial), and every intihuatana stone the Spanish could find. The accidental preservation by geographic isolation makes Machu Picchu the **best window we have on Inca religious-architectural intention**.
