## Abu Bakr al-Razi (Rhazes) — The First Systematic Laboratory Chemist

**Abu Bakr Muhammad ibn Zakariya al-Razi** (854–925 CE), known in medieval Europe as **Rhazes**, was a Persian physician and alchemist working in Ray (near modern Tehran) and Baghdad. He is the first person in history to produce a systematic written classification of chemical substances and laboratory procedures — a document that anticipates modern chemical taxonomy by over a millennium.

His *Kitab al-Asrar* (Book of Secrets) is not mystical allegory. It is a laboratory manual.

**The Kitab al-Asrar: A Laboratory Classification System**

The *Kitab al-Asrar* opens with a classification of all chemical substances into six categories:

1. **Spirits** (*ruh*): volatile substances that escape when heated — sulfur, mercury, arsenic, sal ammoniac, camphor. Modern chemistry: volatile compounds.
2. **Bodies** (*jasad*): the seven metals — gold, silver, copper, iron, tin, lead, and mercury/quicksilver. Modern chemistry: elemental metals.
3. **Stones** (*hijara*): pyrites, marcasite, magnesia, tutia (zinc oxide), lapis lazuli, malachite, turquoise. Modern chemistry: minerals and ores.
4. **Vitriols** (*zajat*): sulfates — green vitriol (iron sulfate), white vitriol (zinc sulfate), black vitriol. Modern chemistry: metal sulfates.
5. **Boraces** (*bawariq*): borax, natron, plant alkali, salt. Modern chemistry: borates and alkalis.
6. **Salts** (*amlah*): common salt, bitter salt, sea salt, alkali salt, and related compounds. Modern chemistry: salts.

This is a recognizable taxonomic system — substances classified by their observable physical properties and behavior under heat and manipulation, not by astrological correspondence or numerological properties. Al-Razi is observing how substances actually behave and classifying them accordingly.

**The Laboratory Equipment List**

*Kitab al-Asrar* contains the first complete laboratory equipment list in history. Al-Razi enumerates:

*Smelting equipment*: furnace, bellows, crucible, molds, tongs, shears, hammer, file
*Glass vessels*: alembic (distillation head), cucurbit (distillation flask), receiver, aludel (sublimation vessel), beaker, phial, flask

The majority of these names entered European languages through Arabic. **Alembic** (from Arabic *al-anbiq*), **crucible** (medieval Latin *crucibulum* from Arabic *kuhl*), **alcohol** (from *al-kuhl*, antimony powder, later extended to distilled spirits), **alkali** (from *al-qili*), **amalgam** (from *al-malagam*) — al-Razi's laboratory is the etymological origin of most Western chemical terminology.

**The Empirical Method vs. the Jabir Contrast**

The contrast between al-Razi and [[alchemy-jabir-ibn-hayyan]] is foundational for understanding Islamic alchemy:

- **Jabir ibn Hayyan** (c.721–815): theoretical and numerological. Jabir held that each metal was constituted by sulfur and mercury in specific proportions, and that these proportions followed perfect numerical ratios (derived from the "science of the balance"). His alchemical corpus is vast (over 100 texts) but often allegorical and deliberately obscured. His project is fundamentally Neoplatonic-mathematical — understanding the universal ratios underlying material transformation.

- **Al-Razi** (854–925): empirical and classificatory. Al-Razi does not theorize the cosmic ratios underlying substances. He classifies them by what they do in the laboratory. His *Kitab al-Asrar* gives detailed procedure descriptions: what to mix, at what temperature, for how long, what the result looks like. He notes experimental failures and corrections.

Together, Jabir and al-Razi define the **two poles of all subsequent Western alchemy**: theoretical-cosmological (the Great Work as cosmic process) and laboratory-empirical (the Great Work as operational chemistry). The tension between these poles runs through Paracelsus, through Newton's alchemical notebooks, through the 17th-century chymistry debate — and arguably never resolves until the formal separation of chemistry from alchemy in the 18th century.

**Al-Razi as Physician**

Al-Razi's medical achievements are independently enormous. His *Kitab al-Mansuri* (Liber Almansoris) and *al-Hawi* (Continens) were standard medical references in European universities through the 16th century. He made the first clinical distinction between smallpox and measles (first recorded in history). He described kidney stones and their treatment. He used alcohol-soaked wool as wound dressing — the first antiseptic application. He wrote that the physician's purpose is to prevent, not merely treat, disease.

The same person who produced the first systematic chemical taxonomy also produced the first clinical differential diagnosis. His dual achievement illustrates that in 9th-century Islamic Baghdad, medicine and alchemy were not separate disciplines. They were the same inquiry at different scales: what are substances, how do they combine and separate, and how can this knowledge restore proper mixture in the human body?

## Cross-Tradition

Al-Razi's project — empirical classification of substances combined with practical medicine — finds structural parallels across traditions:

- **Indian Ayurveda's *dravyaguna* system**: classification of medicinal substances by properties (*guna*), actions (*karma*), and post-digestive effects — a systematic pharmacological taxonomy developed over centuries, independent of Arabic chemistry, with structural parallels to al-Razi's six categories. See [[tradition-vedic]].
- **Chinese materia medica** (*bencao*): the great Chinese pharmacological tradition culminating in Li Shizhen's *Bencao Gangmu* (1596, 1,892 substances) — systematic empirical classification of natural substances by observable properties, developed independently of Arabic taxonomy, with some shared principles (substances as mixtures of qualities rather than elements).
- **Greek Dioscorides**: *De Materia Medica* (1st c. CE) — al-Razi's taxonomic project is explicitly a continuation of the Greek empirical tradition (Dioscorides, Galen, Theophrastus) as transmitted through Arabic translation. Unlike Jabir, who transforms Greek sources through Neoplatonic number theory, al-Razi extends them through the same empirical method they employed.

## MASSIVE WIN

**Al-Razi's empirical method is recognizably modern chemistry; his theoretical framework is still fully Greek-Arabic alchemy. The same man straddles the gap.** His classification by observable properties and behavior (what we now call chemistry) coexists with his belief in metallic transmutation as a real possibility and his Aristotelian theory of elemental composition (what we now call alchemy). He is not confused — he is working in a period when the two approaches had not yet been separated by institutional distinction.

The modern separation of chemistry from alchemy is partly arbitrary — it reflects which parts of al-Razi's program eventually proved operationally productive (the laboratory procedures, substance taxonomy, distillation equipment) versus which parts proved unproductive (metallic transmutation, perfect-proportion theory). Al-Razi himself would not have recognized the distinction as fundamental. The same laboratory, the same equipment, the same systematic observation — applied to different ends.
