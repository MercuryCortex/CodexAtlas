# Sūrat Yūsuf (Quran 12 — Joseph)

## What it is
The only sura that tells a single, continuous story from start to finish — which the Quran itself calls "the most beautiful of stories" (aḥsan al-qaṣaṣ, 12:3). It retells the life of Joseph: his prophetic dream, the jealousy and treachery of his brothers, his sale into Egyptian slavery, the temptation by his master's wife, his imprisonment and dream-interpretation, his rise to power, and the moving reconciliation with the brothers who wronged him. The narrative parallels Genesis 37–50 closely while reframing Joseph as a prophet and exemplar of patient trust in God.

## Primary text

### Quran 12:90–92
*Trans. Abdel Haleem (2004)*
> They said, "Could it be that you are Joseph?" He said, "I am Joseph, and this is my brother. God has been gracious to us... God rewards those who do good." ... "You will hear no reproaches today. May God forgive you: He is the Most Merciful of the merciful."

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** Meccan Islam; the sura is traditionally a consolation to Muhammad, mirroring the rejected-then-vindicated prophet in Joseph.
- **Narrative.** Uniquely sustained and self-contained, a showcase of Quranic storytelling.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Fully canonical; a perennial favorite of devotional recitation.

## Themes and motifs
- Patient endurance (ṣabr) — Joseph's trust through betrayal, slavery, and prison.
- Divine providence — God's hidden plan turning evil to good.
- [[messianism]] — Joseph as prophet, his story a type of the suffering-then-exalted righteous one.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **The Quran's retelling of Genesis Joseph — shared story, transformed theology.** Sūrat Yūsuf follows the Genesis Joseph narrative beat for beat (the dream, the pit, the sale, Potiphar's wife, the prison-dreams, the rise, the reunion), making it one of the most extended scriptural overlaps between the Bible and the Quran. The differences are the interesting part: Joseph becomes a *prophet* who receives revelation; the women of the city cut their hands at his beauty (a detail absent from Genesis but found in Jewish midrash); and the moral is recast around ṣabr and tawḥīd. The episode became, through Persian and Sufi poetry, the supreme literary emblem of beauty and longing (Jāmī's *Yūsuf u Zulaykhā*) — a Genesis patriarch reborn as the icon of divine-human love in Islamic culture (Reynolds 2010 pp.96–108; Nasr 2015 pp.587–593).
