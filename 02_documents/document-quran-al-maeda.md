# Sūrat al-Māʾida (Quran 5 — The Table Spread)

## What it is
A long, late-Medinan sura of law and covenant, traditionally among the last portions of the Quran revealed. It legislates on food, contracts, theft, and pilgrimage; engages closely with Jews and Christians as "People of the Book"; tells of Cain and Abel; and closes with a remarkable dialogue in which God questions Jesus about whether he told people to worship him and his mother. It takes its name from the "table" (māʾida) the disciples ask Jesus to bring down from heaven — a clear echo of the eucharistic tradition.

## Primary text

### Quran 5:32
*Trans. Abdel Haleem (2004)*
> On account of [Cain's murder of Abel], We decreed to the Children of Israel that if anyone kills a person — unless in retribution for murder or spreading corruption in the land — it is as if he kills all mankind, while if any saves a life it is as if he saves the lives of all mankind.

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** Late Medinan Islam in sustained dialogue and dispute with Judaism and Christianity.
- **Christology.** 5:116 has God ask Jesus whether he commanded the worship of himself and his mother — the Quran's pointed rejection of a (misperceived) Christian Trinity that includes Mary.
- **Discovery / transmission.** A foundational legal sura, fully canonical.

## Themes and motifs
- Sanctity of life — 5:32, the universalized value of a single life.
- Covenant and law — dietary, contractual, and penal legislation.
- [[messianism]] — Jesus (ʿĪsā) as prophet and messiah, but emphatically not divine.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **Quran 5:32 is the cleanest documented case of Talmudic material entering Islamic scripture.** The verse's logic — that to kill one person is to destroy a whole world, and to save one is to save a whole world — is drawn almost verbatim from the Mishnah, Sanhedrin 4:5, which says exactly this of a single human life (Adam was created alone "to teach that whoever destroys a single soul... it is as if he destroyed an entire world"). The Quran even preserves the rabbinic framing ("We decreed to the Children of Israel"), marking it as inherited tradition. It is the textbook example of a rabbinic teaching passing into the Quran, and one of the most-quoted verses in modern interfaith and human-rights discourse (Reynolds 2010 pp.32–35; Nasr 2015 pp.292–296).
