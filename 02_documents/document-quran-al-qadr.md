# Sūrat al-Qadr (Quran 97 — The Night of Glory)

## What it is
A luminous five-verse Meccan sura on **Laylat al-Qadr**, the Night of Decree (or Power, or Glory), on which the Qur'an began its descent. The night is declared "better than a thousand months"; on it the angels and "the Spirit" (al-rūḥ, traditionally Gabriel) descend with God's permission "on every errand"; and it is wholly *peace* until the break of dawn. From these verses comes the Islamic observance of the night — sought especially in the odd nights of the last ten days of Ramadan — as the holiest vigil of the year.

## Primary text

### Quran 97:1–5
*Trans. Abdel Haleem (2004)*
> We sent it down on the Night of Glory. What will explain to you what that Night of Glory is? The Night of Glory is better than a thousand months; on that night the angels and the Spirit descend again and again with their Lord's permission on every task; [there is] peace that night until the break of dawn.

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** Meccan; the sura turns the moment of first revelation into a recurring sacred night.
- **Tradition.** Classical theology distinguishes the Qur'an's single descent "to the lowest heaven" on this night from its gradual revelation to Muhammad over twenty-three years.
- **Discovery / transmission.** The night's exact date is deliberately hidden, so that the faithful seek it across many nights of devotion.

## Themes and motifs
- A single decisive night — time concentrated, "better than a thousand months."
- The descending hosts — angels and the Spirit crossing into the world.
- Peace until dawn — the night as pure blessing.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **The one night the heavens open.** Sūrat al-Qadr distills a motif shared across the Abrahamic calendar: a single night set apart because on it heaven touched earth. For Israel it is *leyl shimmurim*, "the night of watching" (Exodus 12:42), the Passover night kept as vigil because God passed over and delivered; later Jewish piety adds the all-night study of Shavuot, the night the Torah was given. For Islam it is Laylat al-Qadr, the night the Qur'an descended, when angels and the Spirit pour down "on every errand" and the whole night is *salām*, peace. Both traditions take a unique historical descent of revelation and turn it into a *recurring* liturgical night of vigil, blessing, and decree — and both deliberately wrap it in mystery (the Passover's midnight, the Qadr's hidden date) so that watchfulness itself becomes the worship. The sura's framing of the Qur'an's descent also anchors the distinctive Islamic doctrine of two-stage revelation: all at once to the lowest heaven, then piecemeal to the Prophet (Reynolds 2010 pp.301–308; McAuliffe 2006 pp.41–44; Nasr 2015 pp.1551–1556).
