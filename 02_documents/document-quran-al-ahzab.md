# Sūrat al-Aḥzāb (Quran 33 — The Confederates)

## What it is
A Medinan sura set around the siege of 627, when a confederation of clans (al-aḥzāb) marched on Medina and were turned back by a defensive trench and a providential storm. Around that crisis it gathers some of the most consequential verses in the Qur'an: the declaration that Muhammad is "the Messenger of God and the Seal of the Prophets" (33:40); regulations for the Prophet's household and his wives ("the Mothers of the Believers"); the controversial marriage to Zaynab that ended the Arabian custom of treating adopted sons as blood kin; and, at its close, the awesome image of the **Trust** offered to the heavens, the earth, and the mountains, who shrank from it — and which humankind alone agreed to bear.

## Primary text

### Quran 33:40
*Trans. Abdel Haleem (2004)*
> Muhammad is not the father of any one of you men; he is God's Messenger and the seal of the prophets: God knows everything.

### Quran 33:72
*Trans. Abdel Haleem (2004)*
> We offered the Trust to the heavens, the earth, and the mountains, yet they refused to undertake it and were afraid of it; mankind undertook it — they have always been inept and rash.

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** Medinan; the community survives an existential siege and consolidates its law and identity.
- **Tradition.** 33:40 is the textual anchor of *khatm al-nubuwwa*, the finality of prophethood in Muhammad.
- **Discovery / transmission.** The Trust (amāna) of 33:72 became a deep theme of Islamic ethics and Sufism — the burden of moral freedom.

## Themes and motifs
- The Seal — prophecy completed and closed in Muhammad.
- The Trust — the unique moral responsibility borne by humankind.
- The tested community — deliverance from the confederates as a sign.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **The Seal of the Prophets and the Trust of freedom.** Two ideas in al-Aḥzāb reach far across the traditions. First, "the Seal of the Prophets" (33:40): the doctrine that revelation is now *complete and closed* answers a question the whole prophetic religion had to face. Judaism had already developed the view that prophecy ceased after Haggai, Zechariah, and Malachi (b. Yoma 9b), and rabbinic authority replaced it; Christianity held the canon closed with the apostolic age. Islam states the principle most explicitly — after Muhammad, no prophet — and the verse became the dividing line over which later claimants (and the communities that follow them) are judged inside or outside the faith. Second, the Trust (al-amāna, 33:72): heaven, earth, and mountains decline the burden of moral responsibility, and only the human accepts it — a mythic image of the same conviction as Genesis's making humankind in God's image and charging it alone with dominion and command (Genesis 1–3). Where Genesis dramatizes that freedom through a prohibition and a fall, the Qur'an dramatizes it as a cosmic *offer* that only the human was reckless and brave enough to take — making moral agency itself the thing that distinguishes humanity from all creation (McAuliffe 2006 pp.163–180; Lings 1983 pp.215–235; Nasr 2015 pp.1011–1037).
