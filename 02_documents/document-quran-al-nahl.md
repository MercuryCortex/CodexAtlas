# Sūrat al-Naḥl (Quran 16 — The Bee)

## What it is
A Meccan sura sometimes called "the sura of blessings," because it is a long, exuberant catalogue of the signs (āyāt) of God in the natural world: the rain that revives dead earth, cattle that give milk "pure and pleasant to drink," the alternation of night and day, the stars for navigation, ships that cut the sea, mountains as pegs — and the bee, inspired by God to build in the hills and bring forth honey, "a drink of varying colours, in which there is healing for people" (16:69). The argument is cumulative: a creation so ordered and so generous testifies to one Creator. The sura ends with the classic statement of method for calling others to faith (16:125).

## Primary text

### Quran 16:68–69
*Trans. Abdel Haleem (2004)*
> And your Lord inspired the bee, saying, "Build yourselves houses in the mountains and trees and what people construct. Then feed on all kinds of fruit and follow the ways made easy for you by your Lord." From their bellies comes a drink of different colours in which there is healing for people. There truly is a sign in this for those who think.

### Quran 16:125
*Trans. Abdel Haleem (2004)*
> Call people to the way of your Lord with wisdom and good teaching. Argue with them in the most courteous way.

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** Meccan; the sura answers denial not with threat alone but with the evidence of the visible world.
- **Tradition.** The "signs in nature" (āyāt āfāqiyya) became a whole genre of Islamic reflection linking revelation and the cosmos.
- **Discovery / transmission.** 16:125, the daʿwa verse, is the Quran's charter for persuasion "with wisdom and good preaching."

## Themes and motifs
- The book of nature — creation read as a catalogue of signs.
- Graded inspiration — the same word, waḥy, used of the bee and of the prophets.
- Gentle invitation — argument "in the most courteous way."

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **The book of nature and the argument from design.** Sūrat al-Naḥl is the Quran's fullest piece of natural theology: rather than command belief, it itemizes the world — rain, milk, honey, stars, ships — and lets the sheer fitted generosity of it stand as proof of one wise Maker. This is the same move as Psalm 19 ("the heavens declare the glory of God") and the vast cosmic inventory of Psalm 104, and it runs forward into Stoic natural theology (Cicero's *De Natura Deorum*), the Christian "two books" of scripture and nature, the design arguments of Islamic kalām, and ultimately Paley's watchmaker. The Quran adds a striking twist of vocabulary: the bee is *inspired* — the verb is *awḥā*, the same root as the *waḥy* given to prophets — so revelation becomes a graded cosmic phenomenon reaching from Gabriel down to the honeybee, every creature "following the ways made easy for it by its Lord." The honey "in which there is healing" (16:69) also seeded a long tradition of prophetic medicine (al-ṭibb al-nabawī) (McAuliffe 2006 pp.97–113; Nasr 1993 pp.94–110; Nasr 2015 pp.671–693).
