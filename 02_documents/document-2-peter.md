# Second Epistle of Peter

## What it is
Almost certainly the last-written book of the New Testament, cast as the apostle Peter's deathbed testament. It defends the promised return of Christ against "scoffers" who mock its delay, reworks the Epistle of Jude's polemic against false teachers, and — strikingly — refers to a collection of Paul's letters as "scripture," offering an early glimpse of the New Testament canon taking shape.

## Primary text

### 2 Peter 3:8–10
*Trans. NRSV*
> But do not ignore this one fact, beloved, that with the Lord one day is like a thousand years, and a thousand years are like one day... But the day of the Lord will come like a thief, and then the heavens will pass away with a loud noise, and the elements will be dissolved with fire.

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** A second-century church wrestling with the non-arrival of the Parousia and the consolidation of authoritative writings.
- **Authorship.** The strongest pseudonymity case in the NT; doubted in the early church longer than any other book.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Among the antilegomena; its canonicity unsettled into the 4th century.

## Themes and motifs
- The delay of the Parousia — answered by divine timelessness (3:8) and certainty of judgment.
- Cosmic conflagration — the dissolution of the heavens and elements by fire (3:10).
- Scripture and canon — Paul's letters grouped with "the other scriptures" (3:16).

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[document-jude]] — *redaction-of*: 2 Peter 2 reworks the bulk of Jude, the clearest literary borrowing among the Catholic Epistles.
- → [[document-revelation]] — *parallel-motif*: shared eschatology of the Day of the Lord and cosmic judgment.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **2 Peter watches the New Testament canon being born.** In 3:15–16 the author refers to "our beloved brother Paul," to a known collection of his letters, and to people who twist them "as they do the other scriptures" (tas loipas graphas) — placing Paul's epistles in the category of "scripture" already. Written ~100–150 CE, the latest book in the NT, 2 Peter is the canon's own earliest internal witness to the idea of a fixed body of authoritative Christian writings alongside the Jewish scriptures (Bauckham 1983, WBC 50 pp.327–334).
