# Sūrat Nūḥ (Quran 71 — Noah)

## What it is
A short Meccan sura given over almost entirely to the prophet Noah's anguished first-person report to God: he has warned his people "night and day," openly and in secret, and they have only stopped their ears and grown more obstinate. The sura ends with the Flood as judgment and Noah's prayer that no unbeliever be left on the earth. Uniquely, it names the five idols of Noah's people (71:23) — Wadd, Suwāʿ, Yaghūth, Yaʿūq, and Nasr — deities independently attested in pre-Islamic South Arabian inscriptions.

## Primary text

### Quran 71:1
*Trans. Abdel Haleem (2004)*
> We sent Noah to his people: "Warn your people, before a painful punishment comes to them."

### Quran 71:25–26
*Trans. Abdel Haleem (2004)*
> They were drowned and sent to Hell for their evildoings: they found no one to help them against God. And Noah said, "Lord, do not leave any of the disbelievers on the earth."

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** Meccan Islam; Noah is the archetype of Muhammad's own situation — a warner rejected by his own people, vindicated by God.
- **Tradition.** Noah heads the line of resolute prophets (ulū al-ʿazm) and opens the qiṣaṣ al-anbiyāʾ cycle that runs through the Quran.
- **Discovery / transmission.** The five-idol list (71:23) ties the universal Flood narrative to the specific Arabian idolatry the Meccan revelation confronts.

## Themes and motifs
- The rejected warner — preaching "night and day" to no avail.
- The Flood as divine judgment, compressed almost to a single line.
- Idolatry named and dated — the five gods as the root of the people's ruin.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **One Flood, four civilizations, and five named gods.** The deluge is the most widely attested narrative of the ancient Near East: the Sumerian Eridu Genesis (Ziusudra), the Akkadian *Atrahasis* and *Gilgamesh* XI (Utnapishtim), Genesis 6–9 (Noah), and Sūrat Nūḥ. The Quran's retelling is theologically distinctive — there is no ark blueprint, no sacrificial bird, no rainbow sign; the whole weight falls on Noah as preacher and on his people's idolatry. And in a move no other version makes, 71:23 *names the gods*: Wadd, Suwāʿ, Yaghūth, Yaʿūq, and Nasr — five deities that turn up in pre-Islamic Sabaean and Minaean inscriptions and in the idol-lists of Ibn al-Kalbī's *Kitāb al-Aṣnām*. By retrojecting historically Arabian idols onto Noah's antediluvian world, the sura fuses the universal Flood story with the concrete polytheism of sixth-century Mecca, making Noah's failed mission a mirror of Muhammad's own (Reynolds 2010 pp.49–58; Tottoli 2002 pp.18–24; Nasr 2015 pp.1418–1424).
