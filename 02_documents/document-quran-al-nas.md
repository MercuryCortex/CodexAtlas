# Sūrat al-Nās (Quran 114 — Mankind)

## What it is
The final sura of the Qur'an and the second of the two "refuge-seeking" suras. Where al-Falaq guards against *external* harms — darkness, sorcery, envy — al-Nās guards against the *internal* one: "the slinking whisperer" (al-waswās al-khannās) who breathes suggestion into human hearts and then withdraws, working "from among jinn and men." The worshipper takes shelter in God named three ways — Lord, King, and God of mankind — a rising scale of sovereignty over the very creature being tempted. So the codex that opened with al-Fātiḥa's plea for guidance closes on a plea for protection from the voice that misguides.

## Primary text

### Quran 114:1–6
*Trans. Abdel Haleem (2004)*
> Say, "I seek refuge with the Lord of people, the Controller of people, the God of people, against the harm of the slinking whisperer — who whispers into the hearts of people — whether they be jinn or people."

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** The sura interiorizes evil: the danger is not only the witch and the dark but the suggestion within.
- **Tradition.** It grounds the doctrine of waswasa — the satanic prompt — and the istiʿādha, the formula "I seek refuge in God from Satan" said before recitation.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Recited with al-Falaq each night and over the sick; together they close every complete reading of the Qur'an.

## Themes and motifs
- The inner whisperer — temptation as a voice that comes and slinks away.
- Threefold lordship — Lord, King, God of mankind.
- The codex's close — the Recitation ends in refuge.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **The whisper within — temptation as a voice.** al-Nās closes the Qur'an by locating the subtlest evil inside the human breast: a *whisperer* who suggests and then slinks off, leaving the person to think the prompting was their own. This is one of scripture's oldest and most psychologically acute images of temptation. It begins with the serpent of Eden, whose attack is purely verbal — "did God really say…?" (Genesis 3:1) — insinuation rather than force; it runs through the *yetzer ha-raʿ*, the rabbinic "evil inclination" that argues inside a person; the New Testament's tempter who reasons with Jesus in the wilderness (Matthew 4); and the desert monks' analysis of intrusive *logismoi*, the tempting thoughts catalogued by Evagrius. The Qur'an's *al-waswās al-khannās* gives the type its sharpest name — "the one who whispers and withdraws" — and crucially adds that the whisperer works "from among jinn *and men*," so that human persuaders to evil are demonic in function. That the muṣḥaf ends here, on refuge from the inner voice, mirrors its beginning: al-Fātiḥa asks to be kept on the straight path; al-Nās asks to be guarded from what pulls one off it (Reynolds 2010 pp.301–310; McAuliffe 2006 pp.181–195; Nasr 2015 pp.1587–1591).
