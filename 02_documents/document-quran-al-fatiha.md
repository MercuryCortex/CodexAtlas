# Sūrat al-Fātiḥa (Quran 1 — The Opening)

## What it is
The short, seven-verse opening sura of the Quran — and the single most-recited text in Islam, said in every cycle of the five daily prayers. Called "the Mother of the Book," it is a compact prayer: praise of God the Lord of the worlds, the Merciful; acknowledgment of the Day of Judgment; a pledge of worship and reliance; and a petition to be guided on "the straight path." It functions in Muslim devotion much as the Lord's Prayer does in Christianity.

## Primary text

### Quran 1:1–7
*Trans. Abdel Haleem (2004)*
> In the name of God, the Lord of Mercy, the Giver of Mercy! Praise belongs to God, Lord of the Worlds, the Lord of Mercy, the Giver of Mercy, Master of the Day of Judgement. It is You we worship; it is You we ask for help. Guide us to the straight path: the path of those You have blessed, those who incur no anger and who have not gone astray.

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** Late-antique Arabia's monotheist ferment; al-Fātiḥa's vocabulary of mercy, judgment, and guidance sits within the shared Jewish-Christian-ḥanīf religious atmosphere of the Hijaz.
- **Liturgical role.** Its primacy is functional: as the obligatory recitation of ṣalāt, it is repeated more often than any other scripture passage on earth.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Fixed in the Uthmanic codex; universally memorized.

## Themes and motifs
- Divine mercy — the doubled raḥmān/raḥīm framing God's defining attribute.
- The straight path (al-ṣirāṭ al-mustaqīm) — guidance as the central petition.
- Worship and judgment — God as Lord of the worlds and Master of the Day.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **The most-recited prayer on earth, and the Islamic counterpart to the Lord's Prayer.** Al-Fātiḥa's seven verses are said by observant Muslims at least seventeen times a day (once per prayer-cycle across the five daily prayers), making it almost certainly the single most-recited piece of scripture in human history. Structurally it parallels the Lord's Prayer (Matt 6:9–13): address and praise of God, acknowledgment of his rule, and petition — a shared late-antique liturgical shape in which a short text becomes the heartbeat of a tradition's prayer life (Nasr 2015, *Study Quran* pp.3–9; Reynolds 2010 pp.36–40).
