# Epistle of James

## What it is
A letter of practical wisdom and moral exhortation, closer to Proverbs and the Sermon on the Mount than to Paul's argumentative epistles. Addressed to "the twelve tribes in the Dispersion," it strings together teaching on trials, the dangers of the tongue, partiality toward the rich, and above all the insistence that genuine faith must show itself in works — the New Testament's most direct challenge to a "faith alone" reading of Paul.

## Primary text

### James 2:17, 26
*Trans. NRSV*
> So faith by itself, if it has no works, is dead... For just as the body without the spirit is dead, so faith without works is also dead.

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** Jewish-Christian wisdom piety; a community marked by economic inequality and the temptation of empty profession.
- **Authorship.** Disputed between an early letter of James the Just and a later pseudonymous composition.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Among the antilegomena; its canonicity was long contested, and Luther ranked it below the "chief" books.

## Themes and motifs
- Faith and works — 2:14–26, the counterpoint to Pauline justification.
- The tongue — the ungovernable power of speech (ch. 3).
- Care for the poor — "pure religion" as visiting orphans and widows (1:27); woes on the rich (5:1–6).

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[document-romans]] — *polemic-against* (or *corrective-of*): James's "justified by works and not by faith alone" (2:24) stands in deliberate tension with Romans' "justified by faith apart from works."
- → [[phase-3-017-gospel-of-matthew]] — *parallel-motif*: James echoes the Sermon on the Mount more than any other NT letter.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **James versus Paul: the faith-and-works fault line that split Western Christianity.** James 2:24 — "a person is justified by works and not by faith alone" — reads as a direct rebuttal of the Pauline formula (Rom 3:28). The early church held the two in canonical tension; the Reformation could not. Luther, prizing Paul's *sola fide*, dismissed James as "an epistle of straw" with "nothing of the gospel about it" and relegated it in his Bible. The Catholic–Protestant divide over justification is, in part, a quarrel between two books of the same New Testament (Johnson 1995, AYB 37A pp.58–64, 247–252; Allison 2013, ICC).
