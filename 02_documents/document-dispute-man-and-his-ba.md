# The Dispute between a Man and his Ba

## What it is
A Middle Kingdom literary dialogue of remarkable psychological depth, in which a man so weary of life that he longs for death argues the matter out with his own ba — his soul. The ba resists, warning that if the man forces an untimely death it will desert him, leaving him without the proper burial his afterlife requires. The debate rises to four superb lyric poems: on the man's ruined name, on the loneliness of his suffering, on death as a longed-for healing and homecoming, and on the persistence of life beyond it.

## Primary text

### The third poem
*Trans. Lichtheim (1973)*
> Death is before me today like a sick man's recovery, like going outdoors after confinement. Death is before me today like the fragrance of myrrh, like sitting under sail on a breezy day.

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** Middle Kingdom reflection on mortality, the soul, and the worth of life amid social upheaval.
- **Genre.** Egyptian "pessimistic" wisdom, the meditative counterpart to the confident instruction texts.
- **Discovery / transmission.** A single, justly famous papyrus.

## Themes and motifs
- The value of life versus the longing for death.
- The self in dialogue with its soul — an early interior debate.
- Death as release and homecoming.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **Egypt's Job — a soul debating whether life is worth living.** Centuries before Job cursed the day of his birth (Job 3) and Qoheleth judged the dead more fortunate than the living (Eccl 4:2–3), this Middle Kingdom Egyptian poem set a weary man against his own soul to argue the worth of existence and the sweetness of death. It is the earliest and most lyrical member of the ancient Near Eastern "pessimistic wisdom" tradition — the questioning, anti-confident strand that runs through Ludlul, the Babylonian Theodicy, Job, and Ecclesiastes — and shows that the problem of suffering and the value of life was being probed with literary subtlety along the Nile two thousand years before the biblical wisdom books (Lichtheim 1973 pp.163–169; Allen 2011).
