# Poetic Edda

## What it is
A collection of 29 Old Norse mythological and heroic poems preserved primarily in a single manuscript, the Codex Regius (Iceland, ~1270). The Mythological poems include *Vǫluspá* ("Prophecy of the Seeress" — the principal Norse cosmogonic-eschatological poem, narrating creation from the body of Ymir, the cosmic tree Yggdrasill, Baldr's death, and Ragnarǫk); *Hávamál* (Odin's wisdom-and-conduct sayings, including the "Rúnatal" passage on Odin's self-sacrifice for the runes); *Vafþrúðnismál* and *Grímnismál* (cosmographic catechisms); *Lokasenna* (Loki's flyting); *Þrymskviða* (Thor and the giants); *Skírnismál* (Freyr's wooing of Gerðr); and others. The Heroic poems cover the Volsung-Niflung cycle (Sigurðr / Sigfrid; Brynhild; the Niflung tragedy). The Eddic meter is *fornyrðislag* / *málaháttr* / *ljóðaháttr*. The Poetic Edda is the principal source for what we can reconstruct of pre-Christian Germanic religion.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Iceland was settled ~870–930 CE by Norse migrants. The Icelandic Commonwealth (930–1262) governed itself through the Alþingi; Christianity was officially adopted by political decision (the *kristnitaka*) in 1000 CE, but the Alþingi's compromise permitted private continuation of pagan practice for a transitional period. Iceland thus preserved pre-Christian Germanic religious material into the Christian era *in writing* in a way that no continental Germanic culture did — because the same families (Christian by ~1000) maintained literary interest in their pagan ancestors' material.
- **Religious milieu.** The poems themselves are products of pagan-Germanic religious tradition (Æsir-Vanir cosmology, ritual feasting, *blót* sacrifice, fate-doctrine, valor-ethos). Their preservation, in a Christian Iceland, is a paradox: Christian scribes preserved pagan poetry. The Christian frame is sometimes visible (some scholars argue *Vǫluspá* shows Christian apocalyptic influence) but the underlying material is unambiguously pre-Christian.
- **Who was behind it.** Anonymous Icelandic scribes ~1270 compiled the Codex Regius from older oral and possibly written sources. The poems themselves are by anonymous skalds across centuries; *Vǫluspá* is conventionally dated late 10th-c.; *Hávamál* in its stratified form draws on older material.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Codex Regius rediscovered 1643 by Bishop Brynjólfur Sveinsson, who initially (mistakenly) attributed it to the 12th-c. Icelandic priest Sæmundr fróði — hence the older name "Sæmundar Edda." First printed editions from 17th–18th c. opened the Norse material to European Romantic and later academic engagement.

## Themes and motifs
- [[norse-cosmogony]] — *Vǫluspá*'s cosmogony: the gap *Ginnungagap*, the giant Ymir from whose dismembered body the cosmos is shaped, the world-tree *Yggdrasill*, the three norns *Urðr/Verðandi/Skuld*. The body-dismemberment cosmogony is structurally akin to Mesopotamian (Tiamat) and Indic (Purusha) types.
- [[ragnarok-apocalyptic]] — the cosmic-final battle and world-renewal; Norse eschatology is *cyclical* (the world ends and a new world begins) rather than terminal.
- [[fate-and-doom]] — *wyrd* / *urðr*: the inexorable fate that even gods are subject to. Differs structurally from Greek *moira* and Iranian *xwarrah*.
- [[chaoskampf]] — Thor vs. the Midgard-Serpent at Ragnarǫk; Týr lost his hand binding Fenrir; the Norse pattern is cosmic-restraint of chaos-beings rather than primordial-defeat.
- [[divine-self-sacrifice]] — Odin hangs himself on Yggdrasill in *Hávamál* 138–141 to win the runes (*"sjálfur sjálfum mér"* — "myself to myself"). The pattern's structural relation to the Christian cross is genuine; whether contact-influenced or independent is debated.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-8-015-prose-edda-snorri]] — *commentary-on*: Snorri's Prose Edda (~1220) is the principal interpretive commentary on the Eddic poetic tradition; Snorri quotes many of the poems and gives narrative summaries of the myths they assume.
- → [[phase-8-018-kalevala]] — *parallel-motif*: northern-European pre-Christian mythological-poetic tradition preserved in late textualization. Finnish runo-poetry and Norse Eddic poetry are independent traditions but share structural features (oral-formulaic composition; cosmogonic concerns).
- → Enuma Elish (Phase 1) and Purusha-sūkta (Rigveda 10.90, Phase 1 Vedic) — *parallel-motif*: cosmogony-by-dismemberment is an Indo-European-and-broader Eurasian deep-structure motif.
- → Christianity (Phase 4 / Phase 5) — *contested-influence*: Odin's self-sacrifice on the tree; *Vǫluspá*'s eschatological imagery; possible Christian-context influences on late Eddic poetry. Most scholars accept *some* late Christian-context shaping (Lindow 2001; McKinnell 2005) while preserving the substantial pre-Christian core.
- → modern Ásatrú / Heathenry (Phase 7) — *foundational-text*: contemporary Heathen reconstruction draws principally on the Eddas.

## Disputes
- **Date of Vǫluspá.** Late 10th c. is mainstream; some argue earlier (pre-Christian), some later (with Christian apocalyptic influence). Compromise: pre-Christian core, late-10th-c. final form possibly redacted under Christian-era influence.
- **Pre-Christian vs. Christian-era composition.** Each poem must be assessed individually. Hávamál stratification is well-established (Dronke); the *Rúnatal* portion may be among the oldest material.
- **Reliability for reconstructing pagan-Germanic religion.** Stable consensus that the Eddic poems are the best evidence we have; less consensus on how to weight literary-Christian-era reshaping.
