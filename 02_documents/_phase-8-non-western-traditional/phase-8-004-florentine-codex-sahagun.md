# Florentine Codex (Historia general de las cosas de Nueva España)

## What it is
A twelve-book bilingual Nahuatl-Spanish ethnographic encyclopedia of pre-conquest Mexica civilization, compiled by Franciscan friar Bernardino de Sahagún with a team of Indigenous Nahua collaborators (former students of the Colegio de Santa Cruz de Tlatelolco) between ~1545 and 1577. Book content: I — the gods; II — the calendar feasts and veintenas; III — Aztec mythology of the gods' origins; IV — the divinatory calendar; V — omens; VI — rhetoric and philosophy (the *huehuetlatolli* — speeches of the elders); VII — astronomy; VIII — kings and rulers; IX — merchants; X — Nahua peoples (ethnography proper); XI — natural history (animals, plants, stones); XII — the Indigenous-side account of the Spanish conquest (1519–1521). The Nahuatl column is in many places more detailed than the Spanish; Sahagún's "Spanish" is often a summary rather than a translation.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** ~50–70 years after the Spanish conquest of Tenochtitlan (1521). The Mexica elite was destroyed politically but their cultural memory remained accessible through still-living elders. New Spain was being constructed; the Catholic Church's evangelization required understanding what it was replacing.
- **Religious milieu.** Active Catholic missionary suppression of Indigenous religion, paired with paradoxical scholarly need to understand it. Sahagún (1499–1590) explicitly framed his ethnographic project as preparation for more effective evangelization — a doctor must know the disease to cure it — but in practice he produced a sympathetic and unprecedentedly detailed record.
- **Who was behind it.** Bernardino de Sahagún, Franciscan, working with named Nahua collaborators: Antonio Valeriano of Azcapotzalco, Alonso Vegerano of Cuauhtitlan, Martín Jacobita of Tlatelolco, Pedro de San Buenaventura and Andrés Leonardo. These were tri-lingual (Nahuatl, Latin, Spanish) products of the Colegio de Santa Cruz. Sahagún also interviewed elders in Tepepulco, Tlatelolco, and Mexico-Tenochtitlan. The Codex is genuinely collaborative ethnography — not pure colonial imposition.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Philip II ordered the work suppressed in 1577 — fear that vernacular accounts of pagan religion would impede Christianization. Sahagún's drafts were nearly destroyed; the final Florence copy survived through the Medici library. First published in part in 1829–30 (Bustamante's Spanish edition); Anderson-Dibble's English-from-Nahuatl translation (1950–1982) made the Indigenous voice accessible.

## Themes and motifs
- [[mesoamerican-cosmogony]] — Book III preserves the most detailed surviving account of the five suns, Quetzalcoatl-Tezcatlipoca rivalry, and the creation of humans from bones from Mictlan.
- [[creation-by-word]] / divine fiat — preserved in the *teotlatolli* (god-speech) sections; the gods' decisive utterances structure cosmic events.
- [[sacrifice-ritual]] — Book II describes the eighteen 20-day veintenas with their associated rituals in extensive detail; the principal pre-conquest source.
- [[divinatory-calendar]] — Book IV documents *tonalpohualli* divination as actually practiced.
- *huehuetlatolli* (speeches of the elders, Book VI) — Nahua rhetorical-ethical wisdom; long speeches on parenthood, marriage, statecraft, death. Compare Egyptian wisdom literature, Proverbs.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-8-002-codex-borgia]] — *commentary-on*: the Florentine Codex is the verbal-ethnographic complement to the Borgia-group pictographic codices; reading Borgia *requires* the kind of explanation Sahagún provides.
- → [[phase-8-001-popol-vuh]] — *shared-milieu* / *parallel-motif*: both are post-conquest salvage of pre-Columbian religious tradition, one K'iche' Maya, one Mexica Nahua. Different but related Mesoamerican cosmogonies preserved in parallel by the same colonial crisis.
- → [[phase-8-005-chilam-balam]] — *parallel-motif*: Yucatec Maya post-conquest religious-prophetic compilations are the Maya analogue (less centrally controlled, more dispersed) to Sahagún's Mexica encyclopedia.
- → European wisdom-literature traditions — *parallel-motif*: Book VI *huehuetlatolli* is structurally comparable to Egyptian Instructions, Proverbs, Hesiod's *Works and Days*. The comparison is interpretive, not historical.

## Disputes
- **Methodological status.** Some critics (David Carrasco, others have nuanced) have warned against reading the Florentine as a transparent window onto pre-conquest religion — it is a colonial document with its own selection biases (e.g., emphasis on human sacrifice for European-audience purposes; suppression of erotic content). Lockhart and León-Portilla defend the underlying reliability *when read carefully through the Nahuatl column*.
- **Authorship attribution.** How much of the work is "Sahagún" and how much is his Nahua collaborators? Modern scholarship (León-Portilla 2002, Klor de Alva, Burkhart) increasingly emphasizes the collaborators' role.
