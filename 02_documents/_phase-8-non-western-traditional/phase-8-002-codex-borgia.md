# Codex Borgia

## What it is
A 76-page pre-Columbian Mesoamerican ritual-divinatory manuscript, painted on deer-hide gesso in a screenfold format. The codex is entirely pictographic — there is no alphabetic or hieroglyphic text — and is structured around the 260-day divinatory calendar (*tonalpohualli*) and the 365-day solar year. Major sections include: the 20 day-signs with their patron deities; the nine "Lords of the Night"; the Venus tables and Venus-related ritual; an extraordinary cosmogonic and theurgic central section (pp. 29–46) showing a journey through underworld and celestial layers; the 18 monthly veintenas (festivals); marriage divination tables; and ritual instructions for diviner-priests (*tonalpouhque*).

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Late Postclassic central Mexico (~1400–1521). The Triple Alliance (Mexica-Tenochca, Texcoco, Tlacopan — the "Aztec empire") dominated the Valley of Mexico, but the Borgia codex itself was probably produced in a non-Mexica polity — Tlaxcala, Cholula, or the Mixteca-Puebla region — that remained outside or in tension with Tenochtitlan.
- **Religious milieu.** Pan-Mesoamerican religious system organized around the dual calendar, sacred-bundle deities, and reciprocal sacrifice. The codex documents the operational theology of the *tonalpouhque* (calendar-diviner), not popular belief — it is a working priest's reference book.
- **Who was behind it.** Anonymous *tlacuilo* (painter-scribe), trained in a calmecac (priest school). The codex is one of five extant pre-conquest Mesoamerican religious-divinatory codices outside the Maya area (the "Borgia Group": Borgia, Vaticanus B, Cospi, Laud, Fejérváry-Mayer).
- **Discovery / transmission.** Brought to Europe within decades of the conquest. Survived in the Borgia family library, Italy; entered the Vatican Library 1804. Survived the conquistadors' systematic destruction of Mesoamerican religious books that destroyed perhaps 99%+ of pre-Columbian texts.

## Themes and motifs
- [[mesoamerican-cosmogony]] — the Borgia's pages 29–46 are the most detailed pre-conquest visualization of Mesoamerican cosmic structure; the diviner-priest mentally journeys through these layers.
- [[divinatory-calendar]] — the entire codex is organized around the 260-day count; calendrical divination is *the* operational religious technology of late-Postclassic Mesoamerica.
- [[tonalpohualli]] — 20 × 13 = 260 days; pairs of day-sign and number; each combination has divinatory significance.
- [[underworld-descent]] — pages 29–46 depict descent through nine underworld levels and ascent through thirteen celestial levels.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-8-003-dresden-codex-maya]] — *parallel-motif* (divinatory codex genre); Dresden is the Maya analogue — Venus tables, eclipse tables, ritual almanacs.
- → [[phase-8-001-popol-vuh]] — *shared-milieu* / *parallel-motif*: same religious-cultural sphere broadly; Borgia is what the Popol Vuh's pre-conquest material was *recorded in* before alphabetization.
- → [[phase-8-004-florentine-codex-sahagun]] — *commentary-on* / *shared-milieu*: Sahagún's Florentine Codex is the post-conquest ethnographic recovery of the same religious system that the Borgia documents from inside it.
- → Mesopotamian divinatory genres (Enūma Anu Enlil) — *parallel-motif*: structurally analogous priest-divination apparatus arising independently. The comparison must remain typological, not historical.

## Disputes
- **Provenance.** Tlaxcala-Puebla vs. Cholula vs. Mixteca-Puebla region; no firm consensus. The "Mixteca-Puebla" stylistic horizon overlaps multiple polities.
- **Reading order and central section.** Pages 29–46 are particularly disputed; Anders/Jansen/Reyes García (1993) read them as a coherent ritual-cosmological journey; other scholars (Boone 2007) read them as separate ritual scenes.
