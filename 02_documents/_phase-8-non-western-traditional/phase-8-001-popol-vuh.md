# Popol Vuh

## What it is
The principal surviving narrative of highland Maya (K'iche') cosmogony and dynastic history. The text comprises: (1) a creation account in which the Heart of Sky (Huracán) and the Plumed Serpent (Tepeu/Q'uq'umatz) speak the world into being from primordial sea; successive failed humanities (mud, wood, monkeys) destroyed; (2) the Hero Twins narrative — Hunahpu and Xbalanque defeat the lords of Xibalba (the Maya underworld) through ballgame, deception, and self-resurrection, then ascend as Sun and Moon (or Sun and Venus); (3) the creation of "true humans" from maize; (4) the migrations and dynastic genealogies of the K'iche' lineages down to the colonial-era ruling houses.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Composed ~1554, three decades after Pedro de Alvarado's conquest of the K'iche' kingdom (1524). The K'iche' elite, recently dispossessed, transcribed their pre-conquest oral tradition (and probably pre-conquest hieroglyphic books, now lost) into Latin script — partly to legitimate surviving lineage claims to the Spanish crown, partly to preserve knowledge they correctly anticipated would otherwise be destroyed.
- **Religious milieu.** Catholic missionary pressure was intense; the text never mentions Christianity directly but its existence is shaped by it (clandestine transmission; preserved within a parish; later transcribed by a Dominican). The narrative remains religiously alive among present-day K'iche' communities.
- **Who was behind it.** Anonymous K'iche' scribes of the Quiché elite ~1554. Francisco Ximénez, Dominican parish priest of Chichicastenango (~1701), discovered and transcribed the manuscript in parallel K'iche'/Spanish; his copy is the only surviving witness.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Ximénez's manuscript circulated in obscurity until Carl Scherzer (1857) and Charles Étienne Brasseur de Bourbourg (1861) published it. Karl Schultze-Jena (1944) produced the first critically-aware German translation; Edmonson (1971), Tedlock (1985), and Christenson (2003) produced the modern scholarly editions.

## Themes and motifs
- [[creation-by-word]] — the Heart of Sky speaks the cosmos: "earth" arises at the sound of the word. Compare Genesis 1, Memphite Theology.
- [[primordial-waters]] — initial state: only sky above, only sea below. Compare Enuma Elish, Genesis 1:2.
- [[hero-twins]] — Hunahpu and Xbalanque; the defining Mesoamerican mythologem, attested in Classic-period iconography centuries before the colonial text.
- [[underworld-descent]] — descent into Xibalba; the Twins survive trials through cunning and self-sacrifice. Compare Inanna's descent, Persephone, Christ's harrowing.
- [[mesoamerican-cosmogony]] — humans made from maize; the agricultural cycle is cosmologically grounded.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-8-002-codex-borgia]] — *parallel-motif*: Mesoamerican cosmic-cycle iconography. Codex Borgia is central-Mexican (Nahua/Mixtec sphere) but shares the underworld-descent and creation-cycle structure attested in Maya material.
- → [[phase-8-003-dresden-codex-maya]] — *shared-milieu*: pre-Columbian Maya ritual-astronomical book; Dresden is one of three to four surviving Maya hieroglyphic codices and the closest physical witness to the kind of book the Popol Vuh's narrative would have been recorded in pre-conquest.
- → [[phase-8-004-florentine-codex-sahagun]] — *shared-milieu*: parallel colonial ethnographic salvage project on the Aztec side; Sahagún and the Popol Vuh scribes belong to the same generation-long crisis moment.
- → [[phase-8-005-chilam-balam]] — *shared-milieu* / *parallel-motif*: Yucatec Maya post-conquest religious compilation; together with Popol Vuh forms the textual surviving residue of Maya religion.
- → Genesis 1 (Phase 2) — *parallel-motif* (creation-by-word + primordial waters); no contact hypothesis, but a clean example of independent cosmogonic convergence on similar narrative grammar. Ref: Christenson 2003 introduction; Tedlock 1996.

## Disputes
- **Pre-Columbian attestation of the narrative.** Some 19th- and early-20th-century scholars suggested the text was substantially shaped by Christian contact. The discovery of San Bartolo's Late Preclassic murals (~100 BCE) by William Saturno (2001 onward) showing the Maize God and proto-Hero-Twin imagery, plus extensive Classic-period vase iconography of the Twins (Coe 1989), now demonstrates the *narrative core* is unambiguously pre-Columbian. Selection, framing, and Christian-language calques in the 1554 text are real but do not undermine the underlying Maya substrate.
- **Author identity.** Anonymous. Tedlock argues for an identifiable K'iche' lineage authorship; Christenson is more cautious. The author was certainly literate in both K'iche' and Latin script, and member of the K'iche' elite.
