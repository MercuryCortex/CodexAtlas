# Huarochirí Manuscript

## What it is
The only extensive surviving narrative of pre-Hispanic Andean religion in an Indigenous language (Quechua). 31 chapters plus supplements, compiled ~1598–1608, recording the mythology, ritual practices, and *huaca* (sacred-being) cult of the Yauyo people of the Huarochirí province east of Lima. The manuscript narrates: (1) the cosmogonic primacy of Pariacaca and Tutay Quiri (the local mountain-storm gods); (2) the trickster-creator Cuni Raya Viracocha's wanderings and seductions; (3) the world-renewing flood from which Vichama and his mother survive on the mountain peaks; (4) the chronicle of the Yauyo and Huarochirí ethnic groups' migrations; (5) the post-conquest persistence of *huaca* cults in modified form. Each chapter typically narrates one *huaca* — a localized sacred being inhering in a mountain, spring, rock, or ancestral mummy — and the ritual obligations owed to it.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** ~75 years after Pizarro's conquest (1532–33), in the Viceroyalty of Peru under the early-colonial encomienda system. The Third Council of Lima (1582–83) had decreed systematic extirpation of Indigenous religion; Francisco de Ávila launched the major Lima-archdiocese extirpation campaigns from 1608.
- **Religious milieu.** Active campaign to destroy *huaca* cult — Ávila publicly burned *huaca* idols in Lima cathedral square (1610), tortured suspected sorcerers, destroyed mummified ancestors. The manuscript was commissioned precisely as ammunition for this campaign: Ávila needed to know what Indigenous religious practices were so he could identify and suppress them.
- **Who was behind it.** Francisco de Ávila (1573–1647), a half-mestizo "extirpator of idolatries" who collected the material from Indigenous informants in the Huarochirí region. The actual narration is by anonymous Quechua-speakers. One mestizo collaborator named Tomás may be partly responsible for the writing.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Bound with Ávila's other papers, ignored for centuries. Hermann Trimborn published a partial German translation (1939); Arguedas's Spanish translation (1966) made it widely known; Salomon and Urioste (1991) produced the critical English edition.

## Themes and motifs
- [[andean-cosmogony]] — Andean creation is *not* ex nihilo: the world has existed through cycles; current humanity descends from survivors of cataclysm.
- [[creator-trickster]] — Cuni Raya Viracocha appears in rags, seduces women through deception, vanishes; conjoined trickster and creator (compare Norse Loki, Yoruba Eshu).
- [[huaca-veneration]] — every distinctive landscape feature is or contains a sacred being; the religion is fundamentally place-bound. The Andean *huaca* concept has no precise European or Mediterranean equivalent.
- [[ancestral-pact]] — the chapters repeatedly establish reciprocal obligations between named ethnic groups and named *huacas*; ethnic identity is religious-genealogical.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-8-001-popol-vuh]] — *parallel-motif* / *shared-milieu*: parallel post-conquest salvage of Indigenous American religion in Indigenous language. Both texts exist *because* the conquest happened; both are testaments under suppression.
- → [[phase-8-004-florentine-codex-sahagun]] — *parallel-motif*: Sahagún's Mexica encyclopedia and Ávila's Huarochirí material are colonial-ethnography genres responding to the same continental crisis with different methods (Sahagún: Indigenous-collaborative encyclopedia; Ávila: extractive interrogation).
- → Inca state religion (oral / Garcilaso, Cieza, Cobo) — *shared-milieu*: Huarochirí preserves a *local* religious tradition that the Inca incorporated into their empire-wide cosmology; the Yauyo region was Inca-administered but retained pre-Inca religious specificity. Ref: Salomon 1991 intro.
- → Genesis 6–9 flood, Atrahasis flood — *parallel-motif*: world-destroying flood with mountain-peak survivors. Independent convergence; no contact hypothesis.

## Disputes
- **Authorial agency.** How much of the manuscript reflects Indigenous narration vs. Ávila's editorial selection is contested. Salomon argues that the Quechua textual texture is genuinely Indigenous; Adorno notes Ávila's framing distorts content selection.
- **Dating and unity.** Likely composed over multiple sessions ~1598–1608; whether it is a single text or a compilation is debated.
- **Local vs. pan-Andean.** Whether Huarochirí material is representative of Andean religion generally or specifically Yauyo is debated; comparative work with Cajatambo extirpation records (Duviols) shows broad structural commonality with local variation.
