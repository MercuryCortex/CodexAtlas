# Gayanashagowa (The Great Law of Peace)

## What it is
The **constitutional covenant of the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) Confederacy** — its founding law of union, attributed to the **Peacemaker** and his spokesman **Hiawatha**, who (the tradition relates) ended a cycle of blood-feud and brought five warring nations into a single league of peace. The Great Law is at once a religious and a political document: it establishes the council of fifty sachems, distributes authority among the nations and the clans, vests in the **clan mothers** the right to choose and to depose chiefs, prescribes the **condolence ceremony** for installing new leaders, and lays down procedures for consensus deliberation. It was carried not in writing but in **wampum** and in the trained memory of the council, and written down only at the turn of the 20th century.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** The Northeast Woodlands before and after European contact; the Confederacy was a major power that the British and French treated as a sovereign diplomatic actor.
- **Religious milieu.** The Great Law is grounded in the Haudenosaunee cosmology of the Sky World and the condolence tradition; the Peacemaker is a culture-hero of quasi-prophetic status.
- **Who was behind it.** The Peacemaker and Hiawatha in tradition; the council of chiefs as transmitters; Newhouse and Parker as early recorders.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Oral and wampum-encoded for centuries; the written versions (Newhouse, Parker) are 20th-century compilations that vary and are themselves objects of study.

## Themes and motifs
- [[divine-kingship]] — inverted: the Great Law replaces hereditary autocracy with a council and with clan-mother oversight, a sacralized *anti*-monarchical sovereignty in which leaders are "raised up" and can be removed.

## Disputes
- **Date.** Strongly contested. Haudenosaunee oral tradition and some scholars (e.g. Mann & Fields, via a solar-eclipse correlation) argue for a date as early as the 12th century; most academic historians place the league's formation around **1450–1600**.
- **The "Iroquois influence thesis."** The claim that the Great Law **shaped the United States Constitution** is genuinely debated and must be stated carefully. *What is well documented:* **Benjamin Franklin invoked the Iroquois league** as an example of intercolonial union (his 1751 letter and the 1754 Albany Congress), and a 1988 U.S. Congressional concurrent resolution (H.Con.Res. 331) formally acknowledged Iroquois contributions to American governance. *What is contested:* the strong thesis — that the framers consciously modeled the Constitution on the Great Law — argued by Grinde & Johansen (1991) is **rejected or heavily qualified by most historians** (notably Elisabeth Tooker 1988, and Levy, Rakove, and Shannon), who note the framers' overwhelmingly European political sources and the absence of the Great Law's defining features (clan mothers, consensus, the condolence ritual) from the Constitution.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **A documented Indigenous federalism — and an honest case study in disputed transmission.** The Great Law is, on its own terms, one of the world's oldest functioning constitutions and a sophisticated Indigenous theory of federal union, clan-based checks, and women's political authority (Fenton 1998). Its *relationship* to American constitutionalism is exactly the kind of cross-tradition claim the Atlas is built to handle with tier-discipline: a **real, limited, documented contact** (Franklin's invocation of the Iroquois example; the 1988 Congressional acknowledgment) around which a **stronger, contested influence thesis** (Grinde & Johansen) has grown that mainstream historians dispute (Tooker 1988). The value here is precisely in marking where solid evidence ends and advocacy begins — not in asserting the strong claim as settled.
