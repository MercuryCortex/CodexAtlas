# Santería / Lucumí Liturgical Tradition

## What it is
The Cuban Yoruba-Catholic syncretic religion, also called *Regla de Ocha* (the Rule of the Orishas) or *La Regla Lucumí*. "Lucumí" is the Cuban-Yoruba ethnonym (derived from the Yoruba greeting *oluku mi*, "my friend") and is the preferred internal term for practitioners; "Santería" ("way of the saints") was originally a derogatory Cuban Catholic term but has been internally reclaimed. The system is structured around the worship of *orishas* — Yoruba deities transmitted via the Atlantic slave trade — paired with Catholic saints in operational syncretism. Each orisha has *caminos* (paths / aspects), characteristic colors, numbers, foods, songs, and Catholic-saint pairings: Obatalá ↔ Our Lady of Mercy / Our Lady of Las Mercedes; Yemayá ↔ Our Lady of Regla; Oshún ↔ Our Lady of Charity of El Cobre (patroness of Cuba); Shangó ↔ St. Barbara; Ogún ↔ St. Peter; Elegguá ↔ Holy Child of Atocha. Practitioners are initiated through *asiento* (becoming "seated" with a specific orisha, the *ángel de la guarda*), a week-long initiation costing significant resources. The associated *Regla de Ifá* (Ifá-practice) is reserved for male *babaláwo* who undergo separate, more extensive initiation.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Spanish colonial Cuba; the 19th-c. sugar boom enslaved a massive new Yoruba population (especially after the collapse of the Oyo Empire ~1836 — many of the captives shipped to Cuba in the 1840s–1860s were Yoruba). Slavery in Cuba was not abolished until 1886. The Spanish *cabildos de nación* (ethnic-national religious confraternities, originally Catholic-Spanish) became the institutional space within which Lucumí religious organization continued under Catholic cover.
- **Religious milieu.** Cuban Catholicism's strong saint-cult devotion provided the ready operational pairings for Yoruba orishas. After Cuban independence (1898–1902) and especially after the 1959 Cuban Revolution (which initially repressed religion, then in the late Soviet/post-Soviet period permitted and even normalized Afro-Cuban practice), Santería became a major and increasingly publicly visible religious presence. The Cuban diaspora (especially Miami after 1959, NYC) carried Santería into the United States; the *Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye v. Hialeah* US Supreme Court case (1993) struck down Hialeah ordinances banning animal sacrifice and is a landmark religious-freedom decision.
- **Who was behind it.** No founder; emergent collective tradition with named lineages. Priesthood: *santero* / *santera* (Ocha-priesthood), *babaláwo* (Ifá-priest, men only); each initiate is the spiritual "child" (*ahijado/a*) of an initiator (*padrino/madrina*), creating extended ritual-kinship networks.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous practice; ethnographic-textual emergence with Fernando Ortiz (early 20th c., *Los negros brujos* 1906 with significant initial racism, later more sympathetic work) and Lydia Cabrera (*El Monte* 1954, working with practitioners). Late-20th-c. scholarship (Brandon, Murphy, Mason, Palmié) defines current state.

## Themes and motifs
- [[afro-diasporic-syncretism]] — Santería is, with Haitian Vodou and Brazilian Candomblé, one of the three principal New-World African-religious-syncretic traditions; Yoruba substrate is more dominant here than in Vodou.
- [[possession-ritual]] — orishas "mount" devotees during *toques de santo* (drum-and-song ceremonies); possession-state communication is the principal religious technology.
- [[orisha-pantheon]] — direct transmission of the Yoruba orisha system; the *Dilogún* (sixteen-cowries oracle, a Santería-modified version of Yoruba Ifá divination) is the principal divinatory apparatus for *santero* practice (Ifá itself reserved for *babaláwo*).
- [[divination-systems]] — *Dilogún* uses 16 cowrie shells; *Ifá* uses palm-nuts or *opele*; both produce binary-signature *odu* with associated verse-corpus precedents.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-8-007-ifa-divination-corpus]] — *manuscript-transmission* (oral, diasporic): Lukumí Ifá-practice is the closest Caribbean diaspora preservation of Yoruba Ifá. Cross-Atlantic priesthood exchanges (Cuban *babaláwos* trained in Nigeria, Nigerian Ifá leaders received in Cuba) have intensified since the 1980s.
- → [[phase-8-016-haitian-vodou-liturgical-tradition]] — *parallel-motif* / *shared-milieu*: parallel Caribbean African-religious syncretism, drawing on different substrate proportions (Vodou: Fon-Yoruba-Kongo; Santería: predominantly Yoruba). Many orishas (Yoruba) and *lwa* (Haitian) are recognizably the same figure transformed by different Caribbean contexts.
- → Spanish Catholic saint-cult (Phase 5–6) — *syncretic-identification*: orisha-saint pairings are theologically functional, not mere camouflage. Murphy 1988 argues this was genuine syncretism within Catholic plausibility-structure.
- → Brazilian Candomblé — *parallel-motif*: parallel Brazilian Yoruba-Catholic syncretism with similar structure but distinct development; not covered in this Phase 8 set but adjacent.
- → 20th-c. anti-Santería policing — *polemic-against*: throughout the 20th c. Santería has faced periodic legal suppression (Cuban revolutionary 1960s; US municipalities pre-1993); media depictions have been racist and sensationalist.

## Disputes
- **Yoruba vs. broader-African substrate proportions.** Older scholarship overstressed Yoruba; current work (Brandon, Palmié) restores attention to Kongo (Regla de Palo) as an associated but distinct Afro-Cuban tradition often co-practiced with Lucumí.
- **Catholic syncretism — internal or external.** Whether saint-pairings are theologically internalized or operational-protective varies by individual practitioner and historical period. Older missionary-derived interpretation (camouflage only) is now considered methodologically inadequate.
- **Authenticity disputes vis-à-vis African Yoruba religion.** Some 20th-c. Nigerian Yoruba ritual specialists have argued Cuban Lukumí practice has diverged significantly from "authentic" Yoruba religion; some Cuban *babaláwo* counter that Lukumí preserves material lost in Africa. The exchanges (Wande Abimbola visiting Cuba; Cuban *babaláwo* training in Nigeria) have been productive on both sides.
