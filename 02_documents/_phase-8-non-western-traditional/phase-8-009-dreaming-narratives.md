# Australian Aboriginal Dreaming Narratives (the Dreaming / Tjukurpa / Altyerre)

## What it is
"The Dreaming" — translation of various Aboriginal-language terms (Tjukurpa, Altyerre, Jukurrpa, Wongar, and many others) — is the religious-cosmological category by which Aboriginal Australian peoples understand the creative-foundational events that established country (*Ngurra*), Law, kinship, and the relationships between humans, animal-plant species, and landscape features. The Dreaming is not "in the past" in the way English temporality suggests; Stanner (1979) coined the term "everywhen" to convey that the Dreaming events are simultaneously primordial and presently active. Specific Dreaming narratives are *country-specific*: each tract of land has named ancestral beings whose journeys (recorded as *songlines* / *Yiri*) traverse the country, creating distinctive features (rocks, waterholes, hills) where those beings stopped, fought, transformed, died. Ritual knowledge of these narratives is graded by initiation level and is *restricted* — much sacred-secret knowledge is not appropriately published.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Continuous Aboriginal occupation of the Australian continent for at least 65,000 years (the longest documented continuous human occupation of any landmass). British colonization (1788) initiated catastrophic disruption: frontier killings, smallpox epidemics, the removal of children (Stolen Generations, ~1905–1969), suppression of language and ceremony, dispossession of country. Aboriginal religion has nonetheless persisted; Mabo (1992) and Native Title legal recognition reframed Australian law around acknowledgment of continuing Aboriginal religious-territorial relationship.
- **Religious milieu.** Aboriginal religion is profoundly *local*. There is no pan-Aboriginal canon; there are several hundred distinct nation/language groups, each with its own Dreaming corpus. Some elements are widespread (Rainbow Serpent attestation across northern Australia; ancestral-beings-shape-landscape motif universal), but specifics are non-negotiably particular.
- **Who was behind it.** Aboriginal senior custodians (*tjilpi*, *kirda*, *kurdungurlu* in various languages) hold and transmit Dreaming knowledge across generations through ceremony, song, dance, painting, and country-walking. Ethnographic transcription is by non-Aboriginal anthropologists (Spencer-Gillen 1899; T.G.H. Strehlow 1930s–60s; Stanner 1953 onward; Munn 1973; the Berndts) and increasingly by Aboriginal scholars (Larissa Behrendt, Marcia Langton, Aileen Moreton-Robinson).
- **Discovery / transmission.** From outside: Spencer and Gillen *The Native Tribes of Central Australia* (1899) is the foundational ethnographic text. Strehlow's *Songs of Central Australia* (1971, posthumous) is the largest single Aboriginal-language song-corpus published. Bruce Chatwin's *The Songlines* (1987) introduced the concept to broad audiences (popular, not scholarly). From inside: the Aboriginal art movement (Papunya Tula 1971 onward; Yirrkala bark painting) made Dreaming knowledge globally visible *as art* while preserving distinction between public and restricted content.

## Themes and motifs
- [[ancestral-beings]] — animal-human-landscape ancestors whose journeys created country; not "gods" in a monotheistic sense, not "totems" in a 19th-century anthropological sense, but bound to specific lineages and country in ways that defy easy translation.
- [[landscape-cosmogony]] — the landscape *is* the religious text; features are the bodies and tracks of Dreaming beings. Country is read.
- [[songline-geography]] — long-distance ritual song-routes connecting countries across great distances; each segment owned by specific groups; the system functioned as continental religious and trade-route infrastructure pre-contact.
- [[ritual-knowledge-transmission]] — gradedinitiation: novice (*malulu*) → initiated (*wati*) → senior; restricted knowledge is non-public *not* because of esoteric mystery-cult logic but because release is governed by kinship-and-country relationship.

## Disputes
- **Pan-Aboriginal generalization.** Older anthropology (especially Stanner, sometimes Eliade citing Stanner) presented "the Dreaming" as if a single pan-Aboriginal concept. Current scholarship (Sutton 2003; Morphy 1991; Rose 1992) emphasizes that the Dreaming is a *family-resemblance* category across nations, not a unified doctrine.
- **Baiame and pan-Aboriginal "High God".** Andrew Lang and W.E.H. Stanner (in some moods) read certain southeastern Aboriginal sky-figures (Baiame, Daramulun, Bunjil) as evidence for a "High God" structure across Aboriginal religion. Most current scholarship treats these as specific southeastern-Australian figures, not pan-Aboriginal; the "Aboriginal High God" thesis is largely an artifact of 19th-c. comparative religion seeking proto-monotheism in "primitive" religions.
- **Strehlow's archive.** T.G.H. Strehlow (1908–1978), raised at Hermannsburg Mission, was given access to restricted Arrernte material on the condition of safekeeping. His handling of this material (especially the publication of *Songs of Central Australia* and the disposition of his archive) is intensely contested. The Strehlow Research Centre's archive remains under access restrictions negotiated with Arrernte custodians.
