# Dresden Codex

## What it is
A 39-page (folded; 74 leaves unfolded) pre-Columbian Maya hieroglyphic book — the most complete and most-studied of the three or four surviving Maya codices. Content is overwhelmingly ritual-astronomical: Venus tables (pp. 24, 46–50) tracking the planet's 584-day synodic cycle and the 8-year Venus-Earth resonance; eclipse warning tables (pp. 51–58); a Mars table; ritual almanacs keyed to the 260-day *tzolk'in*; agricultural and divinatory passages; and a closing world-destruction-by-flood scene (p. 74) showing the celestial caiman discharging waters from the sky. The Venus table is so accurate over long durations that the Maya astronomers must have used a correction algorithm — calculated from observation centuries before Copernicus.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Postclassic Maya (~1200–1500 CE), in the long aftermath of the Classic Maya political collapse (~800–900 CE). The Yucatec polities (Chichén Itzá, Mayapán) had absorbed strong central-Mexican (Toltec) cultural influence, visible in the codex's Quetzalcoatl/Kukulkan iconography.
- **Religious milieu.** Maya religion organized around long-count and short-count calendars; the *ajk'uhuun* (priest-scribe) class maintained astronomical observation and ritual scheduling.
- **Who was behind it.** Anonymous priestly scribe(s), drawing on traditions traceable to Classic-period astronomical inscriptions (the Group of the Cross at Palenque, Late Classic stelae). The codex synthesizes centuries of accumulated tabulation.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Acquired by the Saxon Court Library in Dresden in 1739 (from a private collector in Vienna; precise pre-1739 path unknown — likely sent to Europe from Mexico in the 16th century). Förstemann published the first scientific facsimile in 1880, recognizing the astronomical content. Yuri Knorozov (1952) used the Dresden Codex as the principal testbed for his syllabic decipherment of Maya script — the breakthrough that transformed Maya studies.

## Themes and motifs
- [[divinatory-calendar]] — every page is structured by the 260-day *tzolk'in* and the 365-day *haab*; calendrical conjunctions are the operational object of Maya ritual planning.
- [[venus-cycle]] — Dresden's Venus table is the highest-precision pre-modern astronomical document outside Mesopotamia.
- [[eclipse-prediction]] — Dresden's eclipse table covers 405 lunations (~33 years) and identifies dates of eclipse danger.
- [[mesoamerican-cosmogony]] — the closing flood scene (p. 74) shows the world being destroyed by celestial waters; one of the most striking pre-Columbian apocalyptic images.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-8-002-codex-borgia]] — *parallel-motif*: central-Mexican counterpart genre; both are ritual-divinatory codices but Borgia is fully pictographic while Dresden has hieroglyphic text. Their existence together demonstrates the breadth of the Mesoamerican religious-book tradition.
- → [[phase-8-001-popol-vuh]] — *shared-milieu*: K'iche' highland-Maya cosmogonic narrative ↔ Yucatec lowland-Maya ritual-astronomical tabulation. Same religious universe, different textual registers.
- → [[phase-8-005-chilam-balam]] — *shared-milieu* / *manuscript-transmission* (typological): Yucatec post-conquest religious-prophetic books continue, in alphabetic script, the genres preserved hieroglyphically in Dresden. Some of the Books of Chilam Balam transcribe astronomical and ritual material almost certainly drawn from codices like Dresden.
- → Enūma Anu Enlil (Mesopotamian celestial-omen series, Phase 1) — *parallel-motif*: independent development of priestly astronomical-omen technology. Comparison must remain typological.

## Disputes
- **Number of surviving Maya codices.** Dresden, Madrid, Paris are accepted. The Grolier/Sáenz/Mexico City codex was challenged as a 20th-century forgery for decades; Coe et al. (2015–2018) have argued it is authentic, naming it the Mexico Maya Codex; debate continues.
- **Dating.** Dresden's content draws on earlier Classic traditions but the physical book is Postclassic. Bricker & Bricker (2011) propose the Venus table records were updated ~1200 CE.
