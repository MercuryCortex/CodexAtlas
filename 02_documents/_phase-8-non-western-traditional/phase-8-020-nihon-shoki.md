# Nihon Shoki (Chronicles of Japan)

## What it is

The **Nihon Shoki** (日本書紀, "Chronicles of Japan," 720 CE) is Japan's second-oldest chronicle and its first official state history. Compiled in **Classical Chinese** (*kanbun*) by **Prince Toneri** and **Ō no Yasumaro** and presented to **Emperor Genshō** in 720 CE — eight years after the Kojiki — it covers Japanese history from the age of the gods through the reign of Empress Jitō (686–697 CE). At 30 volumes it is substantially longer than the Kojiki and was, for centuries, the publicly authoritative text: Chinese-literate officials and diplomats could read it; the Kojiki's hybrid phonetic script could not.

The Nihon Shoki's single most remarkable editorial feature is its **systematic recording of variant mythological traditions** alongside each other. Rather than collapsing competing versions of the same myth into a single authoritative narrative, the compilers explicitly preserve multiple traditions with the notation "one account says..." (*ippon ni iwaku*). This makes it a uniquely transparent mythological archive.

## Structure

Thirty volumes (*kan*):

- **Volumes 1–2** (Jindai-ki, "Age of the Gods"): the cosmogony and mythological age
- **Volumes 3–30**: reigns of the emperors from Jimmu (legendary first emperor) through Jitō

The cosmological and mythological material that dominates the entire Kojiki is compressed into just 2 volumes here, flanked by 28 volumes of dynastic history.

## The variant-tradition method

The Nihon Shoki's most investigatively important feature is its editorial practice of recording **multiple competing versions** of the same events. For example:

- The Izanagi/Izanami creation story appears in multiple variant forms, each attributed to a different source tradition
- Some variants describe seven generations of creator deities; others compress to a single primordial pair
- The birth order of Amaterasu, Tsukuyomi, and Susanoo differs across variants
- The underworld encounter has variant endings

This practice has no parallel in the Kojiki, which presents a single authoritative narrative. The Nihon Shoki's multi-version approach is the product of Chinese historiographical method (the annalistic history genre *zhengshi*) combined with a pluralist oral-tradition environment.

## Cross-tradition MASSIVE WIN parallels

**1. Multiple variant traditions = Documentary Hypothesis parallel**
The Nihon Shoki's explicit preservation of competing mythological accounts — "one account says X, another account says Y" — is the editorial equivalent of the **Documentary Hypothesis** in the Hebrew Pentateuch: the recognition that a single authoritative text contains distinct source-traditions (J, E, D, P) preserved side by side with visible seams. The difference is that the Nihon Shoki's editors were *consciously transparent* about the multiplicity, whereas the Pentateuch's redactors harmonized their sources into a surface-unified narrative that later scholars had to decompose analytically. The Nihon Shoki shows us what the Hebrew Bible might have looked like if its redactors had preserved the variant versions as variants. The **canonical Gospels** (Matthew, Mark, Luke, John) offer a similar structure at the level of the canon — four variant accounts of the same events — but without explicit "one account says" notation.

**2. Kanbun as civilizational legitimation = Septuagint parallel**
The Nihon Shoki was deliberately written in Classical Chinese — not because its content was originally in Chinese, but because Chinese was the prestige language of East Asian civilization. Writing Japan's sacred history in Chinese was a claim to equal civilizational standing with the Tang Dynasty, comparable to the **Septuagint** (Hebrew scripture translated into Greek to enter the Hellenistic cultural sphere), to **Islamic Arabic** as the sacred-universal language, and to **Latin** as the universal language of the Christian West. Language choice is theological politics.

**3. Buddhism's arrival recorded from inside the host tradition**
The Nihon Shoki records the transmission of Buddhism to Japan (552 or 538 CE, via the Korean kingdom of Baekje) as a dated historical event — the Korean king sends a Buddha image and sutras to the Yamato court; there is a court dispute over whether to adopt the foreign religion; the pro-Buddhist Soga clan prevails over the anti-Buddhist Mononobe and Nakatomi clans. This is one of the only cases in world religious literature where a **sacred text records the arrival of a foreign religion from within the host tradition's own canonical source**, with named factions, a political decision, and a timeline. It is the Japanese equivalent of the conversion of Constantine recorded in a contemporary Roman source, or of Islam's reception of the Bible documented in Hadith literature.

**4. The divine genealogy of the imperial line = divine kingship universal**
Like the Kojiki, the Nihon Shoki constructs the imperial lineage as a direct descent from Amaterasu through Ninigi and Jimmu. Written in the prestige language of the Chinese imperial system — whose emperors were Sons of Heaven (*tianzi*) — the Nihon Shoki asserts Japan's claim to an equally legitimate divine-mandate system. This is the **divine kingship legitimation** structure (Egyptian pharaoh/Horus, Mesopotamian en/lugal/An, Chinese tianming, Hebrew anointed/mashiach) translated into Japanese institutional form with Chinese textual clothing.

**5. Izanagi/Izanami cosmogony = primordial-pair creation universal**
The Nihon Shoki's creation accounts — in their multiple variant versions — include the full Izanagi/Izanami narrative of creation-by-stirring, the descent to Yomi, and the birth of Amaterasu from the purification ritual. All the cross-tradition parallels documented in the Kojiki entry apply here too, with the addition that the Nihon Shoki's variant accounts make visible the *range of competing Japanese oral traditions* about these events — a reminder that every creation narrative is a selection from a wider field of competing stories.

## The Nihon Shoki and modernity

The Nihon Shoki's divine-lineage theology was weaponized in Japanese imperial nationalism (Meiji Constitution 1889: "The Empire of Japan shall be reigned over and governed by a line of Emperors unbroken for ages eternal"; State Shintō 1868–1945). The postwar Allied occupation required the removal of State Shintō and the emperor's renunciation of divinity (January 1, 1946). The Nihon Shoki thus has a more explicitly political modern reception history than almost any other ancient religious text — a 720 CE chronicle directly shaped a 20th-century fascist state. This makes it a crucial case study for the Atlas's investigation of how sacred texts become ideological weapons.
