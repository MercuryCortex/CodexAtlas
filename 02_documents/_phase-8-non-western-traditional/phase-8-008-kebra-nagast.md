# Kebra Nagast

## What it is
The Ethiopian national epic-cum-religious chronicle ("The Glory of the Kings"), 117 chapters in Ge'ez. The narrative core: (1) Solomon and the Queen of Sheba (here Makeda, Queen of Ethiopia) — she travels to Jerusalem, debates Solomon's wisdom, and conceives a son by him; (2) Menelik I, their son, returns as a young man to Jerusalem to know his father; (3) Menelik I and his retinue secretly remove the Ark of the Covenant from the First Temple and bring it to Aksum, where it has remained ever since — by Ethiopian tradition, in the Chapel of the Tablet at the Church of Our Lady Mary of Zion, guarded by a single monk-custodian. The Solomonic dynasty of Ethiopia (which traces its descent from Menelik I and held power 1270–1974) is theologically grounded in this narrative. Subsequent chapters identify Ethiopia as the *new* chosen people; the Jews, having let the Ark depart, lose their election to Ethiopia.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Composed in its surviving form ~1314–1322 under the early Solomonic dynasty (restored 1270 by Yekuno Amlak, who claimed Solomonic descent against the preceding Zagwe dynasty). The work legitimates the Solomonic restoration.
- **Religious milieu.** Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Christianity (christianized ~330s CE under Ezana; Miaphysite christology after 451 Chalcedon); the Kebra Nagast integrates the Ethiopian church with the Solomonic monarchy as a single theological-political project. The Old Testament Solomonic descent is theologically central in a way it is not in most Christian traditions.
- **Who was behind it.** Colophon names Nebura'ed Yeshaq (chief priest of Aksum) as the redactor of the present Ge'ez form, working from Arabic and Coptic sources. The Solomon-Sheba-Menelik core is much older and circulated in Near Eastern Christian and Jewish material centuries earlier (Targum Sheni; Quran Surah 27; Coptic legends).
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous in Ethiopian use. Brought to European awareness by Portuguese encounter with Ethiopia (Francisco Álvares, 1520s) and later by the British Magdala expedition (1868), which carried off manuscripts. Bezold's 1905 critical edition opened scholarly study.

## Themes and motifs
- [[messianic-genealogy]] — Davidic-Solomonic lineage transferred to Ethiopia; the Ethiopian emperor is Lion of Judah (Rev 5:5) and Davidic heir.
- [[ark-of-the-covenant]] — the Ark's relocation from Jerusalem to Aksum is the text's central transfer-of-sanctity claim.
- [[divine-kingship]] — Solomonic kingship is sacralized; the emperor's authority is theologically required.
- [[chosen-people]] — substitution theology: Ethiopia replaces Israel as the *new* chosen people (a move structurally parallel to but distinct from supersessionist Christian claims).

## Disputes
- **Date depth of narrative core.** Some scholars argue the Solomon-Sheba narrative reflects genuine pre-Christian Aksumite tradition; others argue it is entirely a medieval Christian elaboration of biblical and Arabic material onto Ethiopia. Most current scholarship (Munro-Hay 2006) sees a long compositional history with the narrative *core* circulating in the Ge'ez area centuries before the 1322 redaction.
- **Historical Ark.** The Ethiopian church's claim that the actual Ark of the Covenant resides in Aksum is religiously asserted but not archaeologically testable (the chapel is closed to all but the monk-custodian). The vast majority of secular scholars treat this as theological claim, not historical fact.
