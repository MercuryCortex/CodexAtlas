# Books of Chilam Balam

## What it is
A genre of post-conquest Yucatec Maya manuscript compilation — not a single book but a textual *tradition* preserved across multiple town-specific manuscripts named for their towns of origin (Chumayel, Tizimín, Maní, Kaua, Ixil, Tusik, Nah, Tekax). Each manuscript is heterogeneous, mixing: katun prophecies (each *k'atun* — 20-year cycle of ~7,200 days — had a prophetic burden associated with its name-day-sign), historical chronicles of Yucatec dynasties, ritual formulas, medical recipes, calendrical tables, post-conquest Christian-syncretic material, and the famous *libro de los enigmas* (catechism of riddles by which Maya nobility identified themselves to each other). The works are named for "Chilam Balam" — the Jaguar Priest (Balam Na), a famous late-pre-conquest prophet whose name became the genre's eponym, though no surviving book is "by" him.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Yucatán was conquered between 1527 and 1546; the encomienda system imposed; Diego de Landa's *auto-da-fé* of Maní (1562) destroyed 27 hieroglyphic codices and ~5,000 cult objects. The Chilam Balam tradition is what survived underground, transcribed into Latin script by Maya scribes literate from missionary schools.
- **Religious milieu.** Yucatec Maya religious tradition continued in modified form: pre-conquest deities (Itzamná, Kinich Ahau, Chaac, Ixchel) coexisted with Catholic figures; the *k'atun* prophetic cycle continued generating new content well into the colonial period; the books became increasingly hybrid (incorporating Christian apocalyptic material into Maya katun structures).
- **Who was behind it.** Anonymous Yucatec Maya scribes, members of *ah dzib* (writer) lineages associated with specific towns. Likely belonged to noble (*almehen*) families who retained Maya literacy across generations.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Crescencio Carrillo y Ancona collected manuscripts in the late 19th century; Daniel Brinton produced the first scholarly study (1882); the Chumayel was photographed by George Gordon (1910s) and acquired by the University of Pennsylvania Museum, later moved to Princeton. Other manuscripts remain in Mérida and private hands. Roys's 1933 translation is the foundational English edition.

## Themes and motifs
- [[prophetic-cycle]] — the katun-prophecy genre: each 20-year unit's name-day generates predictions; Yucatec prophecy is *not* eschatological in the Abrahamic sense but cyclical-political-meteorological.
- [[divinatory-calendar]] — Maya tzolk'in and haab calendars still operating in colonial Yucatec religion; the books include calendrical instruction.
- [[mesoamerican-cosmogony]] — fragmentary creation passages parallel to the Popol Vuh (highland Maya); preserve Yucatec variants.
- [[apocalyptic-tradition]] — colonial-era passages syncretize Maya katun-prophecy with Christian apocalyptic; Last Judgment merged with Maya cyclical destruction.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-8-001-popol-vuh]] — *parallel-motif* / *shared-milieu*: K'iche' highland-Maya post-conquest narrative compilation ↔ Yucatec lowland-Maya post-conquest compilation. Different regional traditions of the same broad Maya religious universe.
- → [[phase-8-003-dresden-codex-maya]] — *manuscript-transmission* (typological): the Dresden Codex represents the pre-conquest hieroglyphic form of much of what the Chilam Balam books preserve in colonial alphabetic form. Some Chilam Balam astronomical passages may be direct transcriptions from such codices.
- → [[phase-8-004-florentine-codex-sahagun]] — *parallel-motif*: parallel colonial-era preservation of pre-conquest religion; Sahagún's project was systematic European-led ethnography, the Chilam Balam books are Indigenous-led self-preservation.
- → Daniel apocalyptic (Phase 3) — *parallel-motif* (post-colonial reframing of prophecy as resistance literature). Yucatec katun-prophecy under Spanish rule is structurally analogous to Jewish apocalyptic under Seleucid rule. The comparison is typological.

## Disputes
- **Pre-Columbian content fraction.** What percentage of Chilam Balam material is genuinely pre-conquest vs. colonial composition is genre-by-genre contested. Calendrical and katun-prophecy material has strong claims to pre-conquest origin; explicit cosmogonic narratives are more debated.
- **Historical Chilam Balam.** A 15th-century Yucatec prophet named Chilam Balam is mentioned by Diego de Landa and is part of the books' framing, but no extant text can be securely attributed to him.
