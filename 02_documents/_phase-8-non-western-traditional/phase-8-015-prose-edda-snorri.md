# Prose Edda (Snorra Edda)

## What it is
A four-part medieval Icelandic mythological-poetic handbook composed by Snorri Sturluson (1179–1241), ~1220. The four parts: (1) *Prologue* — Snorri's euhemerizing-Christian framing of the Norse gods as ancient Trojan refugees deified by later generations (probably influenced by Geoffrey of Monmouth); (2) *Gylfaginning* ("The Beguiling of Gylfi") — the principal narrative section, structured as a dialogue between King Gylfi and three personae of Odin, in which the cosmogony, the pantheon, and Ragnarǫk are systematically retold; (3) *Skáldskaparmál* ("Poetry-language") — a treatise on skaldic *kennings* (compressed metaphorical phrases) with extensive mythological-narrative explanations of each kenning's mythic referent; (4) *Háttatal* ("List of meters") — a 102-stanza commentated poem in honor of King Hákon Hákonarson and Earl Skúli, demonstrating 100 different skaldic meters. The Prose Edda is the principal *systematized* presentation of Norse mythology; without Snorri, we would have the Eddic poems but no coherent narrative integrating them.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Iceland during the late Commonwealth period (1220s–1241), entering the Age of the Sturlungs (civil-war factionalism among Icelandic chieftain-families). Snorri himself was one of the most politically powerful Icelanders of his generation — twice *lögsögumaðr* (lawspeaker of the Alþingi), a major chieftain, and politically entangled with the Norwegian crown. He was assassinated 1241 on the orders of King Hákon IV via Gizurr Þorvaldsson.
- **Religious milieu.** Iceland had been Christian for 220 years by Snorri's time; Snorri was a baptized Christian writing about pagan ancestors' religion. His project is mythographic-poetic-aesthetic, *not* devotional. The Prologue's euhemerism (Norse gods are ancient deified humans) is the conventional medieval Christian frame for legitimately writing about pagan material; whether Snorri actually believed this or used it as a fig leaf is debated.
- **Who was behind it.** Snorri Sturluson, member of the Sturlung clan; nephew of the chieftain-poet Egill Skallagrímsson (the subject of his *Egils saga*). Snorri also wrote *Heimskringla* (the sagas of the Norwegian kings) and is likely the author of *Egils saga*; a foundational figure of medieval Icelandic prose.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Multiple medieval manuscripts (Codex Upsaliensis ~1300 is oldest). First printed edition: Resen, Copenhagen 1665. The Prose Edda was the principal channel through which post-medieval Europe learned Norse mythology; Wagner, Tolkien, and modern Heathenry read Snorri before they read the Poetic Edda.

## Themes and motifs
- [[norse-cosmogony]] — Snorri's *Gylfaginning* is the principal narrative presentation of cosmogony from Ginnungagap → Ymir → cosmos.
- [[ragnarok-apocalyptic]] — Snorri systematizes the Ragnarǫk narrative; pulls together passages from *Vǫluspá*, *Vafþrúðnismál*, *Grímnismál* into a coherent eschatological chronicle.
- [[poetic-craft]] — *Skáldskaparmál* and *Háttatal* are the principal medieval source on skaldic poetics; preserves hundreds of kennings.
- [[mythological-systematization]] — Snorri's distinctive contribution: turning a poetic tradition's assumed mythology into an explicit narrative system. He is the first systematic mythographer of Norse religion.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-8-014-poetic-edda]] — *commentary-on*: Snorri quotes the Eddic poems extensively, especially *Vǫluspá*, *Vafþrúðnismál*, *Grímnismál*, *Hávamál*. He preserves passages of otherwise-lost Eddic poetry (parts of *Heimdalargaldr*, etc.).
- → Geoffrey of Monmouth's *Historia regum Britanniae* — *parallel-motif*: euhemerizing-mythographic framing of pre-Christian material into medieval Christian narrative. Snorri likely knew Geoffrey's pattern.
- → Wagner's *Ring of the Nibelung* — *manuscript-transmission* (long-range): Wagner's principal Norse-mythological sources were Snorri (via 19th-c. translations) and the *Nibelungenlied*. The standard popular image of the Norse gods descends through Snorri to Wagner to modern reception.
- → Tolkien — *manuscript-transmission* (long-range): Tolkien's mythopoetic project (*Silmarillion*, *Lord of the Rings*) draws on Snorri (Gandalf's name from *Vǫluspá*'s dwarf-list quoted by Snorri; Middle-earth from Norse *Miðgarðr*).

## Disputes
- **Snorri's euhemerism — sincere or pragmatic.** Some scholars (Faulkes leaning) read the Prologue's "the gods were ancient men" as a clerical fig leaf permitting the otherwise-impermissible recording of paganism. Others (Wanner 2008) read Snorri as genuinely committed to Christian-mythographic syncretism.
- **Reliability for reconstructing pre-Christian Norse religion.** The Prose Edda is *the* systematic source but is two centuries removed from active pagan practice and shaped by Christian-medieval categories. Mainstream method: cross-reference with the Eddic poems and skaldic poetry; where Snorri agrees with independent pre-Christian-attested sources, trust him; where he stands alone, treat with care.
- **Authorship of the Prologue.** Some scholars argue the Prologue is a later interpolation, not Snorri's; Faulkes defends Snorrian authorship; the question remains open.
