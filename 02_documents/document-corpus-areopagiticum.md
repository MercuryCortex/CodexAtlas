# The Corpus Areopagiticum (Pseudo-Dionysius)

## What it is
A set of four treatises and ten letters — the Divine Names, the Mystical Theology, the Celestial Hierarchy, the Ecclesiastical Hierarchy — written around 500 CE by an anonymous Christian author masquerading as "Dionysius the Areopagite," the Athenian whom Paul converted (Acts 17:34). Beneath the apostolic disguise is a thoroughgoing Christianization of late Neoplatonism, above all of Proclus: God is beyond being and beyond all names, approached only by stripping away every concept (the apophatic "way of negation") until the soul enters the "divine darkness"; and reality descends through ranked hierarchies — nine angelic orders in three triads, mirrored by the church's own orders.

## Primary text

### The Mystical Theology
*Trans. Luibheid (1987)*
> The cause of all is... beyond assertion and denial. We make assertions and denials of what is next to it, but never of it, for it is... beyond every limitation; it is also beyond every denial.

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** Christian theology absorbing the last great pagan philosophy, under cover of apostolic authorship.
- **The mask.** The pseudonym gave Neoplatonic mysticism quasi-apostolic authority — and was believed until 1895.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Translated into Latin by Eriugena (9th c.), it became foundational for Western mysticism.

## Themes and motifs
- Apophatic theology — God known only by unknowing, beyond all names.
- The celestial hierarchy — nine angelic orders; reality as graded descent and return.
- Mystical ascent — into the "divine darkness" above intellect.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **The most successful forgery in Christian history — and the pipe that carried Neoplatonism into the church.** By writing as Paul's convert, Pseudo-Dionysius gave the metaphysics of the pagan Proclus apostolic authority, and for a thousand years the church received Neoplatonic apophatic mysticism and the angelic hierarchies as if from the apostolic age. Thomas Aquinas cites "Dionysius" some 1,700 times; the entire Western mystical tradition — Eckhart, the *Cloud of Unknowing*, John of the Cross — flows from him, as does the standard Christian doctrine of the nine orders of angels. When Koch and Stiglmayr proved in 1895 that the corpus quietly paraphrases Proclus (who died in 485), they revealed that one of Christianity's deepest theological currents had been, all along, baptized Neoplatonism (Rorem 1993; Louth 1989; Luibheid 1987).
