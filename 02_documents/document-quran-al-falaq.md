# Sūrat al-Falaq (Quran 113 — Daybreak)

## What it is
The first of the two "refuge-seeking" suras (al-muʿawwidhatān), recited together with al-Nās for protection. In five short verses the worshipper takes shelter in "the Lord of daybreak" — the one who splits the dawn out of the dark — from four named evils: the evil of all He created, the evil of darkness when it gathers, the evil of "the women who blow on knots" (sorcery), and the evil of the envier when he envies. The sura is the Qur'an's classic apotropaic prayer, recited at night, in illness, and as ruqya (protective recitation).

## Primary text

### Quran 113:1–5
*Trans. Abdel Haleem (2004)*
> Say, "I seek refuge with the Lord of daybreak against the harm of what He has created, the harm of the night when darkness gathers, the harm of those who blow on knots, the harm of the envier when he envies."

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** The sura names the everyday fears of late-antique Arabia — sorcery, the dark, the evil eye — and binds them all to one protecting Lord.
- **Tradition.** Hadith reports the Prophet reciting both muʿawwidhatān nightly, breathing into his cupped hands and wiping over himself.
- **Discovery / transmission.** "Those who blow on knots" reflects a known form of knot-magic; the sura redirects the remedy from counter-magic to God alone.

## Themes and motifs
- Refuge in the Lord of dawn — light cleaving darkness as the image of rescue.
- Named evils — darkness, sorcery, envy made explicit.
- Protection without counter-magic — the cure is taking shelter in God.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **The prayer against the dark, the witch, and the evil eye.** al-Falaq belongs to one of the oldest genres of religious speech: the apotropaic prayer that names the night's dangers and seeks shelter from them. Its closest biblical cousin is Psalm 91 — "you will not fear the terror of the night… the pestilence that stalks in darkness," sheltered beneath the divine wings — a text used as a protective prayer in Judaism (the *shir shel pegaʿim*, "song against afflictions," recited at bedtime and at funerals) much as the muʿawwidhatān are in Islam. Both belong to a Near Eastern world thick with apotropaic incantation, from Babylonian *šurpu* tablets to the Aramaic incantation bowls buried under thresholds, all warding off the evil eye, the demon, and the curse. The Qur'an's decisive move is theological compression: the four feared evils — creation's harm, the gathering dark, the knot-blowing sorceress, the envier — are all referred to *the Lord of daybreak* and to Him alone, converting a landscape of counter-magic into a single act of refuge in God (Reynolds 2010 pp.301–310; McAuliffe 2006 pp.181–195; Nasr 2015 pp.1581–1585).
