# Deuteronomy

**Deuteronomy** (Hebrew *Dəḇārīm*, "Words"; Greek *Deuteronomion*, "second law") is the fifth and concluding book of the **Torah**, framed as **Moses' farewell addresses** on the plains of Moab before Israel enters the land. Distinct in voice and theology from the preceding books, it restates the law in hortatory form, demands the **centralization of worship** at "the place YHWH will choose," proclaims the **Shema** ("Hear, O Israel: YHWH our God, YHWH is one," 6:4) as the heart of covenant loyalty, and closes with the blessings and curses of the covenant, the Song and Blessing of Moses, and his death.

## Composition

Deuteronomy is the work of the **Deuteronomic (D)** school — the **fourth source** of the Documentary Hypothesis — and a core of it is widely identified with the **"book of the law" discovered in the Temple during Josiah's reform** (621 BCE, 2 Kings 22), whose program of cult-centralization and purification it supplies. It became the theological lens for the entire **[[phase-2-019-deuteronomistic-history|Deuteronomistic History]]** (Joshua through Kings), which judges every king by Deuteronomy's standard.

## The cross-tradition wire

Deuteronomy's covenant form is now recognized as modelled on the **Neo-Assyrian suzerain–vassal treaty** — above all the **Succession Treaty (Vassal Treaties) of Esarhaddon** (672 BCE). The parallels are detailed and structural: the exclusive **loyalty oath** to one lord (transferred from the Assyrian king to YHWH), the stipulations, and — most strikingly — the **curses of Deuteronomy 28**, which follow the *same sequence* as the Esarhaddon treaty curses. Israel's covenant theology here **appropriates the language of imperial domination and turns it toward God**, recasting subjects of Assyria as the covenant people of YHWH alone.

## Reach forward

Deuteronomy is the most influential book of the Torah for later religion: the **Shema** became Judaism's central confession; it is the Torah book most quoted by Jesus (the great commandment, the wilderness temptations); and its covenant-and-constitution logic runs deep in Western political theology.
