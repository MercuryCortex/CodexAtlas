# The Descent of Ishtar to the Underworld

## What it is
A compact Akkadian myth, the shorter sister of the great Sumerian "Inanna's Descent." The goddess Ishtar descends to the realm of her sister Ereshkigal, passing through seven gates and surrendering at each a garment or ornament until she stands naked and powerless; Ereshkigal has her killed. With Ishtar dead below, all sexual fertility ceases on earth, forcing the gods to contrive her release — at the price of a substitute, her consort the shepherd-god Tammuz (Dumuzi), who must take her place for half the year.

## Primary text

### The seventh gate
*Trans. Foster (2005)*
> At the seventh gate he let her enter, stripping from her the loincloth round her body. "Why, gatekeeper, did you strip the loincloth round my body?" "Enter, my lady: such are the rites of the Mistress of the Netherworld."

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** The Tammuz cult and its seasonal lament; the myth underwrites a fertility-and-death liturgy.
- **Sources.** An Akkadian abridgement of the Sumerian Inanna's Descent, with a sharper focus on the fertility consequence.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Cuneiform tablets, including Ashurbanipal's library.

## Themes and motifs
- [[underworld-descent]] — the seven-gate journey, stripping, death, and return.
- Dying-and-rising — Tammuz's seasonal death and the world's barrenness.
- Substitution — a life given to ransom a life from death.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **The seven-gate descent and the dying-rising god.** Ishtar's stripping at seven gates, her death, and her ransomed return is the archetypal underworld-descent — paralleling her Sumerian self (Inanna), Greek Persephone's seasonal half-year below, and read by some into the harrowing-of-hell. Its consort Tammuz, the dying shepherd whose death barrens the land and whose mourning was a fixed festival, is the type-case of J.G. Frazer's "dying-and-rising god" — and the Hebrew Bible itself records the cult's penetration of Jerusalem (Ezek 8:14, "women weeping for Tammuz"). The myth is a headwater of the death-and-renewal pattern that runs through the ancient Near East into later resurrection symbolism (Foster 2005 pp.498–505; Lapinkivi 2010).
