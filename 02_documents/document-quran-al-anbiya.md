# Sūrat al-Anbiyāʾ (Quran 21 — The Prophets)

## What it is
A Meccan sura that parades the prophets one after another — Abraham smashing his people's idols and surviving the fire, Moses and Aaron, Lot, Noah, David and Solomon, Job (Ayyūb) in his patient affliction, Jonah (Dhū al-Nūn) in the belly of the fish, Zechariah and John, and Mary and her son — to argue that they all preached one message: there is no god but God. It is the Quran's clearest statement that the biblical prophets are Islam's own.

## Primary text

### Quran 21:25, 87
*Trans. Abdel Haleem (2004)*
> We never sent any messenger before you without revealing to him: "There is no god but Me, so serve Me." (21:25)
> And remember the man with the whale [Jonah], when he went off angrily, thinking We could not restrict him, but then he cried out in the deep darkness, "There is no god but You, glory be to You, I was wrong." (21:87)

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** Meccan Islam claiming the entire prophetic heritage as a single line of preachers of tawḥīd.
- **Sources.** Draws on the full biblical roster plus aggadic legend (Abraham and the idols).
- **Discovery / transmission.** A foundational text for Islamic prophetology.

## Themes and motifs
- One prophetic message — every messenger preached the same monotheism (21:25).
- Abraham the idol-breaker — the prototype ḥanīf who shatters his people's gods.
- Patient endurance — Job and Jonah delivered after affliction.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **Islam claims the whole Bible's prophets as its own.** Al-Anbiyāʾ is the Quran's manifesto of prophetic continuity: Abraham, Moses, Job, Jonah, David, Solomon, Zechariah, John, Mary, and Jesus are not figures of a rival scripture but earlier links in the single chain that Muhammad completes — each preaching the identical tawḥīd (21:25). This is the structural claim that lets Islam regard itself not as a new religion but as the restoration of the original Abrahamic monotheism, and it is why the Quran can retell biblical narrative as authoritative while subordinating the Bible's own versions as corrupted. The sura also preserves the extra-biblical Abraham-and-the-idols legend (21:51–70), shared with Jewish midrash (Genesis Rabbah), showing the common late-antique story-pool the three traditions drew from (Reynolds 2010 pp.87–95; Nasr 2015 pp.811–828).
