# Gospel of John

The **Gospel of John** — the Fourth Gospel — is the most theologically ambitious book of the New Testament and the single greatest cross-tradition hub in early Christian literature. Distinct in style and content from the Synoptics (**[[phase-3-016-gospel-of-mark|Mark]]**, Matthew, **[[phase-3-018-luke-acts|Luke]]**), it presents a Jesus who speaks in long meditative discourses, performs seven "signs," and utters the great "I am" sayings. It opens with the **Prologue** (1:1–18) — "**In the beginning was the Word (Logos), and the Word was with God, and the Word was God**" — the most influential single passage in the history of Christology.

## Composition

John is the product of a distinct **Johannine community or school**, composed and redacted in stages around 90–110 CE. Its dualistic vocabulary of **light and darkness, truth and falsehood, above and below** closely resembles that of the **[[phase-3-011-dead-sea-scrolls|Dead Sea Scrolls]]**, anchoring it in Second Temple Jewish sectarian thought even as its philosophical framing reaches toward the Greek world.

## The cross-tradition wire (the MASSIVE-WINS)

The Prologue's **Logos** is the great synthesis: it fuses the **Jewish Wisdom tradition** — the pre-existent **Sophia** who was with God at creation (Proverbs 8; Sirach 24; the **[[phase-3-012-wisdom-of-solomon|Wisdom of Solomon]]**) — with the cosmic **Logos** of **[[phase-3-013-philo-of-alexandria|Philo of Alexandria]]**, Stoicism, and Middle Platonism, and deliberately rewrites the opening of **[[document-genesis|Genesis]]** ("In the beginning"). The result is a bridge spanning Israelite scripture, Hellenistic-Jewish philosophy, and Greek metaphysics — which is precisely why the Gospel was so prized by **Christian Gnostics** (the Valentinians wrote the first commentary on it) and became the charter text of orthodox **Trinitarian Christology** at Nicaea and Chalcedon. Its "Word" Christology even reverberates in the Qurʾānic title of ʿĪsā as a **"Word (kalima) from God."**

## Reach forward

No text did more to make Jesus the divine, pre-existent Word; John is the fountainhead of conciliar Christology, of Christian mysticism, and of two millennia of debate over the relation of the human and the divine.
