# Sūrat al-Naṣr (Quran 110 — Divine Support)

## What it is
A three-verse sura, by long tradition among the very last revealed — some hold it the final complete sura. It announces the moment the long struggle is won: God's help and the victory (fatḥ) have come, and people are entering the faith "in crowds." But its command is not to celebrate so much as to *glorify and ask forgiveness* — and the earliest community read in that turn a coded farewell. ʿUmar and Ibn ʿAbbās are reported to have understood it as the sign that the Prophet's work was complete and his death near: triumph crowned not with conquest but with praise and repentance.

## Primary text

### Quran 110:1–3
*Trans. Abdel Haleem (2004)*
> When God's help comes and He opens up your way, when you see people embracing God's faith in crowds, celebrate the praise of your Lord and ask His forgiveness: He is always ready to accept repentance.

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** Late Medinan, c. 631–632; the conquest of Mecca has turned the tide and Arabia is entering Islam.
- **Tradition.** Read as the announcement of the Prophet's imminent death — the task done, the call to take leave.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Its placement near the end of the revelation gives the codex a tone of completion and humility.

## Themes and motifs
- Victory as gift — the triumph belongs to God, not the victor.
- Triumph answered by repentance — praise and seeking forgiveness at the summit.
- The farewell — accomplishment as the sign to depart.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **The founder's farewell — triumph and the turn toward death.** al-Naṣr reads the way the great founder-narratives of scripture end: at the very moment the mission succeeds, the leader is called to take his leave. Moses ascends Nebo to see the land he has led Israel toward and dies there without entering it, his work finished (Deuteronomy 34:1–8). Jesus' "it is finished" (John 19:30) makes accomplishment and death a single moment. The Buddha attains *parinirvāṇa* once the Dharma is established. Sūrat al-Naṣr belongs to this family — but with a distinctive Quranic inflection: the response demanded at the height of victory is *istighfār*, the asking of forgiveness, and *tasbīḥ*, praise. The conqueror, at the instant of total success, is told to humble himself and repent — and the community heard in that command the announcement of the Prophet's own death, so that the revelation closes not on the spoils of victory but on a man turning, accomplished, back toward his Lord (Lings 1983 pp.341–345; McAuliffe 2006 pp.181–195; Nasr 2015 pp.1573–1576).
