# Book of Revelation (Apocalypse of John)

## What it is
The only full apocalypse in the New Testament canon, and its final book. Cast as a circular letter to seven churches of Roman Asia, it relays a chain of visions seen by "John" on Patmos: the exalted "one like a son of man" among the lampstands; the throne-room and the slain-yet-standing Lamb; the seven seals, trumpets, and bowls of judgment; the woman, the dragon, and the two beasts; the fall of "Babylon the great"; the thousand-year reign; the last judgment; and the descent of the New Jerusalem, where "the sea was no more." Its imagery is almost entirely woven from the Hebrew prophets — chiefly Daniel and Ezekiel — recombined into a new key. Its Greek is the roughest in the NT, full of deliberate Semitisms, the work of a Hebrew-thinking seer.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** The Roman province of Asia under the Flavian emperors, where the imperial cult was politically and economically pervasive. "Babylon" — the city "drunk with the blood of the saints," seated on seven hills (17:9) — is Rome; the beast whose number is 666 encodes (in Hebrew gematria) *Nerōn Kaisar*. Whether the pressure was a formal Domitianic persecution or a subtler economic-social coercion to participate in emperor-worship is debated; Revelation reads it as a cosmic war.
- **Religious milieu.** Jewish-Christian apocalypticism in the lineage of Daniel and 1 Enoch, in conflict both with Rome and with rival Christian teachers (the "Nicolaitans," "Jezebel," the "synagogue of Satan").
- **Who was behind it.** A prophet named John, leader of a circle of Asian Christian prophets — not, on the critical view, the apostle.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous Greek transmission, but with the slowest and most contested route into the canon of any NT book, especially in the East.

## Themes and motifs
- [[apocalyptic-revelation]] — the genre at its NT apex: heavenly vision, angelic interpreter, cosmic dualism, imminent end, symbolic numerology.
- [[chaoskampf]] — Revelation 12–13: the great red dragon and the beast rising "out of the sea" are the chaos-monster of the ancient Near Eastern combat myth; God's promise that in the new creation "the sea was no more" (21:1) is the final defeat of chaos.
- [[son-of-man]] — Rev 1:13 and 14:14: "one like a son of man," drawn straight from Daniel 7, now identified with the risen Christ.
- [[four-kingdoms-schema]] — the beast with the features of all four of Daniel's beasts (13:2) telescopes Daniel's succession of empires into Rome.
- [[messianism]] — the Lamb who was slain yet conquers, the Lion of Judah, the rider called Faithful and True; a paradoxical militant-yet-sacrificial messiah.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **Revelation is the New Testament terminus of the 2,000-year chaos-combat myth.** The dragon of Revelation 12 and the beast that rises "out of the sea" (13:1) are the latest avatars of the chaos-monster slain at creation across the ancient Near East: Tiamat split by Marduk in the *Enuma Elish*; Yam and the seven-headed Litan (Lotan) defeated by Baʿal in the Ugaritic cycle; the [[leviathan]] / Rahab whom YHWH crushes in Psalm 74, Isaiah 27:1, and Job 26. Revelation takes the myth to its only possible end: not merely a defeated sea-monster but the abolition of the sea itself — "and the sea was no more" (21:1) — chaos permanently undone in the new creation. Adela Yarbro Collins's *The Combat Myth in the Book of Revelation* is the classic demonstration that John consciously deploys this inherited pattern (Collins 1976; Aune 1998, WBC 52B pp.667–674).
- **666 and Nero-redivivus.** The number of the beast (13:18) is the clearest surviving instance of Hebrew gematria embedded in a Greek Christian text: *nrwn qsr* (Nero Caesar) sums to 666 — and the variant reading 616 corresponds to the Latin spelling *Nero*, a textual fingerprint confirming the solution. The terror of the dead-and-returning Nero became the prototype of the Antichrist (Bauckham 1993, *Climax of Prophecy* pp.384–407; Koester 2014, AYB 38A pp.596–599).
- **The millennium.** The thousand-year reign (Rev 20) injects a temporally-bounded messianic interregnum into Christian eschatology — structurally akin to the bounded world-ages and final renovation (*frašōkereti*) of Zoroastrian cosmology, the most-discussed candidate for cross-cultural influence on apocalyptic periodization (Koester 2014 pp.760–774).
