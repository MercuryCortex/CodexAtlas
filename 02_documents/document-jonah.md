# Book of Jonah

## What it is
The odd book out among the Twelve: not a collection of oracles but a tightly-crafted didactic short story, gently satirizing the reluctant prophet who flees God's call, is swallowed by a great fish, is vomited onto dry land, grudgingly preaches to Nineveh, and then sulks when the hated city repents and is spared. Its single prophetic "oracle" is five Hebrew words long. Its real subject is the scandalous breadth of divine mercy.

## Primary text

### Jonah 4:10–11
*Trans. NRSV*
> Then the LORD said... "And should I not be concerned about Nineveh, that great city, in which there are more than a hundred and twenty thousand persons who do not know their right hand from their left, and also many animals?"

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Set against Nineveh, capital of the Assyrian empire that annihilated the northern kingdom in -722 — making the city's repentance and rescue deliberately provocative.
- **Religious milieu.** A post-exilic counter-voice to the exclusivist nationalism of the Ezra–Nehemiah reforms: YHWH cares even for Israel's genocidal enemies and their cattle.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous canonical transmission within the Twelve.

## Themes and motifs
- Divine mercy and universalism — God's compassion overflows ethnic and national boundaries.
- The reluctant prophet — Jonah as the anti-hero who resents the success of his own message.
- Resurrection sign — three days and nights in the belly of the fish, later read typologically.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[assyrian-empire]] — *shared-milieu*: Nineveh, the empire's capital, is the story's setting and the object of YHWH's mercy.
- → [[phase-2-010-hebrew-bible-early-prophets]] — *shared-milieu*: the grouping node for the Twelve's older layer; retained as a lens.
- → [[phase-3-017-gospel-of-matthew]] — *direct-quote*: Jesus's "sign of Jonah" (Matt 12:39–41) makes Jonah's three days in the fish a type of his own burial and resurrection.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **The "sign of Jonah" is Jesus's own chosen emblem of the resurrection.** When asked for a sign, Jesus answers that none will be given "except the sign of the prophet Jonah. For just as Jonah was three days and three nights in the belly of the sea monster, so for three days and three nights the Son of Man will be in the heart of the earth" (Matt 12:39–40). Jonah-and-the-fish became, in turn, the single most common resurrection image in early Christian catacomb art — a post-exilic satire about mercy to enemies repurposed as the visual shorthand for rising from the dead (Sasson 1990, AYB 24B pp.327–356).
