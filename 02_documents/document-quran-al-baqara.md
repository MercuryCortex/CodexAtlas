# Sūrat al-Baqara (Quran 2 — The Cow)

## What it is
The longest sura of the Quran (286 verses) and the first major revelation of the Medinan period, when Muhammad's movement became a governing community. Named for the cow Moses commands the Israelites to sacrifice (2:67–73), it ranges across creation and the fall of Adam, the covenant with Israel, the foundation of the Kaʿba by Abraham, the change of the prayer-direction to Mecca, and a great body of law — fasting, pilgrimage, marriage, and the prohibition of usury. It contains the Quran's single most-revered verse, the Throne Verse.

## Primary text

### Quran 2:255 — Āyat al-Kursī (the Throne Verse)
*Trans. Abdel Haleem (2004)*
> God: there is no god but Him, the Ever Living, the Ever Watchful. Neither slumber nor sleep overtakes Him. All that is in the heavens and in the earth belongs to Him... His throne extends over the heavens and the earth; it does not weary Him to preserve them both. He is the Most High, the Tremendous.

## Context
- **Political / religious milieu.** Medina, where Islam became a polity; the sura legislates for the new community and engages the city's Jewish tribes.
- **Theology.** The Throne Verse crystallizes Islamic monotheism — God's unity, life, omniscience, and sustaining sovereignty.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Foundational and universally transmitted.

## Themes and motifs
- Divine unity and sovereignty — the Throne Verse (2:255).
- Adam and the fall — the angels' prostration and Iblīs's refusal (2:30–39), a key cross-scriptural retelling.
- [[messianism]] — the prophetic lineage from Adam and Abraham toward Muhammad; 2:62's promise to "those who believe" across communities.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **The qibla and Adam: al-Baqara rewrites Genesis and reorients prayer.** Two of the sura's moves are pivotal cross-tradition events: (1) the prostration-of-the-angels-before-Adam and Iblīs's refusal (2:30–39) reworks a tradition also found in the Latin *Life of Adam and Eve*, making the devil's fall a refusal to honor humanity; and (2) the change of the qibla from Jerusalem to the Kaʿba (2:142–150) ritually severs the new community from the older Abrahamic centers while claiming Abraham himself (who, with Ishmael, "raised the foundations of the House," 2:127) as its founder — a deliberate reclamation of the shared patriarch (Reynolds 2010 pp.39–54; Nasr 2015 pp.108–115).
