# Epistle of Jude

## What it is
A single-chapter firestorm of a letter, warning a community against "ungodly" intruders who pervert grace into license, and marshalling a dense chain of judgment-examples to assure their doom. Brief and ferocious, it is most famous for what it quotes: Jude cites the non-canonical 1 Enoch as genuine prophecy and alludes to the Assumption of Moses, making it a key witness to which "scriptures" some early Christians actually used.

## Primary text

### Jude 14–15
*Trans. NRSV*
> It was also about these that Enoch, in the seventh generation from Adam, prophesied, saying, "See, the Lord is coming with ten thousands of his holy ones, to execute judgment on all..."

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** A community invaded by antinomian teachers; Jude answers with apocalyptic threat.
- **Authorship.** Ascribed to Jude the brother of James; debated.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Among the antilegomena, doubted in antiquity largely *because* of its Enoch quotation.

## Themes and motifs
- [[apocalyptic-revelation]] — the Lord coming with myriads of holy ones for judgment (the Enoch citation).
- Judgment typology — the wilderness generation, the fallen angels/Watchers, Sodom, Cain, Balaam, Korah.
- Contending for the faith — "once for all delivered to the saints" (v. 3).

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[document-2-peter]] — *redaction-of*: 2 Peter ch. 2 extensively reworks Jude, the clearest literary dependence among the Catholic Epistles.
- → [[phase-3-004-1-enoch]] — *direct-quote*: Jude 14–15 quotes 1 Enoch 1:9 as authoritative prophecy.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **A New Testament book quotes a non-canonical apocalypse as scripture.** Jude 14–15 cites 1 Enoch 1:9 by name as the prophecy of "Enoch, the seventh from Adam," and Jude 9 draws on the Assumption of Moses — two works that did not enter the Jewish or most Christian canons. Jude thus preserves hard evidence that the boundaries of "scripture" were still open in the first century, and that the Enochic literature was authoritative for some early Christians. The discomfort this caused delayed Jude's own canonization and, ironically, contributed to 1 Enoch's marginalization everywhere except the Ethiopian church, which kept both (Bauckham 1983, WBC 50 pp.94–96).
