# Epistle to the Hebrews

## What it is
A sustained, rhetorically masterful sermon — its author calls it a "word of exhortation" — arguing the superiority and finality of Christ over every institution of the old covenant: superior to the angels, to Moses, to the Levitical priesthood, to the Temple sacrifices. Its governing image is Christ as the eternal **high priest "according to the order of Melchizedek,"** who has entered the true, heavenly sanctuary and offered himself once for all. Around this runs a pastoral warning to a community in danger of drifting back, capped by the great roll-call of faith (ch. 11) and the "cloud of witnesses." Anonymous, of unknown destination, in the finest Greek of the New Testament, Hebrews is at once the canon's most enigmatic book and one of its most theologically ambitious.

## Primary text — the Melchizedek priesthood

### Hebrews 7:1–3
*Trans. NRSV*
> This "King Melchizedek of Salem, priest of the Most High God, met Abraham as he was returning from defeating the kings and blessed him"; and to him Abraham apportioned "one-tenth of everything." His name, in the first place, means "king of righteousness"; next he is also king of Salem, that is, "king of peace." Without father, without mother, without genealogy, having neither beginning of days nor end of life, but resembling the Son of God, he remains a priest forever.

*Reading Genesis 14 through Psalm 110:4, Hebrews builds an entire Christology on Melchizedek's lack of recorded genealogy, birth, or death: a priesthood not inherited and not ended — therefore "forever" — and so a fitting type of the risen Christ's unending heavenly priesthood, superior to the mortal, inherited Levitical line.*

## Context
- **Political / religious milieu.** A Greek-speaking Jewish-Christian community, somewhere in the Diaspora (Rome is the leading guess), under social pressure and tempted to fall away. The Temple cult is the foil throughout — whether still standing or freshly destroyed (70 CE) is the crux of the dating debate.
- **Who was behind it.** An unknown, highly educated author trained in Greek rhetoric and Alexandrian-style allegorical exegesis — clearly not Paul, despite the manuscript tradition that filed Hebrews among the Pauline letters.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Transmitted within the Pauline corpus (𝔓⁴⁶ places it after Romans) but admitted to the Western canon only once authorship anxieties eased in the 4th century.

## Themes and motifs
- The Melchizedek high-priesthood — the structural spine: an eternal, royal, non-Levitical priesthood (chs. 5, 7).
- [[messianism]] — Christ as the royal-priestly Messiah of Psalm 110 (king at God's right hand *and* priest forever).
- [[divine-kingship]] — the Son enthroned above the angels (ch. 1, weaving Pss 2, 45, 110).
- Better covenant / supersession — the "new covenant" of Jeremiah 31 (quoted in full at Heb 8:8–12) renders the first "obsolete"; the heavenly sanctuary makes the earthly a mere "shadow."
- Faith as endurance — ch. 11's litany from Abel to the prophets, defining faith as steadfast trust in the unseen promise.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[document-genesis]] — *commentary-on*: Hebrews 7 is an extended midrash on Genesis 14:18–20 (Melchizedek and Abram).
- → [[phase-3-011-dead-sea-scrolls]] — *parallel-motif*: 11QMelchizedek (11Q13) independently makes Melchizedek a heavenly, eschatological redeemer-judge who proclaims atonement — proving the exalted-Melchizedek tradition was alive in Second Temple Judaism, not a Christian invention.
- → [[document-leviticus]] — *polemic-against* / *commentary-on*: the Levitical sacrificial system is the very thing Hebrews argues Christ's heavenly self-offering renders obsolete.
- → [[document-romans]] — *shared-milieu*: transmitted alongside Romans in the Pauline corpus, though distinct in authorship and idiom.
- → [[phase-3-006-septuagint]] — *manuscript-transmission*: Hebrews' Old Testament is the Greek Bible; several of its arguments (e.g. Heb 10:5 quoting Ps 40 "a body you have prepared for me") work only on the LXX wording.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **The Melchizedek spine: Genesis 14 → Psalm 110 → 11QMelchizedek → Hebrews 7.** A mysterious priest-king of Salem who blesses Abram and vanishes from Genesis 14 in three verses becomes, via the royal oracle of Psalm 110:4, a model of eternal priesthood — and is independently elevated at Qumran (11Q13) into a heavenly redeemer who executes the eschatological jubilee and atonement. Hebrews fuses these strands into the claim that Christ is an eternal high priest of Melchizedek's non-Levitical order. This is one of the cleanest demonstrable cases of a shared Second Temple exegetical tradition surfacing in both a sectarian Jewish text and the New Testament from a common scriptural seed (Kobelski 1981; Attridge 1989, Hermeneia pp.187–195; Koester 2001, AYB 36 pp.338–349). It also reaches back to the Canaanite "God Most High" (El Elyon) whom Melchizedek serves in Genesis 14 — a thread into the headwater question of Israelite religion's Canaanite substrate.
- **The longest New Testament use of Jeremiah.** Hebrews 8:8–12 quotes Jeremiah 31:31–34 in full to argue the first covenant is "obsolete and growing old" — the hinge on which the entire Christian category of a "New Testament" turns (Attridge 1989 pp.226–229).
