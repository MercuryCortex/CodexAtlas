# Sūrat Yā Sīn (Quran 36)

## What it is
A central Meccan sura, called in a much-cited hadith "the heart of the Qur'an" (qalb al-Qurʾān), and recited across the Muslim world at the bedside of the dying and over the dead. It opens with the disconnected letters Yā-Sīn, affirms Muhammad's prophethood, and tells the parable of a town that rejected its messengers until "a man came running from the far side of the city" to believe and be martyred. Its great theme is **resurrection**: God revives the dead earth with rain, splits the seed, drives the sun and moon on their reckoned courses — and so will raise the dead. It ends by answering the scoffer who holds up a crumbling bone and asks who could ever give it life.

## Primary text

### Quran 36:78–79
*Trans. Abdel Haleem (2004)*
> Producing arguments against Us, forgetting his own creation. He says, "Who can give life to bones after they have decayed?" Say, "He who created them in the first place will give them life again: He has full knowledge of every act of creation."

### Quran 36:82
*Trans. Abdel Haleem (2004)*
> When He wills something to be, His way is to say, "Be" — and it is.

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** Meccan; the Meccans denied the resurrection above all, and Yā Sīn is the Qur'an's sustained answer.
- **Tradition.** Its recitation for the dying ("read Yā Sīn over your dying") makes it the most pastoral of suras.
- **Discovery / transmission.** The reviving of dead land after rain is its master-image for the raising of the dead.

## Themes and motifs
- The heart of the Qur'an — its concentrated statement of faith and resurrection.
- The town and its messengers — rejection, and the lone believer who runs to faith.
- "Be, and it is" — creation and resurrection as a single divine word.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **"Who will give life to these bones?" — resurrection across the scriptures.** The denier of Yā Sīn holds up a rotted bone, crumbles it, and asks how anyone could revive it (36:78); the sura's answer is that the One who made it from nothing can remake it, and the proof offered is the annual miracle of dead earth greened by rain. This is the same scene, and nearly the same words, as Ezekiel's vision in the valley of dry bones, where God asks "Son of man, can these bones live?" and the scattered skeletons are reassembled with sinew, flesh, and breath into a living army (Ezekiel 37:1–14) — the foundational image of bodily resurrection in the Hebrew prophets, taken up by Daniel 12:2, by Pharisaic and rabbinic Judaism, and by the Christian creed of "the resurrection of the body." The Qur'an inherits that conviction whole and intensifies its pastoral force: where Ezekiel's bones prophesy the *national* restoration of Israel, Yā Sīn turns the image toward *every individual's* rising, and the community then reads the sura over its own dying — so the bone that the scoffer crumbled becomes the chapter whispered at the deathbed (Reynolds 2010 pp.171–180; McAuliffe 2006 pp.136–149; Nasr 2015 pp.1069–1085).
