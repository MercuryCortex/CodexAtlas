# Enki and Ninhursag (The Dilmun Myth)

## What it is
A Sumerian poem set in Dilmun (Bahrain), imagined as a pure, primordial paradise where there is no sickness, predation, aging, or death. The wise god Enki impregnates the mother-goddess Ninhursag and successive generations of goddesses; then he eats eight special plants and is cursed by Ninhursag, falling mortally ill in eight parts of his body. Reconciled at last, she creates eight healing deities, one for each ailment — among them Ninti, born to heal his rib. The myth is celebrated for the wordplay that links it, distantly, to the Garden of Eden.

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** Sumerian cosmology; Dilmun as the idealized pure land and Enki as the crafty god of fresh water and wisdom.
- **Genre.** A mythological poem rich in Sumerian punning, where the names of the healing deities pun on the afflicted body-parts.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Chiefly an Old Babylonian tablet from Nippur; a classic of the Sumerian literary corpus.

## Themes and motifs
- The pure land — Dilmun without sickness, age, or death.
- Forbidden eating and its curse — Enki's consumption of the eight plants.
- Healing through wordplay — the eight deities born to cure the eight ailments.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **Dilmun and Eden — and the Sumerian pun behind Eve from Adam's rib.** Enki and Ninhursag describes a deathless paradise, a forbidden eating that brings a curse, and a healing built on a pun that has fascinated comparativists since Samuel Noah Kramer: when Enki's *rib* (Sumerian **ti**) ails him, Ninhursag creates the goddess **Nin-ti** to heal it — and *ti* means both "rib" and "to make live." In Genesis 2–3, the woman is built from Adam's **rib** and is named **Ḥawwah/Eve**, explained as "mother of all **living**." The same double sense — *rib* and *life* — that is a living pun in Sumerian survives, fossilized and split, in the Hebrew story (rib in the act, "living" in the name). It is one of the most-discussed possible threads from Sumerian myth to the Eden narrative (Kramer 1961 pp.147–149; Kramer & Maier 1989 pp.22–30).
