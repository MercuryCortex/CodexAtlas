# Book of Judith

## What it is
A taut patriotic novella, set in a deliberately impossible history (it has Nebuchadnezzar reigning over the Assyrians from Nineveh — a wink that the story is parable, not chronicle). When the general Holofernes besieges the Judean town of Bethulia and its elders are ready to surrender, the beautiful, devout widow Judith goes to the enemy camp, beguiles Holofernes, and beheads him in his drunken sleep — routing his army and saving her people.

## Primary text

### Judith 13:8
*Trans. NRSV (Apocrypha)*
> And she struck his neck twice with all her might, and cut off his head.

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** Hasmonean-era resistance piety; the heroine embodies fidelity, prayer, and fasting turned to deliverance.
- **Composition.** A Hebrew original (lost), preserved in Greek; the historical "errors" are intentional genre markers.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Deuterocanonical for Catholics, Orthodox, and Ethiopians; absent from the Jewish and Protestant canons.

## Themes and motifs
- Deliverance through a woman — in the line of Jael (Judges 4–5).
- Piety as power — fasting, prayer, and observance as the source of Judith's victory.
- Reversal — the mighty general undone by the unarmed widow.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **Judith and Holofernes: a deuterocanonical scene at the center of Western art.** The image of Judith beheading Holofernes became one of the most-painted subjects in European art — Donatello's bronze, Caravaggio's and (above all) Artemisia Gentileschi's canvases — read variously as civic liberty, divine deliverance, and female agency. A book excluded from the Jewish and Protestant Bibles thus supplied one of Christendom's most enduring visual archetypes (Moore 1985, AYB 40 pp.62–67).
