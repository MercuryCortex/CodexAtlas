# The Pythagorean Texts (The Golden Verses and the Acusmata)

## What it is
The body of teaching attributed to Pythagoras of Samos and his brotherhood at Croton — preserved not in Pythagoras's own (non-existent) writings but in the early ritual maxims (akousmata/symbola), the later versified ethical compendium known as the Golden Verses, and the reports of Aristotle and the Neoplatonist biographers. At its heart: that **number is the principle of all things**; that the soul is immortal and transmigrates through bodies; that musical harmony reflects whole-number ratios (and so the cosmos is a harmony, the "music of the spheres"); and that philosophy is an ascetic, communal *way of life* aimed at purifying the soul.

## Primary text

### The Golden Verses (close)
*Trans. after Thom (1995)*
> And when, having put off the body, you come to the most free aether, you will be a god, immortal, incorruptible, no longer subject to death.

## Context
- **Intellectual milieu.** A fusion of mathematics, mysticism, and ascetic communal religion in the Greek West.
- **Two strands.** The "acusmatici" preserved ritual maxims and taboos; the "mathematici" pursued number and cosmology — both claiming Pythagoras.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Entirely indirect, through later witnesses; the Golden Verses circulated widely into late antiquity.

## Themes and motifs
- Number as principle — reality as fundamentally mathematical and harmonic.
- Transmigration — the immortal soul's passage through lives, and the kinship of all life.
- The way of life — purification, self-examination, and the divinization of the soul.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **"All is number" — the root of sacred mathematics from Plato to the Kabbalah.** The Pythagorean conviction that number is the principle of reality, that the cosmos is a harmony expressible in whole-number ratios, and that the soul is divine and immortal, became — via Plato's Timaeus and the Neoplatonists — the great Western tradition of *sacred mathematics*: the belief that number and proportion are the language of the divine order. It underlies Neoplatonic and Christian number-symbolism, the theology of cosmic harmony, the architecture of cathedrals, Kepler's astronomy, and the Jewish gematria and Christian numerologies that read meaning in the numerical values of sacred words. A semi-legendary 6th-century sage's fusion of mysticism and mathematics became one of the deepest currents in how the West (and the Abrahamic mystical traditions) imagined God's relation to the world (Burkert 1972; Kahn 2001; Riedweg 2005).
