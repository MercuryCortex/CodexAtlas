# The Instruction of Ptahhotep

## What it is
The classic of Egyptian wisdom literature: thirty-seven maxims of right conduct, framed as an aged vizier's instruction to the son who will succeed him. Ptahhotep counsels measured speech and listening, deference to superiors and kindness to inferiors, honesty in justice, restraint in appetite and toward women, and the dignity of old age — all anchored in Ma'at, the cosmic order of truth and right that conduct must embody. Preserved on the Middle Kingdom Papyrus Prisse, it is among the oldest substantial books in the world.

## Primary text

### Maxim 1
*Trans. Lichtheim (1973)*
> Don't be proud of your knowledge, consult the ignorant and the wise; the limits of art are not reached, no artist's skills are perfect. Good speech is more hidden than the emerald, yet it is found among the maidservants at the grindstones.

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** The Egyptian ethic of Ma'at — order, truth, justice — internalized as personal conduct.
- **Genre.** The sebayt ("instruction"), the father-to-son wisdom form that dominated Egyptian ethical literature.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Papyrus Prisse and school copies; a curriculum classic for a millennium.

## Themes and motifs
- Ma'at as conduct — right speech, justice, and restraint as the social face of cosmic order.
- The disciplined courtier — listening, deference, eloquence.
- Generational transmission of wisdom.

## Connections (provisional)

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **Egyptian wisdom in the Book of Proverbs.** Ptahhotep founds the Egyptian "instruction" genre that runs a thousand years to the Instruction of Amenemope (~1100 BCE) — and Amenemope's "thirty sayings" are the demonstrable source of Proverbs 22:17–24:22, which even refers to its "thirty sayings" (Prov 22:20) and reproduces Amenemope's maxims in order. The discovery (Budge, 1923) that a block of the Bible's wisdom is adapted Egyptian instruction was a landmark in showing the international, cross-cultural character of ancient Near Eastern wisdom — and Ptahhotep is the genre's venerable head (Lichtheim 1973 pp.61–80; Simpson 2003 pp.129–148, 223–243).
