# Numbers

**Numbers** (Hebrew *Bəmiḏbar*, "In the wilderness"; the English name from the Greek *Arithmoi*, after the two censuses) is the fourth book of the **Torah**, narrating Israel's **forty years of wilderness wandering** between Sinai and the plains of Moab. It interleaves **census lists** and priestly legislation with a vivid cycle of **rebellion narratives** — the spies and the forfeited generation, the revolt of Korah, the water from the rock, the bronze serpent, the apostasy at Baal-Peor — and culminates in the strange and brilliant **Balaam** episode.

## Composition

Numbers is a **composite** of J, E, and especially **Priestly (P)** material, redacted in the exilic/post-exilic period, but it embeds genuinely **archaic poetry** — the Song of the Well, fragments of the "Book of the Wars of YHWH," and above all the **oracles of Balaam** (chs. 22–24), whose antiquity is widely recognized.

## The cross-tradition wire

The most striking external link is the **Balaam** tradition. A plaster inscription discovered at **Deir ʿAlla** (Jordan, c. 8th c. BCE), written in an Aramaic-related dialect, narrates the visions of **"Balaam son of Beor, a seer of the gods"** — the *same named diviner* who appears in Numbers — demonstrating that a **non-Israelite West Semitic prophetic figure was independently known across the region** and absorbed into Israel's scripture. Separately, the **Priestly Blessing** of Numbers 6:24–26 was recovered on the **Ketef Hinnom silver amulets** (c. -600), making it the **oldest physically attested biblical text** in existence.

## Reach forward

Numbers fixed the paradigm of the **wilderness generation** — testing, rebellion, and the long road to the promise — that runs through Jewish liturgy, the New Testament (the bronze serpent as a type of the cross; the wilderness as trial), and the moral memory of Balaam in all three Abrahamic traditions.
