# Erra and Ishum (The Erra Epic)

## What it is
A first-millennium Akkadian poem of divine violence. Erra — the god of plague, war, and scorched destruction, identified with Nergal — is roused from torpor and, in his bloodlust, persuades Marduk to step down from his throne in Babylon "to clean his regalia," whereupon the cosmic order lapses and Erra rampages, devastating Babylon and its people. His levelheaded vizier Ishum repeatedly counsels restraint, and in the end Erra is appeased and even decrees blessing — a turn that suited the poem's use as a protective charm against pestilence.

## Primary text

### Tablet I (Ishum to Erra)
*Trans. Foster (2005)*
> "Warrior Erra, would you put to death the just man with the unjust? Would you put to death the one who sinned against you together with the one who did not sin against you?"

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** Babylonia amid the disorders of the early first millennium — invasions, the temporary loss of Marduk's statue — read theologically as Erra's rage.
- **Authorship.** Unusually, the poem names its author, Kabti-ilāni-Marduk, who claims it was revealed to him whole in a dream.
- **Use.** Lines of the poem were copied onto amulets as protection against plague — scripture as talisman.

## Themes and motifs
- [[chaoskampf]] — order (Marduk's reign) overturned into violent chaos and then restored.
- Divine violence and theodicy — the just destroyed with the unjust (Ishum's protest).
- The restraining counselor — Ishum as the voice that tempers the destroyer.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-1-008-enuma-elish]] — *parallel-motif*: where Enuma Elish establishes Marduk's ordering kingship, Erra dramatizes its violent suspension.
- → [[phase-1-013-baal-cycle]] — *parallel-motif*: the wider Near Eastern theology of a violent storm/war deity and the threat of cosmic disorder.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **Scripture used as a plague-amulet — and the problem of divine violence.** The Erra Epic is the clearest case of a Mesopotamian literary text functioning apotropaically: because the poem ends with Erra's promise to bless those who honor it, its closing lines were inscribed on amulets and house-tablets to ward off pestilence — a striking instance of a sacred text deployed as protective magic, comparable to later Jewish mezuzot and Christian charm-texts. Theologically, Ishum's protest — "would you put to death the just man with the unjust?" — is the same objection Abraham raises to God over Sodom (Gen 18:23–25), a shared ancient-Near-Eastern wrestling with collective and indiscriminate divine punishment (Cagni 1977; Foster 2005 pp.880–911).
