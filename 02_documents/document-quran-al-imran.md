# Sūrat Āl ʿImrān (Quran 3 — The Family of Imran)

## What it is
The Quran's most sustained engagement with Christianity, named for the family of ʿImrān — the lineage of Mary. It narrates Mary's miraculous birth and her dedication and upbringing in the Temple under Zechariah's care (paralleling the Protoevangelium of James), the annunciation, and the birth, miracles, and "raising up" of Jesus — whom it calls "a Word from God" while insisting he is a created servant, not divine. It also lays down the key interpretive principle of clear versus ambiguous verses, and records the Mubāhala, Muhammad's challenge to the Christians of Najrān.

## Primary text

### Quran 3:45, 59
*Trans. Abdel Haleem (2004)*
> The angels said, "Mary, God gives you news of a Word from Him, whose name will be the Messiah, Jesus, son of Mary..." (3:45)
> In God's eyes Jesus is just like Adam: He created him from dust, said to him, "Be," and he was. (3:59)

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** Medinan Islam in close argument with Christianity; the sura affirms Jesus's virgin birth and messiahship while denying his divinity and the Trinity.
- **Sources.** Mary's Temple upbringing draws on the Protoevangelium of James; the annunciation on Luke.
- **Discovery / transmission.** A foundational sura for Islamic Christology and hermeneutics.

## Themes and motifs
- [[messianism]] — Jesus as Messiah, "Word," and "spirit," yet a created prophet like Adam (3:59).
- Mary's sanctity — her birth, dedication, and miraculous provision in the Temple.
- Scriptural interpretation — the muḥkam (clear) vs. mutashābih (ambiguous) verses (3:7).

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **"Jesus is just like Adam" — the Quran's counter-Christology, built from Christian sources.** Āl ʿImrān affirms what Christians affirm — the virgin birth, the title Messiah, Jesus as God's "Word" and "spirit" — and then turns each affirmation against the divinity it underwrites: Jesus is "just like Adam," created from dust by the word "Be" (3:59), so his miraculous origin proves not godhood but God's creative power. The sura even adopts Christian apocryphal narrative (Mary's Temple childhood, from the Protoevangelium of James) to build its case. It is the clearest single text of how Islam absorbs the Christian story whole and re-reads it through strict monotheism — the engine of fifteen centuries of Muslim-Christian theological argument (Reynolds 2010 pp.130–147; Nasr 2015 pp.130–160).
