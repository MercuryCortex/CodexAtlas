# Sallustius — On the Gods and the World

## What it is
A short, lucid handbook of Neoplatonic pagan theology, written around 360 CE by a friend of the emperor Julian — in effect a "catechism" of philosophical paganism. In a few brisk chapters it lays out the nature of the gods (eternal, unchanging, beneficent, never the cause of evil), the eternity and providential order of the cosmos, the classification and allegorical meaning of myths, the soul's immortality and transmigration, and the proper attitude of piety. It is the clearest brief statement of what an educated late-antique pagan was meant to believe.

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** Julian's attempt (361–363) to revive a reformed, intellectually-respectable paganism against a now-dominant Christianity.
- **Function.** A concise doctrinal digest — paganism organizing itself, under Christian pressure, into something like a creed.
- **Discovery / transmission.** A modest Greek treatise long prized as a window onto late-pagan belief.

## Themes and motifs
- The impassible gods — divine goodness that never causes evil; evil as human failing.
- Myth as allegory — sacred stories classified and decoded for their hidden meaning.
- The eternal cosmos — providence, the soul's immortality, and transmigration.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **Paganism writes a creed — under Christian pressure.** Sallustius's handbook is a revealing historical moment: confronted by a Christianity with catechisms, creeds, and clergy, the philosophical paganism of Julian's revival produced its own compact statement of doctrine — the gods, providence, the soul, the meaning of myth, the ethics of piety — modeled, in effect, on its rival. It shows late antique paganism becoming self-consciously systematic precisely as it lost the contest, and it preserves the clearest short summary we have of the Neoplatonic theology that, ironically, the victorious Christianity would absorb through Pseudo-Dionysius and Boethius (Nock 1926; Cameron 2011; Dillon & Gerson 2004).
