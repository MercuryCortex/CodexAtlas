# Guru Granth Sahib (Adi Granth)

## What it is
The central scripture and — uniquely among major world traditions — the living and eternal *Guru* of Sikhism. The text is a 1430-page anthology in the Gurmukhi script of devotional and theological compositions in mixed North Indian vernaculars: hymns of the first five Sikh Gurus (Nanak, Angad, Amar Das, Ram Das, Arjan), plus the ninth Guru Tegh Bahadur (added 1705); *bhagat bani* of fifteen pre-Sikh sants and Sufis including Kabir, Ravidas, Namdev, and the Punjabi Sufi Sheikh Farid; and bardic *bhatt bani*. The compositions are organized not by author but by *rāg* (musical mode), reflecting the centrally liturgical-musical character of the corpus. The opening *Mūl Mantra* — *Ek Onkar Sat Nam Karta Purakh Nirbhau Nirvair Akal Murat Ajuni Saibhang Gur Prasad* ("One Divine Reality, True Name, Creator-Being, Without Fear, Without Enmity, Timeless Form, Unborn, Self-Existent, By the Guru's Grace") — is the Sikh credal statement. After the death of the tenth Guru Gobind Singh in 1708, the Granth was installed as the eternal Guru; Sikhs treat the text-as-physical-presence with the protocols of a living teacher.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Mughal India from Akbar (r. 1556–1605, religiously eclectic) to Aurangzeb (r. 1658–1707, militantly Sunni). Guru Arjan compiled the Adi Granth in 1604 under Akbar's relatively tolerant rule but was executed in 1606 under his son Jahangir for political-religious reasons; Guru Tegh Bahadur was executed in 1675 under Aurangzeb for refusing forced conversion; Guru Gobind Singh's 1699 founding of the Khalsa (the disciplined martial-religious order) and his 1708 closing of the canon both responded to the Mughal pressure.
- **Religious milieu.** Pre-modern Punjab — at the meeting-point of Hindu, Muslim, and emergent syncretic traditions. The Sant tradition of Kabir and Ravidas had created a North Indian devotional idiom that drew on both Hindu *nirguna* (formless Brahman) theology and Sufi devotional grammar. Guru Nanak's panth (path) crystallized this idiom into a distinct community with its own succession of Gurus and its own scriptural canon.
- **Who was behind it.** Guru Arjan and Bhai Gurdas as the 1604 compilers; Guru Gobind Singh as the closer of the canon in 1706. The compositions were edited from earlier collections including the Goindval Pothis (collated under Guru Amar Das) — the earliest extant pre-canonical compilations (Mann 1996).
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous active use since 1604/1708; no recension problems beyond the 1604/1706 redactional layer. The text is performed musically (*kirtan*) daily in every gurdwara; the *akhand path* (continuous reading) is a key liturgical practice.

## Themes and motifs
- [[divine-name]] — *Nam-simran* (remembrance of the Divine Name) is the central spiritual practice; the Name is at once the means of approach and the divine reality itself.
- [[hidden-god]] — the Sikh Akal Purakh is *nirguna* (without attributes) yet self-revealing through Name, Word, and Guru.
- [[bhakti-devotion]] — Sikh devotion is grounded in the Bhakti tradition but disciplined into ethical monotheism without idolatry.
- [[ethical-monotheism]] — radical equality (one God, one humanity, langar / communal kitchen, rejection of caste discrimination); social ethics is theologically grounded.
- [[religious-pluralism]] — the Granth canonically includes Muslim and Hindu saint-poets as co-authoritative voices without converting them.
- **Guru as mediator** — the Guru-Granth-Sangat triad: the divine Word (*shabad*) speaks through the Guru, is fixed in the Granth, and is realized in the Sangat (community).

## MASSIVE WIN: Sheikh Farid's Poetry Canonized in a Sikh Scripture

[[sheikh-farid]] (1173–1266 CE) was a Muslim Sufi saint — the founding figure of the Chishti order in Punjab, a direct disciple in the chain from Khwaja Moinuddin Chishti of Ajmer. His Punjabi devotional verses (*shaloks*), composed 350 years before the Guru Granth Sahib, were preserved through Sufi oral tradition and were included by Guru Arjan in the 1604 Adi Granth as canonical *bhagat bani* — the compositions of the holy ones.

This is the most dramatically documented case of Hindu-Muslim-Sikh synthesis in world religion: a Muslim Sufi saint's mystical poetry, composed in the 12th–13th century, is canonically authoritative scripture in a 17th-century Sikh text. The Granth does not mark Farid's verses as foreign, commentary, or historical artifact — they are read alongside the Gurus' own compositions, in liturgical rotation, as the voice of the divine. Sheikh Farid appears in the Granth under the name "Farida" and his *shaloks* form a distinct subsection of the *bhagat bani*.

The Farid inclusion is not a syncretic accident. Guru Arjan's editorial theology explicitly affirmed that realized saints across religious boundaries speak the same divine Word (*shabad*). The Sufi mystic [[tradition-sufism]] and the Sikh Guru tradition [[tradition-sikhism]] here produce a single canonical corpus — which is why the Granth uses "Ram," "Hari," "Allah," and "Khuda" as interchangeable names for the same reality, without doctrinal preference.

**Source:** Pashaura Singh, *The Guru Granth Sahib: Canon, Meaning and Authority* (Oxford UP, 2000) — the foundational academic treatment of the bhagat bani and the Farid inclusion. McLeod, *Sikhism* (Penguin, 1997).

## MASSIVE WIN: The "Living Guru" Theology — The Book as Divine Presence

After the death of Guru Gobind Singh in 1708, the Sikh Gurus' succession ended. No human was appointed tenth successor. Instead, the Guru Granth Sahib itself was declared the eternal, living Guru — *Shabad Guru*, the Word-Guru — and has been treated as such ever since.

This is theologically without parallel in the world's major scriptural traditions. Comparison:

| Tradition | Scripture | Human mediator retained? |
|---|---|---|
| [[tradition-judaism]] | Torah scroll — venerated, carried in procession, undressed and dressed in elaborate ritual | Yes — rabbi mediates interpretation and practice |
| [[tradition-islam]] | Quran — the literal word of God (*kalam Allah*), memorized and recited as divine sound | Yes — imam leads prayer, ulama interpret |
| [[tradition-christianity-canonical]] | Bible — inspired word of God; in Catholic tradition, magisterium interprets; in Protestant tradition, direct access via Holy Spirit | Yes (Catholic) / mediating Spirit (Protestant) |
| [[tradition-sikhism]] | Guru Granth Sahib — **the Guru itself**, not a record of revelation but the active divine presence; woken, dressed, fanned, put to bed | **No** — the book is the Guru; no human successor |

The Granth is ritually treated as a living being: it is installed in a *palki* (palanquin) in the gurdwara's sanctum, fanned with a *chauri* (flywhisk of authority, the mark of royalty), opened ceremonially each morning (*prakash*), and closed at night (*sukhasan*). Touching it with unclean hands is prohibited; approaching barefoot and with covered head is required. The text does not "represent" divine authority — it *is* the Guru's presence.

This doctrine has no equivalent in any other scriptural religion. The Torah is not a rabbi; the Quran is not a caliph; the Bible is not the Pope. The Guru Granth Sahib *is* the Guru.

## MASSIVE WIN: Shabad Theology — The Word as Divine Medium

The *shabad* (divine Word, literally "sound") is the Granth's central ontological category. The divine reality is not primarily experienced through image, rite, pilgrimage, or caste-marked identity but through the *shabad* — the divine sound that is both the medium and the content of divine self-disclosure.

Cross-tradition parallels:

- **John 1:1** (*Logos*): "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God." The Logos theology of the Gospel of John and later Christian mysticism (Origen, Meister Eckhart's *Wort*) treats the divine Word as the ontological ground of creation — identical with but not confused with the Father. The Sikh *shabad* occupies a structurally parallel position: the divine sound through which Akal Purakh self-discloses without ceasing to be formless (*nirguna*).
- **Islamic *kalam Allah*** — the Quran as the uncreated Word of God (*kalamu'llah*). In Ash'ari theology, the Quran is divine speech subsisting in God's essence; reciting it participates in the divine. The Sikh Granth is similarly not a human record of divine events but the divine Word fixed in written form.
- **Hindu *Shabda-Brahman*** — the Vedic and Tantric doctrine that the *Veda* is not composed but *heard* (*shruti*) — the eternal divine sound crystallized into human language. The *Om* as the primordial sound-ground of all reality.
- **Jewish *dibur* / *memra*** — the Word as God's executive instrument in creation and revelation; the Aramaic targumim introduce *memra* (Word) as a hypostasis mediating between the transcendent God and the world.

What distinguishes the Sikh *shabad* is that it combines the Vedic ontological freight of eternal divine sound with the Islamic claim that this sound is definitively fixed in a single human-language text, and then — uniquely — that the text itself has become the personal Guru.

## MASSIVE WIN: Ik Onkar — "One Divine Reality"

The scripture opens not with a word but with a numeral and a syllable: *Ik Onkar* — literally "One, Onkar" (Onkar being the Punjabi form of Om). This two-syllable declaration is the Granth's entire theology in compressed form.

*Ik Onkar* is neither the purely personal God of popular Hindu bhakti (Krishna, Rama — divine figures with biography, form, and relational personality) nor the purely impersonal Absolute of Advaita Vedanta (Shankara's *nirguna Brahman* in which no personal relation is ultimately real). It is a singular divine reality that is formless (*nirguna*) yet addressed in devotion (*bhakti*), one yet self-revealing, transcendent yet personally approachable through the Guru's Word.

The parallel to Islamic *tawhid* ("the divine unity") is unmistakable and deliberate. Both Islam and Sikhism are rigorously anti-idolatrous, anti-polytheistic, and anti-caste (the Granth's egalitarianism echoes the Quran's "no distinction among Muslims"). Both use multiple divine names as pointers to one reality. But Sikhism retains the full devotional warmth of *bhakti* — the intimate, longing, love-saturated relationship with the divine Beloved — which is not the dominant register of mainstream Islamic theology (though it is precisely the register of Sufi poetry, which is why Sheikh Farid fits so naturally into the Granth).

*Ik Onkar* is inscribed on every gurdwara, on Sikh jewelry, on the *Khanda* symbol. It is the most compressed cross-tradition theological statement in the bhakti world: one divine reality, reached through loving devotion, expressed in the sound of the divine Word.

## Disputes
- **The Kartarpur Bir.** The authenticity of the manuscript held by the Sodhi family at Kartarpur as the original 1604 Adi Bir has been periodically contested in Sikh studies; Mann (2001) treats it as substantially authentic with some interpolations.
- **Mira's omission.** Why Mirabai's compositions are not in the Granth (when her contemporaries are) is variously explained — her songs may not yet have circulated in Punjab in 1604; her overtly Krishna-form devotion may have been deemed too *saguna* (form-attached) for the Sikh canon's *nirguna* register.
- **The "fifth scripture problem."** Sikh tradition also recognizes the *Dasam Granth* (attributed to Guru Gobind Singh) as scriptural; the relationship and the disputed authenticity of parts of the Dasam Granth are live issues.
