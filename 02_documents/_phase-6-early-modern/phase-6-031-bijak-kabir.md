# Bījak

## What it is
The principal Kabir-Panth recension of the corpus of poetic-religious sayings attributed to the 15th–early-16th-c. Muslim weaver-poet [[kabir]] of Banaras. The *Bijak* (literally "Seed" or "Account-book") is one of three textual recensions of the broader Kabir corpus (alongside the Rajasthani *Kabir-Granthāvalī* and the Sikh Adi Granth selections). Structurally organized in three genres: *Ramainī* (~84 stanzas in *caupāī*-*dohā* form, doctrinal-meditational), *Śabda* (~115 longer compositions, sung-poetic), and *Sākhī* (~350+ *dohā* couplets — Kabir's signature aphoristic register, sometimes called the "Sākhī of Kabir"). The poems polemicize against Brahmanical ritualism (caste-distinction, temple-worship, image-worship, Vedic mantra-recitation), against Islamic external practice (the five pillars treated as outward formality), and against Yogic / Nath siddha asceticism (the formal disciplines of the body); they call instead for direct devotion to an unnameable, formless, ungendered divine reality addressed indifferently as *Rām*, *Hari*, *Allāh*, *Khudā*, *Sain*, *Sahib*. The verse is in mixed North Indian vernacular (Bhojpuri / Awadhi / Khari Boli — the *sant-bhāṣā*, "the saints' language") with characteristic short lines, rhythmic punch, blunt directness, and astonishing image-density.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Late Delhi Sultanate / Lodi-period North India (Kabir d. 1518; the *Bijak* textualization continues through the 17th c. under Mughal patronage). The Mughal *sulh-i kull* atmosphere from Akbar (r. 1556–1605) onward is the cultural framework in which the Kabir corpus is canonized and circulated.
- **Religious milieu.** The North Indian Sant tradition — Kabir, Ravidas, Namdev, Dadu, Sundardas — flourishing in the same cultural-linguistic zone where the Sufi Chishti, Suhrawardi, and Naqshbandi orders were also institutionally established, and where the Vaishnava bhakti (Vallabha, Chaitanya, Tulsidas), Shaiva-Shakta Tantric, and Nath sampradaya traditions were all simultaneously active. The Sant tradition explicitly transcends religious-community boundary — Kabir is the most-cited example of a poet whose followers were Hindu and Muslim in roughly equal proportions, with both communities subsequently claiming him.
- **Who was behind it.** Kabir himself (~1440–1518) is the historical core; the Kabir-Panth sectarian organization at Banaras (~16th–17th c. consolidation) is responsible for the *Bijak* textualization specifically.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous Kabir-Panth oral and manuscript tradition. The 1604 Adi Granth (Sikh scripture) is the earliest dated written attestation of substantial Kabir material; the *Bijak* manuscript tradition is later (~17th–18th c. textualization). The 20th-c. global reception was inaugurated by Rabindranath Tagore's 1915 *Songs of Kabir* (a loose literary translation that established Kabir's modern global reputation but is textually unreliable); Hess & Singh's 1983/2002 *Bījak of Kabir* is the foundational scholarly access.

## Themes and motifs
- [[non-duality]] — Kabir's *Rām* is not the *avatara* Rāma of Tulsidas but the nameless formless Ultimate (*nirguṇa*); the *Bijak* is the most uncompromising *nirguṇa* (formless-divinity) statement in the Kabir corpus.
- [[divine-name]] — but in a critical-iconoclastic mode: *all names* are pointers to the nameless reality; *Rām* and *Allāh* are equally valid (or equally inadequate) as names for what cannot be named.
- [[critique-of-idolatry]] / [[critique-of-ritualism]] — against Brahmanical caste-and-ritual orthodoxy, against Islamic external practice, against Hindu and Yogic ascetic disciplines, against pilgrimage, against scripture-study-without-realization. Kabir's polemic spares no religious institution.
- [[religious-pluralism]] — but pluralism of a peculiar kind: not the modern interfaith-affirming pluralism, but a *transcendence-of-religion* pluralism that treats all religious externals as equally unnecessary in light of direct realization.
- [[hidden-god]] / [[ground-of-being]] — the formless divine is the immanent ground present *within* the body of the seeker; the famous Kabir lines on the divine as the seeker's own breath, as the ocean within the drop, as the lover within the beloved.
- [[bhakti-devotion]] — but *nirguṇa-bhakti*: devotion to the formless. The structural opposition to the Vaishnava *saguṇa-bhakti* (devotion to the personal-form divine — Krishna, Rāma) is a major medieval theological-poetic distinction.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-6-016-guru-granth-sahib]] — *direct-quote*: the Adi Granth (1604) incorporates ~270 Kabir hymns / *śabads* as canonical scripture for the Sikh tradition. Kabir is one of the *bhagats* (devotees) whose verse is read alongside the compositions of the ten Sikh Gurus.
- → [[guru-nanak]] / Sikhism — *direct-influence*: Guru Nanak (1469–1539) was Kabir's near-contemporary and may have met him (some traditional accounts have an encounter; historicity contested); the Sikh tradition's *Sant* lineage and its *nirguṇa* devotional theology have Kabir as a foundational precursor.
- → Sufi vernacular tradition — *parallel-motif* / *shared-milieu*: late-medieval Indo-Persian Sufi poetry (especially Chishti vernacular *qawwali* and the Sufi-Hindi *bhakti-marfa* literature) shares with Kabir: the addressed-Beloved divine, the critique of external religious practice in favor of direct realization, the bridal-mystical and intoxication-mystical idioms, the cross-religious-vocabulary identification of *Rām* / *Allāh*. Vaudeville 1993 and Schimmel 1975 trace the Sufi-Sant idiom-sharing in detail. The shared idiom was so thorough that Kabir's own religious-community identity (Hindu? Muslim? Sufi-influenced Hindu? Hindu-influenced Sufi?) has been contested for 500 years.
- → [[phase-5-025-rumi-masnavi]] / [[phase-5-021-ibn-arabi-fusus-al-hikam]] — *parallel-motif*: structural parallels in the Sufi metaphysical-poetic tradition (*waḥdat al-wujūd*) — the formless-singular divine, the inadequacy of all conceptual-religious externals, the soul's homecoming to the Beloved.
- → Bengal Śākta Sant tradition ([[ramprasad-sen]], ~18th c.) — *parallel-motif*: structural-functional analog within the Bengali Śākta devotional tradition (formless Kālī worship, anti-formalist devotional poetry).
- → 20th-c. global Kabir reception — *direct-influence*: Tagore 1915, *Songs of Kabir*; the Beat poets and the 1960s-70s Western counterculture absorbed Kabir alongside Rumi and the Tao Te Ching; the modern global "spiritual but not religious" idiom has Kabir as one of its principal canonical voices.
- → Modern Indian anti-caste politics — *direct-influence*: B.R. Ambedkar invoked Kabir as a foundational anti-caste voice; modern Dalit Bahujan political movements have repeatedly reclaimed Kabir.

## Disputes
- **Authorship of specific compositions**: the corpus problem is severe. Hess & Singh and Vaudeville have demonstrated that significant portions of the popular Kabir corpus are post-Kabir accretion; the historical-Kabir corpus is smaller than the popular corpus. The *Bijak*-specific compositions are textually distinct from the *Granthāvalī* and the Adi Granth selections in ways that allow disentangling.
- **Kabir's religious identity**: contested for 500 years. The traditional Hindu account claims him as Hindu; the traditional Muslim account claims him as Muslim. The modern scholarly consensus (Vaudeville, Hess, Lorenzen) reads him as deliberately transgressive of the boundary — a Muslim by birth who composed within and across Hindu and Sufi devotional registers.
- **Dating**: 1440–1518 (long-life tradition) is the most-cited; substantial earlier (1398–) and later (1455–1530) datings have been argued.
- **Recensional priority**: which of the three textual recensions is closer to the historical-Kabir core is a continuing philological question.
