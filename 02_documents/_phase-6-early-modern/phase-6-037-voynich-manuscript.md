# Voynich Manuscript

## What it is
A 15th-century illustrated manuscript codex of approximately 240 surviving pages, written in an undeciphered script ("Voynichese", ~25–30 distinct glyphs) in an unknown language or cipher, and copiously illustrated with botanical, astronomical / astrological / cosmological, balneological (women bathing in elaborate plumbing systems), biological, and pharmaceutical-section drawings. Radiocarbon analysis (University of Arizona 2009) dates the parchment to **1404–1438**. Held at **Yale University's Beinecke Rare Book and Manuscript Library as MS 408**. **The signature mystery-object of the Yale Beinecke collection and one of the most studied and least-deciphered documents in Western intellectual history.**

The manuscript is divided by its illustrations into approximate sections:
- **Botanical / herbal** (~ folios 1–66) — drawings of plants, most unidentifiable with known species.
- **Astronomical / zodiacal** (~ folios 67–73) — circular diagrams with zodiacal figures.
- **Balneological** (~ folios 75–84) — naked women in elaborate plumbing.
- **Cosmological** (~ folios 85–86) — large folding folios with rosette / mandala structures.
- **Pharmaceutical** (~ folios 87–102) — small plant parts and jars.
- **Recipes** (~ folios 103–116) — short paragraphs in Voynichese.

## Context

- **Physical attestation:** parchment 1404–1438 (Arizona 2009); ink-and-pigment analysis consistent with early-15th-c. Northern Italian / Bohemian production.
- **Provenance trail (incomplete):** First documented owner Georg Baresch (Prague, mid-17th c.) → Johannes Marci (1665, who sent it to Athanasius Kircher seeking decipherment; Kircher failed) → Society of Jesus library, Rome → Villa Mondragone, Frascati → **Wilfrid Voynich** (1912 purchase) → Voynich's widow Ethel Voynich → Hans P. Kraus → Yale Beinecke (1969 donation).
- **Rumored earlier provenance** (unconfirmed): Marci's 1665 cover letter to Kircher claimed the manuscript had been purchased by **Emperor Rudolf II of Prague** in the 1580s–90s for 600 ducats, possibly from John Dee or Edward Kelley (who were at Rudolf's court 1584–1589). The Rudolfine origin is *plausible* given the Rudolfine Hermetic-court's known collecting practices but **unconfirmed by independent documentation**.

## Scholarly state-of-debate
- **Genuine encrypted text in an unknown / lost language?** The character-frequency and entropy distributions of Voynichese are *language-like* — not random gibberish — which has supported the hypothesis that Voynichese encodes a real language with substantial information content. (D'Imperio 1978; Bennett, Currier, Tiltman, Friedman analyses through the 20th c.)
- **A cipher of a known language?** Many cipher-hypotheses have been proposed and tested; none has reliably decoded the text. The text resists every classical cipher analysis (substitution, polyalphabetic, transposition, nomenclator).
- **A constructed / artificial language?** Plausible — a 15th-c. private language constructed by a single author.
- **An asemic / glossolalic / nonsense text?** Some 21st-c. analyses (Rugg 2004) proposed it is a deliberate gibberish constructed with grille-cipher techniques to produce language-like statistics. Other 21st-c. work (Bowern, Reddy, Knight) found patterns inconsistent with the gibberish hypothesis.
- **A hoax?** Voynich himself, or someone earlier, is sometimes proposed as a forger. **The 2009 radiocarbon dating decisively excludes Voynich himself or anyone post-1500 from authorship** — the parchment is genuinely 15th-c.
- **Decoded?** Multiple claimed decipherments (Gerard Cheshire 2019, etc.) have been rejected by mainstream cryptographic-linguistic scholarship. The text remains undeciphered.
