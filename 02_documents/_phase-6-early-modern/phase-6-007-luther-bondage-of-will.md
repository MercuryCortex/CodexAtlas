# De Servo Arbitrio (On the Bondage of the Will)

## What it is
Luther's full-length reply (~330 pages in the Weimar edition) to Erasmus' *Diatribe seu Collatio de Libero Arbitrio* (1524). Erasmus had argued, with carefully balanced humanist moderation, that scripture supports a small but real measure of free human cooperation with grace (the *liberum arbitrium*). Luther replies — with deliberate rhetorical violence — that the human will, post-Fall, is entirely enslaved either to God or to Satan; there is no neutral middle in which a free choice toward God is possible; salvation is wholly God's work by sovereign grace, and *all events* (including human acts and the damnation of the reprobate) fall under God's foreordination. The work systematizes the predestinarian-Augustinian theology that the 95 Theses had only implied, and is the doctrinal cornerstone of the Reformation.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Saxony 1525 — the year of the German Peasants' War, in which Luther's anti-papal critique was being radicalized by Thomas Müntzer into social revolution; Luther's *Against the Murderous and Thieving Hordes of Peasants* (May 1525) had already broken with the radicals. Wittenberg's status as a Reformation center was now politically established.
- **Religious milieu.** The European humanist reform consensus that had partly enabled Luther's early success was now visibly fracturing. Erasmus' *Diatribe* was the Catholic humanist's effort to find a moderate position; *De Servo Arbitrio* was Luther's refusal of moderation.
- **Who was behind it.** Luther personally, with Melanchthon's intellectual support but in a register Melanchthon would later modulate.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous Lutheran reception; the work was central to the Lutheran self-understanding through the *Formula of Concord* (1577). Calvin and the Reformed tradition treat it as an authority on grace (though Lutheran and Reformed predestinarianism diverge in detail).

## Themes and motifs
- [[justification-by-faith]] — articulated in its predestinarian foundation: justification is God's work, not human cooperation.
- [[predestination-protestant]] — Luther's strongest predestinarian statement; he calls the doctrine of God's hidden will (*Deus absconditus*) the "essential" foundation of Christian faith.
- [[sola-scriptura]] — Luther argues throughout from Scripture's "clarity" against Erasmus' appeal to ecclesiastical tradition.
- **Deus absconditus** — God's revealed will (kindness in Christ) is distinguished from God's hidden will (sovereign foreordination); the distinction is Luther's most influential theological-philosophical move, picked up by later thinkers from Calvin through Kierkegaard.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-6-005-luther-95-theses]] — *same-author / doctrinal-development*: makes explicit the soteriology underlying the 1517 indulgence critique.
- → [[phase-6-010-calvin-institutes]] — *direct-influence*: Calvin's doctrine of double predestination develops Luther's framework with greater systematic precision.
- → [[phase-4-028-augustine-confessions]] / Augustine anti-Pelagian writings — *direct-revival*: Luther repeatedly cites Augustine; *De Servo Arbitrio* is in form an Augustinian-Pauline argument.
- → Erasmus *Diatribe* — *direct-polemic*: the work's occasion and target.

## Disputes
- **Calvin's predestinarianism vs. Luther's.** Calvin reads Luther as a clear double-predestinarian; some modern Lutheran theology (Bultmann, the early Karl Barth's reading) emphasizes the *Deus absconditus*–*Deus revelatus* distinction as not strictly equivalent to Calvinist double predestination.
- **Rhetorical violence.** *De Servo Arbitrio* is one of the most polemically intense theological treatises in Christian history; whether Luther's overstatement is to be read literally or as deliberate rhetorical maximalism is a long-running interpretive debate.
