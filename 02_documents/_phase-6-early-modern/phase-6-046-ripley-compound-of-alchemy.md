# The Compound of Alchemy / The Ripley Scroll

## Overview

**George Ripley** (c.1415–1490) — Augustinian Canon of Bridlington Priory, Yorkshire, who allegedly spent time in Italy and Malta studying alchemy — is the canonical English alchemist of the 15th century. His *Compound of Alchemy* (1471), composed in seven-line *rhyme royal* stanzas, describes the Great Work through twelve allegorical *Gates* (Calcination, Dissolution, Separation, Conjunction, Putrefaction, Congelation, Cibation, Sublimation, Fermentation, Exaltation, Multiplication, Projection).

The *Ripley Scroll* — a painted parchment roll up to six metres long, showing a toad devouring a serpent, the alchemical king, the pelican feeding her young with her blood, the philosophical tree, and the crowned hermaphrodite — is one of the most remarkable visual documents in English mystical history. Twenty-three copies survive, ranging from c.1490 to c.1570, each slightly different, suggesting an active workshop tradition of scroll-production alongside the written text.

## The Twelve Gates

The Compound's twelve gates correspond to the twelve operations of the Great Work, which Ripley frames as a parallel to the twelve months, the twelve apostles, and the twelve degrees of spiritual transformation:

1. **Calcination** — the burning away of the gross (corresponds to purification of the soul)
2. **Dissolution** — return to prime matter (corresponds to dissolution of ego)
3. **Separation** — division of pure from impure (discernment)
4. **Conjunction** — reunion of the separated parts (the sacred marriage)
5. **Putrefaction** — the black stage, *nigredo* (death before rebirth)
...through Fermentation, Exaltation, Multiplication to Projection (the completed Stone)

The spiritual-alchemical parallel is explicit: Ripley addresses the work equally as a physical laboratory procedure and as an interior transformation of the operator.

## Reception

Elias Ashmole placed Ripley at the centre of his *Theatrum Chemicum Britannicum* (1652) — the great anthology of English alchemical poetry — explicitly framing him as the founder of the English Hermetic tradition. George Starkey's *Ripley Reviv'd* (1677–78, 576 pages) is a section-by-section commentary so exhaustive it generated a mini-literature of its own. Newton's alchemical manuscripts reference the Ripley tradition. The scroll continued to be copied and painted through the mid-16th century in what appears to be a deliberate scribal tradition at English monasteries.
