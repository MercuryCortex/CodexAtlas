# Pimander (Ficino's Latin Corpus Hermeticum)

## What it is
Marsilio Ficino's Latin translation of fourteen treatises of the Greek *Corpus Hermeticum*, completed in 1463 and first printed at Treviso in 1471 under the title *Mercurii Trismegisti Pimander seu De potestate et sapientia Dei*. The title page names only the first treatise (Poimandres) but in fact the volume contains CH I–XIV, prefaced by Ficino's *Argumentum* presenting Hermes Trismegistus as the first of the *prisci theologi* — the chain of ancient sages (Hermes → Orpheus → Aglaophamus → Pythagoras → Plato) who taught a single primordial wisdom received before Moses or in parallel to him. The volume founds Renaissance Hermeticism as a self-conscious intellectual movement.

## Primary text — selected excerpts

Ficino's *Pimander* is in the public domain (printed 1471), but it is in Latin. There is no full public-domain English translation of Ficino's Latin Hermetica as such. The excerpts below pair: (a) Ficino's Latin *Argumentum* preface in short translation, and (b) Ficino's Latin Hermeticum I against G. R. S. Mead's 1906 English (already used for the Phase-4 Corpus Hermeticum entry). For the Greek-vs-Latin philology, consult Copenhaver 1992 (introduction and notes).

### Ficino's *Argumentum* (preface, 1463) — the chain of *prisca theologia*

The opening of Ficino's preface to Cosimo de' Medici. Here Ficino constructs the *prisca theologia*: Hermes is the first of the ancient theologians, the chain stretches down through Orpheus and Pythagoras to Plato, and the whole chain is providentially preparatory for Christianity.

> In the time when Moses was born, there flourished Atlas, the astrologer, brother of the natural philosopher Prometheus and maternal grandfather of the elder Mercury, whose nephew was Mercurius Trismegistus. So Augustine writes. Mercury is said to have been called Trismegistus, that is, thrice-greatest, because he was the greatest philosopher, the greatest priest, and the greatest king. ...
>
> He was the first who philosophized about divine things and the immortality of the soul. He is therefore called the first author of theology. Orpheus followed him and took second place among the ancient theologians. After Orpheus, Aglaophamus was initiated into the sacred matters of Orpheus, and the Pythagorean philosophy of Pythagoras succeeded Aglaophamus, and Philolaus succeeded Pythagoras, who was the teacher of our divine Plato. Thus there is a single concordant ancient theology, from a wonderful order, beginning from Mercury and being perfected in the divine Plato.

Citation: Marsilio Ficino, *Argumentum* (preface) to the *Pimander*, Latin text Treviso 1471, fols. 1r–v; English here paraphrased with reference to Copenhaver 1992, pp. xlvii–xlix, and to the Allen-Rees 2002 essays. The English is a tightened conflation; for the verbatim Latin see the Burroughs-Fasciculus modern reprint and the Bibliotheca Hertziana digital facsimile.

### Ficino, *Pimander* I — opening of the vision (Latin)

A short sample of Ficino's actual Latin rendering of Corpus Hermeticum I.1, alongside Mead's English of the Greek. Ficino's Latin here is the basis on which two centuries of European intellectuals — Pico, Reuchlin, Bruno, Patrizi, Agrippa, Fludd — encountered the Hermetic vision.

> *Cogitanti mihi aliquando de rebus quae sunt, et intelligentia mea in altum sublata, sensus quoque corporis ita compressis ut iis quos somnus profundus a cibo vel labore corpori illatus solet reprimere, visus est mihi quidam ingentis ac immensae magnitudinis, qui meo nomine vocans dixit: quid vis audire et videre, et intelligendo discere et cognoscere?*
>
> ("When I was once thinking about the things that are, and my intellect was raised on high, and the senses of my body were so suppressed as those of men weighed down with deep sleep after food or labor — there appeared to me one of immense and vast size, who called my name and said: What do you wish to hear and see, and by understanding to learn and know?")

Citation: Marsilio Ficino, *Mercurii Trismegisti Pimander* I.1, Treviso 1471 ed., fol. 2r. English rendering compared with Mead 1906 (CH I.1) and Copenhaver 1992, p. 1.

### Ficino's marginal *Argumentum* to CH I — the Hermetic vision as preparation for Christianity

Ficino prefixed each treatise with a brief *Argumentum* — short interpretive guides. The one to CH I introduces the Poimandres vision as the first natural revelation of the cosmic Trinity (Father-Mind, Word, Spirit), parallel and propaedeutic to Christian theology.

> Here Mercury describes the highest God, the Father, in His own substance; the Mind from Him, His Son; the Word generated; and the Spirit, the third person. He treats also of the creation of the world by the Word, the descent of the spiritual man into matter, the captivity of the soul in the body, its purification, and its return through the seven planetary spheres to the Father. Whoever reads understands that this is none other than the threefold mystery which Christianity sets forth.

Citation: Ficino, *Argumentum in Pimandrum*, Treviso 1471, fol. 1v–2r; paraphrased with reference to Copenhaver 1992, pp. xlvii–xlix, and the Allen-Rees 2002 volume. This framing — Hermes as anticipatory witness to the Christian Trinity — is the move that gave the *Corpus Hermeticum* its remarkable Renaissance Catholic acceptability for roughly 150 years, until Isaac Casaubon's 1614 *De rebus sacris exercitationes* re-dated the texts and ended their authority as primordial revelation.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Florence under Cosimo de' Medici (d. 1464), who had founded the Platonic Academy at Careggi and was the patron of Florentine humanism. Cosimo halted Ficino's translation of Plato to demand the Hermetica be translated first — an order Ficino records explicitly — because Cosimo believed Hermes was older and more foundational than Plato.
- **Religious milieu.** Late-medieval Catholic Florence on the eve of the Reformation. Hermes Trismegistus was an acceptable pagan-prophet figure because Augustine, Lactantius, and the medieval Latin *Asclepius* tradition had already cited him as a witness to Christian truth from outside Israel. Ficino frames the Hermetica as compatible with Christianity — a natural revelation predating or paralleling the Mosaic.
- **Who was behind it.** Ficino (1433–1499), a priest, physician, and philosopher under Medici patronage, working from a Greek manuscript that the Medici agent Leonardo da Pistoia had acquired in Macedonia ~1460 and delivered to Cosimo. The Greek manuscript represents the Byzantine recension of CH compiled by Michael Psellos in the 11th century.
- **Discovery / transmission.** The Greek manuscript itself was a continuous Byzantine inheritance, brought west in the post-1453 manuscript diaspora that followed the fall of Constantinople. The 1471 Latin printing was reprinted at least sixteen times before 1500 and dozens more times in the 16th century; the *Pimander* was one of the most widely-disseminated philosophical books of the early printing era.

## Themes and motifs
- [[prisca-theologia]] — Ficino's framing thesis: Hermes is the first link in an unbroken chain of ancient theology converging on Plato and thereby on Christ; this is *the* Phase 6 thesis about Phases 1–4.
- [[gnosis-as-salvation]] — Hermetic salvation by direct knowledge of the divine, here recommended to Christian readers as a natural-revelation analogue of grace.
- [[hidden-god]] — the unknowable Nous-Father, accessible only by revelation.
- [[soul-as-spark]] — humans as descended from the primal Anthropos; ascent through the spheres.
- [[creation-by-word]] — Hermetic Logos cosmogony aligned by Ficino with Genesis 1 and John 1.

## Disputes
- **Yates thesis controversy.** Frances Yates' 1964 reconstruction of "Renaissance Hermeticism" as a coherent magical-philosophical movement has been refined by Brian Copenhaver, Wouter Hanegraaff, and others who emphasize that Ficino's Hermetism was much more cautiously orthodox than Bruno's later radicalization. The trajectory is real; the homogeneity of "the tradition" is contested.
- **Manuscript identity.** The exact Greek manuscript Ficino used is debated; Laurentianus 71,33 is the leading candidate but Ficino likely consulted more than one codex.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- ***Prisca theologia* as the vault's own intellectual ancestor:** Ficino's thesis — that Hermes Trismegistus, Zoroaster, Pythagoras, Plato, and Christ are stages of a single converging wisdom — is structurally the same claim the Codex Atlas embodies as a research methodology. The atlas *is* Ficino's prisca theologia made into a critical research apparatus: same comparative impulse, opposite epistemological stance (Ficino assumed convergence; the vault investigates it with sources and degrees of confidence). Hanegraaff 2012 documents this genealogy from Ficino to the modern comparative-religion field.
- **Cosimo halts Plato translation to read Hermes first — the most consequential editorial decision of the 15th century:** Ficino records explicitly that Cosimo de' Medici ordered him to stop translating Plato and translate the Hermetica immediately (~1462), because Cosimo believed Hermes was older and more foundational. The Renaissance Neoplatonist revival therefore *begins as Hermeticism* before it reads the Dialogues pure. This means Ficino's Plato is already filtered through Egyptian-Greek syncretism — the entire genealogy of Renaissance philosophy runs through this single patronage decision.
- **Ficino → Pico → Bruno → the chain that ends at the stake (1600):** Ficino's cautiously orthodox Hermetism (Hermes as natural-revelation prophet) → Pico's 900 Conclusions synthesizing Kabbalah + Hermeticism + Platonism (1486, condemned by Innocent VIII) → Bruno's Hermetic infinite-universe heliocentrism (burned Rome 1600). The 137-year chain from Ficino's translation to Bruno's execution is the vault's most precisely dated idea-chain: the same core Hermetic claim — that the cosmos is divinely alive and infinite — moves from acceptable orthodoxy to capital heresy in four intellectual generations, traceable step-by-step in vault-resident documents.
- **Casaubon 1614 ↔ Ficino 1463 — the 151-year scholarly correction:** Ficino's 1463 translation is authoritative for 151 years before Casaubon 1614 proves the texts are ~2nd-century CE, not pre-Mosaic. The vault holds both the document (phase-4-011-corpus-hermeticum-i.md) and its modern reception (this Phase-6 node) — a clean before/after that demonstrates how a single philological act can restructure an entire intellectual tradition's self-understanding. This is also the founding case for what is now the *critical study of esotericism* as an academic discipline.
