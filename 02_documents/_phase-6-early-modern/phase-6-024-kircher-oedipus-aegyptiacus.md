# Oedipus Aegyptiacus

## What it is
Athanasius Kircher's three-folio-volume universal-history-and-philology of Egyptian wisdom, printed at Rome 1652–1654 under Pope Innocent X's patronage. The work attempts a sweeping decipherment of Egyptian hieroglyphs (Kircher believed they were symbolic-philosophical, not phonetic — a hypothesis Champollion's 1822 decipherment would refute), a documentation of the prisca-theologia transmission from Egypt through Hermes Trismegistus, Pythagoras, Plato, and the Hebrew patriarchs into Christianity, a substantial treatment of Hebrew Kabbalah (drawing on Reuchlin, the Zohar, and Christian Kabbalists), an unprecedented printing of Coptic grammatical material (Kircher correctly identified Coptic as the late-period vernacular descendant of ancient Egyptian), and a catalogue of Egyptian, Chaldean, Greek, Roman, Arabic, and Chinese wisdom traditions framed as participants in a single revealed ancient theology converging on Christianity. The work is the last great synthesis of the Renaissance Hermetic-Kabbalist universal-history project and the most ambitious failed Egyptian decipherment of the pre-Champollion era.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Counter-Reformation Rome under Pope Innocent X; the period of the Pamphili-family patronage of Bernini's *Fontana dei Quattro Fiumi* (1651, with its obelisk surmounted by the Pamphili dove) for which Kircher provided the Latin hieroglyphic decipherment.
- **Religious milieu.** Catholic Counter-Reformation universalism: Kircher's Society of Jesus project of comprehending and assimilating all world traditions into a Catholic-universal framework. The Jesuit China mission (Ricci, Schall) was bringing back Chinese material that Kircher integrated; the Jesuit Egypt collection at the Collegio Romano was the European center of Egyptian study.
- **Who was behind it.** Kircher personally — a German Jesuit polymath who had been at the Collegio Romano since 1635, with the resources of the Society's global mission network and the patronage of multiple popes.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous early-modern reception as the authoritative Egyptian-and-universal source; later 17th-c. critique (Marcus Meibom; the Hardouinian skeptics) and 18th-c. Enlightenment dismissal; mostly displaced by Champollion in 1822. Modern scholarly rehabilitation (Curran 2007, Findlen 2004) treats Kircher seriously as the late-Renaissance synthesist.

## Themes and motifs
- [[prisca-theologia]] — Kircher's central frame: ancient Egyptian theology was the original divine revelation, transmitted through Hermes-Mercurius-Trismegistus (whom Kircher, post-Casaubon, still defended as the Egyptian sage despite Casaubon's redating) to the Hebrew patriarchs, Pythagoras, Plato, and Christianity. *The Oedipus is the principal post-Casaubon attempt to preserve the prisca-theologia framework after its philological foundation had been demolished.*
- **Hieroglyphic decipherment** — Kircher reads the hieroglyphs as symbolic-philosophical compounds (each sign a compressed theological proposition); the entire program is wrong on the linguistic mechanism but correct in identifying Coptic as the descendant language.
- [[hidden-god]] — the apophatic-Hermetic Father.
- **Universal harmony** — the underlying conviction that all wisdom traditions converge.
- **Kabbalah** — substantial treatment, drawing on Reuchlin, Cordovero (via excerpt), and the Lurianic literature reaching Rome through North African Jewish manuscripts.

## Disputes
- **Kircher's competence.** Modern Egyptological consensus: his decipherment was wrong in principle (the hieroglyphs are phonetic-logographic, not purely symbolic). His Coptic identification was correct. His philological scope was unprecedented and largely defensible by 17th-century standards.
- **Kircher and Casaubon.** Kircher acknowledged Casaubon's 1614 redating in scholarly notes but continued to use the Hermetica as authoritative ancient evidence; whether this was philological obtuseness or programmatic-confessional resistance is debated. Yates' reading favors the latter.
