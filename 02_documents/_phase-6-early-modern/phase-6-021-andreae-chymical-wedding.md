# Chymical Wedding of Christian Rosenkreutz

## What it is
An allegorical alchemical novella in seven "days" by Johann Valentin Andreae, composed (he later claimed) in his teens around 1605 and printed at Strasbourg in 1616 as the third of the foundational Rosicrucian texts. The narrative, in the first person of Christian Rosenkreutz, recounts the protagonist's invitation to a royal alchemical wedding, his journey to a magnificent castle, his sorting through tests, the wedding ceremony of the King and Queen, their ritual death and alchemical resurrection through seven days of operations (corresponding to alchemical stages), Rosenkreutz's role in the work, and his return home. The text is by turns playful, satirical, devotional, and structurally precise — a layered allegory in which the alchemical operations stand simultaneously for spiritual transformation, Christ's death and resurrection, and the union of opposites within the soul. Andreae later called the *Fama* and *Confessio* a *ludibrium* but treated the *Chymische Hochzeit* differently — it is in form a literary allegory, not a manifesto.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Württemberg under Duke Johann Friedrich; the years immediately before the Thirty Years' War.
- **Religious milieu.** Lutheran orthodoxy with mystical and Paracelsian undercurrents. Andreae was a Lutheran pastor — eventually a senior court chaplain at Stuttgart — and his life-long theological project was *praxis pietatis* (practical Christian piety) and educational-social reform. The *Chymical Wedding* fits this trajectory as alchemy in service of inward Christian transformation.
- **Who was behind it.** Andreae himself, in the same Tübingen circle (with Tobias Hess and Christoph Besold) that produced the *Fama* and *Confessio*.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous early-modern circulation in German and (from 1690) English; through the 18th century the *Chymische Hochzeit* became foundational for the initiatory structures of higher-degree Freemasonry and Rosicrucian-aligned orders. In the 19th–20th centuries, the work was read literarily (Goethe, Hesse) and psychologically (Jung).

## Themes and motifs
- [[alchemical-marriage]] — the *coniunctio oppositorum*: the union of King and Queen, Sun and Moon, sulphur and mercury, masculine and feminine — central image of alchemical allegory.
- [[initiation]] — a seven-stage initiatory journey; the structure influences later Masonic and esoteric initiations.
- [[microcosm-macrocosm]] — the operations are simultaneously cosmic and psychic.
- [[ascent-of-the-soul]] — Rosenkreutz's journey is an ascent through tests to participation in the divine wedding.
- **Christ as alchemical King** — the implicit-explicit identification: the King who dies and rises is at once the alchemical substance and Christ; the alchemical work is christological.

## Disputes
- **Andreae's authorship.** Now firmly established; Andreae himself claimed the *Hochzeit* in his autobiography *Vita ab Ipso Conscripta*.
- **Andreae's later position.** Andreae sustained the *Hochzeit* as his own composition even when distancing himself from the *Fama* and *Confessio*; this is read by some as indicating that he meant the *Hochzeit* as serious religious allegory while the *Fama*/*Confessio* had been a youthful *ludibrium*.
- **Literal alchemical content.** Whether the work was meant to encode actual alchemical operations (cf. some 18th-c. and 19th-c. readings) or is a purely spiritual-allegorical narrative (current scholarly consensus, following Brecht and Edighoffer) is debated.
