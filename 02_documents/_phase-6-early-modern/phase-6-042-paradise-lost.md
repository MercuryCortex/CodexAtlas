# Paradise Lost

## Structure and argument

*Paradise Lost* is an epic poem in twelve books of blank verse (~10,565 lines), published in 1667 (first edition in ten books; revised to twelve books in 1674). Composed blind — dictated from approximately 1658 onward to Milton's daughters and amanuenses — it is the most intellectually ambitious poem in the English language. Its stated purpose is announced in the opening invocation: "I may assert Eternal Providence, / And justify the ways of God to men" (*PL* 1.25–26). The argument runs: Satan and the rebel angels are cast out of Heaven after a failed War; Satan, consigned to Hell, resolves to corrupt God's newly created humans as an act of cosmic revenge; he traverses the primordial Chaos between Hell and the new creation; enters the Garden of Eden; tempts Eve through the serpent with the fruit of the Tree of Knowledge of Good and Evil; Eve eats and persuades Adam; both fall; the Archangel Michael expels them from Paradise but reveals the entire subsequent history of human salvation culminating in Christ's redemption. Paradise is lost and the promise of a paradise "within" — the interior life of virtue and faith — is offered as its substitute. The poem's cosmology is deliberately ambiguous: Copernican heliocentrism is in the poem (Raphael in Book 8 refuses to adjudicate between the two systems), embedding the contemporary astronomical revolution without resolving it.

## The War in Heaven and its deep roots

The War in Heaven (Books 5–6) — Satan's rebellion and the angelic battle ending in the rebel angels' three-day combat with the loyal angels, resolved by Christ's intervention and their expulsion through Chaos into Hell — traces to a chain of predecessors that makes it the most overdetermined narrative event in the poem.

**Hesiod's *Theogony* and Titanomachy** (~700 BCE — [[phase-2-009-hesiod-theogony-works-and-days]]): Zeus versus the Titans is structurally identical to Milton's War in Heaven. A younger divine order (the Olympians) defeats the older rebels (the Titans) and casts them into Tartarus, the pit beneath the earth. The Titans had occupied the cosmos; the Olympians defeat them and establish a new divine governance. The structural parallel is exact: rebellion by older-generation divinity, cosmic warfare, defeat of rebels, confinement in subterranean pit. Milton knew Hesiod directly in the Greek and drew on this structure explicitly.

**The Anzu myth** (Sumerian/Akkadian, ~2000 BCE): The divine eagle Anzu steals the Tablet of Destinies from Enlil, claiming supreme divine power over the cosmos. The god Ninurta (in the standard Babylonian version) is commissioned by the divine assembly to defeat Anzu and recover the Tablet. [[ninurta]] defeats the rebel and restores divine order. This is the oldest surviving "rebel steals divine prerogative → cosmic warfare → defeat → restoration of divine order" narrative. The specific innovation of the Anzu myth — that the rebel's act is an assault on divine governance itself (the Tablet of Destinies determines the fates of gods and humans) — is structurally identical to Satan's rebellion against God's supreme authority. The transmission path is: Anzu → Watchers traditions → Enochic angelology → Milton.

**Isaiah 14:12–15** ("How you have fallen from heaven, Helel ben Shahar!" — "shining one, son of dawn"): This passage was originally a political taunt against the king of Babylon or a mythological allusion to a Canaanite-Syrian morning-star deity. In the Latin Vulgate (Jerome, ~380 CE), *Helel* became *Lucifer* — "light-bearer" — and Tertullian, Origen, and Augustine read the passage as describing Satan's primordial fall. The **transmission chain of the Lucifer myth** spans 2,000 years: Babylonian political taunt (Isaiah 14, ~700 BCE) → Patristic identification with Satan's fall (Origen *De Principiis* 1.5.5, ~230 CE; Augustine *City of God* 11.15, ~410 CE) → Dante's *Inferno* (Lucifer at the frozen bottom of Hell, ~1320 CE) → Milton's Satan in full epic elaboration (1667 CE). This chain is fully documented in [[lucifer]] and [[theme-satan-angra-mainyu-transfer]].

**1 Enoch chapters 6–16** (Watchers rebellion — [[phase-3-004-1-enoch]]): [[semyaza]] leads two hundred Watchers in an oath at Mount Hermon to descend to earth, take human wives, teach forbidden knowledge, and beget the Nephilim giants — a rebellion against divine order that pollutes the entire antediluvian world. God commissions Michael, Gabriel, Raphael, and Uriel to bind the rebels underground until the eschatological judgment. This is the direct literary precedent for Milton's Satan leading rebel angels in defiance of divine command. Milton knew 1 Enoch through the scholarship of Joseph Justus Scaliger (1540–1609), who established the Ethiopic transmission of the Enochic corpus, and through the patristic citations that kept the Watchers tradition visible. The structural debt is massive: Milton's Satan as leader of a rebellious angelic host, bound in a pit of fire, is the Watchers narrative transposed from second-temple apocalyptic to seventeenth-century epic. See [[watchers-and-fallen-angels]].

**Revelation 12** ("War broke out in heaven; Michael and his angels fought against the dragon"): This New Testament passage is Milton's most direct source for the War in Heaven as a narrative event — Michael versus the dragon-Satan, the defeat of the dragon, his casting down to earth. *Paradise Lost* Books 5–6 expand this compressed Revelation 12 passage into a three-day epic battle with Homeric conventions (individual combats, divine intervention, superhuman scale). The synthesis Milton achieves: Revelation 12 provides the plot, Homer provides the narrative form, Hesiod provides the structural template (Titanomachy), and the Watchers tradition provides the theological stakes (angelic rebellion as origin of evil).

## Satan as Prometheus

Milton's Satan in Books 1–2 is the most compelling character in the poem — defiant, magnificent, rhetorically powerful: "Better to reign in Hell than serve in Heaven" (1.263), "The mind is its own place, and in itself / Can make a Heaven of Hell, a Hell of Heaven" (1.254–255). This is not accidental. Milton knew Greek tragedy and philosophy to the same depth as the Hebrew tradition, and the Promethean template is structurally present throughout Satan's characterization.

**Prometheus in Aeschylus's *Prometheus Bound*** (~460 BCE): The chained Titan who stole divine fire for humanity, defiant against Zeus's tyranny, proclaims his foreknowledge of Zeus's downfall and refuses submission: "I know I am more wise than he who judged me thus." The structural DNA of Milton's Satan is here: rebel against the supreme power, cosmic punishment (chaining/confinement), heroic defiance of suffering, rhetorical self-justification. [[prometheus]] and Satan are not identical — Prometheus gives fire to benefit humanity while Satan seeks to destroy it — but the formal template of the heroic divine rebel is the same. Milton builds his Satan by placing this Promethean formal template in the service of a being whose actual motivation (injured pride, narcissism, hatred) cannot sustain the heroic posture. The gap between Satan's rhetoric and his actual moral condition is the poem's central irony.

**The Romantic reception proves the identification was immediately legible:** Blake (*The Marriage of Heaven and Hell*, 1793) declared Milton was "of the Devil's party without knowing it" — Satan's energy is Promethean creative power, God's law is Urizen's repressive tyranny. Shelley (*Prometheus Unbound*, 1820) wrote his Prometheus as an explicit response to Milton's Satan: "The only imaginary being resembling in any degree Prometheus, is Satan; and Prometheus is, in my judgment, a more poetical character than Satan, because, in addition to courage and majesty and firm and patient opposition to omnipotent force, he is susceptible of being described as exempt from the taints of ambition, envy, revenge, and a desire for personal aggrandizement." Mary Shelley's *Frankenstein* (1818) epigraphed from *Paradise Lost* and was subtitled *The Modern Prometheus*: the creature who reads *Paradise Lost* in the forest and identifies with Adam-without-Eve and Satan-without-redemption is the intersection of both archetypes. The Prometheus-Satan identification was not imposed by later criticism — it was what Milton's immediate literary successors saw.

**The Zoroastrian background**: [[angra-mainyu-ahriman]] rebels against Ahura Mazda at the beginning of cosmic time and contaminates the good creation — the same cosmic-rebel-corrupts-paradise structure underlies Milton's Satan's corruption of Eden. The question is whether this is parallel (independent mythological convergence) or source (transmission). The documented answer, via [[theme-satan-angra-mainyu-transfer]]: the Zoroastrian cosmic dualism was a structural input into apocalyptic Judaism (via the Dead Sea Scrolls' Two Spirits tractate, the Qumran War Scroll's dualism of light and darkness), which fed into the Christian Satan narrative's development of Satan as an independent cosmic adversary, which Milton inherited. The shadow of Angra Mainyu falls across Satan through this documented chain.

## The Fall of Eve and its deep roots

The forbidden knowledge motif — Eve eating the fruit of the Tree of Knowledge of Good and Evil at the serpent's persuasion — participates in a Mesopotamian narrative tradition reaching back 3,000 years before Milton.

**The Adapa myth** (Akkadian, ~14th c. BCE): Adapa, a sage created by the god Ea, is summoned before the god Anu. Ea has warned him not to eat or drink what Anu offers, because (Ea claims) it will be the food of death. When Anu offers the bread and water of *life*, Adapa refuses — and loses immortality through obedience to a trickster god. The structure: a mortal, a divine prohibition about food, a divine being who counsels refusal, the loss of immortality. This is an *inverted* form of the Genesis/Milton fall narrative: in Adapa, the mortal loses immortality by *obeying* the counsel (not to eat); in Genesis/Milton, Eve loses paradise by *disobeying* the command (not to eat). The serpent in Genesis occupies structurally the same advisory position as Ea in Adapa — the being who tells the mortal what to do regarding the divine food. The same mythological furniture — serpent/counselor, prohibited divine food, loss of immortality — is being arranged and rearranged across 3,000 years of Near Eastern narrative.

**Gilgamesh losing the plant of immortality** ([[phase-1-004-gilgamesh-old-babylonian]], Tablet XI): Gilgamesh, having completed his heroic journey to the edge of the world, recovers the plant of immortality from the sea bottom. A serpent steals it while he sleeps and sheds its skin, thereby gaining the renewal of youth that was meant for humanity. Gilgamesh weeps. The serpent, the loss of immortality, the proximity of immortality that is then lost — this is the same narrative core as the Eden story. The serpent that robs humanity of immortality in the Gilgamesh epic is the same serpent that destroys humanity's paradisial state in Genesis 3 and Milton's Books 9–10, differently oriented: from thief (Gilgamesh) to tempter-counsel (Genesis/Milton). The *Epic of Gilgamesh* predates Genesis by centuries and shares the same Mesopotamian literary culture that produced the J-source.

**The Prometheus parallel** (Hesiod's *Works and Days*, ~700 BCE — [[phase-2-009-hesiod-theogony-works-and-days]]): In Hesiod, humans gain divine knowledge (fire, the means of civilization) through a cosmic transgression by Prometheus, and as a consequence Pandora opens the jar releasing evils into the world and the Golden Age — humanity's paradisial primordial state — is permanently lost. The structure: transgression involving divine knowledge → divine knowledge gained → paradise (golden age) lost → present state of suffering. This parallels the Genesis fall narrative with the roles distributed differently — Prometheus is the transgressor where Eve is the transgressor; divine knowledge is fire where divine knowledge is the fruit of the tree; the loss of the golden age parallels the loss of Eden. The two traditions are parallel rather than derivatively linked, reflecting the shared mythological inheritance of the Near Eastern-Greek cultural continuum. See [[fall-of-humanity]].

**The Gnostic re-reading of Eve**: In Valentinian and Sethian Gnosticism (the *Apocryphon of John*, the *Hypostasis of the Archons*), the Demiurge-creator forbids Adam and Eve from eating the fruit because knowledge would reveal their true divine pneumatic nature and liberate them from his control. In this reading, the serpent is not Satan but the emissary of Sophia or the true transcendent God, bringing the liberation of gnosis. The expulsion from Eden is not punishment for sin but the demiurge's punishment for awakening. Milton explicitly rejects this reading — his God is the good creator, his Satan is a tempter, not a liberator — but the Gnostic subtext was part of the theological conversation Milton inhabited. See [[gnosis-as-salvation]] and [[sophia-pleroma]].

## Milton's Chaos and primordial cosmogony

Satan's traversal of Chaos (Books 1–2) — the vast, dark, formless realm between Hell and the new creation — deploys a concept with deep cross-tradition roots in the problem of what existed before divine creation.

**Hesiod's Chaos** (*Theogony* 116: "In the beginning there was Chaos") is the yawning void before creation — the first thing Hesiod names in the cosmogony. Milton's Chaos is a conscious engagement with this tradition: the primordial state before God's creative ordering.

**Egyptian Nun** — the primordial waters of chaos from which the first mound (*benben*) rises in Egyptian cosmogony — is the same theological problem: the pre-creation state as dark, formless water. Egyptian cosmogony influenced Hellenistic Jewish and Greek cosmogony through Alexandria.

**Tiamat and Apsu** in the *Enuma Elish* (Babylonian — [[phase-1-008-enuma-elish]]): the primordial salt-water (Tiamat) and fresh-water (Apsu) chaos before creation, whose mingling generates the first gods. Marduk's defeat of Tiamat and creation of the ordered cosmos from her body is the archetypal combat-myth cosmogony. Milton's Chaos is the un-created void that persists even after God's act of creation — a theologically complicated space that is neither Hell nor Heaven nor the created world.

**Neoplatonic Matter** (Plotinus, *Enneads*): absolute matter as the privation of all form, the dark receptacle furthest from the One — the Neoplatonic version of the primordial chaos problem. Milton's engagement with Neoplatonism through the Cambridge Platonists and through Ficino's translations shapes his treatment of Chaos as a liminal space between non-being and being.

All of these traditions are working through the same theological problem: what was the state before ordered creation, and what is its ontological status? Milton's Chaos is a deliberately unstable answer — it is neither God's creation nor entirely independent of God — and its instability is philosophically inherited from the entire tradition.

## Celestial hierarchy and ancient roots

Milton's angels are organized in the standard hierarchy drawn from **Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite** (*Celestial Hierarchy*, ~500 CE — [[phase-4-030-pseudo-dionysius]]): Seraphim, Cherubim, Thrones, Dominions, Virtues, Powers, Principalities, Archangels, Angels. Pseudo-Dionysius claimed to be Dionysius the Areopagite (Paul's Athenian convert, Acts 17:34), giving his Neoplatonic angelology apostolic authority. The "apostolic" authority is fictional — the text is a ~500 CE Neoplatonizing synthesis — but it was not exposed until Laurentius Valla and Erasmus in the 15th–16th centuries, and even after the exposure Milton uses the hierarchy because it was the established Christian framework.

The genealogy of this hierarchy: Pseudo-Dionysius's nine angelic orders derive from Colossians 1:16 and Ephesians 6:12 (Pauline "cosmic powers" — *thronoi*, *kyriotetes*, *archai*, *exousiai*) filtered through Proclus's Neoplatonic emanation-theory. The Pauline cosmic powers themselves derive structurally from the **Zoroastrian Amesha Spentas** — the six divine emanations of Ahura Mazda (Vohu Manah, Asha Vahishta, Khshathra Vairya, Spenta Armaiti, Haurvatat, Ameretat) — each embodying an aspect of the divine perfection and intermediating between Ahura Mazda and the created world. This Zoroastrian angelic-emanation structure was one input into the Second Temple Jewish angelology (the archangel system, the divine council) which Paul's letters reflect. The Mesopotamian divine assembly — the Anunnaki and Igigi — is the even earlier layer: a hierarchical heavenly court of ranked divine beings attending the chief deity.

Milton's celestial hierarchy therefore has a genealogy: Mesopotamian divine assembly → Zoroastrian Amesha Spentas → Second Temple Jewish archangels → Pauline cosmic powers → Pseudo-Dionysius's nine orders → Milton's *Paradise Lost*. Each transmission filtered and theologized the prior layer. See [[theme-satan-angra-mainyu-transfer]] and [[watchers-and-fallen-angels]].
