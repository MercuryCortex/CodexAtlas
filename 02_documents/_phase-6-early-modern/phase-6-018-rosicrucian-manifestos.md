# Fama Fraternitatis + Confessio Fraternitatis

## What it is
The two German Rosicrucian manifestos — *Fama Fraternitatis Rosae Crucis* ("Report of the Brotherhood of the Rosy Cross," published Kassel 1614, in circulation in manuscript from ~1610) and *Confessio Fraternitatis Rosae Crucis* ("Confession of the Brotherhood," Kassel 1615) — that announce the existence of a secret Christian-Hermetic brotherhood founded by one Christian Rosenkreutz (1378–1484), call for a "universal reformation" of arts and sciences, and invite the learned of Europe to make themselves known to the Brotherhood. The *Fama* narrates Rosenkreutz's pseudo-biography (born in Germany, traveled to Damascus and Fez and Egypt to receive Arabic-Egyptian wisdom, founded the Brotherhood at his return, his tomb rediscovered 120 years after his death with miraculous inscriptions and an undecaying body) and lists the Brotherhood's six "axioms." The *Confessio* presents the doctrinal program: a Lutheran but mystically-saturated Christianity, Paracelsian medicine, Hermetic-Kabbalist natural philosophy, expectation of an imminent providential reformation. The manifestos sparked one of the great pamphlet wars of the early seventeenth century — hundreds of responses both in support and in alarm — though no member of the Brotherhood ever surfaced in answer.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Pre-Thirty Years' War Württemberg and Hesse-Kassel — Protestant principalities ruled by Lutheran (Württemberg) and Calvinist (Hesse-Kassel under Moritz the Learned) princes with active intellectual and esoteric interests. Frederick V of the Palatinate's brief Bohemian kingship (1619–1620) and the Protestant cause it represented form the political horizon Yates identifies as central.
- **Religious milieu.** Lutheran orthodoxy crystallizing into post-*Concordia* Protestant scholasticism; mystical-Spiritualist currents (Schwenckfeld, Weigel, Böhme) and Paracelsianism providing an alternative. The manifestos express a hope for a "third stage" of reformation beyond Luther's and Calvin's that would integrate the natural-philosophical and mystical dimensions.
- **Who was behind it.** Consensus authorship is the Tübingen circle around Johann Valentin Andreae (the youngest member), Tobias Hess, and Christoph Besold. Andreae later claimed *The Chymical Wedding* (1616) as his own youthful work but called the *Fama* a *ludibrium* (a hoax / play); the consensus is that the Tübingen circle authored all three texts, with Andreae and the older Hess as principal hands.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Manuscript circulation 1610–1614 (Adam Haslmayr printed a response to the *Fama* manuscript in 1612, before the *Fama*'s own publication); print explosion 1614–1622. The Rosicrucian "current" was carried through the 17th century by readers such as Michael Maier, Robert Fludd, and the various proto-Masonic and esoteric currents.

## Themes and motifs
- [[prisca-theologia]] — Christian Rosenkreutz's traveled education (Damascus, Fez, Egypt) inscribes the prisca-theologia trajectory of ancient wisdom-from-the-East into a Protestant Christian frame.
- **Universal reformation** — *Universalreformation der ganzen weiten Welt*; not just confessional reform but an integrated philosophical-medical-spiritual transformation.
- [[microcosm-macrocosm]] — Paracelsian framework: the natural-magical knowledge the Brotherhood possesses is grounded in the macro-microcosm correspondence.
- **Secret brotherhood / invisible college** — the trope of the hidden adept fellowship, taken over by later esoteric and Masonic movements.
- **Apocalyptic providential history** — the conviction that the age is on the threshold of a divinely-arranged transformation.

## Disputes
- **Did the Brotherhood exist?** Modern consensus (Gilly, van Dülmen): no — the Brotherhood is a literary construction, possibly a *ludibrium* in Andreae's later terminology, designed to provoke a Reformation response. The manifestos generated the movement they purported to describe.
- **The Yates thesis on Frederick V.** Yates (1972) made the manifestos the propaganda of a Protestant-esoteric political project surrounding Frederick V; Gilly and most current scholarship treat this as overdrawn, locating the manifestos more narrowly in Tübingen-Württemberg-Hesse esoteric circles.
- **Andreae's later disavowals.** Andreae later disowned the *Fama* and *Confessio* as youthful indiscretions and called the whole enterprise a *ludibrium*; how seriously to take this is debated.
