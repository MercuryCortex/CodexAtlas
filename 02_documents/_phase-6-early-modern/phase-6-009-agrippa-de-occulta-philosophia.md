# De Occulta Philosophia Libri Tres

## What it is
Agrippa's three-book systematic synthesis of Renaissance magic, organized around the Pichian three-worlds schema: Book I treats *natural magic* (the elemental and natural world — herbs, stones, animals, signatures, sympathies, antipathies); Book II treats *celestial magic* (number, music, planetary correspondences, talismans, astrology); Book III treats *ceremonial magic* (the intelligible/angelic world — divine names, Kabbalistic *gematria* and *notarikon*, the Sefirot, the seventy-two-fold name of God, angelic invocation, theurgy). The work is the most influential single compendium of Renaissance magic, drawing together Hermetic, Neoplatonist, Kabbalist, Pythagorean, Arabic-medieval, and folk-magical materials into one synthetic system. It is the principal channel by which Pichian and Reuchlinian Christian Kabbalah enter the broader European magical tradition.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Holy Roman Empire under Charles V; Agrippa's peripatetic biography (Cologne, Paris, Avignon, Italy, Lyon, Antwerp, Mechelen, Grenoble) included service to multiple princes, repeated falls from favor, and constant friction with church and university authorities.
- **Religious milieu.** Reformation-era confessional volatility; Agrippa is doctrinally Catholic but his magical-Kabbalist program is regarded with deep suspicion. His *De Vanitate Scientiarum* (1530), a paradoxical recantation of all human learning, did not protect him from charges of sorcery.
- **Who was behind it.** Agrippa personally; the 1510 manuscript was sent to Johannes Trithemius, who praised it but cautioned secrecy. Two decades of revision and expansion preceded the 1533 print.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous early-modern reception in Latin and (from 1651) in James Freake's English translation; foundational for 17th-century occult philosophy (Fludd, Kircher) and the 19th-century occult revival (Eliphas Lévi, Mathers, the Golden Dawn).

## Themes and motifs
- [[microcosm-macrocosm]] — the operating principle of all magic: the human as the meeting-point of the three worlds.
- **Three-worlds schema** — elemental / celestial / intellectual; an explicit Pichian inheritance.
- [[sefirot]] — Book III is essentially Reuchlin's Christian Kabbalah operationalized for magical practice.
- [[prisca-theologia]] — Hermes, Zoroaster, Orpheus, Pythagoras, Moses-and-the-prophets as the ancient sources of magical wisdom.
- **Signatures and correspondences** — every natural object corresponds to a celestial intelligence and through it to a divine name; the network is mapable.
- [[ascent-of-the-soul]] — the magus's program is the ordered ascent through the three worlds.

## Disputes
- **Did Agrippa later recant?** *De Vanitate Scientiarum et Artium* (1530) appears to condemn magic among the vanities of learning; Nauert argues this is paradoxical-rhetorical (a humanist *paradoxon*), not a serious recantation, since Agrippa proceeded to print *De Occulta Philosophia* three years later. The interpretive debate continues.
- **The *Fourth Book*.** The *Liber Quartus de Occulta Philosophia* (1559) is now consensus pseudo-Agrippa.
