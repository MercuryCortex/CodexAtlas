# De la Causa, Principio et Uno + De gli Eroici Furori

## What it is
Two of the six Italian-language philosophical dialogues that Giordano Bruno composed and printed at London in 1584–1585. *De la Causa, Principio et Uno* (Cause, Principle, and Unity) is a systematic metaphysical work in five dialogues arguing for an infinite, animate, unitary universe in which a single divine substance is at once the efficient cause, the formal principle, and the underlying matter of all things — a fully developed philosophical pantheism. *De gli Eroici Furori* (The Heroic Frenzies) is a sequence of emblematic-Petrarchan sonnets with allegorical commentaries, depicting the ascent of the philosophical soul toward the One through nine progressive *furori* (heroic erotic enthusiasms) modeled on Plotinian and Ficinian Platonic ascent. The pair together exhibits the two faces of Bruno's program: the metaphysical-cosmological argument and the mystical-erotic ascent.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Elizabethan London where Bruno was lodged with the French ambassador Castelnau (1583–1585); his English sojourn included an unhappy Oxford visit, contact with John Dee's circle, and discussions of Copernicus.
- **Religious milieu.** Bruno had fled the Dominican order in 1576 after dropping religious images from his cell; he had passed through Calvinist Geneva (where he was briefly excommunicated), Lutheran territories, and Catholic France with rolling theological troubles. In London under quasi-Protestant protection, he could publish what would have been suicidal in Catholic Italy.
- **Who was behind it.** Bruno personally, with the printer John Charlewood willing to apply false continental imprints to disguise the London origin.
- **Discovery / transmission.** The London dialogues circulated but were placed on the Catholic Index after Bruno's 1600 execution; the texts survived in protestant Europe. Bruno's metaphysical pantheism was rediscovered by German Idealism (Jacobi, Schelling, Hegel) and by the 19th-c. Italian Risorgimento (Bertrando Spaventa, the 1889 monument in Campo de' Fiori).

## Themes and motifs
- [[divine-immanence]] — Bruno's *De la Causa* is the most extreme Renaissance statement of God-as-immanent: there is no transcendent God outside the world, only one infinite substance with infinite attributes.
- **Infinite worlds** — Bruno extends Copernicus by removing the outer sphere of fixed stars and asserting an infinite cosmos with innumerable inhabited worlds (*De l'infinito universo et mondi*, 1584).
- [[hidden-god]] — the Brunian One is approachable only via the *furori* — heroic intellectual passion modeled on Platonic eros.
- [[ascent-of-the-soul]] — the nine *furori* track the philosophical-mystical ascent.
- [[microcosm-macrocosm]] — the human magus contemplating the infinite universe is the microcosm operating on the macrocosm.
- [[prisca-theologia]] — Hermes, Pythagoras, and the Egyptians as priscan witnesses to a primordial wisdom that Bruno's pantheism recovers.

## Disputes
- **The Yates thesis on Bruno.** Yates (1964) made Bruno a Hermetic magus first, a philosopher and proto-scientist second. Hilary Gatti (1999) and Saverio Ricci (2000) have argued for a more philosophically and scientifically serious Bruno who took Copernican astronomy at full strength. Most current scholarship sees the Hermetic and scientific commitments as integrated.
- **Reason for execution.** The Inquisition's list of charges against Bruno was wide — Antitrinitarianism, denial of Christ's divinity, the eternity of the world, the existence of innumerable worlds, Hermetic-magical claims, denial of the Eucharist. Which were decisive is debated; the surviving Roman trial records are incomplete (Cardinal Caetani's volume of the dossier is lost).
