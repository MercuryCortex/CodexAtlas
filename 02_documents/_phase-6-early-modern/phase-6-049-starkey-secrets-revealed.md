# Secrets Reveal'd / Introitus Apertus

## Overview

**George Starkey** (1628–1665) — born in Bermuda, educated at Harvard (class of 1646), died in London during the plague — was the most important practical alchemist of the 17th century. Writing under the pseudonym **Eirenaeus Philalethes** ("the Peaceful Lover of Truth"), his treatises became the most-read alchemical texts of the later 17th century and shaped the alchemical practices of the two figures who demolished the Hermetic framework he worked in: **Robert Boyle** and **Isaac Newton**.

## The Newton Connection — The Decisive Transmission

Newton's alchemical manuscripts (the Keynes MSS at King's College Cambridge, sold at the 1936 Sotheby's auction and only studied systematically by Dobbs from the 1970s) show Newton copying Philalethes texts **by hand**, extensively annotating them, and cross-referencing them against Sendivogius, Ripley, and the Musaeum Hermeticum. Dobbs identified Philalethes/Starkey as **Newton's most important single alchemical source**. Newton was working on alchemy at the same time he was developing mechanics and optics — the Newtonian physics of universal gravitation is contemporaneous with intensive Hermetic alchemical practice.

This is one of the vault's most important transmission facts: **the father of classical mechanics was a practicing alchemist who studied Starkey**.

## The Boyle Connection

Boyle's correspondence (published as *The Correspondence of Robert Boyle*, 6 vols.) documents his engagement with Starkey before and after Starkey's death. Starkey supplied Boyle with chemical preparations and communicated his theory of the *mercurial* principle in matter. Newman and Principe (2002) demonstrate that Boyle's turn to systematic experimental investigation of chemical phenomena was partly shaped by Starkey's operational practice. The Royal Society's early experimental chemistry inherits, without acknowledging it, the Philalethes-Starkey alchemical program.
