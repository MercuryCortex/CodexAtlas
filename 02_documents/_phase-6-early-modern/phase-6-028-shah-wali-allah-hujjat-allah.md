# Hujjat Allah al-Baligha (The Conclusive Argument from God)

## What it is
Shah Wali Allah's mature Arabic-language theological-juridical-Sufi summa, composed in Delhi c. 1735–1750, organized in two volumes: the first treats theology proper (the divine names, prophecy, the Sufi metaphysical doctrines of *waḥdat al-wujūd* and *waḥdat al-shuhūd*, eschatology, the structure of human nature), and the second treats the philosophical-sociological foundations of Islamic law (*shari'a*) — the rationales (*asrār*) of the commandments, the typology of human societies, the place of ritual in human formation, and a systematic exposition of *fiqh*. The work is the most influential intellectual product of 18th-century Sunni Islam, both as the founding text of the Indian Naqshbandi reform tradition (which through the Deobandi movement reaches modern South Asian Islam) and as one of the earliest systematic attempts to ground Islamic law in a rational-anthropological framework that anticipates 19th–20th-century Islamic reformist discourse.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Mughal India in steep decline after Aurangzeb's death (1707), Nadir Shah's 1739 sack of Delhi, Maratha incursions, and the East India Company's gathering ascendancy. Shah Wali Allah's response to political collapse was religious-intellectual reform: a return to hadith study (he had performed Hajj 1731–1732 and studied with leading Medinese hadith scholars, especially Abu Tahir al-Kurani), a re-grounding of Sunni jurisprudence in a non-sectarian rational framework that drew on all four madhhabs, and a continuation of the Naqshbandi *mujaddidi* (renewer) Sufi tradition founded by Ahmad Sirhindi.
- **Religious milieu.** Sunni Indian Islam was contested between competing Sufi tariqas (Chishti, Naqshbandi, Suhrawardi, Qadiri), Shi'i communities, the residue of Akbar's Din-i Ilahi syncretism, and Hindu majoritarian context. Shah Wali Allah's Naqshbandi-Mujaddidi affiliation oriented him toward strict Sunni orthodoxy combined with sophisticated Sufi metaphysics; his synthesis of Ibn 'Arabi's *waḥdat al-wujūd* with Sirhindi's *waḥdat al-shuhūd* is one of the work's distinctive moves.
- **Who was behind it.** Shah Wali Allah personally; his father Shah Abd al-Rahim had founded the Madrasa-i Rahimiyya in Delhi, which Wali Allah inherited and transformed; his four sons (especially Shah Abd al-Aziz) carried the tradition.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous Indian-Sunni reform transmission; the Deoband seminary (founded 1867 by Muhammad Qasim Nanautavi and Rashid Ahmad Gangohi, both descended intellectually from Shah Wali Allah's school) became the principal institutional vehicle in modern times. Pakistani Islamic political thought (Mawdudi's Jama'at-i Islami) draws partly on Shah Wali Allah's anthropological-political theology.

## Themes and motifs
- **Rationales of the law (*asrār al-shari'a*)** — Shah Wali Allah's distinctive project: every commandment of the *shari'a* has a discernable purpose grounded in human nature and social well-being. The framework anticipates 19th-c. reformist appeals to *maslaha* (public benefit) and 20th-c. *maqasid al-shari'a* discourse.
- **Typology of human societies** — Shah Wali Allah proposes four stages of human social development (*irtifaqat*), each requiring different ritual-juridical responses; an early sociological framework for Islamic law.
- ***Waḥdat al-wujūd* and *waḥdat al-shuhūd*** — synthesis of Ibn 'Arabi's metaphysical "oneness of being" with Sirhindi's epistemological "oneness of witnessing"; a sophisticated mediation between two earlier Sufi metaphysical positions.
- **Universal prophethood / particular prophethood** — Shah Wali Allah's account of how divine guidance reaches all peoples through prophets, with Muhammad as the universal seal.
- [[ethical-monotheism]] — the *shari'a* as the divinely revealed ethical regime for human formation.

- → Modern Indian-Pakistani Islamic reform — Sayyid Ahmad Khan, Muhammad Iqbal, Abul A'la Maududi — *direct-influence*: each engages Shah Wali Allah explicitly.

## Disputes
- **Reformist or traditionalist?** Modern reformers and modern traditionalists both claim Shah Wali Allah; he is genuinely both — methodologically innovative on the *asrār* of the law while doctrinally committed to traditional Sunni orthodoxy and Naqshbandi Sufism.
- **Relation to Wahhabi reform.** Shah Wali Allah was a contemporary of Muhammad ibn 'Abd al-Wahhab (1703–1792); both were hadith-centered reformers but their projects differ substantially (Shah Wali Allah preserves Sufi metaphysics and Sunni jurisprudential pluralism that Wahhabism rejects).
