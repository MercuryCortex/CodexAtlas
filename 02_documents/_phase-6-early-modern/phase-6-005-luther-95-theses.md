# 95 Theses (Disputatio pro declaratione virtutis indulgentiarum)

## What it is
A Latin disputation of 95 propositions composed by Augustinian friar and Wittenberg theology professor Martin Luther on 31 October 1517, attacking the sale of indulgences — particularly the campaign that Albrecht of Mainz had authorized for sales-agent Johann Tetzel in Saxony, the proceeds of which were partly destined for the rebuilding of St. Peter's in Rome. The Theses argue (1) that true penance is the Christian's lifelong turning to God, not a paid transaction; (2) that the Pope cannot remit divine penalty, only ecclesiastical; (3) that the indulgence-traffic teaches a false security; (4) that the Pope's wealth ought to fund the rebuilding from his own purse rather than impoverished Germans'. Although intended as a scholastic disputation in Latin, the *95 Theses* became — when printed and translated — the trigger document of the European Reformation.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Holy Roman Empire under Maximilian I; Saxony under Frederick the Wise, Luther's patron. The Empire was a patchwork of territorial princes increasingly resentful of papal fiscal extraction.
- **Religious milieu.** Late-medieval Catholic Christianity with widespread popular complaints about clerical wealth, indulgence-trafficking, and the deferred Fifth Lateran Council reform program. The Albrecht-Tetzel campaign of 1517 was unusually brazen and bound up with Albrecht's own fiscal arrangements with the Fuggers.
- **Who was behind it.** Luther himself — but riding a swell of pre-existing Augustinian Observant reform energy, German humanist anti-Roman sentiment (Erasmus, Reuchlin), and lay devotional movements (the *devotio moderna*).
- **Discovery / transmission.** Sent privately to Archbishop Albrecht on 31 October 1517 with a covering letter; placarded (per tradition) on the door of the Wittenberg Castle Church the same day (the dating of the *posting* event is debated by historians but the *composition* date is firm). Printed at Leipzig and Nuremberg by December 1517; in German translation by early 1518; spreading via the new printing press faster than ecclesiastical machinery could respond.

## Themes and motifs
- [[justification-by-faith]] — implicit; not yet fully articulated but the underlying soteriology that drives the indulgence critique.
- **Penance / metanoia** — Luther reads Mt 4:17's "do penance" (Vulgate *paenitentiam agite*) as the Greek *metanoeite* — lifelong inward turning, not a sacramental transaction (Thesis 1).
- **Papal authority** — the Pope can remit only what he has imposed, not divine penalty (Theses 5–6, 20–22).
- **The treasury of merits** — the doctrinal foundation of indulgences is critiqued (Theses 56–62): the true treasure of the Church is the Gospel.
- **Pastoral concern** — Theses 41–55 protest that indulgence-preaching distracts from preaching the cross.

## Disputes
- **Did Luther post them on the door?** The *posting* itself is attested only later (Melanchthon, 1546). The composition and dispatch to Albrecht are firm. Most historians treat the posting as plausible (the door of the Castle Church functioned as a university notice-board) but not narratively decisive.
- **Were the 95 Theses revolutionary in 1517?** Brecht and Roper emphasize that Luther's stance in October 1517 was still that of a reform-minded Augustinian within the Catholic Church; the rupture deepened over 1518–1521 (Leipzig Disputation 1519; Diet of Worms 1521).
