# The Constitutions of the Free-Masons (1723)

## What it is
The founding charter of modern speculative Freemasonry, compiled by the Presbyterian minister James Anderson under commission from the Grand Lodge of London and Westminster (founded 1717) and printed at London in January 1723. The volume contains four principal parts: (1) a *history* of Masonry tracing the craft back through medieval operative guilds to Solomon's Temple and ultimately to Adam, drawing on the older "Old Charges" manuscripts of operative masonry; (2) the *Charges of a Free-Mason* — six articles of behavior (1. of God and Religion; 2. of the Civil Magistrate; 3. of Lodges; 4. of Masters, Wardens, Fellows, and Apprentices; 5. of the Management of the Craft in working; 6. of Behavior) that constitute the ethical-juridical framework of the lodge; (3) the *General Regulations* governing Grand Lodge and lodge operation; and (4) songs. The Charges' first article — that a Mason must "obey the moral law" and "be of that Religion in which all Men agree, leaving their particular Opinions to themselves" — is the textual foundation of the Masonic *non-sectarian deism* that has defined the institution: a Mason worships the Great Architect of the Universe (G.A.O.T.U.) under whatever confessional name his particular religion provides. The 1723 *Constitutions* marks the boundary between medieval operative stonemason guilds and modern speculative Freemasonry as a fraternal-philosophical institution.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Hanoverian England under George I; the post-1688 Protestant succession had stabilized but Jacobite challenges (1715, 1745) and Whig-Tory tensions remained alive. Many of the early Grand Lodge officers were Whig Hanoverian sympathizers and Newtonian natural philosophers; the institution's deism and non-sectarianism reflect post-1688 English Latitudinarian Protestantism.
- **Religious milieu.** The Newtonian-Latitudinarian moment: natural theology, religious toleration (within Protestantism; Catholics and non-Christians remained civilly disadvantaged), and the gentlemanly clubbability of the early Enlightenment. Anderson was a Presbyterian; Desaguliers (Grand Master 1719) was a Huguenot refugee, Anglican priest, and Newton's experimental demonstrator at the Royal Society. The lodges of the 1720s drew their members from this overlap of clergy, natural philosophers, antiquarians, and gentlemen.
- **Who was behind it.** Anderson as compiler-author; the Grand Lodge as commissioning body; Desaguliers as the intellectual-scientific patron; older operative lodges (especially in Scotland — Stevenson 1988 traces the operative-to-speculative transition to 17th-c. Scottish lodges including the Lodge of Edinburgh, with the famous 1641 reception of Sir Robert Moray) as the institutional ancestor.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous Masonic transmission worldwide; the 1738 second edition substantially revised the history (more legendary, less verifiable); subsequent editions further. The 1723 edition is the textual canonical source.

## Themes and motifs
- **Great Architect of the Universe** — non-sectarian monotheist deity invoked as the source of moral order; Anderson's deistic-Latitudinarian solution to the problem of confessional diversity within a single fraternal institution.
- [[prisca-theologia]] — the *History* part traces Masonry through Solomon's Temple, the ancient world, Pythagoras, and the medieval cathedral-builders into the 18th century; the Renaissance prisca-theologia framework is here operationalized for an institution.
- [[universal-reformation]] — the Rosicrucian-inflected universal-reformation impulse, now institutionalized in fraternal-Enlightenment form.
- [[ethical-monotheism]] — the Charges privilege ethical observance over confessional specificity.
- **Initiatory degrees** — the *Constitutions* governs the three "Craft" degrees (Entered Apprentice, Fellowcraft, Master Mason); the higher-degree systems (Royal Arch, Scottish Rite, Rectified Scottish Rite, etc.) develop later in the 18th century, drawing on Rosicrucian and other esoteric materials.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-6-018-rosicrucian-manifestos]] — *indirect-influence*: Yates' contested thesis links the Rosicrucian impulse of 1614 to the speculative-Masonic emergence of 1717. Some specifics are disputed; Stevenson (1988) emphasizes the Scottish operative continuity over a Rosicrucian transmission.
- → [[phase-6-021-andreae-chymical-wedding]] — *indirect-influence*: the initiatory-allegorical materials of Rosicrucian literature were absorbed into the higher-degree systems of late-18th-c. Masonry.
- → Newtonian natural theology (Phase 6/7 boundary) — *direct-influence*: Desaguliers' Newtonian framework is part of the cultural matrix.
- → 18th-c. continental Enlightenment (Voltaire, Lessing, Mozart — Phase 6 late / Phase 7) — *direct-influence*: Freemasonry was the principal organizational vehicle of Enlightenment sociability across confessional and political lines.
- → American founding — *direct-influence*: Washington, Franklin, Hancock, Revere, and many of the Constitutional framers were Freemasons.
- → 19th-c. esoteric and political secret societies — *direct-descendants*: from the Carbonari to the Theosophical Society's organizational forms, Masonic structures provide a template.

## Disputes
- **Origins of speculative Freemasonry.** Three competing accounts: (a) descended from medieval operative guilds (the traditional view, with Stevenson's substantial Scottish-operative refinement); (b) descended from Rosicrucian initiatory currents (Yates, contested); (c) constructed in 1717 essentially de novo from the Old Charges plus the gentlemanly clubbability of the period (the minimalist Enlightenment-sociability reading, e.g., Jacob). Current consensus combines (a) and (c) with limited (b).
- **Anderson's *History*.** The 1723 *History* contains substantial legendary material (Adam as the first Mason); the 1738 second edition is even more so. Modern Masonic scholarship distinguishes "traditional history" (a constituent narrative of the institution) from historical fact.
