# Mirabai's Bhajans

## What it is
A large and fluid corpus of devotional songs (*padas* / *bhajans*) in mixed North Indian vernacular (Old Rajasthani, Braj, Old Gujarati, Hindi) attributed to the 16th-century Rajput princess and Krishna-devotee Mirabai. The songs are in the *viraha* mode of the female devotee yearning for her divine Beloved Krishna (typically addressed as Giridhar Gopal — "the cowherd who lifted Mount Govardhan"). They express defiance of social-marital constraint (Mirabai's biography narrates her resistance to her in-laws' Rajput court orthodoxy at Chittor), abandonment of caste-and-rank for direct devotion, and the *sant* (saint-poet) ideal of bhakti as superior to ritual and learning. The Mirabai corpus is one of the central voices of the North Indian *bhakti sant* tradition.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Sisodia Rajput kingdom of Mewar in the mid-16th century — the period of Maharana Sanga, Vikramaditya, and Udai Singh II. Mirabai's traditional biography places her marriage to the heir Bhojraj (son of Sanga); her devotion to Krishna and her refusal of expected courtly observance produce a sustained conflict with her in-laws, culminating in her departure for Vrindavan and finally Dwarka.
- **Religious milieu.** North Indian devotional renaissance ~1500–1700. The *Sant* tradition (Kabir, Ravidas, Dadu) emphasizes formless devotion across caste lines; the *Krishna-bhakti* tradition (Vallabhacharya's Pushti Marg, the Gaudiya movement of Chaitanya, the Radhavallabh school) emphasizes embodied devotion to Krishna with Radha. Mirabai sits at the intersection: her Krishna is unambiguously the personal Lord, but her social posture is *sant*-like (caste-defiant, ritual-defiant).
- **Who was behind it.** The historical Mirabai (c. 1498–c. 1547), but the corpus was substantially extended by anonymous singers and scribes over the following three centuries, in part because Mira's voice became a model for women's devotional voice across the North Indian vernacular tradition.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Oral transmission supplemented by manuscripts from the late 17th century onward; substantial selections in the Dadu-panthi anthologies, in Rajasthani manuscript collections, and in colonial-era popular printings. The Mira corpus has been at the center of Hawley's scholarly project to disentangle historical-poet attributions from corpus-formation in the bhakti tradition.

## Themes and motifs
- [[bhakti-devotion]] — direct, embodied, ecstatic devotion to a chosen personal deity; the central form of pre-modern North Indian religious life.
- [[divine-feminine]] — Mirabai is a female voice, and the model of the female devotee yearning for the divine Beloved is one of the great types of medieval Hindu (and parallel Sufi) devotional poetry.
- [[soul-as-bride]] — Mirabai is "married to Giridhar" — Krishna is her husband in a register that displaces and supersedes her actual Rajput marriage.
- **Caste-defiance and gender-defiance** — Mirabai eats with the *sants*, drinks from a low-caste poet's cup, leaves the women's quarters to sing in public; the social register is critical, not just devotional.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[krishna-deity-vaishnava]] — *direct-devotion-to*: Krishna as Giridhar Gopal is the addressee of nearly all the songs.
- → Bhagavata Purana (Phase 5) — *direct-influence*: the cowherd Krishna of Vrindavan is the Krishna of the Bhagavata's Book X.
- → [[phase-6-020-chaitanya-charitamrita]] — *parallel*: Chaitanya's Gaudiya Vaishnavism (founded c. 1510) is the contemporary Bengali Krishna-bhakti movement; Mirabai is its Rajasthani cousin (tradition reports a meeting, but historicity is uncertain).
- → Sant tradition (Kabir, Ravidas, Dadu — Phase 5/6) — *shared-milieu*: caste-defiance and devotional minimalism.
- → [[phase-6-016-guru-granth-sahib]] — *parallel*: the Adi Granth incorporates Hindu and Muslim bhakti poets but does not include Mirabai's compositions; the omission is itself studied.

## Disputes
- **The attribution problem.** Hawley (2005) and earlier critics (Parashuram Chaturvedi) have argued that the bulk of the popular Mirabai corpus is post-Mira accretion; the historical Mirabai's actual compositions may be a small subset.
- **Historical biography.** The 19th-century Mira biography is heavily hagiographic; the firmest historical data are sparse.
