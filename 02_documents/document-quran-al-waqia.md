# Sūrat al-Wāqiʿa (Quran 56 — The Inevitable)

## What it is
A powerful Meccan sura on the Hour — "the Inevitable" — when the earth is shaken and the mountains crumble to scattered dust, and humankind is sorted into three ranks: the **foremost** (al-sābiqūn), brought near to God; the **companions of the right**, in gardens of ease; and the **companions of the left**, in scalding wind and black smoke, fed from the bitter tree of Zaqqūm. The sura then dares the deniers: you yourselves do not create the seed you sow, the water you drink, or the fire you kindle — so who raises you? It closes on the soul at the throat of a dying man, when nothing can hold it back.

## Primary text

### Quran 56:7–11
*Trans. Abdel Haleem (2004)*
> You will be sorted into three classes. Those on the Right — what people they are! Those on the Left — what people they are! And those in front — ahead indeed! They are the ones brought nearest to God.

### Quran 56:63–64
*Trans. Abdel Haleem (2004)*
> Consider the seeds you sow in the ground — is it you who make them grow or We?

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** Meccan; an early, urgent statement of resurrection and judgment.
- **Tradition.** The three ranks became a standard frame for Islamic eschatology and spiritual hierarchy.
- **Discovery / transmission.** A hadith commends reciting al-Wāqiʿa for sustenance, making it a sura of daily devotion.

## Themes and motifs
- The Hour — cosmos undone, certainty arrived.
- Three ranks — the foremost, the right, the left.
- The argument from providence — you grow nothing, kindle nothing, of yourselves.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **The sorting of humankind at the end.** al-Wāqiʿa stages the Last Day as a great triage: the whole human race divided — the foremost near God, the right hand in gardens, the left hand in fire (56:7–56). The motif of an end-time *separation* into the saved and the damned is one of the deep currents of apocalyptic religion. Its earliest unambiguous scriptural form is Daniel 12:2 — the dead waking "some to everlasting life, some to shame" — which crystallized in the Second Temple period and flows into the Gospel image of the shepherd dividing the sheep from the goats (Matthew 25:31–46), the Zoroastrian judgment at the Bridge of the Separator (the Chinvat), and the Egyptian weighing of the heart against Maʿat. The Qur'an's distinctive contribution is the *third* rank — the sābiqūn, "those brought near," above the merely righteous of the right hand — introducing a hierarchy of nearness to God that Sufi thought would develop into its whole map of spiritual ranks. The sura's closing argument (you neither grow the seed nor kindle the fire) makes resurrection a simple corollary of the creation you already depend on (Reynolds 2010 pp.261–270; McAuliffe 2006 pp.150–162; Nasr 2015 pp.1347–1361).
