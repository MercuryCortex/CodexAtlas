# Epistle to Philemon

## What it is
The shortest and most personal of Paul's undisputed letters — a single page appealing to a Christian slave-owner, Philemon, on behalf of his slave Onesimus, who had run away (or been sent), met Paul in prison, and become a believer. With great rhetorical delicacy Paul sends Onesimus back, asking Philemon to receive him "no longer as a slave but more than a slave, a beloved brother," and hinting unmistakably that he should be freed.

## Primary text

### Philemon 15–16
*Trans. NRSV*
> Perhaps this is the reason he was separated from you for a while, so that you might have him back forever, no longer as a slave but more than a slave, a beloved brother... both in the flesh and in the Lord.

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** A slave-holding Greco-Roman society in which Christian "brotherhood" cut awkwardly across legal status.
- **Authorship.** Undisputed Pauline; a genuine private letter preserved in the public canon.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Within the Pauline corpus.

## Themes and motifs
- Slavery and brotherhood — a slave reframed as "a beloved brother."
- Christian persuasion — Paul appeals "on the basis of love" rather than command.
- Reconciliation — the restoration of a broken household relationship "in the Lord."

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **The little letter at the center of the slavery debate.** Because Philemon is the New Testament's only sustained treatment of a single slave's case, it became a battleground in the 18th–19th-century arguments over abolition: defenders of slavery noted that Paul returned the slave to his master and did not condemn the institution, while abolitionists pointed to "no longer as a slave but... a beloved brother" (v. 16) as the gospel's quiet detonation of slavery's logic. A one-page private appeal thus carried outsized weight in one of history's great moral arguments (Fitzmyer 2000, AYB 34C pp.33–37; Barth & Blanke 2000, AYB 34B).
