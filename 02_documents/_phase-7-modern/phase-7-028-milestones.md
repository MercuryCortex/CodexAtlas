# Milestones

## What it is
Sayyid Qutb's 1964 manifesto, composed largely during his decade of imprisonment (1954–1964) under Egyptian President Gamal Abdel Nasser's regime. The book argues: (1) the modern world — including ostensibly Muslim societies that operate by human-made law rather than divine law — is in a state of *jahiliyyah* (pre-Islamic ignorance) analogous to the Arabia of Muhammad's time; (2) Muslims have a duty to establish *hakimiyyah* — divine sovereignty in legal and political life — by reviving Islam as a comprehensive political-religious system; (3) this revival requires a vanguard of true Muslims who will progressively transform society through *da'wah* (call) and, where necessary, *jihad* (struggle), interpreted in *Milestones* as including offensive armed action against systems of *jahiliyyah*. The book was used as evidence against Qutb in his 1966 retrial; he was executed by hanging on 29 August 1966.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Nasser's Egypt; the suppression of the Muslim Brotherhood after 1954; Qutb's imprisonment included documented torture. *Milestones* is partly a prison-radicalization document.
- **Religious milieu.** Earlier Islamist political theology (al-Banna 1928 founding Muslim Brotherhood; Maududi in Pakistan from 1941 with the Jamaat-e-Islami) had established the project of *political* Islam; *Milestones* gives it a militant theological vocabulary that proves consequential in the late 20th and early 21st centuries.
- **Who was behind it.** Qutb personally; written in installments and smuggled out of prison; collected and published 1964.
- **Discovery / transmission.** First edition 1964; rapid international circulation through the Muslim Brotherhood network; widely cited in late-20th-century Islamist literature; central to the intellectual genealogies of al-Zawahiri (Egyptian Islamic Jihad and later al-Qaeda) and through them of the 9/11 generation of militants. Mainstream Muslim Brotherhood publicly disavowed armed jihad in the 1970s; the Qutbist tendency continued in splinter movements (*Takfir wa'l-Hijra*, Egyptian Islamic Jihad, GIA, al-Qaeda, eventually ISIS).

## Themes and motifs
- [[jahiliyyah]] — Qutb's central theological concept, generalized from its original 7th-century Arabian usage to any society not governed by divine law.
- [[hakimiyyah]] — divine sovereignty in law; the positive counterpart to *jahiliyyah*.
- [[jihad-offensive]] — Qutb argues that *jihad* is not only defensive (the modernist-apologetic reading of Abduh, Rida) but legitimately offensive against systems of *jahiliyyah*. This reading is contested by most Sunni traditional jurists.
- **Vanguard ethic** — Qutb's vocabulary of a vanguard movement transforming society is structurally Leninist; the Egyptian context of the 1950s–60s (with strong Soviet-influenced Nasserism) makes this parallel less surprising than it might first appear.

## Connections (provisional)
- → al-Banna (Muslim Brotherhood founder, 1928) — *direct-influence*: Qutb joined the MB ~1953 and became its leading intellectual.
- → Maududi (*Four Basic Quranic Terms*, 1941+) — *direct-influence*: the term *jahiliyyah* in the generalized form Qutb uses is Maududian in origin.
- → al-Zawahiri / al-Qaeda — *direct-influence (downstream)*: al-Zawahiri's *Knights Under the Prophet's Banner* (2001) explicitly traces its theological lineage to Qutb.
- → [[tradition-islam]] (mainstream) — *polemic-against (by mainstream)*: most Sunni jurists and major Islamic institutions (Al-Azhar most notably) reject *Milestones*'s expanded *jahiliyyah*-and-jihad framework as deviating from classical fiqh; the work is *not* a mainstream Islamic text but a specifically Qutbist-Islamist one.

## Disputes
- **Theological status.** Whether *Milestones* is a defensible (if radical) reading of Islamic political theology or a substantial departure from classical Sunni jurisprudence. Mainstream classical-Sunni position: substantial departure. Qutbist position: necessary recovery from modernist accommodation.
- **Authorial development.** Qutb's earlier *Social Justice in Islam* (1949) is markedly different in tone — modernist, reformist, less militant. The shift to *Milestones* (1964) is sometimes attributed to his prison experience and torture (the Calvert 2010 biography develops this).
