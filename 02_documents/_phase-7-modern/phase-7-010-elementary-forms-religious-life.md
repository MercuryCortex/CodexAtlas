# The Elementary Forms of Religious Life

## What it is
Émile Durkheim's culminating sociological study, 1912, taking Aboriginal Australian totemism (as reported by Spencer and Gillen) as the case-study for what religion *is* across all human societies. Durkheim defines religion as "a unified system of beliefs and practices relative to sacred things — that is to say, things set apart and forbidden — beliefs and practices which unite into one single moral community called a Church, all those who adhere to them." The book argues: (a) the *sacred/profane* distinction is the universal core of religion; (b) the proper object of religious worship is not gods or supernatural beings but *society itself* in transfigured representation; (c) "collective effervescence" — the heightened emotional state of group ritual — is the empirical mechanism by which sacredness is generated and renewed; (d) the basic categories of human thought (space, time, cause, class, totality) are socially produced through religious classification — a sociological reworking of Kant.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Third Republic France; militant laicism (1905 separation of church and state); Durkheim is an Alsatian Jew who became the founding figure of French academic sociology (Sorbonne chair from 1902). The book is partly a secular-republican alternative to Catholic and to evolutionist-rationalist (Frazerian) understandings of religion.
- **Religious milieu.** The British evolutionist comparative school (Tylor, Frazer) was dominant; Durkheim's sociological functionalism is a rival framework competing for the explanation of religion's persistence in modern society.
- **Who was behind it.** Durkheim and his *Année sociologique* circle (Marcel Mauss especially); the book is in part the culmination of a school project.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Major impact on 20th-century sociology and anthropology (Radcliffe-Brown, Lévi-Strauss, Goffman, Geertz); the book is the foundational text of sociology of religion as a discipline.

## Themes and motifs
- [[sacred-and-profane]] — Durkheim's master dichotomy, taken up and reframed by Eliade.
- [[collective-effervescence]] — the mechanism by which the sacred is generated; Durkheim's concept remains alive in sociology of religion (revivals, sports, political rallies as secular collective-effervescence).
- [[totemism]] — the chosen case-study; Durkheim argues totemism is the *simplest* religion, hence reveals the mechanism most clearly. Lévi-Strauss (*Totemism*, 1962) later dismantles "totemism" as a coherent category.
- **Religion as society** — the controversial reductive claim: when worshippers worship a god, they are in fact (sociologically) worshipping their own collectivity in symbolic form.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-7-007-golden-bough]] — *parallel-development with method-rivalry*: Frazer (1890–1915) and Durkheim (1912) offer competing comprehensive theories of religion; Durkheim's wins the long sociological game, Frazer's dominates the literary-comparative reception.
- → [[phase-7-022-sacred-and-profane]] — *direct-influence*: Eliade adopts the sacred/profane dichotomy as title and master-frame while rejecting Durkheim's sociological-reductive reading in favor of a phenomenological one.
- → Weber's *Sociology of Religion* (1922, posthumous) — *parallel-development*: Weber's typology of religious authority (charismatic, traditional, rational-legal) and theory of religion-economy interaction is the competing classical-sociological framework.

## Disputes
- **Australian data adequacy.** Durkheim relied on Spencer-Gillen and Strehlow ethnographies that subsequent fieldwork has complicated. Whether his generalization from "simplest" religion to all religion holds.
- **Reductionism.** Whether his account reduces religion to sociology (= eliminative) or describes religion *as* a sociologically constituted phenomenon (= compatible with religious self-understanding). Both readings have defenders.
- **Totemism as category.** Lévi-Strauss (1962) argued that "totemism" is a heterogeneous category not a coherent religious type; this complicates Durkheim's case study.
