# Ofudesaki (Tip of the Divine Writing Brush)

**Ofudesaki** ("Tip of the Divine Writing Brush") — written by Nakayama Miki (1798–1887) in 1,711 waka-form verses across 17 parts between 1869 and 1882 — is the primary canonical scripture of Tenrikyo ("Religion of Divine Wisdom"), a Japanese new religion founded by Nakayama Miki following a series of divine possessions beginning in 1838. Nakayama Miki believed she was the vessel of *Oyagami* ("God the Parent"), and wrote the Ofudesaki as divine dictation — the tip of her brush moved by a divine hand, not her own will.

## Cross-tradition significance

**Female founder, female scribe, female divine voice**: Nakayama Miki is one of the clearest cases in world religious history of a woman founding a new religion through direct divine revelation, producing its canonical scripture, and building an institutional structure that survives her death. The parallel with other female prophetic founders — Ann Lee (Shakers), Mary Baker Eddy (Christian Science), Mirza Tahirih (early Bahai) — is one of the most under-studied patterns in 19th-century religious history. All arose within a 60-year window (1770–1840) across different continents.

**Healing theology and the *joyous life***: Tenrikyo theology centers on the *Joyous Life* (*yokigurashi*) — the idea that human suffering comes from spiritual "dust" (*hokori*) that obscures the original pure mind, and that ritual, service, and right living clear the dust and restore joyous health. The healing-as-spiritual-clearing model has structural parallels with Christian Science's metaphysical healing, Daoist *yangsheng* (nourishing life) practices, and Buddhist notions of the pure mind obscured by defilements. It is not influence but structural convergence.

**Tenrikyo as the model for Japanese new religions**: Tenrikyo is the oldest surviving member of the *shin shūkyō* ("new religion") category — the wave of Japanese new religious movements that began in the 1830s–1840s (including also Konkokyo, Kurozumikyo, and later Oomoto, Soka Gakkai, and dozens more). Tenrikyo's organizational model — charismatic founder → institutionalized church → global mission → headquarters city (Tenri, Japan, is effectively an entire city owned by the religion) — became the template.
