# The Sacred and the Profane

## What it is
Eliade's 1957 popular-introductory distillation of the *Patterns in Comparative Religion* methodology, written for Rowohlt's Deutsche Enzyklopädie series. Four chapters: (1) sacred space and making the world; (2) sacred time and myth; (3) the sacredness of nature and cosmic religion; (4) human existence and sanctified life. The book made the *hierophany*, *sacred-vs-profane*, *eternal-return*, and *cosmos-vs-chaos* vocabulary widely available to non-specialist readers; it functioned as Eliade's most-read text and the principal vehicle of his methodology in mid-20th-century North American religious studies, comparative literature, and theology curricula.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Cold War period; Eliade was newly Sewell Avery Distinguished Service Professor at the Chicago Divinity School (from 1957).
- **Religious milieu.** Religious studies as a discipline was being formed in the late 1950s; Eliade's Chicago program would become its leading North American institutional center.
- **Who was behind it.** Eliade alone.
- **Discovery / transmission.** German 1957 → English 1959 → standard introductory text for two generations.

## Themes and motifs
- [[sacred-and-profane]] — the dichotomy Eliade inherited from Durkheim and Otto and made his own.
- **Sacred space** — the center of the world (*axis mundi*), the founding of a temple, the sacralization of a city; archaic spatial ordering.
- **Sacred time** — ritual return to *illud tempus*, the time of origins; the religious calendar as a structure for participating in the cosmogonic.
- **The modern condition** — Eliade's recurring theme: modern secularized humanity has lost the experiential framework of the sacred but retains structurally analogous practices (work, the New Year, the home as cosmos-in-microcosm) in degraded form.
