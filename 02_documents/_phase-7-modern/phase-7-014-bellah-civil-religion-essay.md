# Civil Religion in America (Bellah)

## What it is
**"Civil Religion in America"** is Robert N. Bellah's landmark 1967 essay (published in *Daedalus*), which argued that, alongside its many churches, the United States possesses a genuine **civil religion**: a coherent set of beliefs, symbols, and rituals — distinct from any denomination yet genuinely religious — that sacralize the nation itself. Reading presidential inaugural addresses (especially Kennedy's), Bellah showed how American public discourse invokes a non-sectarian "God" who has a special covenant with America, casts national history in biblical terms (the Exodus, the chosen people, sacrifice and rebirth), and consecrates events and figures — Thanksgiving, Memorial Day, the martyred Lincoln, the war dead — through quasi-liturgical observance. Civil religion is neither mere patriotism nor establishment Christianity but a third thing: the religious dimension of national self-understanding, which can both ennoble (the civil-rights appeal to a higher law) and corrupt (the sacralization of state power).

## Significance
Bellah's essay launched a vast literature in the sociology of religion and remains the classic statement of how modern secular nations generate their own sacred order — a modern, demotic transposition of the ancient fusion of religion and political legitimacy ([[divine-kingship]]). It sits among the framework-setting works of twentieth-century religious studies alongside [[phase-7-013-eliade-myth-of-eternal-return]] and [[phase-7-022-hick-interpretation-of-religion]].
