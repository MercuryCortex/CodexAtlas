# An Interpretation of Religion (Hick)

## What it is
**An Interpretation of Religion: Human Responses to the Transcendent** (1989), based on John Hick's 1986–87 Gifford Lectures, is the fullest statement of his **pluralist hypothesis**. Hick proposes that the great world faiths are not rivals competing for a single exclusive truth, but **different culturally-conditioned responses to one ultimate reality**, which he calls "the Real" (*an sich*). Borrowing a Kantian distinction, he argues that the Real-in-itself is beyond human conceptual grasp; what each tradition encounters is the Real-as-humanly-experienced — the personal *deities* (Yahweh, the Trinity, Allah, Vishnu) and the non-personal *absolutes* (Brahman, the Dharmakāya, the Tao, the Void) being so many authentic but partial phenomenal manifestations of the one noumenal Real. The test of a tradition's authenticity is **soteriological**: whether it effectively turns persons "from self-centredness to Reality-centredness," a transformation Hick finds comparably present across the major faiths.

## Significance
An Interpretation of Religion is the central modern text of religious pluralism and a touchstone of late-twentieth-century philosophy of religion — endlessly debated by exclusivists, inclusivists, and critics who question whether the unknowable "Real" can do the work Hick asks of it. It belongs among the framework-setting works of the academic study of religion alongside [[phase-7-013-eliade-myth-of-eternal-return]] and [[phase-7-014-bellah-civil-religion-essay]], and gives philosophical form to [[religious-pluralism]].
