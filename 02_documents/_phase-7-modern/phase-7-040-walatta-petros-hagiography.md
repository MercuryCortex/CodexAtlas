# Gadla Walatta Petros (Life of Walatta Petros)

## What it is
The **Gadla Walatta Petros** ("Acts/Struggles of Walatta Petros") is the Ge'ez hagiography of the 17th-century Ethiopian female saint Walatta Petros (1592–1642), composed in 1672 — thirty years after her death — by her disciple Galawdewos (a male monk in her monastic foundation; *not* the 16th-century emperor [[galawdewos-emperor]]). The hagiography recounts Walatta Petros's life as a noblewoman who refused her arranged marriage to a senior court official; entered monastic life; led the **anti-Jesuit resistance** against Emperor Susenyos's 1622 conversion of Ethiopia to Roman Catholicism; was exiled and persecuted for her opposition; survived to see Fasilides expel the Jesuits in 1632 and reinstall Tewahedo Christianity as state religion; founded seven women's monastic communities on Lake Tana; and died in 1642 honored as the principal female-saintly leader of the Ethiopian Christian resistance to Catholic conversion.

The text is one of the longest-extant Ge'ez gadls (Acts) of any saint, male or female, and the most thorough Ethiopian biography of a woman from any premodern period. The Belcher-Kleiner 2015 critical translation made the text widely available in English and has become a landmark in modern Ethiopian-female-history and African-women's-history scholarship.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Late-Solomonic Ethiopia in the Jesuit period crisis. The 16th-c. Portuguese-Catholic mission to Ethiopia (begun under João Bermudes, intensified under Pedro Páez and Afonso Mendes 1603–1632) had culminated in Emperor Susenyos's 1622 personal conversion to Roman Catholicism and forced national conversion. The conversion provoked civil war; Susenyos abdicated in favor of his son Fasilides in 1632; Fasilides expelled the Jesuits and reinstated Tewahedo. Walatta Petros's life spans both phases: she resists during Susenyos's reign and is vindicated under Fasilides.
- **Religious milieu.** Ethiopian Christianity under existential threat from Catholic missionary conversion, with the resistance led substantially by female monastics; the text is a witness to female religious-political agency in 17th-c. Ethiopia.
- **Who was behind it.** Composed by **Galawdewos**, a monk of Walatta Petros's community at Q'arata monastery on Lake Tana — author who knew her personally or through still-living first-generation companions. The work is one of the best-attested authored hagiographies in the Ethiopian tradition (the author is named in the colophon and his identity is consistent with internal evidence).
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous Ge'ez manuscript transmission centered on the monasteries Walatta Petros founded. Conti Rossini 1912 critical edition (CSCO); Belcher-Kleiner 2015 modern critical English translation (Princeton UP).

## Themes and motifs
- [[female-monasticism]] — Walatta Petros is the foundational figure of 17th-c. Ethiopian women's monasticism; her seven monastic foundations remain operational.
- [[anti-jesuit-resistance]] — the text's central political-religious theme.
- [[ethiopian-anti-catholic-polemic]] — Walatta Petros's resistance is paradigmatic for the larger Tewahedo defense against Catholic conversion.
- [[hagiography]] — the gadl genre; Walatta Petros is one of the most extensively-documented Ethiopian saints, male or female.

## Disputes
- **Galawdewos's identity.** Confidently identified as a monk of Walatta Petros's community; not (against some 19th-c. misreadings) the 16th-c. emperor.
- **Same-sex relationships in the text.** The text's account of Walatta Petros's intense devotional friendship with the woman Eheta Kristos has been read (Belcher 2015 substantively) as an early extant biographical instance of an African woman whose life included same-sex affectional/devotional intimacy. The reading has prompted both significant scholarly engagement and some Ethiopian-ecclesiastical pushback. Belcher's reading is the academic standard.
- **Historical accuracy.** The gadl is generally historically reliable for the major events (anti-Jesuit resistance, monastic foundations, royal interactions) and hagiographically elaborated for the miraculous-pious episodes.
