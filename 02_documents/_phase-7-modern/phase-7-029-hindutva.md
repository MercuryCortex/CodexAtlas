# Hindutva: Who Is a Hindu?

## What it is
V.D. Savarkar's 1923 pamphlet, composed during his internment at Ratnagiri (a milder form of detention following his 1911–1921 imprisonment in the Cellular Jail at Port Blair). The book proposes that "Hindutva" — *Hindu-ness* — is to be distinguished from "Hinduism" (the religion) and defined as the cultural-national-civilizational identity of those whose *pitribhumi* (fatherland) AND *punyabhumi* (holy land) lie within the territory of historical Bharatavarsha (the Indian subcontinent). By this definition Hindus, Sikhs, Jains, and Buddhists qualify; Muslims and Christians (whose holy land lies outside the subcontinent — Mecca and Jerusalem respectively) do not, regardless of their birth or residence in India. The book is the foundational text of *Hindutva* as a political-ideological project distinct from Hindu religion narrowly construed.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** British India in the early 1920s; the Indian National Congress was moving toward Gandhi's leadership and a Hindu-Muslim-cooperation strategy; the Khilafat movement (1919–1924) had aligned Indian Muslims with the Ottoman Caliphate. Savarkar developed Hindutva partly as a counter-position to Congress's communal-cooperation framework.
- **Religious milieu.** Hindu reform movements (Arya Samaj 1875 onward; Ramakrishna Mission from 1897; Theosophy's Indian presence) had been re-imagining Hinduism for the modern period. Savarkar's move is to displace the *religious* category in favor of an *ethno-civilizational* one.
- **Who was behind it.** Savarkar personally; he published under pseudonym ("a Maratha"), the work being banned for him to publish under his name during his internment.
- **Discovery / transmission.** 1923 publication, widely circulated in Hindu nationalist networks; institutionalized in the RSS (founded 1925 by K.B. Hedgewar, who knew Savarkar) and through it in the broader Sangh Parivar. After Indian independence (1947), Partition (1947), and the assassination of Gandhi (1948, by Hindu-nationalist Nathuram Godse, with Savarkar tried as co-conspirator and acquitted), Hindutva politics receded for a generation before reviving from the 1980s into the BJP-led national-political dominance of the 21st century.

## Themes and motifs
- **Hindutva as ethno-civilizational identity** — the core innovation; *Hindu* re-defined from a religious to a cultural-national category.
- **Pitribhumi / Punyabhumi double criterion** — the territorial-religious dual test that excludes Indian Muslims and Christians from the Hindutva *nation* while including non-Hindu Indic religions (Sikhism, Jainism, Buddhism).
- **Bharatavarsha as sacred territory** — the Indian subcontinent as both ancestral and holy land; the *Akhand Bharat* ("unbroken India") concept that includes territory currently in Pakistan and Bangladesh.

## Connections (provisional)
- → RSS (1925) — *direct-institutional descent*: Hedgewar founded the RSS two years after Savarkar's pamphlet; the RSS is the principal institutional carrier of Hindutva ideology.
- → BJP (1980) — *direct-political descent*: the BJP is the RSS-affiliated electoral vehicle; its 21st-century political dominance has made Hindutva an actively governing ideology.
- → European ethnonationalism (Herder, Mazzini, late-19th-century romantic nationalism) — *direct-influence*: Savarkar's category-structure is recognizably continuous with the European Romantic-nationalist tradition adapted to the Indian context.

## Disputes
- **Religious vs. political category.** Whether Hindutva is correctly classified as a religious tradition (this vault's working classification, with hedging) or as a political ideology using religious-civilizational vocabulary. The Indian Supreme Court (1995 *Manohar Joshi v. Nitin Bhaurao Patil* and related cases) has held Hindutva to be a "way of life" not a religion, with consequences for India's electoral law on appeals-to-religion in campaigns.
- **Savarkar's mercy petitions.** Savarkar wrote multiple mercy petitions to British authorities from the Cellular Jail (1911–1921), pledging political quiescence in exchange for release. Critics treat this as evidence of opportunism; defenders treat it as tactical. The factual record of the petitions is uncontested.
- **Gandhi assassination.** Savarkar was tried and acquitted on conspiracy charges (1948–1949); subsequent commissions of inquiry (Kapur Commission 1969) found stronger evidence of his involvement but did not reverse the acquittal.
