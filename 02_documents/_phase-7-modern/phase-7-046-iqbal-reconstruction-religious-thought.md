# The Reconstruction of Religious Thought in Islam

**The Reconstruction of Religious Thought in Islam** — delivered as six lectures in Madras, Hyderabad, and Aligarh in 1928–1929 and published in 1930 — is the foundational text of Islamic modernism in South Asia and one of the most ambitious attempts in the 20th century to synthesize Western philosophy with Islamic theology. Its author, **Muhammad Iqbal** (1877–1938) — poet, philosopher, lawyer, and the intellectual architect of the Pakistan idea — argued that Islam contains, properly understood, the resources for a thoroughgoing engagement with modern science and philosophy without capitulating to Western secularism.

## The central argument: *khudi* and dynamic selfhood

Iqbal's project turns on the concept of **khudi** (خودی, "selfhood" or "ego") — the dynamic, self-realizing individual consciousness that Iqbal holds to be the supreme religious value in Islam. Against the Sufi tradition of *fanāʾ* (annihilation of the self in God, as in Rumi), Iqbal argues that the Quranic vision of the human being is *khudi* — a personality that achieves its fullest realization not by dissolving into the divine but by becoming more intensely itself in creative engagement with the world.

This is a direct synthesis of Bergson's *élan vital* (the creative, forward-moving life-force that Bergson opposed to mechanical determinism) and Nietzsche's will to power (which Iqbal explicitly engages, acknowledging Nietzsche's insight while redirecting it toward Islamic *tawhid* rather than atheism). The result is an Islam that is *activist* rather than quietist, *creative* rather than traditionalist, and *future-oriented* rather than backward-looking.

## *Ijtihad* as theological duty

The most politically consequential argument in the Reconstruction is Iqbal's insistence that **ijtihad** — independent reasoning applied to Islamic law and theology, traditionally "closed" since the 10th century in mainstream Sunni jurisprudence — must be reopened. Iqbal argues that the "closing of the gate of ijtihad" was a historically contingent political decision, not a theological necessity, and that a living Islam must engage the present with living reason. This argument became the intellectual foundation for Islamic modernist reform movements across the 20th century, and it is what makes the Reconstruction the target of both reformists (who celebrate it) and traditionalist Salafis (who reject it as bid'ah/innovation).

## Primary text passages

**On the Quran and empirical method** (Lecture I):
> "The Quran is a book which emphasizes deed rather than idea. There, I think, lies the origin of that general empirical attitude which we notice in the Quran... The birth of Islam... is the birth of inductive intellect."

**On *ijtihad*** (Lecture VI):
> "The claim of the present generation of Muslim liberals to reinterpret the foundational legal principles, in the light of their own experience and altered conditions of modern life, is, in my opinion, perfectly justified."

**On Bergson and Islamic time** (Lecture II):
> "The truth is that the essential nature of Reality is self-revelation; and the ultimate purpose of human existence is to make this self-revelation perpetually creative."

## Cross-tradition significance

### The Western-Islamic philosophical synthesis: the most ambitious in modern history
The Reconstruction attempts to show that Bergson's theory of creative evolution, Nietzsche's assertion of dynamic selfhood, and Hegelian dialectics are not Western impositions on Islam but recoveries of insights already present in the Quran and Islamic philosophy. This is the Islamic equivalent of Thomas Aquinas's synthesis of Aristotle and Christianity — or of the Kalam theologians' synthesis of Greek philosophy and Islamic theology in the 9th–11th centuries. Iqbal is al-Ghazali's opposite: where al-Ghazali (*Tahāfut al-Falāsifa*, 1095) argued philosophy was dangerous to faith, Iqbal argues philosophy is essential to it.

### *Khudi* vs. *Ātman=Brahman*: the great South Asian philosophical divergence
Iqbal's insistence on *khudi* as irreducible self-hood is an explicit counter to the Vedantic tradition of *ātman=Brahman* — the self dissolving into the universal ground. Iqbal studied Sanskrit and engaged with Vedanta directly; he understood Shankara's Advaita and rejected it as the annihilation of genuine individual personality. The *khudi* vs. *ātman=Brahman* divergence is the philosophical bedrock of the Hindu-Muslim split that produces Pakistan as an idea. This is one of the most important abstract-philosophical divergences in the vault because it shows how metaphysical disagreements (about selfhood and the divine) produce geopolitical realities (the partition of the subcontinent).

### Iqbal vs. Ambedkar: two South Asian reformers, opposite directions
1930 is the same year Iqbal delivers the Reconstruction lectures; it is also the year that Ambedkar begins his serious study of Buddhism as an alternative to Hinduism. Two thinkers in British India simultaneously reconstruct their inherited traditions — one deepens and modernizes his (Islam), one abandons his in favor of another (Hinduism → Buddhism). Both use Western philosophy as a toolkit. Both serve colonized communities. Both generate founding texts for nation-scale movements. The comparison reveals the range of strategies available to colonized religious subjects: reform-from-within vs. conversion-out.

### The Pakistan idea as theological project
Iqbal's 1930 Allahabad Address — delivered the same year as the first edition of the Reconstruction — proposed a Muslim-majority state in northwest India as a social laboratory for the Islamic way of life. Pakistan, as it emerged in 1947, was born from this theological-philosophical project: a nation-state whose justification was not ethnic or linguistic but *religious-philosophical*. Iqbal never used the word "Pakistan" (coined by Rahmat Ali in 1933) but his vision is its intellectual foundation. The Reconstruction is therefore a direct causal ancestor of the partition of British India and the death of ~1–2 million people in communal violence. It is the most geopolitically consequential work of Islamic philosophy in the 20th century.

### *Ijtihad* chain: Iqbal → Islamic modernism → Fazlur Rahman → contemporary reformists
Iqbal's call to reopen the gate of ijtihad inaugurated a 20th-century tradition of Islamic modernism that runs: Iqbal (1930) → Fazlur Rahman (*Islam and Modernity*, 1982) → Khaled Abou El Fadl → progressive Islamic jurisprudence of the 21st century. The same call also provoked its opposite: Mawdudi's response to Iqbal (whom he knew personally) rejected Western-influenced modernism in favor of a totalizing Islamic state based on Sharia. The ijtihad debate is the central fault line of contemporary Islam.
