# Science and Health with Key to the Scriptures

## What it is
The foundational text of Christian Science, written by Mary Baker Eddy (1821–1910) between approximately 1872 and 1875 and revised through approximately 430 editions during her lifetime (the canonical text is the 1910 edition). The book argues that matter, sickness, and death are illusory ("mortal mind") and that reality is wholly spiritual; that Jesus' healings demonstrate the operation of a divine *Science* that any human can practice; and that scriptural understanding is recovered when read through this metaphysical key. The book contains a chapter-by-chapter spiritual exegesis of Genesis, Job, Revelation, and other biblical passages, plus a "Glossary" giving the spiritual meanings of biblical terms (e.g., "Adam: error; a falsity; the belief in 'original sin'..."). Christian Science holds that *Science and Health* is co-equal with the Bible as scripture; the two are read together in every Christian Science Sunday service.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Post-Civil-War United States; the rise of women's authority in heterodox religious movements (Spiritualism, Theosophy, Christian Science, Shakers). Eddy was a chronically ill woman in a society offering chronically ill women few therapeutic options.
- **Religious milieu.** Spiritualism had peaked (Fox sisters 1848 onward); New Thought (Quimby, Warren Felt Evans, Emma Curtis Hopkins) was developing a parallel American mental-healing tradition; American Transcendentalism (Emerson, Alcott) had idealist-monist precedents.
- **Who was behind it.** Eddy alone, claiming direct divine inspiration after a healing experience in 1866 (the "fall on the ice in Lynn, Massachusetts" — she fell, was bedridden, read Matthew 9, and recovered; she dated her discovery from this event). Editorial assistance from her secretary Calvin Frye and others is documented.
- **Discovery / transmission.** First edition 1875 (Eddy self-published, ~1,000 copies). Continuous expansion of the Church of Christ, Scientist (chartered 1879); the Boston Mother Church (1894) and Eddy's *Manual of the Mother Church* (1895) institutionalize the movement.

## Themes and motifs
- **Metaphysical idealism** — only Spirit (= God = Mind) is real; matter is "mortal mind's" illusion.
- **Healing-as-evidence** — Christian Science healing is offered as empirical demonstration of the metaphysics, evidentially comparable to Jesus' miracles.
- **Anti-medical orientation** — reliance on Christian Science treatment rather than medicine; a recurring source of legal and ethical controversy (especially regarding pediatric cases).
- **Female spiritual authority** — Eddy figures herself as the "woman of Revelation 12"; her authority extends posthumously through the Church Manual.

## Connections (provisional)
- → Phineas Quimby (1802–1866) — *contested-influence*: Eddy treated with Quimby 1862–1865 and copied his manuscripts; Quimby's heirs published the *Quimby Manuscripts* (1921) arguing Eddy plagiarized; Eddy and her defenders maintain she received the discovery independently after Quimby's 1866 death. The dispute is a central Eddy-biographical issue (Gill 1998 gives the balanced account).
- → [[phase-7-031-course-in-miracles]] — *parallel-motif*: Schucman's *ACIM* (1976) reads as a Christian-Science-adjacent metaphysical idealism through Vedantic and Freudian lenses; both treat the material world as illusion and offer mind-healing as soteriology.
- → New Thought (Unity, Religious Science) — *direct-influence* (one-way disputed): later New Thought leaders (Charles Fillmore, Ernest Holmes) drew on Eddy's vocabulary while developing more theologically optimistic-pluralist positions; Eddy considered them deviationists.

## Disputes
- **Quimby influence and plagiarism.** Sharpest historiographical dispute around Eddy. Modern non-Christian-Science scholarship (Gill 1998, Fraser 1999) treats Quimby as a real but partial source whose contribution Eddy minimized.
- **Pediatric medical-neglect cases.** Numerous 20th- and 21st-century legal cases involve Christian Science parents and child deaths; the church has progressively distinguished spiritual healing from medical neglect but not abandoned the practice.
- **Membership decline.** From a peak of ~270,000 members in 1936 to estimated <50,000 today; the *Christian Science Monitor* (still respected journalism) has long outlived the membership trajectory.
