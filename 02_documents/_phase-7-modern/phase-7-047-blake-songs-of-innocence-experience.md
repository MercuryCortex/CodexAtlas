# Songs of Innocence and of Experience (Blake)

## What it is
**Songs of Innocence and of Experience**, "Shewing the Two Contrary States of the Human Soul," is William Blake's illuminated poetry collection — *Songs of Innocence* etched in 1789, combined with *Songs of Experience* in 1794. Each poem is a hand-coloured relief etching in which word and image form a single visionary unit, printed by Blake's own method. The two halves are deliberately paired contraries: the pastoral, trusting world of Innocence ("The Lamb," "The Chimney Sweeper" of Innocence) is shadowed by its counterpart in Experience ("The Tyger," the bitter "Chimney Sweeper" of Experience), so that the same subjects recur transformed. Blake's governing conviction — "Without Contraries is no progression" — makes the volume not a fall from innocence to disillusion but a dialectic of two necessary states, through which the soul must pass toward a higher, organized innocence. "The Tyger" ("Did he who made the Lamb make thee?") poses the theodicy of a creator who fashions both gentleness and terror.

## Significance
The Songs are a foundational text of English Romanticism and the most accessible entry into Blake's vast personal mythology (continued in *The Marriage of Heaven and Hell*, *Milton*, and *Jerusalem*). Blake's heterodox, visionary Christianity — God as the imaginative "Divine Humanity," and his structuring of reality through reconciled contraries — is a Romantic kin to the older mystical traditions of [[mysticism]] and the coincidence-of-opposites theology of [[phase-5-021-cusa-de-docta-ignorantia]], staged here as the [[light-and-darkness-dualism]] of Innocence and Experience.
