# The Myth of the Eternal Return (Eliade)

## What it is
**The Myth of the Eternal Return** (1949; English 1954, also titled *Cosmos and History*) is Mircea Eliade's most influential theoretical work. Its thesis is that "archaic" religious humanity lived by a **flight from history**: through myth and ritual, traditional societies abolished ordinary, irreversible time and periodically returned to the sacred "in-that-time" (*illud tempus*) of origins, when gods and ancestors first acted. Every meaningful act — building a house, sowing a field, crowning a king — is real only insofar as it **repeats an archetypal model** established in that primordial time; profane, unrepeatable history is meaningless and even terrifying, to be cyclically dissolved through rites of renewal (the New Year, the recreation of the cosmos). Eliade contrasts this archaic "ontology of repetition" with the linear, irreversible time of Judaism, Christianity, and modernity, in which history itself becomes the arena of meaning — the "terror of history" that archaic humanity had escaped.

## Significance
The Myth of the Eternal Return is a foundational text of twentieth-century comparative religion, fixing concepts — sacred vs. profane time, the [[eternal-return]], the archetype, the *axis mundi* — that shaped a generation of religious studies (and provoked decades of critique of Eliade's method). It stands at the centre of the academic study of religion alongside other framework-setting works such as those of [[phase-7-014-bellah-civil-religion-essay]] and [[phase-7-022-hick-interpretation-of-religion]].
