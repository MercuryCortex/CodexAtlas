# The Buddha and His Dhamma

**The Buddha and His Dhamma** — written by B.R. Ambedkar (1891–1956) between 1950 and 1956 and published posthumously in 1957 — is the foundational canonical text of **Navayana** ("New Vehicle"), the form of Buddhism created by Ambedkar as a vehicle for Dalit liberation from the Hindu caste system. Ambedkar completed the manuscript six days before his death and converted to Buddhism alongside approximately 500,000 followers at Nagpur on October 14, 1956 — the largest single-day mass religious conversion in recorded history.

## What Ambedkar changed

Ambedkar did not simply adopt traditional Buddhism. He performed a deliberate theological reconstruction, explicitly rejecting elements he identified as either inauthentic additions or as ideologically compatible with caste oppression:

1. **Karma and rebirth excised**: Ambedkar argued that the doctrine of karma — that one's current circumstances result from actions in past lives — was a post-Buddha interpolation used by brahminical interpreters to naturalize and justify caste hierarchy. If a Dalit is born into an untouchable caste by karma, then the caste system is cosmic justice. Ambedkar rejected this as theologically fraudulent and socially catastrophic.

2. **Nirvana reframed as social liberation**: Rather than individual escape from the cycle of rebirth, Ambedkar's nirvana means liberation from social suffering — the end of caste discrimination, poverty, and untouchability in this world. This transforms Buddhism from a path of personal transcendence to a program of collective social transformation.

3. **The historical Buddha amplified**: Ambedkar's text emphasizes the historical Siddhārtha Gautama as a revolutionary social reformer who explicitly challenged the brahminical *varna* system and built a *sangha* (community) that accepted Dalits. The metaphysical and cosmological Buddha of Mahayana tradition recedes; the social activist Buddha advances.

4. **Bodhisattva social ethic**: The Mahayana ideal of the Bodhisattva — the being who delays individual enlightenment to liberate all sentient beings — becomes, in Navayana, the template for the Dalit political activist who sacrifices personal advancement for community liberation.

## Primary text excerpts

**On the social meaning of Buddhism** (Part I, Section I):
> "The Buddha's religion was the only religion which gave equal status to women and was the only religion which did not make inequality an article of faith... Buddhism stands for liberty, equality, and fraternity."

**On karma and caste** (Part VIII, Section III):
> "The belief in karma can be made consistent with the social struggle against injustice. It all depends on how karma is interpreted. The karma that Buddha taught was not the karma which the Brahmins taught."

## Cross-tradition significance

### The most radical internal critique of Buddhism
Ambedkar's excision of karma and rebirth is the most radical theological revision within Buddhism since the Mahayana/Theravada split. No other Buddhist school explicitly rejects karma as a casteist ideology. The standard Buddhist response is that karma operates at the individual level and cannot justify collective oppression — but Ambedkar's counter is structural: *regardless of correct interpretation*, the doctrine is sociologically weaponized, and a liberation theology cannot afford the weapon.

### Liberation Theology convergence: same move, different tradition
Ambedkar's Navayana performs exactly the same theological operation as Latin American Liberation Theology (Gustavo Gutiérrez, *A Theology of Liberation*, 1971): identify elements of the traditional religion that have been co-opted to justify oppression, strip them out, and re-read the tradition's core as a mandate for the liberation of the poor. Both: colonial context, structural analysis of oppression, religious tradition as tool of resistance. Gutiérrez reads Jesus as preferentially for the poor; Ambedkar reads the Buddha as anti-caste. The convergence is independent — Gutiérrez and Ambedkar never met, and Liberation Theology arose 15 years after Ambedkar's death.

Also parallel: Donghak/Cheondogyo (Korea, 1861) — *innaecheon* doctrine ("each person is Heaven") used to justify anti-colonial revolt; same theological move of using radical divine immanence to dissolve social hierarchy. And Cao Dai (Vietnam, 1926) — religion explicitly framed as escape from colonial condition. All four: colonized peoples using religion as instrument of liberation.

### Gandhi vs. Ambedkar: the great Hindu internal debate
The Ambedkar-Gandhi conflict is the foundational debate of modern Indian religious politics. Gandhi's "Harijan" ("children of God") approach to untouchability was paternalistic reform from within Hinduism — changing hearts, not structures. Ambedkar's conversion was structural rejection: if the religion legitimizes the caste hierarchy, leave the religion. Their 1932 Poona Pact (which Ambedkar signed under duress to prevent Gandhi's fast unto death) is the most consequential political-theological negotiation in modern Indian history. The debate continues — Gandhi's Congress party vs. Ambedkar's constitution-as-emancipation model — and its implications shape Indian politics to this day.

### The conversion that reshaped South Asian Buddhism
Before 1956, Indian Buddhism was archaeologically significant but demographically negligible — the tradition had been largely extinguished in India by the medieval period. Ambedkar's conversion created, in a single day, a new South Asian Buddhist community of ~500,000, which has since grown to ~7–8 million. Navayana is now the largest form of Buddhism in India. Ambedkar's conversion was a geographical return (Buddhism originated in India) and a theological transformation (a Buddhism stripped of brahminical interpolations). The pilgrimage site at Nagpur where the conversion occurred has become one of modern Buddhism's most important sacred sites.
