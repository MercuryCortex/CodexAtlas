# Major Trends in Jewish Mysticism

## What it is
Gershom Scholem's foundational 1941 work, based on the Stroock Lectures delivered at the Jewish Theological Seminary in New York 1938. Nine lectures cover (1) the general characteristics of Jewish mysticism, (2) Merkavah-Hekhalot mysticism (Phase 4), (3) Hasidei Ashkenaz (Phase 5), (4) Abraham Abulafia's prophetic Kabbalah (Phase 5–6), (5) the *Zohar* and its symbolism (Phase 5/6 — Scholem decisively attributed the bulk to Moshe de León ~1280s), (6) Isaac Luria and the Lurianic Kabbalah (Phase 6), (7) Sabbatianism (Phase 6), (8) Frankism (Phase 6/7), (9) Hasidism (Phase 6/7). The work *constituted* academic Kabbalah studies as a field — before Scholem, Wissenschaft des Judentums historiography (Graetz especially) had treated Jewish mysticism as a regrettable irrational lapse; Scholem reframed it as central to Jewish religious history.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Composed during the rise of Nazism and the Holocaust (the lectures 1938, the book 1941, when Walter Benjamin had just killed himself at Portbou and the European Jewish world was being destroyed). Scholem had emigrated from Berlin to Jerusalem in 1923; he was on the founding faculty of the Hebrew University.
- **Religious milieu.** Religious Zionism, secular Zionism, the destruction of European Jewry, the early Hebrew University milieu (Buber, Bergmann, the Eranos Circle connection from 1949). Scholem's work has been read as a kind of secular-Zionist intellectual recovery of mystical-redemptive currents previously hidden in the Jewish archive.
- **Who was behind it.** Scholem personally; the 1938 New York lectures were translated and revised by him for the 1941 book.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Immediate canonical status in Jewish studies; remained the field's principal reference for decades; complicated and partly revised by Moshe Idel (1988 *Kabbalah: New Perspectives*) but never displaced.

## Themes and motifs
- **Kabbalah as Jewish gnosis** — Scholem's most contested framing. He repeatedly compares Kabbalistic emanation, the *Ein Sof*, the broken vessels (*shevirat ha-kelim*) to Gnostic structures; Idel and others have argued this *Gnostic-comparative* lens distorts Kabbalah's own theurgic-experiential register.
- **Messianism as the dynamizing principle** — Sabbatianism and Frankism are not aberrations but the eruption of Kabbalistic messianic potential into history.
- **Recovery of erased history** — Scholem's larger project: a vast suppressed Jewish religious tradition (Sabbatean, Frankist, post-Sabbatean Hasidic) has been edited out of mainstream Jewish historiography and must be excavated.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-7-013-gnostic-religion]] — *parallel-development*: Scholem and Jonas (later, 1958) both reframe a buried late-antique/medieval mystical-Gnostic current as central to religious history. Their methods differ but the pattern is the same: Phase 7 academic recovery of Phases 4–5 esoterism.
- → [[phase-7-023-bruno-and-hermetic-tradition]] — *parallel-development*: Yates (1964) performs the analogous recovery for Renaissance Hermeticism. **Scholem (Kabbalah), Jonas (Gnosticism), Yates (Hermeticism), and Pagels (Nag Hammadi popular)** form the four-author cluster that made Phases 4–6 esoterism legible as serious history in mid-20th-century scholarship.
- → Moshe Idel (1988) — *direct-influence with revision*: the principal revisionist successor; Idel restores experiential-theurgic dimensions Scholem had de-emphasized.

## Disputes
- **The "Gnostic" framing of Kabbalah.** Idel and others have argued Scholem's Gnostic-comparative lens over-emphasizes the speculative-cosmological at the expense of the practical-theurgic-experiential. The dispute is methodological-foundational for the field.
- **Sabbatean genealogies.** Scholem's argument that Sabbatean / Frankist undergrounds shaped subsequent Hasidic and Haskalah movements is bold; some claims (Maoz Kahana, Pawel Maciejko) have been refined; the basic frame has held.
- **Author attribution.** Scholem's attribution of the *Zohar*'s composition to Moshe de León (~1280s) was challenged briefly by Yehuda Liebes's argument for collaborative authorship; the basic 13th-century Spanish dating is consensus.
