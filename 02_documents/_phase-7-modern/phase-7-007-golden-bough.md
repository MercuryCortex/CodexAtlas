# The Golden Bough

## What it is
James George Frazer's massive comparative-religion study, first published in two volumes in 1890 and expanded across editions to twelve volumes by 1915. The book takes as its starting point a strange detail in Roman religion: the priest of Diana at Nemi (the *Rex Nemorensis*) won his office by killing his predecessor and would in turn be killed by his successor. Frazer treats this as a survival of an archaic pattern of *sacred kingship* in which the king embodies the fertility of the land and must be ritually slain when his powers wane. From this starting point Frazer assembles enormous comparative material — Egyptian Osiris, Phrygian Attis, Hellenistic Adonis, Mesopotamian Tammuz/Dumuzi, Thracian/Greek Dionysus, European harvest folklore — under the heading of *dying-and-reviving gods*, arguing this is a universal vegetal-fertility ritual pattern. The Christianity volume (added in the 2nd edition, partially removed under pressure, restored in the 3rd) implicitly assimilates Jesus to this same pattern — among the most controversial inclusions in 19th-century scholarship.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Late-Victorian Britain at the height of empire; vast amounts of ethnographic data flowed from imperial encounter, much of it usable (and used) without serious local-contextual care. Frazer never did fieldwork.
- **Religious milieu.** *The Golden Bough* sits within the British evolutionist-comparative school (Tylor, Robertson Smith, Andrew Lang) reading religion as a developmental sequence: magic → religion → science. The book's enormous public influence outran scholarly consensus: by ~1920 the methodological framework was already under sustained anthropological critique (Wittgenstein 1931 *Remarks on Frazer*, Malinowski's functionalism, later Boas/Lévi-Strauss).
- **Who was behind it.** Frazer worked at Trinity College, Cambridge; his colleagues included Robertson Smith (whose *Religion of the Semites* (1889) provided the sacrificial theory Frazer adapted); his correspondents included anthropologists (Spencer, Gillen — Aboriginal Australia), missionaries, colonial administrators worldwide who fed him source material via mail.
- **Discovery / transmission.** 1st ed. 1890 (2 vols) → 2nd ed. 1900 (3 vols) → 3rd ed. 1906–1915 (12 vols + Aftermath 1936) → 1922 abridgment by Frazer himself (the most-read version). T.S. Eliot's note to *The Waste Land* names Frazer as a primary source; Eliot's poem and the broader 1920s modernist literary uptake gave *The Golden Bough* a vast non-scholarly readership.

## Themes and motifs
- [[dying-rising-god]] — Frazer's signature category, decisively populated by him in this work. The category's later collapse under J.Z. Smith's critique (Smith 1990 *Drudgery Divine*; Mettinger 2001 partial defense for ANE only) is the most influential test case in 20th-century comparative-religion methodology.
- [[sacred-king]] — the *Rex Nemorensis*; the slain divine king; the king as cosmic-fertility embodiment.
- [[sympathetic-magic]] — Frazer's two laws: similarity (*like produces like*) and contagion (*things once in contact continue to affect each other*).
- **Survivals** — the Tylorian "survival" concept: archaic patterns persist as folklore in cultures whose dominant religion has moved past them.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-7-010-elementary-forms-religious-life]] — *parallel-development*: Durkheim (1912) develops a structurally different but contemporary comparative theory of religion (totemism + collective effervescence) that competed with Frazer's evolutionism.
- → [[phase-7-021-patterns-comparative-religion]] — *direct-influence (critical)*: Eliade (1949) extensively engages Frazer's data, while rejecting his evolutionist and rationalist framework in favor of a phenomenological-hierophany account.
- → [[phase-7-017-psychology-and-alchemy]] — *direct-influence*: Jung knew and used Frazer's comparative material; his archetypal psychology presupposes large parts of the Frazerian comparative database while reinterpreting it psychologically.
- → 20th-century modernist literature — *direct-influence*: Eliot's *Waste Land* (1922), Joyce's *Ulysses* (1922), Lawrence's *The Plumed Serpent* (1926), Robert Graves's *White Goddess* (1948), Joseph Campbell's *Hero with a Thousand Faces* (1949) all stand downstream of *The Golden Bough*.

## Disputes
- **Dying-rising god category.** J.Z. Smith's "When the Bough Breaks" (1973) and *Drudgery Divine* (1990) systematically dismantled Frazer's assemblage, showing that (a) most of his "dying-rising gods" do not in fact rise; (b) the category is constructed largely by Christian-apologetic / anti-apologetic 19th-century framing imposed on heterogeneous materials. Mettinger (2001) *The Riddle of Resurrection* partially rehabilitates the category for some ANE deities (Adonis, Melqart, Eshmun); the broader category remains contested.
- **Method.** Frazer's armchair comparativism, decontextualized data extraction, and evolutionist sequence are now generally rejected. The book is read today as a *historical phenomenon* (a Victorian-imperial cultural document) more often than as anthropology.
