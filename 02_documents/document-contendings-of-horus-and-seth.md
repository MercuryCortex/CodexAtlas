# The Contendings of Horus and Seth

## What it is
The fullest surviving narrative of the central Egyptian myth: the eighty-year contest before the divine tribunal over who shall inherit the throne of the murdered Osiris — his son and avenger Horus, or his fratricidal brother Seth. By turns a solemn charter of kingship and a bawdy, almost satirical comedy (the gods bicker, sulk, and trick one another), it ends with Horus vindicated and enthroned: the mythic guarantee that every living pharaoh is Horus and every dead one Osiris.

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** New Kingdom Egypt; the Osirian succession is the theological backbone of kingship.
- **Tone.** Uniquely irreverent for a sacred narrative — the gods are petty and ribald — yet the outcome is deadly serious for royal legitimacy.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Preserved on a single Ramesside papyrus; the myth's classical form is Plutarch's.

## Themes and motifs
- [[dying-rising-god]] — Osiris, murdered and resurrected, ruling the dead while his son rules the living.
- The avenging son — Horus securing his murdered father's throne.
- Divine kingship — the contest as the charter of pharaonic legitimacy.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **The myth that made the pharaoh a god — and reached Europe through Plutarch.** The Osiris-Horus-Seth cycle is the ideological engine of Egyptian kingship: the dead king becomes Osiris, lord of the resurrected dead; the living king is Horus, the avenging son who defeats the chaos-bringing Seth. This dying-and-rising royal theology, given connected narrative form here, passed into the Greco-Roman world through Plutarch's *De Iside et Osiride* and the Hellenistic Isis-and-Osiris mysteries, and stands behind centuries of comparative debate over dying-and-rising gods and resurrection symbolism (Lichtheim 1976 pp.214–223; Simpson 2003 pp.91–103).
