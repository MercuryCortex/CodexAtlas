# Sūrat al-Qaṣaṣ (Quran 28 — The Story)

## What it is
The Quran's fullest single narrative of Moses' early life: his mother, by divine inspiration, places her infant in the river; he is drawn out and raised in Pharaoh's own household; grown, he kills an Egyptian and flees to Midian, where he waters flocks, marries the daughter of an old man, and at last meets God at the burning bush. The sura closes with **Qārūn** (the biblical Korah), a man of the people of Moses so swollen with wealth that the earth swallows him and his treasure. The frame is consolation: revealed when Muhammad's own community was weak in Mecca, it shows God lifting the hunted foundling to prophethood and burying the rich oppressor.

## Primary text

### Quran 28:7
*Trans. Abdel Haleem (2004)*
> We inspired Moses' mother, saying, "Suckle him, and then, when you fear for his safety, put him in the river: do not be afraid, and do not grieve, for We shall return him to you and make him a messenger."

### Quran 28:88
*Trans. Abdel Haleem (2004)*
> Do not call out to another god beside God, for there is no god but Him. Everything will perish except His Face.

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** Late Meccan; the Moses story is read as a mirror of Muhammad's persecuted community awaiting deliverance.
- **Tradition.** Moses (Mūsā) is named more than any other figure in the Quran; this sura supplies the connective tissue of his biography.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Qārūn (28:76–82) gives Arabic its word for a Croesus-like hoard, *kanz Qārūn*, "Korah's treasure."

## Themes and motifs
- The rescued foundling — the lowly raised by God's hand.
- Reversal — the persecuted prophet exalted, the rich oppressor swallowed.
- "Everything perishes except His Face" (28:88) — the great Quranic statement of God's sole permanence.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **The baby in the basket — from Sargon to Moses to Cyrus.** The infant set afloat to escape a king's slaughter and raised by the very court that would have killed him is one of the most-travelled birth legends of the ancient world. The Akkadian *Legend of Sargon* (its hero placed by his mother in a reed basket sealed with bitumen and floated down the Euphrates, drawn out by Akki the irrigator) predates the biblical Moses by over a millennium; the same shape recurs for Cyrus in Herodotus, for Romulus and Remus, and for Krishna. Exodus 2 gives it to Moses — *tēvāh* (basket) sealed with bitumen, a daughter of the persecuting house as rescuer — and Sūrat al-Qaṣaṣ inherits and reframes it: the Quran's stress falls not on the basket's craft but on the mother's *trust*, calmed by direct revelation (28:7), and on the promise that the child will be returned and made a messenger. The Quran also keeps **Qārūn/Korah** (Numbers 16, the rebel swallowed by the earth) but recasts him from a challenger of priestly authority into the archetype of ruinous wealth — the same character bent to a different lesson (Reynolds 2010 pp.151–162; Tottoli 2002 pp.30–39; Nasr 2015 pp.961–979).
