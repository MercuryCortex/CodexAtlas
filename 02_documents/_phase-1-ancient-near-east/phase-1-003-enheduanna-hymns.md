# Hymns of Enheduanna

## What it is
A corpus of Sumerian devotional and theological poetry attributed to Enheduanna, daughter of Sargon of Akkad and *en*-priestess of the moon god [[nanna-sin]] at Ur (~2300 BCE). Three works are the core of the corpus: (1) the *Temple Hymns*, 42 short hymns each praising the principal temple of a major Sumerian and Akkadian city, ending with a colophon naming Enheduanna as compiler; (2) the *Exaltation of Inanna* (Nin-me-šara, "Lady of all the divine powers"), a 153-line first-person plea to [[inanna-sumerian]] in which Enheduanna recounts her exile from the temple by a usurper (Lugalanne) and prays for restoration; (3) *In-nin ša-gur-ra* (also called *Inanna and Ebih*), a longer hymn celebrating Inanna's cosmic supremacy. Enheduanna is the **earliest named author in human history**.

## Primary text — selected passages

### Nin-me-šara (Exaltation of Inanna), lines 1–7 — opening invocation
*Trans. Helle, S. (2023). Enheduana: The Complete Poems of the World's First Author. Yale UP. Composite text: ETCSL t.4.07.2.*
> Lady of all the divine powers, clothed in radiance,
> righteous woman, resplendent in the night,
> beloved of heaven and earth —
> Inanna, chosen of the great ornament, An's jewel,
> who wears the crown of the high priest,
> who takes up the seven divine powers:
> my queen, keeper of all divine powers.

*The poem opens with a comprehensive catalogue of Inanna's attributes, establishing her cosmic supremacy before the first-person biographical appeal begins.*

### Nin-me-šara, lines 65–73 — the exile complaint (world's first named autobiographical statement)
*Trans. Helle 2023*
> I, Enheduanna, the high priestess of the moon god —
> a rebel has expelled me from the sanctuary.
> He made me fly like a swallow from the window;
> my life is no more. He walked me through the thorn-bushes of the mountain;
> he stripped me of the rightful crown of the high priestess.
> He has given daggers and swords to Nanna's city.
> My queen, beloved of An — will Inanna not return?

*The first documented instance of a named person recording their personal suffering and direct appeal to a deity in their own name — the genre that generates the Hebrew psalms of lament.*

### Nin-me-šara, lines 135–145 — restoration and vindication
*Trans. Helle 2023*
> The judgment of Inanna was given at last —
> Nanna's verdict was overturned.
> An did not curse; it was Inanna's command.
> The holy heart of Inanna was restored.
> The daylight came forth brilliantly.
> Enheduanna, the high priestess, carried the silver diadem.
> You are exalted, like heaven — let it be said!
> You are wide, like the earth — let it be said!
> To destroy, to create, to tear out, to set up:
> it is yours to do — these things are yours.

*Citation: Helle, S. (2023). Enheduana: The Complete Poems of the World's First Author. Yale UP, pp. 55–80. ETCSL t.4.07.2.*

## Context
- **Political milieu.** The Sargonic empire — Sargon of Akkad (~2334–2279 BCE) had unified Sumer and Akkad under the first multi-ethnic Mesopotamian empire. Installing his daughter as *en*-priestess of Nanna at Ur was a deliberate political act: bringing the Sumerian south's premier religious office into the Akkadian royal house. Enheduanna served under her father, her brothers (Rimush, Manishtushu), and her nephew Naram-Sin.
- **Religious milieu.** A bilingual Sumero-Akkadian religious culture in which the older Sumerian pantheon (Anu, Enlil, Enki, Nanna) was being articulated alongside the rising prominence of Akkadian-language cult (especially of Ishtar, the Akkadian Inanna). The *Exaltation* is a key document for the Inanna-Ishtar syncretism — Enheduanna praises Inanna in Sumerian but attributes to her Ishtar-like characteristics (battle, sexuality, cosmic supremacy), beginning a theological consolidation that culminates in Akkadian Ishtar absorbing Inanna's roles.
- **Who was behind it.** Enheduanna herself, by the texts' own attestation. The first-person colophon of the Temple Hymns names her: "The compiler of the tablets was Enheduanna. My king, something has been created that no one has created before." Modern doubts (see Disputes) have not overturned the attribution.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous Old Babylonian scribal-curriculum transmission preserved the corpus through ~500 years until the manuscript copies from which we read them today were buried in destroyed scribal schools at Ur and Nippur (excavated in the late 19th and early 20th centuries). The Old Babylonian recensions are our only access; no Sargonic-era manuscripts have been recovered.

## Themes and motifs
- [[divine-kingship]] — the Temple Hymns weave together civic patron deities under Anu's and Enlil's authority; the *Exaltation* presents Inanna as cosmic queen above all others.
- [[sacred-marriage]] — the *en*-priestess of Nanna at Ur was understood to be the moon god's spouse on earth; Enheduanna writes from within this sacred-marriage institution and applies its language to her relationship with Inanna.
- [[henotheism-monolatry]] — Enheduanna's hymns elevate Inanna to a position of near-supremacy ("at her loud cries, the gods of the land become afraid"), an early step in the Mesopotamian pattern by which a personal/civic deity is praised in terms that approach (but never quite reach) monotheism.
- **Divine wrath and justification** — the *Exaltation* contains an extended autobiographical complaint section in which the speaker has been driven from the temple and asks Inanna to act on her behalf; the form (lament + invocation + restoration) prefigures structures of the later Mesopotamian penitential prayer tradition and (more loosely) the Biblical psalms of lament.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-1-001-kesh-temple-hymn]] — *shared-milieu* / *redaction-of* (probable): the Temple Hymns include a hymn to the Keš temple; the relationship to the much-older Keš Temple Hymn is unresolved (independent participation in a shared genre, or direct literary adaptation). Ref: Sjöberg/Bergmann 1969; ETCSL t.4.80.1.
- → Later Akkadian Ishtar hymns (e.g., the *Great Hymn to Ishtar*, "Agushaya Hymn", *Ludlul bel nemeqi* lament-of-the-righteous structure) — *shared-milieu*: structural and rhetorical continuities, even though the chain of literary descent is not directly traceable. Ref: Foster 2005, *Before the Muses*.
- → Hebrew psalms of lament (e.g., Psalm 22, Psalm 88) — *parallel-motif*: the lament-petition-vindication structure of the *Exaltation* has been compared to the Biblical genre; no direct dependence is claimed by mainstream scholarship but the shared rhetorical form is striking. Ref: Hallo & van Dijk 1968; Helle 2023.
- → Inanna's descent narrative and the broader Inanna/Ishtar literary cycle — *shared-milieu*: Enheduanna's *Exaltation* is the first sustained theological articulation of Inanna's character that later narrative literature presupposes.

## Disputes
- **Authenticity of authorship.** Most Assyriologists accept the attribution to Enheduanna as the earliest named author; a minority (e.g., Black, Civil) have argued the attribution is a later Old Babylonian scribal tradition projecting authorship onto Enheduanna. Helle (2020, 2023) defends the historical authorship as the most likely reading while noting the texts as we have them are Old Babylonian recensions.
- **Genre of the *Exaltation*.** Whether it is genuinely autobiographical, a stylized literary persona, or a ritual-performance text composed *for* the priestess role rather than by a specific woman is debated. Helle (2020) argues against a strong autobiographical reading.
- **Date of composition.** Sargonic (~2300 BCE) for the underlying texts is consensus; how much linguistic and theological updating occurred in Old Babylonian transmission is contested.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges

- **Earliest named author → founding the personal devotional genre**: Enheduanna (~2300 BCE) is the first human to sign their name to a literary work. The *Exaltation*'s three-part structure — crisis complaint addressed to the deity, petition for divine intervention, hymnic vindication — is structurally identical to Hebrew psalms of lament (Ps 22, Ps 88) and formally pre-dates them by ~1,500 years. Every named author in world literature and every personal prayer-petition genre inherits a tradition Enheduanna inaugurated (Helle 2023, *Enheduana*, Yale UP, pp. 1–22; Hallo & van Dijk 1968, YNER 3, pp. 56–60; → [[phase-1-001-kesh-temple-hymn]] for the genre precursor).

- **Inanna henotheism → Ishtar syncretism → Queen of Heaven theology → Marian intercessory devotion**: The *Exaltation* elevates Inanna to cosmic supremacy ("at her loud cries the gods of the land become afraid"), initiating the Inanna-Ishtar consolidation that transforms her from a civic deity into the Mesopotamian goddess par excellence. The Akkadian *Great Hymn to Ishtar*, then the "Queen of Heaven" worshipped by Judeans per Jeremiah 7:18 and 44:17–25, then the structural parallel between Mesopotamian goddess intercessory theology and Marian devotion form a plausible 4,300-year chain of feminine divine mediator theology (Hallo & van Dijk 1968; Foster 2005, *Before the Muses*, CDL Press, pp. 45–53; → [[inanna-sumerian]], → [[phase-1-017-descent-of-inanna]]).

- **First-person spiritual autobiography — founding gesture of personal religious literature**: The *Exaltation* is the first text in which a named individual records personal loss of sacred office, suffering, appeal to the divine, and restoration — in their own name. The genre of first-person devotional autobiography (Augustine's *Confessions*, Mechthild of Magdeburg, al-Ghazālī's *Deliverance from Error*) is formally prefigured here, though descent is generic-cultural rather than documented transmission. Helle (2023, p. 22) calls this "the founding gesture of all personal religious literature."

- **Temple Hymns scribal curriculum → oldest documented sacred-text preservation system**: The 42 Temple Hymns were part of the standard Nippur scribal curriculum for ~500 years through the Old Babylonian period — the earliest documented example of an institutional system intentionally preserving a religious text corpus across centuries. This is the prototype for the mechanisms that transmitted the Hebrew Bible, the Vedas ([[phase-1-031-rigveda]]), and the Quran. The Nippur transmission model demonstrates how political-religious consolidation (Sargon installing his daughter as *en*-priestess) generates lasting textual canon (Sjöberg & Bergmann 1969, TCS 3; Robson 2001, *Mathematics in Ancient Iraq*, Princeton UP, pp. 30–35).
