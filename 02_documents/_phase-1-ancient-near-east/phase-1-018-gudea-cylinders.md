# The Gudea Cylinders (The Building of Ningirsu's Temple)

## What it is
A two-part Sumerian-language royal-cultic composition celebrating [[gudea-of-lagash]]'s rebuilding of the **Eninnu** ("House of Fifty") temple of [[nin-girsu]] at Girsu, the religious capital of the Lagash city-state, ca. -2125 BCE. **Cylinder A** (~840 lines, 30 columns) narrates the project from divine commission through construction; **Cylinder B** (~430 lines, 24 columns) narrates the installation of the gods in the new temple and the dedication festivals. Combined, the two cylinders constitute approximately 1,270 lines — **the longest extant Sumerian-language literary text**. The composition is organized as follows: (1) Ningirsu appears to Gudea in an ambiguous dream demanding a new temple; (2) Gudea travels to [[nanshe-sumerian]] at Nigin for dream-interpretation; (3) Nanshe interprets — the figure with lapis-lazuli tablet is [[nisaba]] holding the temple-plan derived from astronomical-divinatory order; the warrior figure is Ningirsu; (4) Gudea undertakes elaborate ritual preparations and procurements from across Western Asia — cedar from the Amanus mountains, diorite from Magan (modern Oman), copper from Kimash, gold from Ḫaḫḫum, lapis lazuli from afar (the lapis route ultimately stretches to Badakhshan in Afghanistan); (5) the temple is built over months of labor in a sacralized regime where no slave is struck, no widow oppressed, no quarrel admitted in the city; (6) Cylinder B opens with the temple complete and Ningirsu and his consort [[bau-sumerian]] installed; (7) the seven-day dedication festival establishes the temple's cultic life. The text is among the principal sources for the Sumerian understanding of (a) **temple construction as cosmic-architectural act**, (b) the **dream-vision** as legitimate-rulership genre, (c) **long-distance trade** in the late third millennium, and (d) the **Lagashite local pantheon** in its mature form.

## Primary text — selected excerpts (Edzard 1997, RIME 3/1 / ETCSL 2.1.7)

### Cylinder A, opening: Ningirsu's dream-commission (Cyl. A i 1–v 11)

> On the day that destinies were determined in heaven and on earth,
> Lagash raised its head high in great divine powers (*me*).
> Enlil looked at Lord Ningirsu with approving eyes,
> in our city all that was appropriate was made manifest.
> ...
> Then to the shepherd Gudea, in a dream,
> came the man as great as heaven, as great as earth —
> who was the king Ningirsu —
> who told him to build his house.

(Edzard 1997, pp. 69–70; ETCSL 2.1.7, Cyl. A i 1–v 11.)

### Cylinder A iv 14–v 11: The dream as Gudea reports it to Nanshe

The dream is reported in vivid first-person:

> "In the dream, the first man — as great as heaven, as great as earth, as to his head a god, as to his arms the Imdugud (Anzu) bird, as to his lower parts a flood-wave, lions lay on his right and his left — commanded me to build his house, but I did not understand his intent.
> Then *the sun rose* over the horizon, and a woman appeared — I did not know who she was. She held a stylus of refined silver, she carried a tablet of the favorable stars, she was consulting it.
> Then a second man, a warrior, his arm bent, holding a lapis tablet — was making the plan of a house.
> Before me stood a holy *bashur* basket; a holy brick mold was placed; a fixed brick was in the mold...
> A male donkey of my master eagerly scraped the ground for me.
> This dream — what does it mean?"

(Edzard 1997, pp. 72–74; ETCSL 2.1.7, Cyl. A iv 14–v 11.)

### Cylinder A vi: Nanshe's interpretation

Nanshe identifies each figure:

> "My shepherd, your dream I will interpret for you:
> The man, as great as heaven, as great as earth, with a god's head, with Anzu's arms, with a flood for legs, with lions at his side — that is my brother Ningirsu; he commands you to build his shrine, the Eninnu.
> The sun that rose for you on the horizon — that is your god Ningishzida, like the sun he rose for you from the earth.
> The young woman... the one carrying the holy stylus, the one with the tablet of the favorable stars — that was my sister Nisaba; the favorable star she announces to you for the building of the house.
> The second man, the warrior bent over the lapis tablet — that was Nindub; he is drawing for you the plan of the house.
> The holy basket placed before you, the holy brick mold, the brick in the mold — that is the fated brick of the Eninnu."

(Edzard 1997, pp. 75–78; ETCSL 2.1.7, Cyl. A vi.)

The dream-interpretation passage is the single most explicit articulation of **the divine origin of architectural plans** in Sumerian literature: the temple-plan is *not* invented by the king or by human architects but *received* — Nisaba holds the "tablet of the favorable stars" derived from celestial-divinatory order, and the architect-god Nindub draws the plan from it. The temple is cosmically pre-given; Gudea's role is to execute.

### Cylinder A xiii–xv: Procurement of materials

Gudea's procurement is described in encyclopedic detail. Excerpt:

> Cedar trees that no one has cut he caused to be cut for the lord Ningirsu;
> in their length they were enormous; he loaded them on great rafts.
> The mountain of stone — copper-mountain — he opened, and copper for the lord he caused to be brought.
> Out of the mountain of Magan, blocks of *na* stone (diorite) of great size came forth for him.
> Out of the mountain of Meluhha, gold-dust they brought for him.
> Out of Ḫaḫḫum-mountain, the mountain of *na-šu* stone, blocks came forth for him.
> Out of the mountain of Gubin, the cedar forest, fragrant wood came forth for him.
> Carnelian from Meluhha, lapis lazuli from the mountain of Badaxšum...

(Edzard 1997, pp. 80–82.)

The procurement passage is **one of the principal third-millennium primary sources for trans-regional trade in Western Asia**: Magan = Oman (diorite); Meluhha = Indus Valley (carnelian and gold-dust); the lapis route = Badakhshan, Afghanistan. The text is read by archaeologists of trade as direct primary evidence for the Bronze Age long-distance network.

### Cylinder A xvii–xviii: The sacralized labor

> No widow was made to do work,
> no orphan was made to do work,
> no man was struck by the master's whip,
> no female slave the mistress did not strike with the slap,
> all quarrelers were silent...
> The mother did not call to her child harshly,
> all were at peace; no one acted wickedly.
> The strong did not oppress the weak.

(Edzard 1997, p. 84.)

The passage is a striking articulation of *temple-construction as moral-sacralized regime*. Comparison with Solomon's Temple construction in 1 Kings 5–6 has been drawn (parallel-motif; see Connections).

### Cylinder B i–iii: Installation of the gods in the new temple

> The house was finished; like the mountain rising up, it was set in place.
> Its foundation, like a lapis-lazuli enclosure, was firm in the abzu.
> Its roof was a thundercloud floating between heaven and earth.
> ...
> Ningirsu, the warrior, entered his house;
> Bau, the lady of the city, entered her house...

(Edzard 1997, pp. 88–92; ETCSL 2.1.7, Cyl. B i–iii.)

## Context
- **Political milieu.** The late Gutian period in southern Mesopotamia, post-Akkadian Empire collapse. Lagash under the Second Dynasty (Lagash II), beginning with [[ur-bau]] (Gudea's predecessor and probably father-in-law), navigated the political vacuum with extraordinary cultural flourishing. Whether Gudea ruled as nominal Gutian vassal or as effective independent is debated; the Cylinders themselves do not name a Gutian overlord, suggesting effective autonomy. The composition is contemporary with Gudea's reign (~-2144 to -2124 BCE) — likely inscribed as part of the temple's foundation deposit.
- **Religious milieu.** The mature Lagashite pantheon, theologically articulated: [[nin-girsu]] (city god of Girsu, identified with [[ninurta]] of the Nippur tradition); [[bau-sumerian]] (his consort, chief goddess of Girsu); [[nanshe-sumerian]] (goddess of social justice and dream-interpretation at Nigin); [[nisaba]] (patron of scribes and architectural-astronomical wisdom); [[gatumdug]] (Lagash's mother-goddess). The senior pantheon (An, Enlil, Enki, Ninhursag) presides above. The composition is in part a *theological compendium* of Lagashite religion at its mature stage.
- **Who was behind it.** Anonymous court scribes of Gudea's chancellery, composing in the king's voice. Whether Gudea personally participated in the composition is the standard royal-authorship question; he is unambiguously the *constitutional author*.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Both Cylinder A (AO 27) and Cylinder B (AO 4598) were excavated by Ernest de Sarzec at Telloh (ancient Girsu) in the 1877–1878 season — among the earliest large-scale Mesopotamian temple excavations. They are in the Louvre. The first French edition by François Thureau-Dangin (1907) was definitive for decades; Edzard 1997 (RIME 3/1) is the modern English standard.

## Themes and motifs
- [[divine-kingship]] — the king as commissioned by the city-god through dream-vision, with the temple-plan received from celestial-divinatory order. The Mesopotamian theology of legitimate rulership is here articulated in its mature pre-Ur III form: Gudea *executes* what the gods *command*, mediated by the goddess of social justice (Nanshe) and the goddess of scribal-architectural wisdom (Nisaba).
- [[temple-economy]] — the procurement passage is **the principal third-millennium primary source** for long-distance trade in Western Asia: Magan (Oman), Meluhha (Indus Valley), the Amanus mountains (Levant), Badakhshan (Afghanistan). The temple as economic-and-symbolic center of inter-regional flow.
- [[divine-feminine]] — the prominent role of three goddesses — [[nanshe-sumerian]] (dream-interpreter), [[nisaba]] (architectural-astronomical wisdom), [[bau-sumerian]] (consort-queen of the new temple) — articulates a theology in which the senior masculine warrior-god ([[nin-girsu]]) is operationally enabled by feminine wisdom-and-justice deities. Cylinder B's installation of Bau is one of the longest sustained celebrations of a goddess in Sumerian literature.
- [[sacred-marriage]] — the installation of Ningirsu and Bau in the new temple has been read (Suter 2000) as a sacred-marriage frame: the temple-construction as preparation for the divine couple's union.
- [[wisdom-as-cosmic-order]] — Nisaba's "tablet of the favorable stars" articulates the Sumerian theological-mathematical commitment that the architectural plan of the temple is derived from celestial-divinatory order; the temple is *cosmic structure manifest*.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-1-001-kesh-temple-hymn]] — *parallel-motif* (genre): the Kesh Temple Hymn is the earliest preserved Sumerian temple-hymn (Early Dynastic; Old Babylonian copies). The Gudea Cylinders extend the temple-hymn genre to monumental length and integrate it with royal narrative. Ref: Wilcke 1972 *Der aktuelle Bezug der Sammlung der sumerischen Tempelhymnen*.
- → [[phase-1-003-enheduanna-hymns]] — *parallel-motif* (genre): Enheduanna's 42 Temple Hymns (~-2300) are the systematic Sumerian temple-hymn collection; Hymn 20 dedicates the Eninnu of Lagash, naming Ningirsu. The Gudea Cylinders' Eninnu can be read as the expansion of that single Enheduanna-hymn into a 1,270-line royal-narrative composition. Ref: Sjöberg & Bergmann 1969.
- → [[gudea-of-lagash]] (the person node) — *direct attribution*: Gudea is the constitutional author; the Cylinders are first-person royal speech in places.
- → [[ur-bau]] — *contextual-predecessor*: Ur-Bau initiated the Eninnu rebuilding project; Gudea extended and completed it. The Cylinders implicitly continue Ur-Bau's program. Ref: Edzard 1997.
- → 1 Kings 5–6 (Solomon's Temple construction, Hebrew Bible) — *parallel-motif*: the structural similarities are striking — divine commission of the king to build, long-distance procurement of cedar (1 Kings 5:6, cedars of Lebanon; cf. Gudea's Amanus cedars), sacralized labor regime, dedication festival, divine indwelling of the completed temple. The parallel is *parallel-motif* (recurrence of a Near Eastern temple-building-text genre) rather than direct dependence; the Solomonic narrative is centuries later and was composed in a different political-theological frame. Ref: Hurowitz 1992 *I Have Built You an Exalted House*, which systematically compares the Mesopotamian and biblical temple-construction text traditions.
- → [[phase-1-014-ugaritic-ritual-texts]] (Ugaritic temple-construction motifs in the Baal Cycle, esp. KTU 1.4 — Baal's palace) — *parallel-motif*: Baal's palace-construction sequence in the Ugaritic *Baal Cycle* shares structural features with the Gudea Cylinders — divine commission, procurement of materials (cedar from the Lebanon range), dedication. Ref: Smith 1994 *The Ugaritic Baal Cycle*.
- → Ur III royal hymnography (Ur-Nammu cycle; [[shulgi]] hymns A-X) — *direct influence*: the Gudea Cylinders are the immediate predecessor of the Ur III royal-hymn tradition; Ur-Nammu's hymnography directly continues the Lagash II model. Ref: Klein 1981.

## Disputes
- **Date of inscription.** The Cylinders are conventionally dated to Gudea's reign or shortly thereafter (~-2125). Some scholars argue for slightly later (early Ur-Nammu) inscription, but the dominant view is contemporary with Gudea.
- **Sequence of A and B.** Cylinder A precedes Cylinder B in the narrative (commission-and-construction → installation-and-dedication); whether they were inscribed as a single composition divided across two physical cylinders, or as two related compositions, is unresolved. The dominant view is single composition.
- **The dream-interpretation passage's place in Sumerian religious-literary history.** Whether the dream-interpretation framework is conventional (drawing on older Sumerian dream-vision tradition) or innovative (Gudea's contribution) is debated.
- **Reading of the procurement passage.** Specific geographical identifications (Magan = Oman; Meluhha = Indus) are well-established; the identification of "Ḫaḫḫum" and some other place-names is debated.
- **Comparison with 1 Kings 5–6.** Whether the structural parallel reflects shared Near Eastern temple-construction text-genre (Hurowitz 1992) or coincidental convergence is debated; the parallel-motif reading is the majority view.
