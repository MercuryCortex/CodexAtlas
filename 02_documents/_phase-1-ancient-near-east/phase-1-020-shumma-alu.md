# Šumma Ālu (If a City is Set on a Height)

## What it is
*Šumma ālu ina mēlê šakin* is the great Babylonian terrestrial-omen series — a canonical compendium of approximately 107 tablets containing thousands of conditional-form omens drawn from everyday observable phenomena in the urban Babylonian environment. Unlike its sister series [[phase-1-019-enuma-anu-enlil]] (which reads the sky) and [[phase-1-021-shumma-izbu]] (which reads anomalous births), Šumma ālu reads the city, the house, the body, the animal, and the social encounter. It is the largest single body of evidence for the Babylonian *Lebenswelt* — the texture of inhabited daily life from which the omen-scholarship extracted signs. The form is invariant: "If [observed thing], then [predicted outcome for king / householder / nation]."

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Like all the canonical omen series, Šumma ālu functions in the orbit of royal-scholarly statecraft. Reports from court scholars apply specific Šumma ālu omens to events the king is facing; ritual countermeasures (*namburbi* rituals) are deployed when ominous signs are detected in or near the palace. The series is preserved in greatest depth at the Neo-Assyrian royal libraries (especially Ashurbanipal's at Nineveh).
- **Religious milieu.** The Mesopotamian view: every event in the world is potentially a divine sign; the scholarly enterprise is to know how to read it. Šumma ālu makes this commitment encyclopedic — *anything* in the inhabited city, from the orientation of a house to the position of a scorpion, can carry meaning.
- **Who was behind it.** The Babylonian *ṭupšarrūtu* — the scholarly-scribal class. Šumma ālu was studied alongside EAE and Šumma izbu as part of the *iškaru* canonical curriculum. The same scholars who composed astrological reports to the king also handled domestic and terrestrial omens.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Recovered from the great cuneiform excavations beginning in the 1840s, principally Layard and Rassam's work at Nineveh. The series is widely distributed: Hittite-language adaptations from Boğazköy (~-1400 to -1200) show its reach beyond Mesopotamia proper. Freedman's edition (1998–2017) is the first systematic English-language treatment; pre-Freedman scholarship was limited to scattered tablet editions.

## Themes and motifs
- [[divinatory-omen-reading]] — the largest single Babylonian omen compendium by tablet count; the encyclopedic application of the conditional protasis-apodosis form to the entire urban *Lebenswelt*.
- [[wisdom-as-cosmic-order]] — every event in the inhabited world is divinely legible; the scholar's task is reading.
- [[divinatory-calendar]] — substantial date-conditional material (especially in the tablets dealing with hemerological omens, where day-of-month conditions modify the omen's force).

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-1-019-enuma-anu-enlil]] / [[phase-1-021-shumma-izbu]] — *shared-milieu* / *parallel-motif*: the three canonical Babylonian omen series form a coherent scholarly system. EAE reads the sky; Šumma ālu reads the inhabited environment; Šumma izbu reads anomalous births. All three follow the same form and were transmitted within the same scribal curriculum. Ref: Maul 2018; Koch-Westenholz 1995.
- → [[phase-1-007-code-of-hammurabi]] — *shared-milieu*: both products of the OB scholarly-scribal class; both employ the conditional "If X, then Y" form. The legal-causal "If a man does X, then his punishment is Y" and the omen-causal "If a snake enters the house, then..." share an underlying scribal-grammatical structure that some scholars (e.g., Bottéro 1992) have argued is constitutive of Babylonian rationality.
- → Hittite Boğazköy omen texts (~-1400 to -1200) — *manuscript-transmission*: Šumma ālu material is preserved in Hittite-language adaptation in the Boğazköy archives, demonstrating the series's transmission across language and political boundary within the broader Late Bronze Age scholarly network.
- → Aramaic and post-exilic Jewish omen and dream-interpretation traditions — *parallel-motif* / *shared-milieu*: the everyday-event divinatory tradition that Šumma ālu canonicalizes does not vanish with cuneiform; elements pass into the post-Babylonian Jewish, Mandaean, and later Arabic dream- and sign-interpretation traditions. Ref: Annus 2010 (volume).

## Disputes
- **Compositional history.** Whether the canonical 107-tablet structure emerged through gradual aggregation of independent omen-clusters or by a deliberate Middle Babylonian editorial program is debated. De Zorzi 2009 argues for a more deliberate compositional plan than older scholarship (e.g., Nötscher's mid-20th-century work) assumed.
- **Numbering.** The tablet-count "107" is conventional but contested at the edges; some catalogues list 110+ tablets, others slightly fewer. Freedman's edition presents the most stable current count.
- **Sexual-omen tablets.** Tablets 103–104 (sexual omens) were long considered scandalous and were only fully published in the late 20th century (Guinan, in CRRAI 38, 1996, and elsewhere). They remain a major source for the social history of Babylonian sexuality and have generated considerable secondary literature.
