# Rigveda

## What it is

The Rigveda (*Ṛgveda Saṃhitā*, "Collection of Verses of Praise") is the oldest surviving text of the Indo-Iranian tradition and arguably the oldest substantial literary corpus in any Indo-European language still in continuous living use. Its 1,028 hymns (*sūktas*) comprising roughly 10,600 verses are organized into ten books (*maṇḍalas*). Composed in Vedic Sanskrit by hereditary priestly families (*ṛṣis* — seers), the hymns were addressed to the Vedic devas — above all [[agni]] (sacred fire), [[indra]] (storm-warrior sovereign), [[varuna]] (cosmic keeper of truth), and [[soma]] (the ritual pressed-plant drink-deity) — in the context of śrauta sacrifice. The Rigveda is *śruti* ("what was heard"), held by tradition to be beginningless divine revelation, not composed but perceived by the seers and transmitted verbatim down the centuries.

The ten maṇḍalas divide into strata: Maṇḍalas 2–7 (the "family books") form the oldest core, each attributed to a specific ṛṣi lineage. Maṇḍala 1 and 8–9 form an intermediate stratum. Maṇḍala 10 is latest — more speculative and cosmologically experimental, containing the Purusha Sūkta (10.90), the Nāsadīya (10.129), and the first gestures toward a monistic metaphysics that the Upaniṣads will later systematize.

## Historical context / composition

**Political and social milieu.** The Rigveda was composed by pastoral, semi-nomadic, chariot-using tribal societies in the northwest of the Indian subcontinent (the "Sapta Sindhu" or seven-rivers region of the Punjab). The hymns reference cattle raids, inter-tribal conflict (the famous *dāśarājña* "Battle of the Ten Kings" in Maṇḍala 7.18–19), and a society organized around the twin pillars of the chariot-warrior aristocracy (*kṣatra*) and the sacrificial priesthood (*brahman*). No urban centers or states; the unit is the clan and the tribe.

**Indo-Iranian inheritance.** The linguistic and religious correspondences between the Rigveda and the Avesta (the Zoroastrian scriptures) are among the most important data in comparative religion. The two traditions share: cognate deities (Vedic *Mitra* = Avestan *Mithra*; Vedic *Soma* = Avestan *Haoma*; Vedic *Varuṇa* ~ Avestan *Ahura*); cognate cosmic-order concepts (Vedic *ṛta* = Avestan *aša* "truth/order"); cognate ritual vocabulary (*yajña/yasna* "sacrifice"; *hotṛ/zaotar* "sacrificing priest"; *atharvan/āθravan* "fire-priest"). Crucially, the Avestan reform of Zarathustra *inverts* the Vedic valence: what are *asuras* (demonic) in the developed Veda are the supreme divinity in the Avesta (*ahura*); what are *daevas* (divine) in the Veda are demonic in the Avesta. This inversion confirms a shared Indo-Iranian inheritance at the root, with subsequent divergence.

**External attestation.** The Boğazköy tablets (~-1350 BCE) recording the Mitanni-Hittite peace treaty invoke Mitra, Varuṇa, Indra, and the Nāsatya by name as divine guarantors — strong external evidence that the Vedic pantheon predates the composition of the Rigveda's received text and reached as far as the ancient Near East (Thieme 1960, JAOS).

**Transmission.** The oral transmission of the Rigveda is the most elaborate and precisely documented case of oral preservation in world history. The *pada-pāṭha* ("word-for-word" recitation), *krama-pāṭha* ("step" recitation joining successive words), *jaṭā-pāṭha* ("braided" interweaving), and *ghana-pāṭha* ("dense" palindromic recitation) form a system in which the text is checked against itself at multiple layers. Physical manuscripts date from ~1000 CE; textual stability relative to those manuscripts is remarkable.

## Contents / structure

**The ten Maṇḍalas:**
- **Maṇḍalas 1–9:** Core sacrificial hymns. The dominant deities in the family books are [[indra]] (~250 hymns — more than any other deity), [[agni]] (~200 hymns), [[soma]] (all of Maṇḍala 9 is dedicated to Soma), [[varuna]] and [[mitra-vedic]] (together as guardians of *ṛta*), [[usha]] (the dawn goddess — among the most beautiful hymns), [[rudra-shiva-early]] (the fierce storm-archer who must be appeased), [[vishnu-deity]] (early, cosmic-striding form), and [[dyaus-pita]] (sky-father, cognate with Greek Zeus, Roman Jupiter, Norse Tyr — a rare trace of the oldest Indo-European theology).
- **Maṇḍala 10 (latest stratum):** Contains the most philosophically daring hymns:
  - **Purusha Sūkta (10.90):** The primordial being Purusha whose cosmic dismemberment creates the universe — moon from his mind, sun from his eye, sky from his navel, earth from his feet, the four social varṇas from his body-parts. The Vedic locus classicus for [[cosmic-body-cosmogony]].
  - **Nāsadīya Sūkta (10.129):** The "Hymn of Creation" — a profound cosmogonic meditation on non-being and being: "Then was neither non-being nor being... Who really knows? Who shall proclaim it here?" The first articulation of philosophical aporia about origins in Indian thought.
  - **Vivāha Sūkta (10.85):** The wedding hymn; cosmological framework for marriage rituals.

## Defining contributions

**1. The Purusha Sūkta and cosmic-body cosmogony.** Rigveda 10.90 is the direct textual source for the [[cosmic-body-cosmogony]] theme in the vault: the universe is created from the dismembered body of a primordial being. This is a MASSIVE-WIN cross-tradition edge: the same myth structure appears in the Babylonian *Enuma Elish* — where Marduk creates heaven and earth from the dismembered body of [[tiamat]] — and in the Norse *Prose Edda* where the world is created from the slain body of [[ymir]]. Jamison-Brereton 2014 and Elizarenkova 1995 treat the Purusha Sūkta; for the Mesopotamian parallel see [[phase-1-008-enuma-elish]].

**2. Ṛta as cosmic order — the Indo-Iranian root of both Hindu and Zoroastrian ethics.** Vedic *ṛta* (literally "that which has been set in motion correctly") is the cosmic, ritual, and moral truth that Varuṇa and Mitra uphold. This concept is directly cognate with Avestan *aša* (Gathic Avestan *ašā*) — the supreme concept in Zoroastrian cosmology, opposed to *druj* ("the lie"). Both words descend from Proto-Indo-Iranian *\*Hr̥tás*, "truth, cosmic order." This makes the Rigveda the oldest textual stratum for the Indo-Iranian moral dualism that feeds directly into [[tradition-zoroastrianism]] and through it into Jewish-Christian angelology and eschatology (the Satan-as-Angra-Mainyu transfer; see [[theme-satan-angra-mainyu-transfer]] and [[theme-zoroastrian-jewish-exchange]]).

**3. Vedic *asura* / *deva* inversion as the key to Indo-Iranian divergence.** In the Rigveda, *asura* can still denote powerful, lordly divine beings; by the later Veda it shifts to mean "demon." The Avesta inverts this: Zarathustra's *ahura* ("lord") is the supreme good god (Ahura Mazda = "Wise Lord"), and *daēva* are the rejected demonic powers. This inversion — a shared inheritance theologically sundered — is one of the most important phenomena in comparative religion and can be illustrated through the Rigveda → [[phase-2-002-gathas-of-zarathustra]] edge.

**4. The Rigveda as the root of the entire Vedic Hindu tradition.** Every major strand of Hinduism flows from the Rigveda: the Brāhmaṇas (ritual commentary), Āraṇyakas (forest meditation), Upaniṣads (philosophical speculation), Purāṇas (mythological expansion), and the living traditions of [[tradition-vedic-hinduism]].

## Disputes

- **Date and Indo-Aryan migration.** The mainstream position (Witzel, Jamison, Parpola) places the family books at ~1500–1200 BCE and correlates their composition with the Indo-Aryan migration into the subcontinent from the Central Asian steppe via the BMAC. An "Out of India" counter-position (Talageri, some others) argues for earlier and indigenous composition; rejected by the philological mainstream but politically significant in Indian public discourse.
- **Soma plant identification.** What was Soma? Proposals include *Amanita muscaria* (Wasson 1968), *Ephedra* (Falk 1989 — currently favored by Haoma scholarship), *Peganum harmala*, *Cannabis*, and a now-extinct mountain plant. Unresolved.
- **Veda as "scripture".** The Rigveda is *śruti* (revelation), not *smṛti* (remembered tradition); its canonical authority in Hinduism is absolute. Modern Western comparative-religion usage of "scripture" for the Veda requires care — the text was not written down by original intent and its transmission context is oral-liturgical.
