# Tummal Chronicle

## Overview
The **Tummal Chronicle** is a short Sumerian literary text (~42 lines) recording the construction, destruction, and reconstruction of the **Tummal** — a sacred district of the temple of **Ninlil** (consort of Enlil), located near Nippur. The text survives in Old Babylonian copies (~1800–1700 BCE) but records events from the Early Dynastic through Ur III periods (~2700–2000 BCE).

Its primary interest for the vault is as one of the earliest *regnal chronicle* texts: it names specific Sumerian kings ([[enmebaragesi]], [[mes-anepada]], [[gilgamesh-king]], [[shulgi]], [[ur-nammu]]) in connection with specific building acts at a named sacred site. This makes it a key source for the historicity of figures who otherwise appear only in mythological texts.

## Content and structure
The chronicle follows a recurring formula:
> *"[King X] made the Tummal flourish / brought Ninlil to the Tummal. After [king X], the Tummal fell into disuse. [King Y] made the Tummal flourish again..."*

Kings attested in the Tummal Chronicle (in sequence):
1. **[[enmebaragesi]]** of Kish (~2700–2650 BCE) — oldest historically attested Sumerian king; his name appears on physical artifacts from the Early Dynastic I period, giving the Chronicle a datable anchor.
2. **Aka** of Kish — son of Enmebaragesi; conflicts with Gilgamesh in the *Gilgamesh and Akka* poem.
3. **[[mes-anepada]]** of Ur (~2600 BCE) — founder of the First Dynasty of Ur; attested in the Royal Cemetery of Ur.
4. **[[gilgamesh-king]]** (~2600 BCE) — the historical king of Uruk whose deeds were later mythologized in the Epic of Gilgamesh; the Chronicle is one of the main sources for his historical existence.
5. **Nannaya** of Uruk — lesser-known king, otherwise undocumented.
6. Various Ur III kings including [[ur-nammu]] (~2112–2095 BCE) — founder of the Ur III dynasty, famous for the first surviving law code.

## Significance
- **Historicity of Gilgamesh**: The Tummal Chronicle's listing of Gilgamesh as a historical king who built at Tummal is the clearest corroboration that the Epic's protagonist has a real historical foundation. Combined with the **Sumerian King List** and the *Gilgamesh and Akka* poem (which names the historically-attested Enmebaragesi as an adversary), the Chronicle anchors Gilgamesh in ~2600 BCE.
- **Temple-building as dynastic legitimation**: Every major king demonstrates his authority by constructing or restoring a sacred site. The formula "X made Tummal flourish / Tummal fell into disuse" implies that the sacred site's condition reflects the spiritual health of the dynasty — a pattern repeated in the Hebrew Bible (Solomon's Temple / its destruction / Ezra's return).
- **Ninlil / Enlil worship as Nippur orthodoxy**: The Tummal was a Ninlil sanctuary in the Nippur sacred complex. Control of Nippur's temples was the central source of religious legitimacy for Mesopotamian kings; the King List records that kingship was "lowered from heaven" to specific cities. The Tummal Chronicle is the local view of the same dynastic sequence.

## Cross-tradition connections
- **Temple-building chronicle ↔ [[phase-2-034-books-of-kings|Books of Kings]] structure**: Both the Tummal Chronicle and the Hebrew Books of Kings use a similar formula: king X did/built [X], king X died, king Y succeeded. Both evaluate kings by their relationship to a central sanctuary (Tummal / Jerusalem Temple). The structural parallel reflects a shared ancient Near Eastern template for royal-legitimacy narrative.
- **[[gilgamesh-king]] historicity**: The chronicle ties the mythological Gilgamesh to real history, documenting the transformation from historical king to epic hero — the same trajectory as [[enkidu]], [[enmebaragesi]], and [[ur-nammu]] (whose law code is partially preserved).
