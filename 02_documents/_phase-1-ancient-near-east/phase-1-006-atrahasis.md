# Atrahasis

## What it is
A three-tablet Akkadian narrative poem (~1200 lines total when complete) that integrates Mesopotamian creation, human anthropology, and flood into one continuous story. Tablet I: the gods (the Igigi) labor under Enlil's command digging irrigation channels; they rebel; in response, Enki and Ninhursag/Nintu create humanity from clay mixed with the flesh and blood of a slain god (Wê-ila) to take over the work. Tablet II: humans multiply, become noisy, disturb Enlil's rest; Enlil tries successive depopulation strategies (plague, drought, famine), each thwarted by Enki who instructs the chosen human Atrahasis ("Exceeding-Wise") on how to evade them. Tablet III: Enlil resolves to send a flood; Enki, sworn to secrecy, evades the oath by speaking to the reed wall of Atrahasis's house (and thus through the wall to Atrahasis); Atrahasis builds a boat, saves himself and animals; after the flood the gods are starving without human offerings, regret the destruction, and devise demographic controls (barrenness, infant mortality, celibate priestesses) instead of total annihilation. The titular hero's name *Atra-ḫasīs* corresponds to Sumerian Ziusudra and to the SB Gilgamesh's Ut-napishtim.

## Primary text — selected passages

### Tablet I, lines 1–8 — "When the gods like men" (the divine-labor opening)
*Trans. Dalley, S. (2000). Myths from Mesopotamia. Oxford World's Classics.*
> When the gods like men
> bore the work and suffered the toil —
> the toil of the gods was great,
> the work was heavy, the distress was much —
> the great Anunnaki, the seven of them,
> were making the Igigi suffer the work.

*The epic opens with the gods themselves performing labor (digging the Tigris and Euphrates channels) before humans were created to relieve them. The cause of human creation is labor management — the reason humans exist, in Mesopotamian theology, is to serve the gods.*

### Tablet I — Human creation from divine blood and clay (lines ~220–235, reconstructed)
*Trans. Lambert, W.G., and Millard, A.R. (1969). Atra-Ḫasīs: The Babylonian Story of the Flood. Oxford UP.*
> "Let them slaughter one god,
> and let the gods be purified in the immersion.
> From his flesh and his blood
> let Ninhursag mix clay.
> God and man
> shall be mixed together in clay.
> In the flesh of the god let a spirit remain,
> so that the living will know its sign —
> and let it not be forgotten."

*The god Wê-ila ("god with reason") is killed; his divine spirit (eṭemmu) is mixed with clay to create humanity. The divine blood in every human body is the Mesopotamian theological anthropology — and the oldest documented origin of what Genesis 1:26 reframes as the imago Dei.*

### Tablet III — Enki's reed-wall oath-evasion (lines iii.9–18)
*Trans. Lambert & Millard 1969*
> Wall, listen to me!
> Reed fence, attend all my words!
> Destroy your house, build a boat;
> abandon wealth, seek survival.
> Reject possessions, save life!
> Load the seed of all living creatures into the boat.
> The boat that you shall build —
> its dimensions shall be measured.

*Bound by oath not to warn Atrahasis directly of the flood decree, Enki speaks to the wall — technically not addressing the man, while ensuring he hears everything. The verbatim device appears also in the Eridu Genesis and is adapted in Gilgamesh Tablet XI.*

*Citation: Lambert, W.G., and Millard, A.R. (1969). Atra-Ḫasīs: The Babylonian Story of the Flood. Oxford UP. Dalley, S. (2000). Myths from Mesopotamia. Oxford World's Classics.*

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Late Old Babylonian period — specifically, the principal manuscript is dated by colophon to the eleventh year of Ammi-ṣaduqa of Babylon (~1635 BCE), great-great-grandson of Hammurabi. The scribal milieu is Sippar.
- **Religious milieu.** Standard Old Babylonian pantheon, with Enki and Enlil in their classical complementary-antagonistic roles. The text crystallizes the Mesopotamian theological anthropology: humans exist *to serve the gods* (to provide food and labor); the gods need humans, and this mutual dependence is the cosmological frame. Compare the human-creation passages of [[phase-1-008-enuma-elish]] (humans made from Kingu's blood, same purpose).
- **Who was behind it.** Two scribes signed colophons (Ku-Aya, Nūr-Aya); ultimate composer anonymous. The narrative integrates older Sumerian sources — the Sumerian Flood Story / Eridu Genesis attests an independent (and perhaps older) version of the flood whose hero is Ziusudra.
- **Discovery / transmission.** OB tablets entered the British Museum collection in the late 19th century. The SB Library of [[ashurbanipal]] fragments were among those identified by George Smith in the 1870s. The Atrahasis flood story's direct relationship to the SB Gilgamesh XI flood was demonstrated by Lambert & Millard (1969) — Tablet XI is a literary adaptation of Atrahasis Tablet III, sometimes line-for-line.

## Themes and motifs
- [[flood-motif]] — the **prototype Mesopotamian flood narrative**, with all the elements that recur in the SB Gilgamesh and in Genesis 6–9: divine decision, warning of the chosen man, boat construction, deluge, sacrifice after landing, divine regret, post-flood reorganization. The motive in Atrahasis is *noise* (human overpopulation disturbing Enlil); the SB Gilgamesh blurs this; Genesis re-motivates as *human wickedness*.
- [[primordial-waters]] — the flood as cosmic-scale water; the cosmogonic water-chaos returning to undo the human order.
- [[divine-council]] — the gods deliberate, vote, and split: Enlil decrees destruction, Enki saves humanity. The council-divided-on-humanity structure recurs in many traditions.
- [[creation-by-word]] — only partial: humans are made from clay and god-flesh (a mixed material+verbal creation), but the divine decisions are spoken. Atum's creation-by-word (Egypt) and Genesis 1's *fiat* are stronger forms.
- **Demographic control as theological etiology** — the post-flood "solution" — barrenness, miscarriage, infant mortality, celibate priestesses — etiologizes social institutions and biological realities as the gods' response to overpopulation. A striking pre-classical articulation of demographic-theological reasoning.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-1-004-gilgamesh-old-babylonian]] — *shared-milieu* (OB period; same scribal culture). The flood is **not** in the OB Gilgamesh — it is incorporated into the later SB Gilgamesh Tablet XI as a direct adaptation of Atrahasis Tablet III. Ref: George 2003 vol. 1, pp. 22–28; Lambert & Millard 1969.
- → [[phase-1-008-enuma-elish]] — *parallel-motif*: human creation from divine blood (Wê-ila in Atrahasis; Kingu in Enuma Elish). Both ground human existence in slain-god material. Enuma Elish appears to know and rework the Atrahasis anthropology. Ref: Lambert 2013.
- → Hebrew Bible flood (Gen 6–9) — *parallel-motif* / *contested-influence*: the closest extra-biblical parallel is the SB Gilgamesh XI, but SB Gilgamesh XI itself depends on Atrahasis. The Genesis flood narrative is widely understood as an Israelite reception (whether direct or via shared ANE flood-tradition) of the Atrahasis-tradition material. Genesis re-motivates the flood as a response to *moral evil* rather than to *noise*, and structures it around two interwoven sources (J and P) per the Documentary Hypothesis. Refs: Lambert & Millard 1969; Heidel 1949; George 2003.
- → Sumerian Eridu Genesis / Sumerian Flood Story — *redaction-of* / *parallel-motif*: the Sumerian flood story preserves an earlier (or parallel) version whose hero is Ziusudra. Ref: Jacobsen 1981.

## Disputes
- **Direction of influence with Sumerian Flood Story.** Whether Atrahasis is a direct Akkadian redaction of the Sumerian Eridu Genesis, or both derive from a common older oral tradition, is debated.
- **Mechanism of influence on Genesis.** Direct literary dependence (older view); shared ANE flood-tradition without specific literary mediation (current majority); polemical engagement with a known Babylonian tradition during the exile and after (Hartman, Levenson). The literary parallels with SB Gilgamesh XI are too close for accident; whether the Hebrew authors knew Atrahasis directly or via SB Gilgamesh is itself a sub-debate.
- **Date of composition.** OB Ammi-ṣaduqa dating (~1635 BCE) is firm for our principal manuscript; whether the composition was already classical at that point or recent is uncertain.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges

- **Humanity created from divine blood + clay — oldest documented origin of divine-image-in-man theology**: In Atrahasis, the god Wê-ila is killed and from his blood and clay humans are fashioned. "God and man shall be mixed together in clay" — the *eṭemmu* (spirit/ghost) of the slain god lives on in humanity, making humans permanently partly divine. This is the Mesopotamian theological anthropology that Genesis 1:26 ("Let us make man in our image") and Genesis 2:7 ("God breathed into his nostrils the breath of life") both engage and transform. The divine-blood-clay anthropology is the deepest layer of the tradition that generates *imago Dei* theology — the claim that humans are structurally different from all other creatures because they share material with the divine (Lambert & Millard 1969, Oxford UP; → [[phase-2-018-hebrew-bible-p-source]], → [[phase-1-016-eridu-genesis-flood]]).

- **Atrahasis Tablet III → Gilgamesh Tablet XI — the most clearly documented direct literary adaptation in the ancient Near East**: George (2003, *The Babylonian Gilgamesh Epic*, Oxford UP, vol. 1, pp. 22–28) demonstrates that Standard Babylonian Gilgamesh Tablet XI is a near-verbatim adaptation of Atrahasis Tablet III. The reed-wall speech, the boat-construction instructions, the divine oath, the seven-day flood duration, the post-flood sacrifice, and the gods "gathering like flies" over the offerings — all transfer line-for-line. This is the most clearly documented direct literary adaptation in the ancient Near East, comparable in explicitness to Matthew/Luke's use of Mark in the Synoptic Problem. The chain Atrahasis → Gilgamesh XI → Genesis flood is the clearest transmission sequence in ANE-to-Hebrew-Bible comparative study (George 2003; → [[phase-1-004-gilgamesh-old-babylonian]]).

- **Demographic control as theological etiology — the oldest sociology-of-religion argument**: After the flood, Enlil institutes demographic control mechanisms (barrenness, infant death, celibate priestesses) instead of total annihilation. These social institutions are etiologized as the gods' rational solution to the overpopulation that caused the flood. This is the oldest known text to offer a cosmogonic-etiological explanation for why social institutions such as infertility, celibacy, and infant mortality exist — the Mesopotamian equivalent of Durkheim's claim that religion is society explaining itself. Genesis does not inherit this etiology; its silence on the matter is itself theologically significant (Lambert & Millard 1969; Jacobsen 1981, *JBL* 100/4; → [[phase-1-016-eridu-genesis-flood]]).

- **Flood motivation: noise → moral evil — Israelite theological reversal documented as intentional counter-argument**: Atrahasis is explicit: the gods flood the earth because humans are too noisy — Enlil cannot sleep. Genesis 6's framing (human moral wickedness, "every inclination of the thoughts of their hearts was only evil") is a deliberate theological reversal. The Hebrew authors knew the Mesopotamian tradition (Babylonian exile guaranteed exposure to the SB Gilgamesh at minimum); reframing the flood as a moral statement rather than a managerial convenience is ancient Israel's most explicit counter-argument to Babylonian anthropology — transforming the cosmological frame from "humans exist to serve the gods" to "humans are morally accountable agents created in God's image" (Heidel, A. 1949, *The Gilgamesh Epic and Old Testament Parallels*, UChicago; George 2003; → [[phase-2-005-hebrew-bible-j-source]]).
