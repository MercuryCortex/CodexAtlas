# Gilgamesh — Old Babylonian Version

## What it is
The Old Babylonian (OB) Akkadian Gilgamesh is the earliest *integrated* epic redaction of the Gilgamesh material — the first time the older, separate Sumerian Bilgames poems were synthesized into a continuous Akkadian narrative of one hero's life. It survives in multiple incomplete OB tablets (~1800–1700 BCE), of which the Pennsylvania and Yale tablets are the most substantial. The OB narrative covers Gilgamesh's tyranny over Uruk, the creation of Enkidu in the wild, their meeting and friendship, the journey to the Cedar Forest, the killing of the monster Huwawa/Humbaba, Ishtar's rejected proposal and her sending of the Bull of Heaven, Enkidu's death, and Gilgamesh's wandering in grief — culminating (in fragmentary form) in his search for immortality. The famous flood narrative of Tablet XI is **not** present in the OB version; it is incorporated into the later Standard Babylonian recension (~1200 BCE, attributed to Sin-leqi-unninni) by direct adaptation from [[phase-1-006-atrahasis]].

## Context
- **Political milieu.** The Old Babylonian period (~1900–1600 BCE) — the era of Hammurabi and his successors. Akkadian had largely replaced Sumerian as a spoken language; Sumerian remained the prestige scribal language for older literature. The OB scribal schools (*edubba*) curated, taught, and redacted older Sumerian compositions into Akkadian. Gilgamesh's integration is part of this larger movement.
- **Religious milieu.** Polytheistic Mesopotamia with a developed pantheon. The OB Gilgamesh treats the gods as morally ambivalent — Ishtar/Inanna is presented critically (her treatment of past lovers, including Dumuzi, is enumerated); Enlil decrees death; Shamash watches and aids the heroes. The horizon of the epic is sharply mortal: the hero who would be god is taught the limits of humanity.
- **Who was behind it.** Anonymous OB scribal authors working from the older Sumerian Bilgames poems (which they would also have copied as Sumerian). The Sumerian originals known to us include "Bilgames and Huwawa," "Bilgames and the Bull of Heaven," "Bilgames and the Netherworld," "Bilgames and Akka," and "The Death of Bilgames."
- **Discovery / transmission.** Modern recovery began in the 1850s with George Smith's identification of the Babylonian flood tablet (which is in fact Standard Babylonian, not OB). The OB tablets — Pennsylvania, Yale — were published in the early 20th century (Langdon 1917, Jastrow & Clay 1920). Additional OB material has continued to surface, including the Schøyen Collection tablets published by George (2018).

## Themes and motifs
- [[afterlife-judgment]] — though not a courtroom-judgment scene like Egypt, the OB Gilgamesh confronts mortality directly: Enkidu's death sequence and Gilgamesh's grief are some of world literature's earliest sustained meditations on what happens after death.
- [[divine-kingship]] — Gilgamesh is "two-thirds god, one-third mortal," son of the goddess Ninsun. His kingship is hybrid divine; his mortality is the wound that drives the narrative.
- [[flood-motif]] — *latent* in the OB version (not present in surviving OB tablets but the flood story circulates in [[phase-1-006-atrahasis]] in the same period); the SB redactor draws it in.
- [[dying-rising-god]] — *contrast*: Gilgamesh is precisely **not** a dying-rising god. He fails to obtain immortality. The plant of rebirth is stolen by a snake. The epic deliberately rejects the soteriological pattern that succeeds with Dumuzi/Tammuz, Osiris, and Baal — making the OB Gilgamesh a unique anti-soteriological mortality meditation in the ANE.
- [[sacred-marriage]] — Ishtar's marriage-proposal to Gilgamesh is the inverted form: he refuses, enumerating her abuses of past consorts (including [[dumuzi-tammuz]]). This is a critique of the sacred-marriage institution from within Mesopotamian literature itself.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-1-006-atrahasis]] — *direct-quote* (in the later SB recension): the SB Tablet XI flood story is a literary adaptation of Atrahasis Tablet III, lifting whole sequences. The OB Gilgamesh predates this incorporation. Ref: George 2003 vol. 1, pp. 22–28.
- → [[phase-1-005-instructions-of-shuruppak]] — *parallel-motif* / *shared-milieu*: Shuruppak (Ut-napishtim's city in the later flood) is the same setting; the Gilgamesh-flood figure Ut-napishtim is "the man of Shuruppak." Ref: George 2003.
- → [[phase-1-008-enuma-elish]] — *shared-milieu*: contemporary OB / late OB scribal milieu; both are products of the Akkadian literary standardization.
- → Hebrew Bible flood narrative (Gen 6–9) — *parallel-motif* (via Atrahasis and the SB redaction): the strongest Mesopotamian parallel to Genesis is the SB Gilgamesh Tablet XI's flood, which itself derives from Atrahasis. Heidel (1949) and George (2003) are the classic analyses. Ref: Heidel 1949, *The Gilgamesh Epic and Old Testament Parallels*.
- → Hebrew Bible Qohelet/Ecclesiastes — *parallel-motif* (contested): some scholars (e.g., van der Toorn, Jones) note that Qohelet's mortality-and-pleasure counsel resembles the Old Babylonian Gilgamesh advice from Šiduri the alewife in the OB Sippar tablet ("Gilgamesh, where do you run? You will not find the life that you seek. ... Let your stomach be full, make merry day and night..."). Mediation chain is debated. Ref: Jones, "Bound by Bondage: Gilgamesh and Qohelet" (2008).

## Disputes
- **Compositional date.** OB consensus is ~1800 BCE; some scholars date the integrated Akkadian Gilgamesh as early as the late third millennium (Ur III period) or as late as the late OB. George (2003) frames a Hammurabi-era to early late-OB range.
- **Authorship.** The SB version is attributed to Sin-leqi-unninni in colophons (~1200 BCE) as redactor; the OB is anonymous and may itself be composite.
- **Relationship to the Sumerian Bilgames poems.** Whether the OB redactor worked directly from the Sumerian poems, from already-existing Akkadian short narratives, or both, is debated.
- **Qohelet parallel.** Mainstream Hebrew Bible scholarship treats the resemblance as a literary topos within the broader ANE wisdom tradition rather than direct borrowing; minority views argue for closer dependence.
