# Inanna's Descent to the Netherworld

## What it is
A ~412-line Sumerian narrative poem in which the goddess [[inanna-sumerian]] descends from the upper world to the netherworld kingdom of her elder sister [[ereshkigal]], is stripped at the seven gates of her seven *me* (cosmic powers and divine garments), is killed and her corpse hung on a hook for three days and three nights, is revived through the intercession of [[enki-ea]] who sends two sexless beings (the *kurgarra* and *galaturra*) with the food of life and water of life, returns to the upper world bound by the netherworld's law to provide a substitute, and — after finding her vizier Ninshubur and her sons in proper mourning — fixes her substitute on her husband [[dumuzi-tammuz]], who is found in luxurious unconcern. Dumuzi is seized by the netherworld's demons (the *galla*) despite a brief escape attempt aided by the sun god Utu and his sister [[geshtinanna]]; in the closing resolution, Geshtinanna offers herself in substitution, and the two siblings divide the year — Dumuzi in the netherworld for half, Geshtinanna for the other half. The composition is the principal Sumerian articulation of the **[[dying-rising-god]] / divine-descent-and-return** motif complex, and the prototype from which the Akkadian *Descent of Ishtar* (Standard Babylonian period) is directly adapted. It also etiologizes the seasonal absence-and-return of Dumuzi (the seasonal vegetation cycle) and the Mesopotamian theological commitment that **no one ascends from the netherworld without a substitute**.

## Primary text — selected excerpts (Sladek 1974 / ETCSL 1.4.1)

### Inanna's resolution to descend (lines 1–13)

> From the great heaven she set her mind on the great below.
> From the great heaven the goddess set her mind on the great below.
> From the great heaven Inanna set her mind on the great below.
> My mistress abandoned heaven, abandoned earth — to the netherworld she descended.
> Inanna abandoned heaven, abandoned earth — to the netherworld she descended.
> She abandoned her office of holy *en*-priesthood, and to the netherworld she descended.
> ...
> She had the seven divine powers (*me*) in her hand.
> She gathered all the divine powers and grasped them in her hand.

(Sladek 1974, pp. 103ff.; ETCSL 1.4.1, lines 1–13.)

The triple repetition is a Sumerian literary device (*parallelismus membrorum*) marking a high-register narrative opening.

### The seven gates: Inanna stripped at each (lines 119–164, selectively)

> When she entered the first gate,
> the *shugurra*, the crown of the steppe, was removed from her head.
> "What is this?"
> "Be silent, Inanna; the laws of the netherworld are perfect. Inanna, do not question the customs of the netherworld."
>
> When she entered the second gate,
> the lapis-lazuli rod and line was taken from her.
> "What is this?"
> "Be silent..."

The seven gates strip seven items in fixed sequence: crown, lapis rod-and-line, lapis necklace, twin egg-shaped beads, breast-plate ("come, man, come"), gold ring, breech-cloth. By the seventh gate she stands naked before Ereshkigal.

(Sladek 1974, pp. 124ff.; ETCSL 1.4.1, lines 119–164.)

### Inanna's death and the three-day corpse (lines 168–172)

> The seven judges of the netherworld, the Anuna gods, pronounced the judgment against her.
> They looked upon her — it was the look of death;
> they spoke to her — it was the speech of anger;
> they shouted at her — it was the shout of guilt.
> The woman, so afflicted, was turned into a corpse;
> the corpse was hung on a hook.

(Sladek 1974, pp. 132–134; ETCSL 1.4.1, lines 168–172.)

The three-day-corpse-on-a-hook motif is among the most cited in comparative-religion discussions of dying-rising-god narratives. The text states the duration as "three days and three nights" before Ninshubur initiates the rescue.

### Enki's rescue stratagem (lines 219–266)

[[enki-ea]] fashions from the dirt under his fingernails the two sexless beings — the *kurgarra* and the *galaturra* — and instructs them in netherworld protocol: they are to find Ereshkigal moaning ("Oh, my insides! Oh, my outsides!") and to commiserate with her cries ("Oh, your insides! Oh, your outsides!"); she will gratefully offer them gifts; they must refuse all gifts but the corpse on the hook. The plan succeeds; the corpse is restored by the food of life and water of life.

(Sladek 1974, pp. 152ff.; ETCSL 1.4.1, lines 219–266.)

### The substitution and the Dumuzi-Geshtinanna rotation (lines 354–412)

Inanna returns trailing demons who will not depart without her substitute. She refuses to give Ninshubur, Shara, and Lulal — they are properly mourning. She finds Dumuzi seated in royal splendor under an apple tree, not mourning her. She fixes her eyes on him in fury and consigns him. Dumuzi flees; Utu transforms him into a snake; he hides at Geshtinanna's; the demons seize him. The composition closes with Geshtinanna's offer to substitute for half the year:

> "You [Dumuzi] half the year; your sister [Geshtinanna] half the year.
> When you are called, on that day you will stay;
> when your sister is called, on that day you will be released."
> Holy Inanna gave Dumuzi as her substitute...
> Holy Ereshkigal — praise be to you!

(Sladek 1974, pp. 207–210; ETCSL 1.4.1, lines 405–412.)

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Old Babylonian scribal-school transmission (~-1900 to -1600 BCE). The composition's *cultic origin* is considerably older: third-millennium Sumerian Inanna-Dumuzi cult-songs (sacred-marriage texts; Dumuzi laments) attest the religious complex from which this literary composition emerged. The principal cult sites are Inanna's Eanna at Uruk and the Dumuzi sanctuaries at Bad-tibira and Kuara.
- **Religious milieu.** Standard Sumerian theological frame: the senior pantheon (An, Enlil, Enki) above; Inanna's family complex (Nanna-Sin her father, Utu her brother, Ereshkigal her sister, Dumuzi her husband) at the secondary level; the netherworld below. The composition articulates the theological-procedural commitment that the netherworld operates by *fixed law* — even the senior gods cannot freely ascend or release the dead; substitution is the legal instrument.
- **Who was behind it.** Anonymous Sumerian scribal-school composers, principally at Nippur. The composition was standard scribal-school curriculum and is among the most widely-copied Sumerian literary texts.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Fragments of the Sumerian *Descent* were first identified by Stephen Langdon and Edward Chiera in the early 20th century; Samuel Noah Kramer made the composition widely known to non-specialists in the mid-20th century (Kramer 1937, 1942, and successive iterations). Sladek's 1974 Johns Hopkins dissertation is the definitive critical edition. The Akkadian *Descent of Ishtar* — much shorter, ~140 lines — was identified in the Library of Ashurbanipal materials in the 19th century, well before its Sumerian source; Lapinkivi 2010 provides the modern Akkadian edition.

## Themes and motifs
- [[dying-rising-god]] — the **prototype** Mesopotamian instance, and arguably the *prototype Near Eastern instance* of the motif complex. The motif's later instances — the Akkadian Descent of Ishtar; the Ugaritic Baal cycle's Baal-and-Mot section; the Phoenician-Greek Adonis; the Greek Persephone (in Demeter's Eleusinian myth); the Greco-Egyptian Osiris; arguably (with extensive theological reframing) the Christian Passion/Resurrection — all share family-resemblance features with this composition. Frazer's *Golden Bough* assembled the comparative complex; current scholarship (since J. Z. Smith *Drudgery Divine* 1990) is more cautious about the integrity of the "dying-rising-god" category and emphasizes the specifics of each tradition. Nevertheless the *descent-stripping-death-revival-substitution* structural sequence is the recognizable shared template.
- [[sacred-marriage]] — the broader Inanna-Dumuzi complex is the cultic context; the Descent narrates the *rupture* of the sacred marriage and its seasonal resolution through substitution.
- [[divine-council]] — the *seven judges of the netherworld, the Anuna gods* pronounce judgment; the legal-procedural framing of netherworld justice.
- [[psychopomp]] — Ninshubur (Inanna's vizier) and Geshtinanna both play guide-and-substitute roles; Enki's *kurgarra* and *galaturra* are netherworld-traveling agents.
- [[divine-feminine]] — Inanna's status as a major sovereign goddess undertaking and surviving the descent is among the most prominent third-millennium articulations of divine feminine power. The 20th-century feminist-scholarship reception (Wolkstein & Kramer 1983; Meador 1992; many subsequent) made this composition a foundational text in the recovery of the *divine feminine* category.

## Connections (provisional)
- → *Descent of Ishtar* (Akkadian, Standard Babylonian period; Library of Ashurbanipal) — *direct redaction*: the Akkadian *Descent of Ishtar* is a much-condensed direct adaptation of the Sumerian *Inanna's Descent*, retaining the seven-gates motif, the death-and-revival, and the Dumuzi substitution. Lapinkivi 2010 provides the standard edition. The Akkadian text is centrally concerned with the seasonal vegetation cycle and Tammuz-mourning — features secondary in the Sumerian original.
- → [[phase-1-004-gilgamesh-old-babylonian]] — *shared-milieu*: Gilgamesh's encounter with Inanna in the OB Gilgamesh and especially in the SB Gilgamesh Tablet VI (where Gilgamesh rejects Inanna's marriage offer and lists her dead lovers, beginning with Dumuzi) presupposes the *Descent* tradition. Ref: George 2003.
- → [[phase-1-013-baal-cycle]] — *parallel-motif* (Ugaritic): the Baal-and-Mot section of the Baal Cycle (KTU 1.5–1.6) narrates Baal's descent to Mot's netherworld, his death, his sister Anat's mourning and avenging, and his return. The structural parallel with Inanna's Descent is striking, though the Ugaritic composition is centuries later and the family-resemblance may operate through shared ANE common-stock rather than direct dependence. Ref: Smith 1994 *The Ugaritic Baal Cycle*.
- → [[phase-1-015-sumerian-king-list]] — *shared-milieu*: Dumuzi the Shepherd is named as a king in the SKL antediluvian section (in some recensions), and Dumuzi the Fisherman as a post-flood king of Kuara. The SKL's Dumuzi-entries reflect the historicization of the deity into the chronographic frame.
- → Greek Persephone-and-Demeter (Homeric Hymn to Demeter, ~-650 to -550) — *parallel-motif*: Persephone's descent to Hades, her partial return through the pomegranate stratagem, and the seasonal etiology echo the Inanna-Dumuzi/Geshtinanna seasonal substitution. Whether this is *transmission* (via Phoenician/Cypriot intermediaries) or *parallel-motif* (independent development of a Mediterranean common-stock motif) is debated. Most classicists treat it as parallel-motif. Ref: Burkert 1987 *Ancient Mystery Cults*.
- → The Phoenician-Hellenistic Adonis cycle — *parallel-motif* and possibly *transmission*: the Adonis-Tammuz identification is explicit in Hellenistic and Roman period sources (Lucian, *On the Syrian Goddess*). The Adonis cult is more clearly a direct continuation of the Tammuz cult than the Persephone cult is of Inanna's. Ref: Mettinger 2001 *The Riddle of Resurrection*.
- → Christian Passion-and-Resurrection — *parallel-motif* (contested): the three-day death-and-revival of Inanna has been compared to the three-day death-and-resurrection of Christ since the 19th-century comparative religion movement (Frazer; the *Christ-myth* school). Current biblical scholarship is largely skeptical of direct dependence claims; the parallel is *family-resemblance* on the *dying-rising-god* motif level, not literary dependence. Ref: J.Z. Smith 1990 *Drudgery Divine*; Mettinger 2001.

## Disputes
- **Date and authorship.** OB composition is firm for the surviving manuscripts; the underlying cultic tradition is third-millennium. Whether a "single" OB composition emerged from a body of earlier Sumerian Inanna-Dumuzi material, or whether the OB composition is itself the culmination of long literary tradition, is debated.
- **Cultic function.** Whether the *Descent* was performed liturgically (as part of the seasonal Tammuz-mourning ritual) or transmitted purely as scribal-school literature is debated. The *Descent of Ishtar* (Akkadian) shows liturgical use in later periods; whether the Sumerian original had a comparable function is unclear.
- **Reading of the *me* stripped at the gates.** Whether the seven *me* are *literal cult-objects* (crown, rod-and-line, etc.) or *symbolic divine powers* (the *me* in their broader Sumerian-theological sense) is debated; current readings allow both at once.
- **The dying-rising-god category.** Since J.Z. Smith 1990, the integrity of the "dying-rising-god" comparative category has been contested. Mettinger 2001 defends a restricted form of the category; the field is divided. The Inanna-Dumuzi data is the *strongest single basis* for the category.
- **Direct vs. indirect influence on later traditions.** Whether the Greek mystery-cult descent narratives (Eleusinian katabasis, Orphic descent) depend on Mesopotamian-Phoenician transmission or developed independently from Indo-European common-stock is one of the perennial debates of comparative religion.
