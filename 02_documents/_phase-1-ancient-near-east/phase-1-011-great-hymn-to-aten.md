# Great Hymn to the Aten

## What it is
A long Egyptian hymn (~13 stanzas, ~140 lines in modern editions) in praise of the Aten — the visible disc of the sun — composed during Akhenaten's reign (~1353–1336 BCE) and inscribed on the west wall of the tomb of Ay at Amarna. The hymn celebrates the Aten as the sole creator and sustainer of all life: "How manifold are your works! They are hidden from man. O sole god, beside whom there is no other! You created the earth as you wished, you alone." It describes the daily cycle of light and dark, the differentiation of peoples and languages, the dependence of all creatures on the Aten's rays. Crucially, the hymn invokes Akhenaten as the *sole* intermediary between the Aten and humanity: "There is no one else who knows you except your son Neferkheperure-waenre [Akhenaten]." A shorter version of the same hymn appears in several other Amarna tombs.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Amenhotep IV ascended the throne ~1353 BCE; in his fifth year he changed his name to Akhenaten ("Effective for Aten"), moved the capital from Thebes to a virgin site he named Akhetaten ("Horizon of the Aten") at modern Tell el-Amarna, and instituted a religious reform that closed the temples of Amun-Ra and the other gods, defaced their names, and elevated the Aten to sole worshipped deity. The reform lasted only his lifetime; Tutankhamun reversed it within years of Akhenaten's death.
- **Religious milieu.** A radical departure from standard Egyptian religion: Akhenaten elevated the solar disc itself — not Amun-Ra or any anthropomorphic god — to sole creator. The other gods were not just demoted but *erased* from their inscriptions wherever the regime could reach. Whether this constitutes the first true monotheism, a strong henotheism, or a political-pragmatic move to break the power of the Amun priesthood at Thebes is debated (see Disputes). After the reform's collapse, Akhenaten was systematically erased from monumental record; rediscovery in the 19th–20th centuries CE has made him one of the most famous and controversial pharaohs.
- **Who was behind it.** Akhenaten himself is the implied author/voice. The court at Amarna included priests dedicated to the Aten (Meryra, the high priest of the Aten), and the courtier Ay whose tomb preserves the longest version. Whether Akhenaten personally composed the hymn or commissioned its composition is unrecoverable.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Tell el-Amarna was excavated from the 1880s onward (Petrie 1891–92; Borchardt 1907 found the bust of Nefertiti; later British, German, and Egyptian missions). The tombs at the Amarna cliffs were systematically surveyed by Norman de Garis Davies for the Egypt Exploration Fund (1903–08), publishing the Amarna tombs with their inscriptions.

## Themes and motifs
- [[henotheism-monolatry]] — the most-debated case of pre-Mosaic monotheism in the ancient world. The hymn explicitly says "O sole god, beside whom there is no other," uses a singular vocabulary, and Akhenaten's program suppressed competing cults. The traditional ancient-monotheism debate (Freud, Assmann) makes the Amarna reform a candidate first monotheism; revisionists (Reeves, Hornung) argue it is a political maneuver in monotheistic *rhetoric* without the ontological commitments of later monotheism.
- [[creation-by-word]] — the Aten's creative power is presented as enacted through light/word (the verb-forms emphasize speaking-into-existence): "You create the earth as you wished, you alone." Creation-by-light/word, not by combat.
- [[divine-kingship]] — Akhenaten claims sole mediating access: he is the Aten's earthly son and only interpreter. Where standard Egyptian theology distributes priestly access across temples, Atenism concentrates it in the king.
- **Universal beneficence** — the hymn explicitly celebrates the Aten as god of all peoples, including Syrians and Nubians, not just Egyptians: "The lands of Khor and Kush, the land of Egypt — you set every man in his place, you supply their needs."

## Connections (provisional)
- → Hebrew Bible Psalm 104 — *parallel-motif* / *contested direct-influence*: extensive verbal and structural parallels between Psalm 104 (especially vv. 19–30) and the Great Hymn have been noted since J.H. Breasted (1905). Both hymns celebrate the daily cycle, the differentiation of creatures, the dependence of all on the sole creator. The mediation chain — if any — is unknown (post-Amarna Egyptian solar hymnody, Hyksos-era contact, late-2nd-millennium Levantine intermediaries are all candidates). Most current scholarship treats this as shared sun-hymn topos rather than direct dependence, but Assmann (1997, 2014) argues for a stronger Egyptian-Israelite continuity. Ref: Lichtheim 1976; Assmann 1997, 2014.
- → [[phase-1-010-book-of-the-dead]] — *parallel-motif* (sun-hymnody) / *polemic-against* (in effect): Akhenaten's reform suppressed standard Egyptian funerary religion including the Book of the Dead's Osirian program. Amarna tombs lack the standard BD spells; the dead are commended to the Aten's daily passage, not to Osiris's tribunal. Ref: Hornung, *Akhenaten and the Religion of Light* (1999).
- → [[phase-1-002-pyramid-texts]] — *shared-milieu*: ultimately the same Egyptian solar-religious tradition reaches back to the Pyramid Texts; Atenism is the radicalization of one strand within that tradition (the solar) at the expense of the other (the Osirian).
- → Mosaic monotheism / Israelite Yahwism — *parallel-motif* / *speculative-influence*: Freud's *Moses and Monotheism* (1939) speculated that Moses was an Atenist priest; this is Tier 4 and not mainstream. Assmann's "memory of Egypt" thesis (1997) is more nuanced: the *Egyptian* memory of the Amarna disaster as the type-case of religious revolution is a key shaping force on later monotheistic self-understanding.

## Disputes
- **First monotheism?** Whether Atenism is true monotheism, henotheism, or political-rhetorical solar exaltation is contested. Assmann (2014) defends a strong monotheistic reading; Hornung (1999) treats it as henotheism; Reeves (2001) emphasizes political pragmatism.
- **Influence on Psalm 104.** Direct dependence (Breasted, Assmann) vs. shared ANE solar-hymn topos (most current scholarship). The parallels are too close to be accidental; the question is the channel.
- **Akhenaten's mental and political motives.** The "religious visionary" reading vs. the "ambitious king breaking the Amun priesthood" reading are not mutually exclusive but the weighting is contested.
- **Authorship of the hymn.** Akhenaten in person, court scribes, the high priest Meryra — all have been suggested.
