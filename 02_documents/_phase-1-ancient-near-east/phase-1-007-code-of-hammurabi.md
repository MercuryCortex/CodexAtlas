# Code of Hammurabi

## What it is
A monumental Babylonian royal inscription combining (1) a theological prologue (~300 lines) reciting Hammurabi's divine mandate from Anu, Enlil, and especially Marduk; (2) the famous law collection of ~282 numbered laws ("If a man does X, his punishment is Y"); and (3) an epilogue (~400 lines) of curses against any who deface the inscription or alter the laws, invoking Marduk, Shamash, and a long list of gods to punish the violator. Famous laws include the *lex talionis* ("If a man destroys the eye of another man, they shall destroy his eye"; CH 196), differential punishments by social class (*awilum* / *muškēnum* / *wardum*), and provisions on marriage, debt, theft, surgery, agriculture, and false witness. The inscription is at least as much a theological document as a legal one — it establishes Hammurabi as Shamash's chosen righteous king and Marduk's appointed ruler.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Hammurabi (~1792–1750 BCE), sixth king of the First Dynasty of Babylon, expanded Babylon from a regional power to the hegemonic state of southern Mesopotamia. The Code is promulgated late in his reign (~1755–1750 BCE) as a capstone to his rule. It is **not** the first Mesopotamian law collection — the Code of Ur-Nammu (~2100 BCE), Laws of Lipit-Ishtar (~1900 BCE), and Laws of Eshnunna (~1770 BCE) precede it. CH's distinctiveness is its monumental presentation and its tight theological frame.
- **Religious milieu.** Old Babylonian polytheism with Marduk's status under active elevation. The prologue places Marduk *first* in the order of gods who commission Hammurabi — a politically charged ranking, since traditional Sumerian theology placed Enlil first. The Code is part of the long Marduk-elevation that culminates in [[phase-1-008-enuma-elish]]. Shamash, the sun god and god of justice, is presented as the divine source of the laws — Hammurabi receives the laws *from* Shamash on the stele's relief at the top.
- **Who was behind it.** Hammurabi himself in royal voice (the first person of the prologue and epilogue), through his scribal apparatus. The author is *the king as institution*, not anonymous.
- **Discovery / transmission.** The stele was discovered in December 1901–January 1902 by Jacques de Morgan's French archaeological mission at Susa, where the Elamite king Shutruk-Nahhunte (~12th c. BCE) had carried it as war booty. Scheil published the editio princeps in 1902, and the text was a sensation: it pushed back substantively known legal codification far before the Hebrew Bible. Clay-tablet copies confirm the Code was a standard scribal-curriculum text for over a millennium.

## Themes and motifs
- [[divine-kingship]] — the Code's prologue is *the* paradigmatic articulation of Mesopotamian divine kingship: the king is appointed by the gods to embody justice on earth; his laws are not human invention but divine commission. The relief shows Shamash handing the rod-and-ring symbols of authority to Hammurabi.
- [[divine-council]] — the prologue narrates the divine assembly choosing Babylon (and Hammurabi) as supreme: "When lofty Anu, king of the Anunnaki, and Enlil, lord of heaven and earth, who determines the destinies of the land, allotted the divine lordship of the multitude of people to Marduk..." This is divine council in ritualized political-theological narrative form.
- [[creation-by-word]] — only obliquely: the gods *name* and *appoint* Hammurabi (cf. naming as creative act); the laws themselves are pronounced and inscribed, their authority deriving from their having been spoken/written by the king under divine direction.
- **Justice as cosmic order (*kittu* / *mīšaru*)** — the laws articulate the Mesopotamian theological concept that justice is rooted in cosmic order maintained by Shamash; injustice is a metaphysical, not merely social, rupture.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-1-008-enuma-elish]] — *shared-milieu* / *parallel-motif*: both are documents of Marduk's elevation. The Code is an earlier moment in the same theological trajectory; Enuma Elish is the cosmogonic capstone. Ref: Lambert 2013, *Babylonian Creation Myths*.
- → [[phase-1-005-instructions-of-shuruppak]] — *parallel-motif* (loose): both transmit ethical-juridical norms with theological framing, though the genre differs (wisdom proverbs vs. casuistic law). Ref: Lambert 1960, *Babylonian Wisdom Literature*.
- → Hebrew Bible Covenant Code (Exodus 21–23) — *parallel-motif* / *contested direct-influence*: The Covenant Code shows striking structural and sometimes verbal parallels with CH (e.g., the goring-ox provisions Exod 21:28–32 and CH 250–252; the female slave provisions; the *lex talionis* Exod 21:23–25 and CH 196–197). Wright (2009) argues for direct literary dependence of the Covenant Code on CH (a strong minority position). Most scholars hold a *shared-milieu* / *common ANE legal tradition* view. Ref: Wright 2009; Roth 1995; Otto, *Theologische Ethik des Alten Testaments* (1994).
- → Deuteronomic and Holiness Code Hebrew law — *parallel-motif*: similar comparison, structurally more distant.

## Disputes
- **Genre.** Is CH a *prescriptive* law-code actually used in court, an *idealized* royal display-inscription, or a *scholastic* casuistic compilation? Current scholarship (Westbrook, Roth) leans toward the latter two — actual court records from the period only sporadically cite the Code's specific provisions.
- **Direct vs. indirect Hebrew influence.** Wright (2009) argues for direct textual dependence of Exodus 21–23 on CH (he posits a Hebrew author with access to a cuneiform copy). The majority view holds the parallels reflect shared ANE legal *Sitz im Leben* rather than direct literary borrowing.
- **Stele's location of use.** Originally erected in Babylon (or Sippar?); when looted to Susa is debated only in detail (Shutruk-Nahhunte, ~1158 BCE, is the consensus).
