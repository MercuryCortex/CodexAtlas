# Enūma Anu Enlil

## What it is
*Enūma Anu Enlil* ("When [the gods] Anu [and] Enlil...") is the canonical Mesopotamian celestial-omen series — approximately 70 cuneiform tablets containing ~7,000 conditional-form omens of the type "If on the *n*-th day of month X the moon is eclipsed at the watch Y, then [a king will die / the harvest will fail / a flood will come / etc.]". It is the foundational text of celestial divination — the proto-astrological tradition that, transformed via Hellenistic Greek and Egyptian intermediaries, becomes the entire Western and Indian astrological corpus. The series is organized in four sections: Moon omens (Sin), Sun omens (Shamash), weather omens (Adad), and stars-and-planets omens (Ishtar). Each omen pairs a celestial *protasis* with a political/agricultural/military *apodosis* aimed primarily at the king and the state.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** The series functioned as state-intelligence apparatus. Babylonian and Neo-Assyrian kings — most explicitly Esarhaddon (r. -680 to -669) and Ashurbanipal (r. -669 to -631) — retained a corps of scholarly astrologers (*ṭupšar Enūma Anu Enlil*) whose job was to observe the sky nightly, identify omens, and report them to the king. The State Archives of Assyria letter corpus (Hunger 1992) preserves hundreds of such real-time reports. When ominous portents threatened the king, ritual countermeasures (*namburbi*, *bīt mēseri*, and most spectacularly the *šar pūḫi* "substitute king" rite) were enacted.
- **Religious milieu.** The Mesopotamian view: the gods write their decrees in the sky and other natural phenomena; the trained scholar reads these as text. The colophons attribute the series's origin to divine teaching given by [[enki-ea]] to the antediluvian sage Adapa. The conceptual framework — that cosmos is intelligible as inscribed divine speech — is articulated most fully in Rochberg 2004 as the "heavenly writing."
- **Who was behind it.** The Babylonian *ṭupšarrū* / scholarly-scribal tradition; specifically the *barû* (extispicy-diviner) and *ṭupšar Enūma Anu Enlil* (celestial-omen scholar) classes. The series exists in multiple recensions (Babylonian, Assyrian); the Neo-Assyrian recension in Ashurbanipal's library is the most complete and is the basis of the standard modern edition.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Discovered piecemeal in the great excavations of the 19th- and 20th-century Mesopotamian sites: Nineveh (Layard and Rassam, 1840s–1870s), Sippar, Nimrud, Sultantepe, Babylon. Modern editions: Virolleaud (1908–1912) and Weidner (1941–1969) made the first systematic attempts; Reiner and Pingree's BPO series (1975–2005) is the current standard.

## Themes and motifs
- [[divinatory-omen-reading]] — the foundational text of the entire Mesopotamian omen tradition; the conditional protasis-apodosis form is the cuneiform-scholarly genre par excellence and shapes all subsequent omen series.
- [[wisdom-as-cosmic-order]] — the cosmos as a legible divine text; *ṭupšarrūtu* (scribal scholarship) as the discipline of reading what the gods have written.
- [[heavenly-tablets]] — the Mesopotamian background to the later Jewish "tablets of heaven" motif in 1 Enoch and Jubilees: the celestial order as pre-inscribed divine decree.
- [[divinatory-calendar]] — many EAE omens are date-specific (e.g., the *iqqur īpuš* hemerological tradition derives from this milieu); celestial divination is calendrically structured.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-1-020-shumma-alu]] / [[phase-1-021-shumma-izbu]] — *parallel-motif* / *shared-milieu*: the three great Babylonian omen series (celestial = EAE; terrestrial = Šumma ālu; teratological = Šumma izbu) constitute the canonical *ṭupšarrūtu* corpus. They are formally identical (conditional protasis-apodosis) and were studied together by the same scholars. Ref: Koch-Westenholz 1995; Rochberg 2004.
- → Hellenistic Greek astrology (Ptolemy's *Tetrabiblos*, the *Anthologiae* of Vettius Valens, etc.) — *manuscript-transmission*: the transmission of Babylonian celestial-omen content into Greco-Egyptian astrology in the Hellenistic period is one of the most thoroughly documented cases of cross-cultural scholarly transmission. Berossus (~-280) wrote his Greek-language *Babyloniaca* drawing on EAE; Babylonian astronomical diary content travels with it. Refs: Rochberg 2010; Pingree 1998.
- → Indian *jyotiṣa* (the *Yavanajātaka* of Sphujidhvaja, ~150 CE) — *manuscript-transmission*: Greek-mediated transmission of Babylonian celestial omens into the South Asian astrological tradition. Ref: Pingree, *Jyotiḥśāstra* (1981).
- → 1 Enoch's Astronomical Book ([[phase-3-004-1-enoch]]) — *parallel-motif* / *shared-milieu*: the Jewish "heavenly tablets" / celestial-knowledge tradition is partly continuous with Mesopotamian celestial-omen scholarship transmitted into the Persian-period Levant. The 364-day calendar in Enoch reflects engagement with Babylonian astronomical material.
- → [[phase-1-007-code-of-hammurabi]] — *shared-milieu*: both are products of the same Old Babylonian scholarly-scribal class; both employ the conditional protasis-apodosis form ("If a man does X, then..."). The OB legal tradition and the OB omen tradition are formally cognate.

## Disputes
- **Date of canonical fixation.** Mainstream scholarship (Reiner-Pingree, Rochberg) places the canonical 70-tablet structure in the Middle Babylonian / Cassite period (~-1300 to -1100). Some scholars (e.g., Brown 2000, *Mesopotamian Planetary Astronomy-Astrology*) push elements significantly later, into the Neo-Babylonian period.
- **EAE vs. mathematical astronomy.** The relation between omen-astrology (EAE-style qualitative protasis-apodosis) and the much later mathematical astronomy (the System A and System B planetary tables of the Persian and Seleucid periods) is a major topic in Rochberg 2004: the same scholarly class produced both, but they are distinct epistemic enterprises that should not be conflated.
- **Authorship attribution to Adapa / Ea.** The colophonic attribution of the series's origin to divine revelation through Adapa is conventional and not taken as historical by scholars; it is a literary-theological device of the scribal tradition.
