The Edwin Smith Papyrus (surviving copy c. 1600 BCE; original likely c. 2600 BCE Old Kingdom) is arguably the most important medical document in the ancient world — because it is the **first purely rational medical text**: 48 surgical cases examined with systematic methodology and almost no magical content. Named for American Egyptologist Edwin Smith who purchased it in Luxor in 1862.

## The Clinical Format — 3,600 Years Old

Each of the 48 cases follows a strict four-part structure:
1. **Title**: "Instructions for a [condition]"
2. **Examination**: systematic physical findings — what the physician sees, feels, and observes
3. **Diagnosis**: the physician's conclusion
4. **Verdict**: "a disease I will treat" / "a disease I will contend with" / "a disease not to be treated" (triage)
5. **Treatment**: the prescribed intervention

This format — examine → diagnose → prognosis → treat — is the **same logical structure** taught in modern clinical medicine. Its appearance in 1600 BCE is the oldest documented instance of this sequence as a conscious medical methodology.

## The Brain — First Description

Cases 6–8 describe head injuries with damage to brain tissue. The scribe uses a specific term for brain convolutions — compared to "cooled copper slag" (the metallic bubbling appearance of traumatized brain tissue). These cases contain:
- The **oldest known description of the brain as an organ**
- The first documented observation that **brain damage causes contralateral paralysis** (damage to the left side of the brain causes right-side body weakness) — the principle of cerebral lateralization
- The first description of **meningeal pulsation** (the physician can see the brain's pulsation through the skull injury)

This clinical observation — contralateral paralysis — was **not understood mechanistically until the 19th century** when Paul Broca and the localization of brain function were established. The Egyptian scribe observed the fact 3,300 years before its explanation.

## Triage — First Medical Ethics Decision

The verdict "a disease not to be treated" is one of the earliest documented medical ethics decisions: the recognition that some conditions are beyond the healer's ability to help, and that honest acknowledgment serves the patient and family better than futile intervention.

Modern hospice and palliative medicine rest on this same recognition. The Egyptian scribe encoded the triage principle 3,600 years before its modern formalization.
