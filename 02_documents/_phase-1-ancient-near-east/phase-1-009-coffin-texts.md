# Coffin Texts

## What it is
A corpus of ~1185 Middle Kingdom Egyptian funerary spells painted on the interiors of wooden coffins for non-royal elites (~2100–1700 BCE). The Coffin Texts (CT) form the bridge between the royal-only [[phase-1-002-pyramid-texts]] of the Old Kingdom and the broadly accessible [[phase-1-010-book-of-the-dead]] of the New Kingdom: many CT spells are direct adaptations of Pyramid Texts, with the deceased's title shifted from "king" to the personal name of the coffin's owner. The corpus also contains substantial **new** material absent from the Pyramid Texts: the *Book of Two Ways* (a guide-map of the netherworld in two parallel routes, one solar, one Osirian — the earliest illustrated guidebook to the afterlife known); extensive cosmogonic spells presenting the Heliopolitan and Hermopolitan creation accounts in philosophical-theological terms; and the first sustained articulation of the *judgment of the deceased* before Osiris and the forty-two assessors.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Composed across the First Intermediate Period (~2150–2040 BCE — Egypt's first major political fragmentation after the Old Kingdom's collapse) and the Middle Kingdom (~2040–1700 BCE — reunification under the 11th–12th Dynasties). The Old Kingdom's centralized royal mortuary cult had broken; provincial governors (nomarchs) and other elites now claimed for themselves the funerary program previously reserved for kings.
- **Religious milieu.** The "democratization of the afterlife" — a sociologically important shift. Anyone wealthy enough to commission a properly decorated coffin could now access the spells that had been the king's exclusive provision. Osiris's role expands: where the Pyramid Texts emphasize royal solar ascent, the Coffin Texts give increasing weight to the deceased's identification with Osiris and to a netherworld journey under Osiris's lordship.
- **Who was behind it.** Anonymous priestly composition; regional variation suggests multiple compositional centers. The provincial necropoleis at Bersheh (Hare nome), Asyut, Meir, and Gebelein each show local recensional preferences.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Modern recovery has been continuous since the 19th century as Middle Kingdom coffins entered European and American museum collections (especially Cairo, Berlin, Leiden, the British Museum, the Metropolitan, and the Oriental Institute, Chicago). de Buck's 7-volume edition (1935–1961) collated the dispersed coffins into a single standard text-edition.

## Themes and motifs
- [[afterlife-judgment]] — the **earliest explicit articulation** of the post-mortem weighing/judgment scene: CT 335 and related spells set up the Hall of Two Truths, the forty-two assessors, the negative confession ("I have not done X..."), and the weighing of the heart against Ma'at's feather. The later [[phase-1-010-book-of-the-dead]] Spell 125 is the canonical New Kingdom version of this scene; the CT is its prototype.
- [[primordial-waters]] — Nun, the primordial ocean, figures explicitly in CT cosmogonic spells (CT 76–80, the *Book of the Heavenly Cow* tradition's prototypes, etc.); the deceased is sometimes identified with the self-emerging Atum out of Nun.
- [[creation-by-word]] — CT 261 ("I am the Magic") and related spells present *heka* (divine creative speech-magic) as the medium of cosmogenesis. The Memphite Theology (preserved on the much later Shabaka Stone but containing material at least as old as the Middle Kingdom) gives this idea its fullest articulation.
- [[dying-rising-god]] — the Osirian program is sharpened: the deceased is dying-and-revivified-with-Osiris through ritual identification.
- **Book of Two Ways** — the netherworld is mapped: a black road and a blue road, water and land, hostile demons named with the spells that pacify them. The earliest illustrated underworld geography.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-1-002-pyramid-texts]] — *redaction-of* / *direct-quote*: many Coffin Texts are direct adaptations of Pyramid Texts, with the royal language reframed for non-royal owners. The continuity is the textbook example of "democratization" in Egyptian religion. Ref: Faulkner 1973–78; Allen 1988.
- → [[phase-1-010-book-of-the-dead]] — *redaction-of*: the New Kingdom Book of the Dead is the direct heir of the Coffin Texts. Many BD spells trace continuous textual descent through specific CT spells; e.g., BD 17 develops material from CT 335. Ref: Hornung, *The Ancient Egyptian Books of the Afterlife* (1999).
- → Memphite Theology (cf. Shabaka Stone) — *parallel-motif*: creation-by-word doctrine, articulated more fully in the (later-attested but earlier-composed) Memphite Theology. CT 261 is one of the earliest extant articulations of *heka*-cosmogony. Ref: Allen 1988.
- → Greek mystery religion and Christian afterlife judgment — *parallel-motif* (loose): the Egyptian weighing-of-the-heart scene parallels later Greek-mystery and Christian images of post-mortem judgment, though direct mediation chains are debated. Mettinger 2001 and Smith 2017 discuss the relationships. Ref: Smith, *Following Osiris* (2017).

## Disputes
- **Sociological reading.** The "democratization" frame (Pyramid Texts → Coffin Texts → Book of the Dead = progressive widening) is the classic narrative (Breasted, Hornung) but has been refined: some scholars (Willems) argue it is not so much democratization as a shift in *which* social class controlled the funerary program, with strong continuities of restriction in practice.
- **Dating of specific spells.** CT spells often have unknown depth — many incorporate material older than the surviving Middle Kingdom coffins.
- **Regional vs. unified tradition.** Whether the Coffin Texts are a single corpus or a family of regional traditions sharing material is debated; de Buck's edition imposes unity that may be partly editorial.
