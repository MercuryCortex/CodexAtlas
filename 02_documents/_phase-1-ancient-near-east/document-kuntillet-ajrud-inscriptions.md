# Kuntillet Ajrud Inscriptions

## What it is

The Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions are a corpus of roughly twenty Hebrew and Phoenician texts — painted and incised on ceramic pithoi (storage jars), plaster walls, and stone bowls — discovered in 1975–1976 by Ze'ev Meshel at the site of Kuntillet Ajrud (*Ḥorvat Teman*), a remote road-station in the northeastern Sinai Peninsula, roughly halfway between Gaza and Eilat. Carbon-14 dates and palaeographic analysis converge on c. 810–750 BCE, placing the site in the era of the Northern Kingdom of Israel. The site functioned as a caravanserai and may have had cultic or scribal training functions. The inscriptions are among the most significant epigraphic finds for reconstructing pre-exilic Israelite religion.

## Context

**Political milieu.** The site appears to have been maintained during the reigns of the Omride dynasty or its immediate successors in the Northern Kingdom. The formulas "Yahweh of Samaria" (*yhwh šmrn*) and "Yahweh of Teman" (*yhwh htmn*) identify regional cult centers: Samaria as the royal capital of Israel (Northern Kingdom), and Teman as a southern Edomite/Negev region associated with older Yahweh theophany traditions (cf. Hab 3:3: "God came from Teman, the Holy One from Mount Paran").

**Religious milieu.** The site documents what scholars call "folk Yahwism" or "popular religion" — the religious practice of non-elite Israelites alongside, or in tension with, the official Jerusalem Temple theology. The inscriptions show a form of Yahweh worship that does not yet enforce the later Deuteronomic requirement of sole-worship: multiple regional manifestations of Yahweh are invoked simultaneously, and a female divine figure (*Asherah*) appears as Yahweh's paired blessing agent.

**Who was behind it.** The inscriptions appear to be primarily scribal exercises — formulaic blessing letters practised by students or scribes — as well as votive texts. Schniedewind (2004) argues the site was a scribal school associated with the military road network. The mix of sophisticated literacy and informal graffiti is consistent with a training context.

## The central inscriptions

**Pithos A (inscription 1):** "Says ʾAšiyaw the king: say to Yahēliyaw, and to Yawʾāsah, and to [NN]: blessed are you all to Yahweh *of Samaria* (*yhwh šmrn*) and to *his Asherah* (*wʾšrth*)."

**Pithos B (inscription 2):** "I bless you by Yahweh *of Teman* (*yhwh htmn*) and by *his Asherah* (*wʾšrth*)."

These are the key texts. The possessive suffix *-h* ("his") on *ʾšrth* is grammatically anomalous: in Biblical Hebrew, personal names (including divine names) do not normally take possessive suffixes. The anomaly has generated extensive scholarly debate:

- **Goddess reading** (Dever, Hadley, Olyan, Smith): *ʾšrth* refers to the goddess Asherah, functioning as Yahweh's consort in popular religion. This reading is supported by the broader Near Eastern pattern of paired divine blessing formulae and by iconographic evidence (the Khirbet el-Qom inscription, below).
- **Cultic object reading** (Emerton, Blum, others): *ʾšrth* = a wooden cult symbol (the *ʾašērāh* pole/tree), not the goddess. This preserves strict grammatical regularity. Critics note, however, that the symbol derives its meaning precisely from the goddess, making the distinction less sharp than it seems.

The scholarly majority now reads the inscriptions as evidence of a living popular goddess tradition associated with Yahweh worship in pre-Josianic Israel — whatever the precise referent of *ʾšrth* in a given text.

## Iconographic evidence on the pithoi

Pithos A also bears a famous painted scene: two composite figures — bovine lower bodies, leonine faces, standing upright — have been variously identified as Yahweh and Asherah (Beck 1982), the Egyptian dwarf-god Bes (Keel and Uehlinger), or demonic dancers. Below the figures, a seated woman plays a lyre. Talley Ornan (2004) demonstrated that all the Kuntillet Ajrud drawings have direct parallels in Neo-Assyrian palace reliefs, complicating any direct Yahweh-Asherah iconographic identification. The drawing's proximity to the inscription does not necessarily mean the inscription glosses the drawing; they may be independent graffiti.

## Khirbet el-Qom (parallel text)

The Khirbet el-Qom tomb inscription (Judah, c. 750 BCE, discovered 1967) reads: "Blessed is Uriyahu by Yahweh, for from his enemies he saved him by his Asherah (*wʾšrth*)." This inscription, from the heartland of the Southern Kingdom, uses the same formula in a funerary context, demonstrating that the Yahweh-and-Asherah blessing formula was current in *both* Israel and Judah in the 8th century BCE. The combination of both sites removes any possibility that the formula is a regional anomaly limited to the northern frontier.

## Themes and motifs

- [[theme-el-yahweh-merger]] — The epithet "Yahweh of Samaria / Teman" shows regional-cult Yahwism before Deuteronomic centralization; the Asherah pairing continues the El-Asherah dyad from Ugaritic religion ([[el-canaanite]] and [[asherah]] as cosmic couple).
- [[divine-council]] — Background theology; Yahweh still operates within a populated divine world.

## Connections

- → [[yahweh]]: Yahweh as subject of regional blessing formulae; named in both pithos inscriptions.
- → [[asherah]]: The divine figure or symbol paired with Yahweh in both inscriptions; direct evidence of pre-Josianic Asherah veneration within Yahwism.
- → [[el-canaanite]]: The Asherah-Yahweh pairing continues the Ugaritic El-Asherah consort model; Yahweh has absorbed El's role as cosmic patriarch and received Asherah as a paired figure.
- → [[theme-el-yahweh-merger]]: The most cited archaeological evidence for the claim that pre-exilic Israelite religion was not yet strictly monotheistic.

## Disputes

1. **Asherah = goddess or cultic object?** Hadley and Dever argue for the goddess; Emerton, Blum, and others prefer the cultic-object reading. Smith (2002) treats the question as genuinely open but concludes that the popular-religion context makes the goddess reading contextually plausible.
2. **Relationship of inscription to iconography:** The two bowlegged figures may or may not be Yahweh and Asherah. Keel and Uehlinger (1998) argue they are Bes figures; Beck (1982) and Dever maintain the Yahweh-Asherah identification.
3. **Site function:** Scribal school (Schniedewind), road-station cult (Meshel), or way-shrine for pilgrims? The eclectic textual content supports a school/administrative function.
