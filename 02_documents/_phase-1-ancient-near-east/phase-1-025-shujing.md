# Shujing (Book of Documents / Classic of History)

## What it is
A heterogeneous corpus of speeches, royal proclamations, harangues, mythical-sage edicts, and political memoranda presented as documentary records of the early Chinese sage-rulers (Yao, Shun, Yu) and of the early Zhou kings. In the received text, fifty-eight chapters arranged in five sections: documents of Yu, Xia, Shang, Western Zhou, and the post-conquest pronouncements. The earliest authentic stratum — surviving in the *New Text* 28 chapters — consists primarily of Western Zhou speeches addressed to the conquered Shang and the Zhou's own court, articulating for the first time in extant Chinese sources the doctrine of the *Mandate of Heaven* (*tian ming*) under which Heaven legitimates a virtuous dynasty and withdraws its mandate from a corrupt one. The Shujing is the second of the Five Classics; its political-theological vocabulary (the king as Heaven's son and steward; *de* "virtue" as the criterion of rule) is the foundation of two and a half millennia of Chinese state ideology.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** The reliably dateable layer is early Western Zhou (~-1046 to -900). The *Kang Gao*, *Da Gao*, *Jiu Gao*, *Zi Cai*, *Shao Gao*, *Luo Gao*, *Duo Shi*, and *Duo Fang* chapters are Western Zhou speeches (almost certainly authentic in core) addressing the consolidation of the conquest. The *Yao Dian* and *Shun Dian* chapters present mythical-sage-king material in a later (probably Warring States) literary form. The Han-period redaction reflects the canonization of a Confucian classical corpus under imperial patronage.
- **Religious milieu.** Zhou court religion: cult of Heaven (*tian*), royal ancestors, and the sovereignty of the Zhou house as *tianzi* ("Son of Heaven"). The Shujing is the locus classicus for the textual articulation of the Mandate of Heaven doctrine — the doctrine that defined Chinese imperial theology from the early Zhou to 1911.
- **Who was behind it.** Zhou court scribes (for the earliest stratum); Spring-and-Autumn and Warring States editors (for the sage-king material); Han redactors (Fu Sheng for the New Text; later editors for the Old Text). Traditional attribution to Confucius is rejected; he quotes Shujing material but is not its editor.
- **Discovery / transmission.** The text was largely destroyed in the Qin book-burnings (-213). Fu Sheng, a former Qin scholar in old age, reproduced 28 chapters from memory in clerical script (early Han); these are the *Jinwen* 今文 "New Text." The *Guwen* 古文 "Old Text" — purportedly recovered from a wall of Confucius's former house and including 25 additional chapters — was added in the early Han, lost again, and "rediscovered" in the 4th c. CE in a form that scholarship has long suspected of being a third-or-fourth-century forgery (definitively argued by the Qing scholar Yan Ruoqu, *Shangshu guwen shuzheng*, 1745). The Tsinghua bamboo slips (acquired 2008, ~-300 BCE) include early forms of several Shujing chapters — most strikingly a version of the *Bao Xun* (Treasured Instructions) attributed to King Wen — and have re-opened the textual-history debate in important ways.

## Themes and motifs
- **Mandate of Heaven (*tian ming*)** — the foundational doctrine: Heaven appoints a virtuous dynasty; when virtue is lost, Heaven withdraws its mandate. The *Kang Gao* and *Shao Gao* are the textual sources of this idea. Foundational for Chinese political theology to 1911.
- [[divine-kingship]] / [[sacred-king]] — the king as *tianzi*, mediator between Heaven, ancestors, and people.
- **Virtue (*de* 德)** — the moral quality of rule that makes the mandate stable. The Shujing's repeated insistence is that conquest does not legitimate; virtue does.
- **Documentary form** — the Shujing establishes the genre of preserved political pronouncement as authoritative scripture; speeches and edicts as the bones of historical memory.
- [[chosen-people]] (Chinese version) — Heaven's selection of the Zhou as the bearer of cosmic order; functionally parallel to the Hebrew Bible's election theology but theologically conditional (the mandate can be lost).
- [[salvation-history]] (Chinese version) — the dynastic-cycle understanding of history as a sequence of Heaven-given mandates and their losses.

## Cross-tradition substrate
- **Conditional kingship.** The Shujing's doctrine that Heaven *withdraws* the Mandate from corrupt rulers is structurally unusual in ancient theocracies — comparable to (and possibly more developed than) the Deuteronomistic theology of YHWH's conditional covenant with the Davidic line. The parallel is *parallel-motif*; no transmissional claim has been seriously advanced. (Nylan 2001 ch. 3 discusses; for the Hebrew side cf. [[phase-2-011-hebrew-bible-d-source]].)
- **Documentary scripture.** Like the Hebrew prophets and ancient Near Eastern royal inscriptions, the Shujing treats preserved political-religious speech as authoritative. Cross-tradition substrate for the idea that *recorded political speech of a virtuous founder* binds future generations.

## Disputes
- **New Text / Old Text controversy.** The 25 *Guwen* chapters are now generally (though not universally) regarded as a 3rd-4th c. CE forgery; the 28 *Jinwen* chapters preserve a recognizably authentic Western Zhou and later stratum. Nylan 2001 notes the controversy is more nuanced than the standard "all New Text good, all Old Text fake" picture; some Old Text chapters may preserve genuine early material redacted later. The Tsinghua slips (~-300 BCE) include parallels that complicate the simple authentic/forged dichotomy. [claim: contested]
- **Date of the sage-king material.** The *Yao Dian* and *Shun Dian* chapters' literary form is probably Warring States; their *narrative content* (sage-king Yao, Shun, Yu) may preserve older mytho-historical memory but is not historiographically reliable.
- **Confucius's relation.** Traditional Chinese attribution of editorial work to Confucius is rejected by modern critical scholarship.
