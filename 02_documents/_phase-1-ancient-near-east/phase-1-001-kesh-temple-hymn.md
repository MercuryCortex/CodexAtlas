# Kesh Temple Hymn

## What it is
A Sumerian temple hymn praising the sanctuary of the city Keš and its presiding goddess Nintud (= [[ninhursag]]). Eight sections (each closed by the formulaic refrain "Will anyone else bring forth something as great as Keš? Will any other mother ever give birth to someone as great as its hero, Ašgi?"). The composition exists in two main recensions: an archaic version preserved on tablets from Tell Abū Ṣalābīkh and Fara (~2600 BCE — among the oldest decipherable literary compositions in any language), and an Old Babylonian curricular version (~1800 BCE) copied by scribal students. Continuous textual transmission across ~800 years.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Early Dynastic IIIa Sumer (~2600 BCE): a network of independent city-states (Ur, Uruk, Lagaš, Umma, Adab, Šuruppak), each with patron deities and temple-economies. Religious literature is becoming a stable written genre alongside the older administrative cuneiform tradition.
- **Religious milieu.** Polytheistic Sumerian pantheon organized around city-patron deities under An's nominal headship and Enlil's executive command. Temple hymns are a vehicle for civic-religious identity: praising a city's temple is praising its god, which is praising the city.
- **Who was behind it.** Anonymous scribal composition. In the Old Babylonian curriculum it was transmitted alongside other temple hymns; the curricular collection attributed to [[enheduanna]] (Sargon's daughter, ~2300 BCE) contains 42 temple hymns including one for Keš, suggesting the genre's continuity.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Excavated from Tell Abū Ṣalābīkh (1963–65 by the British School of Archaeology in Iraq under Nicholas Postgate, et al.; the religious tablets edited by Biggs). The OB recension known from Nippur and Ur curricular tablets dug throughout the 20th century.

## Themes and motifs
- [[divine-kingship]] — though not the central motif here, the hymn implicitly affirms Anu's and Enlil's role in granting the temple its destiny.
- [[creation-by-word]] — the hymn opens with Enlil pronouncing praises upon Keš, the divine speech functioning to *constitute* the temple's exalted status.
- **Temple-as-cosmic-mountain** — the recurring image "the temple, like a great mountain, has lifted its head to the sky" anchors the cosmological role of the Mesopotamian temple as axis mundi. This image recurs in later Sumerian and Akkadian temple literature (ziggurats explicitly built to materialize it).

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-1-003-enheduanna-hymns]] — *shared-milieu* / *redaction-of*: Enheduanna's 42 Temple Hymns include one for Keš; the genre is continuous, though the relationship between her composition and the older Keš hymn (whether direct adaptation or independent participation in genre conventions) is not fully resolved. Ref: ETCSL t.4.80.1 (Temple Hymns).
- → Later Sumerian/Akkadian temple-dedication literature (cylinders of Gudea, etc.) — *shared-milieu*: same theological grammar of temple-as-mountain, divine destiny, civic patronage.

## Disputes
- **Date.** The archaic Abū Ṣalābīkh and Fara copies are securely dated to ~2600 BCE on palaeographic grounds, but whether the *composition* is contemporary or already older (oral) at the time of those copies is unknown. Biggs argues the archaic written form is close to original composition; others suspect earlier oral tradition.
- **Authorship attribution.** The OB version sometimes appears with a scribal attribution; the archaic version is anonymous. Whether the "named author" in some OB copies is a real tradent or a curricular convention is uncertain.
