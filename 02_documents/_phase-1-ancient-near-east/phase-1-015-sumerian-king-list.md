# The Sumerian King List

## What it is
The Sumerian King List (SKL) is a Sumerian-language chronographic composition listing the kings of Mesopotamia from the moment "kingship descended from heaven" through (in its various recensions) the early second millennium BCE. The text is built around a strict literary frame: each ruler is named, assigned a city, and given a numerical reign-length; cities are grouped into dynasties; the formula "Then X was smitten with weapons; its kingship was carried off to Y" transitions kingship from one city to the next. The principal manuscript — the **Weld-Blundell Prism (WB 444)**, a four-sided clay prism of ~-1800 BCE in the Ashmolean Museum at Oxford — gives the most complete recension, beginning with the descent of kingship from heaven to Eridu in the antediluvian period, listing **eight kings of five antediluvian cities reigning a combined 241,200 years** (numbers vary across recensions; some give 432,000), reporting **"Then the flood swept over"**, and resuming with kingship's re-descent to Kish in the post-flood era. Post-flood, the reign-lengths shorten progressively — the early post-flood kings reign hundreds or thousands of years, by the Akkadian period reigns are in the tens of years (and align broadly with reigns reconstructable from other historical evidence). The list runs through Kish I, Uruk I (including [[gilgamesh-king]]), Ur I, Awan, Mari, Akshak, Kish II, Uruk II, Akkad (including [[sargon-of-akkad]] and [[naram-sin]]), Uruk IV, Gutium, Uruk V, and Ur III (including [[ur-nammu]] and [[shulgi]]) — concluding (in the Larsa recension WB 444) with the Isin dynasty. The text is the principal Mesopotamian chronographic-historical framework and the principal scriptural-style source on the Mesopotamian conception of *kingship as a cosmic institution descended from heaven*.

## Primary text — selected excerpts (Jacobsen 1939 / ETCSL 2.1.1)

### The opening — descent of kingship from heaven (lines 1–9, antediluvian section)

> When kingship from heaven was lowered,
> the kingship was in Eridu(g).
> In Eridu, Alulim became king;
> 28,800 years he ruled.
> Alalgar ruled 36,000 years.
> Two kings ruled for 64,800 years.
> Eridu was abandoned; its kingship was carried off to Bad-tibira.
> In Bad-tibira, En-men-lu-ana ruled 43,200 years...

(Jacobsen 1939, pp. 70–71; ETCSL 2.1.1.)

The combined antediluvian reign-totals vary: the Weld-Blundell Prism gives ~241,200 years across eight kings of five cities (Eridu, Bad-tibira, Larak, Sippar, Shuruppak); other recensions give 432,000. The numbers are sexagesimal multiples (sars of 3,600 years each), encoding cosmological-mathematical patterns rather than chronological data.

### The flood (after the antediluvian section)

> Then the flood swept over.
> After the flood had swept over,
> when kingship from heaven was lowered (a second time),
> the kingship was in Kish.

(Jacobsen 1939, pp. 76–77.)

The flood is treated as a fact of history, not a narrative event. The SKL does not narrate the flood (that is the work of [[phase-1-016-eridu-genesis-flood]], [[phase-1-006-atrahasis]], and the Standard Babylonian Gilgamesh Tablet XI); it merely registers the flood as the discontinuity between the two epochs of kingship. The hero of the flood (Ziusudra of Shuruppak in the Eridu Genesis tradition) is the last antediluvian king in some SKL recensions.

### The post-flood First Dynasty of Kish (with [[enmebaragesi]])

The post-flood reigns begin at 1,200 years (Jushur of Kish) and decrease progressively. [[enmebaragesi]] of Kish — independently attested by contemporary inscriptions, the **earliest historically-attested Mesopotamian ruler** — appears with a 900-year reign. His son Aga, antagonist of [[gilgamesh-king]] in *Gilgamesh and Aga*, closes the First Dynasty of Kish.

### The First Dynasty of Uruk (Gilgamesh's reign)

> In E-anna, Mes-kiag-gasher, son of Utu, became en (lord) and king; he ruled 324 years...
> ...
> Gilgamesh, whose father was a phantom (lillu) — the en (lord) of Kullab — ruled 126 years.

(Jacobsen 1939, pp. 88–89.)

The Gilgamesh entry — 126 years, divine parentage through Lugalbanda and Ninsun — illustrates the SKL's characteristic blend: a likely-historical figure ([[gilgamesh-king]]) wrapped in mythologizing reign-length and divine genealogy.

### The Akkadian and Ur III dynasties

The reigns become historically plausible: Sargon 56 years, Naram-Sin 37 years, Shulgi 46 years (administrative archives document 48; close enough). By the end of the list, the SKL is functionally a historical-chronographic register.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Composed (or substantively redacted) during the Ur III period (~-2100 to -2000 BCE) — the founder-figure of which is [[ur-nammu]] — and continuously updated through the Isin-Larsa period. The Larsa recension WB 444 (~-1800 BCE) is the principal manuscript. The political function is *legitimation by continuity*: each new dynasty is presented as the latest in an unbroken chain of "kingship descended from heaven," and the contemporary ruling dynasty (Ur III, then Isin, then Larsa, depending on recension) is the legitimate inheritor of the heavenly mandate.
- **Religious milieu.** The Nippur theological-scribal establishment provides the frame. **Kingship is a cosmic institution**, lowered from heaven — by [[enlil]] in the Nippur theology — and carried from city to city by divine decision. The "smiting with weapons" that transfers kingship is not principally a military fact but a theological judgment.
- **Who was behind it.** Anonymous Sumerian scribal-school composers and redactors, principally at Nippur (the scribal-religious capital of southern Mesopotamia), with provincial recensions at Larsa, Isin, Tell Leilan (Subartu), and Susa.
- **Discovery / transmission.** The Weld-Blundell Prism was acquired by Herbert Weld-Blundell from antiquities dealers in Iraq before the First World War and donated to the Ashmolean Museum at Oxford; published by Langdon (1923) and re-edited by Jacobsen (1939) in *Assyriological Studies 11*. Jacobsen's edition remains the standard, supplemented by Glassner (2004) and Steinkeller's (2003) revisionist analysis.

## Themes and motifs
- [[divine-kingship]] — the central thesis: kingship is a divine institution descended from heaven, conferred by Enlil, and *carried* from city to city by divine decision. The Mesopotamian political-theological commitment that legitimate rule is cosmically ordered begins here.
- [[flood-motif]] — the flood is the discontinuity dividing antediluvian and post-flood epochs. The SKL's brief "Then the flood swept over" presupposes the narratives of [[phase-1-016-eridu-genesis-flood]] and [[phase-1-006-atrahasis]] but tells none of them; the flood is structural rather than narrative.
- [[antediluvian-civilization]] — the eight antediluvian kings ruling 241,200 (or 432,000) years across five cities represent the *long-lived pre-flood civilization* motif. This is the canonical Sumerian formulation; it is the motif that recurs in (a) Genesis 5's pre-flood patriarchs (Adam–Noah, ten generations with reigns of hundreds of years), (b) Plato's Atlantis chronology in the *Timaeus* and *Critias* (9,000 years before Solon), (c) Berossos's Babyloniaca preserves the SKL antediluvian sequence directly into Hellenistic-period Greek transmission.
- [[four-ages-degeneration]] — the progressive shortening of reigns from 28,800 years (Alulim) through hundreds (post-flood Kish) to tens (Akkadian and Ur III periods) instantiates the *degenerative ages* motif: lifespans/reigns shrink as the world moves away from its primordial origin. The same trajectory structures Hesiod's *Works and Days* (Gold → Silver → Bronze → Iron) and the Hindu *yuga* cycles.
- [[cosmic-cycles]] — the sexagesimal arithmetic embedded in the antediluvian reign-totals (multiples of the sar = 3,600 years) suggests an astronomical-calendrical encoding. Jacobsen 1939 reads the totals as sexagesimal cosmic-cycle figures; the encoding is not fully understood.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-1-006-atrahasis]] — *parallel-motif* / *shared-milieu*: the flood narrated in Atrahasis is the same event referenced as "the flood swept over" in the SKL. Both texts are Mesopotamian scribal compositions of the OB period (Atrahasis is OB; the SKL has Ur III origins with OB redaction). The SKL provides the chronographic frame; Atrahasis provides the narrative content. Ref: Lambert & Millard 1969; Jacobsen 1939.
- → [[phase-1-016-eridu-genesis-flood]] — *parallel-motif* / *shared-milieu*: the Sumerian Flood Story (Eridu Genesis) names **Ziusudra of Shuruppak** as the flood hero; in some SKL recensions Ziusudra is the last antediluvian king. The two compositions are mutually-referential parts of the same Sumerian flood-and-chronography tradition. Ref: Jacobsen 1981; Civil 1969.
- → [[phase-2-005-hebrew-bible-j-source]] (and the broader Hebrew Bible primeval history Genesis 1–11) — *parallel-motif*: the **Genesis 5 antediluvian patriarchs** (Adam, Seth, Enosh, Kenan, Mahalalel, Jared, Enoch, Methuselah, Lamech, Noah — ten generations) parallel the SKL's antediluvian king list (eight kings of five cities, or in some recensions ten kings) in three structural respects: (a) a fixed-length sequence of pre-flood human-figures, (b) extraordinarily long lifespans/reigns that decrease post-flood, (c) culminating in a flood-hero (Ziusudra / Noah) who is the boundary-figure between epochs. The mechanism of transmission is contested; current consensus is *shared ANE common-stock* rather than direct literary dependence (Wenham 1987; Smith 2010), but the structural parallel is among the most striking cross-cultural correspondences in the history of religions. **The Genesis 5 → SKL parallel is one of the foundational pieces of evidence for the ANE-context reading of Genesis 1–11.** Ref: Jacobsen 1939, 1981; Wenham 1987 *Genesis 1–15*; Hess and Tsumura (eds.) 1994 *I Studied Inscriptions from Before the Flood*.
- → [[genesis-5-antediluvian-patriarchs]] — *parallel-motif*: see preceding entry. (Dead-link target — pending Phase 2 deepening; the structural parallel is documented here for cross-reference.)
- → [[phase-3-002-plato-dialogues]] — *parallel-motif*: Plato's **Atlantis narrative** in the *Timaeus* (21e–25d) and *Critias* (108e–121c) presents a pre-flood / pre-cataclysm civilization of extraordinary antiquity (9,000 years before Solon's visit to Egypt — i.e., ~-9600 BCE) destroyed by divine displeasure (Zeus's earthquake-and-flood) and remembered only by Egyptian priestly tradition. The structural parallel with the SKL antediluvian sequence is striking: (a) extraordinarily ancient pre-cataclysm civilization, (b) destruction by flood, (c) preservation of memory by priestly chronographic tradition. Whether this is (a) Plato's literary invention drawing independently on ANE common-stock motifs, (b) genuine Egyptian transmission of an ANE chronographic tradition Plato then received, or (c) coincidence — is contested. Berossos's *Babyloniaca* (~-280 BCE) shows that the Sumerian antediluvian-king tradition was being transmitted into Greek shortly after Plato; whether earlier transmission to Plato is plausible is debated. The parallel is *parallel-motif* in the strict sense — recurrence of the same structural motif without claim of direct dependence. Ref: Verbrugghe & Wickersham 1996; Naddaf 2003 *The Greek Concept of Nature*; Vidal-Naquet *The Atlantis Story*.
- → Berossos's *Babyloniaca* (~-280 BCE, preserved in Eusebius and Polyhistor) — *direct-quote* / *manuscript-transmission*: Berossos, a Babylonian priest of Bel-Marduk writing for Antiochus I, presents in Greek the ten antediluvian kings of Babylonian tradition (Aloros, Alaparos, Amelon, Ammenon, Megalaros, Daonos, Euedorachos, Amempsinos, Otiartes, Xisuthros — the last is Ziusudra), confirming that the Sumerian King List antediluvian tradition was being directly transmitted into Hellenistic-period Greek literature. Through Berossos the SKL tradition is available to Plato's Hellenistic successors. Ref: Verbrugghe & Wickersham 1996; Burstein 1978 *The Babyloniaca of Berossus*.
- → Assyrian King List (~-1100), Babylonian King List A and B — *redaction-of* / *parallel-tradition*: later Mesopotamian king-list traditions extending the SKL frame. Ref: Glassner 2004.

## Disputes
- **Date of composition.** The SKL is a *layered* composition; the bedrock disagreement is on its earliest recension date. Jacobsen 1939 favored a Sargonic-period origin (~-2200) with subsequent recensions; Steinkeller 2003 argues for an Ur III origin (~-2100); Wilcke 1989 takes a middle position. The Old Babylonian principal manuscript (WB 444, ~-1800) is unambiguous.
- **Inclusion / omission of Lagash.** The SKL conspicuously **omits Lagash** despite Lagash being one of the great Sumerian city-states (the dynasty of Ur-Nanshe, Eanatum, Entemena, Urukagina; later [[ur-bau]] and [[gudea-of-lagash]]). The omission is presumed deliberate — Nippur-scribal-tradition bias against Lagash, possibly because Lagash's local theology (Ningirsu, Bau) operated semi-independently of the Nippur theological frame.
- **Historicity of antediluvian kings.** No antediluvian king is independently attested by contemporary inscription. The post-flood First Dynasty of Kish begins with [[enmebaragesi]], who is the *earliest historically-attested* king. Everything before Enmebaragesi is legendary in the strict primary-evidence sense.
- **Genesis 5 parallel — mechanism.** Direct literary dependence (older Wellhausen-school view), shared ANE common-stock tradition (current majority — Wenham 1987; Hess/Tsumura 1994), or independent parallel development (a minority). The structural parallel is uncontested; the mechanism is contested.
- **Plato-Atlantis parallel.** Whether the Atlantis chronology echoes ANE antediluvian-king traditions transmitted via Egyptian priestly mediation, or is Plato's literary invention drawing on shared Mediterranean common-stock, is debated. Most Plato scholars treat Atlantis as Platonic invention; comparative-religion scholarship is more open to ANE-tradition transmission via Egypt. Ref: Naddaf 2003; Vidal-Naquet.
- **Numerical encoding of reign-lengths.** That the antediluvian totals are sexagesimal multiples of the sar (3,600 years) is uncontested; what the encoding *means* (astronomical-calendrical, theological, both) is debated.
