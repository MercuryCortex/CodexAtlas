# The Eridu Genesis (Sumerian Flood Story)

## What it is
A fragmentary Sumerian-language mythological narrative, ~300 lines in its original form (only ~140 partially preserved), that integrates the creation of humanity, the establishment of the antediluvian cities, the flood, and the salvation of the flood hero **Ziusudra** of Shuruppak. The principal manuscript is **CBS 10673**, a six-column tablet from the Nippur scribal-school excavations, now in the Penn Museum, edited by Miguel Civil (1969) as an appendix to the Lambert-Millard *Atrahasis* edition. Thorkild Jacobsen (1981) coined the title "Eridu Genesis" and argued the composition is a continuous creation-and-flood narrative — the closest Sumerian-language analogue to the structural shape of Genesis 1–9. The text's narrative as best reconstructed: (1) the gods An, Enlil, Enki, and Ninhursag establish humanity and the antediluvian cities — Eridu, Bad-tibira, Larak, Sippar, and Shuruppak (the five-city antediluvian sequence preserved also in the [[phase-1-015-sumerian-king-list]]); (2) the gods' decision to send the flood is taken in council, with Enki dissenting; (3) Enki warns the pious king Ziusudra of Shuruppak, instructing him to build a great boat; (4) the flood sweeps over for seven days and seven nights; (5) Utu/Shamash the sun god emerges, Ziusudra opens the boat, prostrates himself before An and Enlil; (6) Ziusudra is granted "life like a god" and is settled in the eastern paradise **Dilmun** (later identified with Bahrain). The Ziusudra of this text is the **Sumerian original of Atra-ḫasīs (Atrahasis)** and **Ut-napishtim (Standard Babylonian Gilgamesh Tablet XI)** and **Xisuthros (Berossos)** and (per the parallel-motif claim) **Noah** of Genesis 6–9.

## Primary text — selected excerpts (Civil 1969 / ETCSL 1.7.4)

### The decision of the divine council (fragments — column iii reconstructed)

> An, Enlil, Enki, Ninhursag —
> the great gods —
> ... sent flood-rain over the cult-places.

(Civil 1969, p. 142; Lambert and Millard 1969, p. 142.)

### The warning to Ziusudra (column iv)

The text-fragment opens with Enki — bound by oath of the divine council not to reveal the decree — speaking *through* the reed wall of Ziusudra's house (the same evasion-of-oath device used in [[phase-1-006-atrahasis]] Tablet III):

> "Wall, listen to me;
> reed-fence, attend my words.
> A flood will sweep over the cult-places
> to destroy the seed of mankind...
> This is the decision of the assembly of the gods."

(Civil 1969, p. 142; cf. ETCSL 1.7.4.)

### The flood (column v)

> All the wind-storms, exceedingly powerful, attacked as one;
> at the same time the flood swept over the cult-places.
> For seven days and seven nights
> the flood swept over the land.
> The huge boat was tossed about by the windstorms on the great waters.
> Utu came forth, the one who sheds light on heaven and earth.
> Ziusudra opened a window in the huge boat;
> the hero Utu brought his rays into the giant boat.
> Ziusudra the king prostrated himself before Utu.
> The king sacrificed oxen and offered innumerable sheep.

(Civil 1969, pp. 143–144; ETCSL 1.7.4.)

### Ziusudra's apotheosis (column vi — the final preserved section)

> "Life like that of a god they gave him,
> breath eternal like that of a god they brought down for him."
> ...
> In the land of crossing, the land of Dilmun, where the sun rises,
> they made him dwell.

(Civil 1969, p. 145; ETCSL 1.7.4.)

The translation of "the land of crossing, the land of Dilmun" — eastward, paradisal — is among the most debated phrases in Sumerian literature. Jacobsen (1981) reads it as cognate with the "garden eastward in Eden" motif of Genesis 2:8; this is *parallel-motif*, not direct dependence.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Old Babylonian period scribal-school transmission (~-1900 to -1600 BCE). The composition's *origin* is presumed earlier — Ur III or even late third-millennium — but only OB copies survive.
- **Religious milieu.** Standard Sumerian theological frame: An (heaven), Enlil (storm/decree), Enki (wisdom/water/savior), Ninhursag (mother goddess) as the senior divine council. The flood is *decreed* by the council (Enlil's initiative) and *evaded* by Enki's stratagem. This is the Sumerian theological pattern shared with [[phase-1-006-atrahasis]] and the later Standard Babylonian Gilgamesh Tablet XI.
- **Who was behind it.** Anonymous Sumerian scribal-school composers, principally at Nippur. The composition belongs to the scholastic-religious literary corpus of the OB Edubba'a curriculum.
- **Discovery / transmission.** CBS 10673 was excavated at Nippur by the University of Pennsylvania expedition (1888–1900). Arno Poebel published the first edition in 1914 (*Historical Texts*, PBS 4/1, pp. 9–70). Kramer revisited and translated portions in the mid-20th century. Civil's 1969 edition — printed as Appendix to the Lambert-Millard *Atrahasis* edition — is the modern standard. Jacobsen's 1981 *JBL* article gave the composition the title "Eridu Genesis" and proposed the integrative reading.

## Themes and motifs
- [[flood-motif]] — the **earliest Sumerian-language preserved version** of the Mesopotamian flood narrative. The structural elements (divine council decision, warning of the chosen king through speech to the reed wall, boat construction, seven-day flood, post-flood sacrifice, divine reconciliation, apotheosis of the hero) are the prototype that recurs (with variation) in [[phase-1-006-atrahasis]], in Standard Babylonian Gilgamesh Tablet XI, and (with significant theological redirection) in Genesis 6–9.
- [[antediluvian-civilization]] — the five antediluvian cities (Eridu, Bad-tibira, Larak, Sippar, Shuruppak) — the same sequence as the [[phase-1-015-sumerian-king-list]] antediluvian section — establish the *pre-flood civilization* as a discrete cosmological epoch. Ziusudra is the boundary-figure between the antediluvian and post-flood epochs.
- [[divine-council]] — the gods deliberate in assembly; the decree is collective; Enki dissents and evades the binding oath. The same structural device as Atrahasis Tablet III.
- [[primordial-waters]] — the flood as the return of the primordial waters; the *re-emergence* of dry land after seven days as cosmogonic *re-creation*. The cosmogonic-resonance of the flood-waters connects this composition to the broader Mesopotamian *abzu/apsu/tiamat* primordial-waters complex.
- [[four-ages-degeneration]] — the antediluvian / post-flood discontinuity is the first instance of the *epochs-of-the-world* schema that recurs in Hesiod's metallic-ages and in the Hindu *yuga* cycles. The flood is the *catastrophic boundary* between epochs.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-1-006-atrahasis]] — *parallel-motif* / *redaction-relationship*: Atrahasis is the Akkadian-language elaboration of the same flood-tradition; the Eridu Genesis is the Sumerian-language witness. Whether Atrahasis is a *direct redaction* of the Eridu Genesis or both derive from a common older oral tradition is debated. The reed-wall warning device and the seven-day flood are common to both. Ref: Lambert & Millard 1969; Civil 1969; Jacobsen 1981.
- → [[phase-1-015-sumerian-king-list]] — *shared-milieu* / *parallel-motif*: the SKL names Ziusudra of Shuruppak as the last antediluvian king in some recensions; the SKL's "Then the flood swept over" is the chronographic shorthand for the narrative content of the Eridu Genesis. The two texts are mutually-referential parts of the same Sumerian flood-and-chronography complex.
- → [[phase-1-004-gilgamesh-old-babylonian]] — *parallel-motif*: the OB Gilgamesh does not yet contain the flood story (which is added in the SB recension Tablet XI, drawing on Atrahasis); but Ziusudra → Atra-ḫasīs → Ut-napishtim is the chain of name-and-narrative transmission. Gilgamesh's encounter with Ut-napishtim in SB XI is the Akkadian inheritance of the Sumerian Ziusudra. Ref: George 2003 vol. 1, pp. 22–28.
- → [[phase-2-005-hebrew-bible-j-source]] (the J-source flood narrative of Genesis 6–9; the P-source overlay is interwoven) — *parallel-motif* / *contested-influence*: the Hebrew Bible flood narrative shares with the Eridu Genesis (and its Atrahasis successor): divine decision, warning of the chosen man, boat construction, the flood, post-flood sacrifice, divine reconciliation, apotheosis (in Genesis re-cast as covenant). The motivation differs — *moral evil* in Genesis vs. *noise/overpopulation* in Atrahasis; the Eridu Genesis is too fragmentary on this point. The transmission mechanism is contested (direct literary dependence; shared ANE common-stock; polemical engagement); the structural parallel is uncontested. Ref: Jacobsen 1981; Hess & Tsumura 1994; Heidel 1949.
- → Berossos's *Babyloniaca* (~-280 BCE) — *manuscript-transmission*: Berossos's Xisuthros is Ziusudra in Hellenistic-Greek transmission, preserving the Sumerian flood tradition into Greek literature.

## Disputes
- **Date of composition.** OB (~-1700 to -1600) is firm for the principal manuscript; the *underlying composition* may be considerably earlier (Ur III; possibly late ED). Civil 1969 is cautious; Jacobsen 1981 leans toward earlier composition.
- **Relationship to Atrahasis.** Direct precursor (Eridu Genesis → Atrahasis) vs. parallel Sumerian-Akkadian compositions drawing on shared oral tradition: debated.
- **The Dilmun apotheosis and the Edenic-paradise motif.** Whether the "land of crossing, land of Dilmun, where the sun rises" anticipates or shares common-stock with the Genesis 2 garden-eastward-in-Eden motif is one of the more debated comparative-religion claims. Jacobsen 1981 finds the parallel suggestive; current consensus is *parallel-motif* without direct dependence.
- **Continuity of the text as preserved.** Jacobsen 1981 reads CBS 10673 as a continuous narrative integrating creation-antediluvian-flood; Civil 1969 is more cautious about the degree of integration achievable from the fragmentary text.
- **Reconstruction of motivations.** Whether the Eridu Genesis's flood is motivated by *noise* (as in Atrahasis) or by some other reason (the relevant lines are not preserved) is unresolved.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges

- **Ziusudra → Atrahasis → Ut-napishtim → Xisuthros → Noah — the oldest flood narrative and the root of a 4,000-year transmission chain**: The Eridu Genesis preserves the oldest Sumerian-language flood narrative (~-2000 to -1600 BCE manuscript, composition possibly earlier). Its structural elements — divine council's decision, the chosen king warned through the reed wall, boat construction, seven-day flood, post-flood sacrifice, apotheosis of the hero — are replicated in Atrahasis, in Standard Babylonian Gilgamesh Tablet XI (George 2003), in Berossos's Hellenistic *Babyloniaca* (Xisuthros), and in Genesis 6–9 (Noah). This is the oldest node in a single transmission chain that shapes Jewish, Christian, and Islamic theology of divine judgment and cosmic reset (Civil 1969; Lambert & Millard 1969; Jacobsen 1981, *JBL* 100/4; → [[phase-1-006-atrahasis]], → [[phase-2-005-hebrew-bible-j-source]]).

- **Enki's reed-wall speech — the oldest documented sacred-information-evasion device in literature**: Enki is sworn by the divine council not to warn humans of the flood. He evades the oath by speaking *to the reed wall* of Ziusudra's house — technically not warning the man, but ensuring the man overhears every word. This "bound-by-oath evasion through intermediary" is one of the oldest documented divine-legal maneuver motifs in religious literature, and it recurs verbatim in Atrahasis Tablet III and SB Gilgamesh Tablet XI. It was old enough by the Old Babylonian period to be a recognized literary convention — the earliest ancestor of the "angel warning through ambiguous intermediary" tradition (Civil 1969; Dalley 2000; → [[phase-1-006-atrahasis]]).

- **Ziusudra at Dilmun — the oldest preserved paradisal apotheosis, anticipating the "garden eastward in Eden"**: After the flood, Ziusudra is granted "life like a god" and settled in "the land of crossing, the land of Dilmun, where the sun rises." This eastward paradisal-settlement of the flood hero is the oldest preserved version of the translated/divinized-survivor motif. Jacobsen (1981) identified the directional phrase ("where the sun rises") as strikingly parallel to Genesis 2:8's "garden eastward in Eden"; current consensus treats it as parallel-motif within the shared ANE cosmogonic grammar without direct dependence (Jacobsen 1981, *JBL* 100/4:513–529; → [[phase-2-005-hebrew-bible-j-source]]).

- **Flood motivation: noise/overpopulation → moral evil — the most consequential theological reversal in ANE comparative religion**: The Eridu Genesis (and Atrahasis more explicitly) shows the flood was motivated by *noise* — human overpopulation disturbing the gods' rest. Genesis 6's motivation (human moral wickedness, the violence filling the earth) is a deliberate reversal of this Mesopotamian frame. The Hebrew redactors knew Mesopotamian flood tradition (the Babylonian exile guaranteed exposure to the SB Gilgamesh at minimum); their reframing as a moral statement rather than a managerial solution is the single most important theological difference between the traditions, and it was intentional. The flood in Genesis is a moral argument against Babylonian anthropology (Heidel, A. 1949, *The Gilgamesh Epic and Old Testament Parallels*, UChicago; Jacobsen 1981; Hess & Tsumura 1994, *I Studied Inscriptions from Before the Flood*, Eisenbrauns; → [[phase-1-006-atrahasis]]).
