# Yoga-Sūtra of Patañjali

## What it is
The foundational text of the classical Hindu Yoga *darśana* — 195 *sūtras* (or 196 in some manuscript traditions) in four *pādas* (chapters): *Samādhi-pāda* (on meditative absorption, 51 sūtras), *Sādhana-pāda* (on practice and the eight limbs, 55 sūtras), *Vibhūti-pāda* (on extraordinary powers gained through yoga, 55 sūtras), *Kaivalya-pāda* (on isolation / liberation, 34 sūtras). The compositional date is contested across ~2nd c. BCE to ~4th–5th c. CE; the strongest recent text-critical position (Maas 2013) places the composite Sūtra + Vyāsa-Bhāṣya work at ~325–425 CE. The Yoga-Sūtra synthesizes Sāṃkhya dualist metaphysics (*puruṣa* / *prakṛti*) with a systematic eight-limb meditative-yogic discipline — the *aṣṭāṅga-yoga*: *yama* (ethical restraints), *niyama* (ethical observances), *āsana* (posture), *prāṇāyāma* (breath control), *pratyāhāra* (withdrawal of the senses), *dhāraṇā* (concentration), *dhyāna* (meditation), *samādhi* (absorption). The fourth-chapter cosmological discussions of *citta* (mind) and its modifications connect Patañjali yoga to broader Indian philosophy-of-mind discourse.

## Primary text — selected passages

### Yoga-Sūtra 1.1–4 — The opening and definition of yoga
*Trans. Bryant, E.F. (2009). The Yoga Sūtras of Patañjali. North Point Press.*
> 1.1: Now, the teachings on yoga [*atha yoga-anuśāsanam*].
> 1.2: Yoga is the cessation of the fluctuations of the mind [*yogaś citta-vṛtti-nirodhaḥ*].
> 1.3: Then the seer abides in its own form [*tadā draṣṭuḥ svarūpe'vasthānam*].
> 1.4: Otherwise [the seer] takes on the same form as the fluctuations [*vṛtti-sārūpyam itaratra*].

*The entire Yoga-Sūtra system is compressed into these four sūtras. YS 1.2 is the single most influential sentence in all of yoga literature: *yoga = citta-vṛtti-nirodha*. When the mind's fluctuations cease, the witnessing consciousness (*puruṣa* / *draṣṭu*) recognizes itself as pure, undisturbed awareness.*

### Yoga-Sūtra 2.29–32 — The eight limbs (*aṣṭāṅga*)
*Trans. Bryant 2009*
> 2.29: The eight limbs of yoga are: restraints [*yama*], observances [*niyama*], posture [*āsana*], breath control [*prāṇāyāma*], withdrawal of the senses [*pratyāhāra*], concentration [*dhāraṇā*], meditation [*dhyāna*], and absorption [*samādhi*].
> 2.30: Among these, the restraints are: non-violence [*ahiṃsā*], truthfulness [*satya*], non-stealing [*asteya*], sexual continence [*brahmacarya*], and non-possessiveness [*aparigraha*].

*The eight-limb structure is Patañjali's most widely recognized teaching and the basis of most modern yoga curricula. The first *yama*, *ahiṃsā* (non-violence), is the ethical principle that Gandhi made the centerpiece of his political theology — tracing explicitly to the yoga and Jain traditions.*

### Yoga-Sūtra 1.23–28 — *Īśvara-praṇidhāna*: devotion to the Lord
*Trans. Bryant 2009*
> 1.23: Or [*samādhi* is attained] through devotion to God [*Īśvara-praṇidhānādvā*].
> 1.24: *Īśvara* is a special kind of *puruṣa*, untouched by afflictions, actions, fruits of actions, or impressions...
> 1.27: His designator is the syllable *Oṃ* [*tasya vācakaḥ praṇavaḥ*].
> 1.28: Its repetition and reflection upon its meaning [is the practice].

*The Yoga-Sūtra's unique *Īśvara* is not a creator-God but a "special *puruṣa*" — an exemplary liberated consciousness used as an object of meditative focus. The identification of *Īśvara* with *Oṃ* links the text to the Upaniṣadic *praṇava* theology and generates the practice of *mantra-yoga*.*

*Citations: Bryant 2009, North Point Press; Larson & Bhattacharya 2008, Motilal Banarsidass.*

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Late-classical North India — late-Kushan to early-Gupta period (~200–425 CE in the most-supported dating). Sanskrit learning is institutionalized in court patronage; *darśana* (philosophical school) literature is being canonized in *sūtra* form across Hindu, Buddhist, and Jain traditions.
- **Religious milieu.** Buddhism and Hinduism are intensely interactive — sharing meditative vocabulary, contesting metaphysical positions, mutually influencing each other's contemplative practice. The Yoga-Sūtra is one of the principal Hindu-Buddhist interface texts; substantial Buddhist *dhyāna* / *abhidharma* psychological vocabulary is absorbed and reframed within a Sāṃkhya metaphysical structure.
- **Who was behind it.** Patañjali — almost certainly a compiler-systematizer rather than an originator. The Yoga-Sūtra is a synthetic text drawing on a longer tradition of yogic practice and reflection (visible already in the Upaniṣads — see [[phase-2-021-shvetashvatara-upanishad]] — and in the early Buddhist suttas).
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous Sanskrit commentary tradition from the Vyāsa-Bhāṣya (~5th–6th c.) to the present. The 19th-c. Western reception was inaugurated by James R. Ballantyne (1852) and decisively shaped by Vivekananda's *Raja Yoga* (1896), which made Patañjali central to the modern global yoga discourse.

## Themes and motifs
- [[ascent-of-the-soul]] — the eight-limb progression from ethical-physical foundations (*yama*, *niyama*, *āsana*) through energetic-perceptual disciplines (*prāṇāyāma*, *pratyāhāra*) to cognitive-meditative absorption (*dhāraṇā*, *dhyāna*, *samādhi*) constitutes a structured ascent toward liberation.
- [[apatheia-and-virtue]] — the *yamas* (non-violence, truthfulness, non-stealing, sexual restraint, non-possessiveness) and *niyamas* (cleanliness, contentment, austerity, study, devotion to *Īśvara*) constitute a moral-disciplinary foundation structurally parallel to Stoic and Hellenistic ethical-philosophical-discipline schemata.
- [[mystery-initiation]] — the soteriological progression to *kaivalya* (isolation, liberation) is a graded initiation into progressively deeper meditative states.
- [[soul-immortality]] / [[non-duality]] — the goal *kaivalya* is the recognition of the *puruṣa* (witnessing consciousness) as utterly distinct from *prakṛti* (the entire material-mental world). This is a *dualist* liberation (Yoga-Sūtra is technically Sāṃkhya-dualist, not Vedāntic non-dualist), but the structure-of-liberation-as-recognition-of-pure-consciousness shares much with the Vedāntic non-dual position.
- **Yogic *siddhis*** — chapter 3 catalogs the supernormal powers (telepathy, levitation, knowledge of past lives, etc.) accessible to advanced yogis. The Yoga-Sūtra is the canonical Sanskrit treatment.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-2-016-early-buddhist-suttas]] — *parallel-motif* / *direct-influence*: the Yoga-Sūtra's *dhyāna* / *samādhi* terminology and meditative-absorption typology is substantially continuous with the Buddhist *jhāna* / *samādhi* schema; the structural overlap is so close that mainstream scholarship (Larson, Bronkhorst 1993 *The Two Traditions of Meditation in Ancient India*) reads the Yoga-Sūtra as substantially absorbing Buddhist meditative technology into a Sāṃkhya frame. The *aṣṭāṅga-yoga* and the Buddhist *āryāṣṭāṅgika-mārga* (Noble Eightfold Path) are structurally parallel.
- → [[phase-2-021-shvetashvatara-upanishad]] — *parallel-motif* / *commentary-on*: the proto-yoga of ŚU 2 (postural-respiratory-meditative discipline) is one of the principal Upaniṣadic sources to which Patañjali's *aṣṭāṅga-yoga* is downstream.
- → [[phase-3-005-stoic-foundational-texts]] — *parallel-motif* (contested-historical-direction): the Stoic *prosochē* (attention to interior states) and *prokopē* (graded progress toward sage-hood) are structurally adjacent to the Yoga-Sūtra's introspective-meditative discipline. The substantial comparative-philosophical literature (McEvilley 2002; Halbfass 1988 with caveats; earlier: Conger 1952) has proposed structural parallels; some scholars have argued for historical contact via the post-Alexandrian Bactrian / Gandharan zone (~300 BCE – 200 CE). Pyrrho of Elis is reported (Diogenes Laertius 9.61) to have met "the gymnosophists" (Indian renouncers) on Alexander's expedition; the report's reliability is contested; if accepted, it would establish a documented Greek-Indian contemplative-philosophical contact ~325 BCE. The mainstream Indological position treats the parallels as structurally suggestive but historically unproven.
- → [[phase-5-014-abhinavagupta-tantraloka]] — *commentary-on* / *direct-influence*: Abhinavagupta cites the Yoga-Sūtra; medieval Kashmir Shaivism integrates Patañjali yoga into its Tantric framework.
- → Modern global yoga (Singleton 2010): the Yoga-Sūtra has been the principal legitimating-authority text for modern global postural yoga since Vivekananda's 1896 *Raja Yoga*, even though modern postural yoga is historically more indebted to the medieval Hatha Yoga tradition than to Patañjali. This is one of the clearest pre-modern → modern textual-canonization shifts in the modern history of Hinduism.

## Disputes
- **Date**: 2nd c. BCE through 4th–5th c. CE; Maas 2013's ~325–425 CE for the Pātañjala-Yoga-Śāstra is the strongest current position.
- **Authorship**: the existence of a compiler is widely accepted; unitary authorship is contested.
- **Buddhist influence direction**: the consensus is substantial Yoga-Sūtra absorption of Buddhist meditative technology; some traditionalist Hindu readings reverse the direction or deny direct influence.
- **Hellenistic-philosophy contact (McEvilley 2002)**: contested; mainstream Indology treats as unproven; the structural parallels (Stoic *apatheia* / yogic *vairāgya*; Pyrrhonian *epochē* / yogic *nirodha*) are real, the historical-causal connection is not established.
- **Modern yoga's relation to Patañjali (Singleton 2010)**: substantially documented; the popular-traditional reading of modern yoga as "Patañjali's yoga" is historically misleading.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges

- **YS 1.2 *yoga = citta-vṛtti-nirodha* + Vivekananda 1896 — most globally exported Indian philosophical concept in history**: The single sentence *yogaś citta-vṛtti-nirodhaḥ* became, via Vivekananda's *Raja Yoga* (1896), the defining formula of the modern global yoga movement, now practiced by an estimated 300 million people worldwide. The path from a ~4th-century Sanskrit sūtra to the largest mass embodied-practice movement in modernity is the most consequential Indian-to-global transmission in the modern history of religion (Singleton, M. 2010. *Yoga Body*. Oxford UP. pp.67–95; De Michelis, E. 2004. *A History of Modern Yoga*. Continuum. pp.149–180; Strauss, S. 2005. *Positioning Yoga*. Berg. pp.1–30).

- **Yogic *samādhi* / Buddhist *jhāna* — most extensively documented cross-tradition meditative convergence in Asian religion**: The Yoga-Sūtra's eight-limbed progression through *dhāraṇā*, *dhyāna*, and *samādhi* is structurally parallel to the Buddhist *jhāna* typology — so close that Bronkhorst's *The Two Traditions of Meditation in Ancient India* (1993) argues the Yoga-Sūtra substantially absorbed Buddhist meditative technology into a Sāṃkhya metaphysical frame. This is the most-documented pre-modern East-East cross-tradition conceptual transfer in the history of religions (Bronkhorst, J. 1993. *The Two Traditions of Meditation in Ancient India*. Motilal Banarsidass. pp.95–117; Larson, G.J. & Bhattacharya, R.S. 2008. pp.65–78; Wynne, A. 2007. *The Origin of Buddhist Meditation*. Routledge. pp.56–67).

- **Stoic *apatheia* / Pyrrhonian *epochē* ↔ yogic *vairāgya* / *nirodha* — most structurally precise East-West philosophical parallel in the literature**: The Yoga-Sūtra's *vairāgya* (non-attachment) parallels Stoic *apatheia* (freedom from passion as the condition of rational action); the Pyrrhonian *epochē* (suspension of judgment) parallels yogic *nirodha* (cessation of mental modifications). McEvilley's *The Shape of Ancient Thought* (2002) documents 50+ structural parallels across 900 pages; Pyrrho's reported encounter with "gymnosophists" on Alexander's expedition (~325 BCE) is the earliest documented Greek-Indian contemplative contact claim (McEvilley, T. 2002. *The Shape of Ancient Thought*. Allworth. pp.393–455; Flintoff, E. 1980. "Pyrrho and India." *Phronesis* 25/1. pp.88–108; Halbfass, W. 1988. *India and Europe*. SUNY. pp.24–35).

- **Aṣṭāṅga-yoga ↔ Noble Eightfold Path — parallel eight-limbed liberation structures across the two great Indian traditions**: Both the Buddhist *āryāṣṭāṅgika-mārga* (Noble Eightfold Path) and Patañjali's *aṣṭāṅga-yoga* are eight-limbed schemes for liberation, beginning with ethical foundations (*sīla* / *yama-niyama*), proceeding through meditative absorption, and culminating in liberating insight (*nibbāna* / *kaivalya*). The structural parallelism is so precise it is cited as primary evidence for mutual formative influence between Buddhist and Hindu meditative traditions ~200 BCE–400 CE (Larson & Bhattacharya 2008. pp.78–86; Gethin, R. 1998. *The Foundations of Buddhism*. Oxford UP. pp.81–88; Harvey, P. 1990. *An Introduction to Buddhism*. Cambridge UP. pp.70–78).
