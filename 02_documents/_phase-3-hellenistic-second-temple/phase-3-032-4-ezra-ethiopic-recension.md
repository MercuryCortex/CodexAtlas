# 4 Ezra (Mashafa Ezra Sutuʾel, the Apocalypse of Ezra)

## What it is
**4 Ezra** (Vulgate Latin *4 Esdrae*; Geʼez *Mashafa Ezra Sutuʾel*; in Protestant Apocrypha and KJV preserved as the second half of *2 Esdras* chapters 3–14) is one of the most theologically powerful and emotionally raw post-Temple-destruction Jewish apocalypses, composed in Hebrew (lost) around 70–120 CE in the aftermath of the [[event-destruction-of-second-temple-70]]. The original Hebrew text was translated into Greek (also lost), and from Greek into the surviving daughter-versions: Latin (Vulgate appendix), Syriac, **Geʼez (canonical in [[tradition-ethiopian-orthodox-tewahedo]])**, Armenian, Arabic, Georgian, and Old Slavonic.

The text is structured as **seven visions** experienced by the post-exilic scribe Ezra (pseudonymously identified — the work is contemporary with 2 Baruch in addressing the *post-70-CE* destruction by retrojecting the question onto the *post-587-BCE* destruction). The seven visions move from raw theodicy-protest in the first three (Ezra demanding answers from God about why Israel suffers, with the archangel Uriel as his interlocutor) through three increasingly-visionary apocalypses (the woman-mourning-her-son who becomes the new Jerusalem; the eagle-with-twelve-wings representing Rome; the Man from the Sea, the pre-existent messianic figure who emerges from the sea to judge the nations) to a final commission-of-Ezra-as-restorer-of-scripture.

The work is among the most theologically searching texts of late Second-Temple Judaism — it does not flinch from the post-70-CE theological problem of how a covenant people can suffer catastrophic defeat. Ezra's questions to Uriel (4 Ezra 3–7) are some of the most direct theodicy-protests in canonical Jewish-Christian literature; the eventual reconciliation comes through eschatological-messianic vision rather than through philosophical solution.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Post-70 CE Roman Judea, after the destruction of the Second Temple. The text is contemporary with 2 Baruch and with the early Yavneh rabbinic consolidation; it represents an *apocalyptic* response to the same crisis that produces the proto-Masoretic-rabbinic *scholarly-juridical* response.
- **Religious milieu.** Late Second-Temple Judaism in its post-Temple shock. The text grapples with theodicy in raw and prolonged form before eventually reaching eschatological resolution. The Manessenic confession (4 Ezra 7:48 — *"O Adam, what have you done? For when you sinned the fall was not yours alone but ours also who descend from you"*) is one of the strongest *endogenous*-origin-of-evil statements in Jewish literature, in tension with the *exogenous*-Watchers-origin of 1 Enoch.
- **Who was behind it.** Anonymous; pseudonymously attributed to the post-exilic Ezra (Ezra 7–10 / Nehemiah 8). The Geʼez recension's distinctive name *Sutuʾel* derives from the throne-name *Sealtiel/Salatiel* associated with Ezra in 4 Ezra 3:1.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous Ethiopian-canonical use; Latin via Vulgate appendix; rediscovered in Western scholarship via the Vulgate.

## Themes and motifs
- [[apocalyptic-revelation]] — the seven-vision structure is the most-elaborated apocalyptic form in any Jewish text alongside 1 Enoch.
- [[theodicy]] — the most direct and prolonged theodicy-protest in canonical Jewish-Christian literature.
- [[final-judgment]] — the eschatological court is central.
- [[messianic-revelation]] — the "Man from the Sea" (4 Ezra 13) and the suffering Messiah-son (7:28–29) are foundational pre-Christian Jewish messianic figures; the Christian engagement is extensive.
- [[ethiopic-canon-broad]] — the Geʼez Mashafa Ezra Sutuʾel is one of the most-prominent Ethiopian-canonical books absent from Protestant Bibles.

## Disputes
- **Christian interpolations.** Latin chapters 1–2 (called *5 Ezra*) and 15–16 (*6 Ezra*) are Christian additions framing the original Jewish 4 Ezra 3–14. Most scholarship identifies these and treats the Jewish core (chs. 3–14) separately.
- **Suffering-Messiah of 4 Ezra 7:28–29.** Whether the death-of-the-Messiah passage is original Jewish or post-Christian interpolation. Stone 1990 accepts it as original-Jewish; some earlier scholarship treated it as Christian.
- **Date.** Most scholarship places composition between 70 and 120 CE, with the eagle-vision (chs. 11–12) sometimes used to date the work specifically to Domitian's reign (~95 CE) — the twelve-wings-three-heads structure being read as a succession-vision of Roman emperors.
