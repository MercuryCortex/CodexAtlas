# Life of Pericles (Plutarch)

## What it is
Plutarch's **biography of Pericles**, the statesman who dominated Athens at the height of its 5th-century-BCE power, written around 100–120 CE as one of the *Parallel Lives* (paired with the Roman general Fabius Maximus). It is the principal continuous ancient account of the **Periclean age** — Athenian democracy and empire, Pericles's oratory and character, his companion Aspasia, and above all the great **building programme on the Acropolis** (the [[parthenon-athens|Parthenon]] and its sister monuments) — and it ends with his death in the plague that struck wartime Athens.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Written under the Roman Empire by a Greek who saw the classical past as a school of virtue; the *Life* idealizes Periclean Athens while preserving real detail.
- **Religious milieu.** Plutarch was himself a priest of [[apollo]] at Delphi; the *Life* treats the Acropolis programme as religious as well as civic — the Parthenon is the temple of [[athena]] Parthenos.
- **Who was behind it.** Plutarch of Chaeronea, philosopher-biographer.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Transmitted through Byzantine manuscripts; the *Lives* became canonical European reading from the Renaissance.

## Themes and motifs
- [[divine-kingship]] — Plutarch's portrait of Pericles's near-monarchical ascendancy within a democracy raises the classic question of the one and the many in sacral-civic authority.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[parthenon-athens]] — *primary-source*: the *Life* is a chief literary witness to the Parthenon's commissioning, cost, and controversy.
- → [[phase-3-024-suetonius-twelve-caesars]] — *genre-parallel*: Greco-Roman biography, here in Plutarch's morally-exemplary Greek mode against Suetonius's Roman archival one.

## Disputes
- **Reliability.** Plutarch writes moral biography centuries after the events; his sources and his idealization must be weighed against Thucydides and the epigraphic record (Stadter 1989).

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **The literary blueprint of a 'Golden Age.'** Plutarch's *Pericles* fixed the enduring Western image of classical Athens as a cultural high-water mark, and it is the narrative through which the [[parthenon-athens|Parthenon]] — the most imitated building in the world — entered the political imagination of the Renaissance and the modern republics (Duff 1999; Stadter 1989).
