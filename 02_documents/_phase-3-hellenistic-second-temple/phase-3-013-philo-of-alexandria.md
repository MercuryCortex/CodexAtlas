# Philo of Alexandria — Philosophical Treatises

## What it is
This node bundles Philo's philosophical-exegetical corpus, focused on the two most influential works:
- **De Opificio Mundi (On the Creation of the World)** — a Platonist reading of Genesis 1: God first creates the *intelligible cosmos* (Plato's world of Forms) as a *thought* in the divine Logos, then uses this as the pattern for the visible cosmos. Genesis 1 is read as the *Timaeus* with Moses replacing Plato.
- **Legum Allegoriae (Allegorical Interpretation)** — three books of detailed allegorical exegesis of Genesis 2–3: Adam = mind, Eve = sense-perception, the serpent = pleasure, the four rivers of Eden = the four cardinal virtues, etc.

These set the program for the *Allegorical Commentary* series (~20 surviving treatises) on Genesis. Other major works: *Quaestiones in Genesim et Exodum* (catechetical question-and-answer format, mostly Armenian); *De Vita Mosis*; *De Specialibus Legibus*; *Quod Deus sit Immutabilis*; *De Fuga et Inventione*; *De Migratione Abrahami*; and the historical works (*In Flaccum*, *Legatio ad Gaium*).

## Primary text — selected excerpts

The excerpts use C. D. Yonge's 1854–1855 *The Works of Philo Judaeus* translation, which is in the public domain and freely available at the Early Christian Writings site and at sacred-texts.com. Yonge is Victorian, sometimes loose, and is now superseded for scholarly use by the Loeb Classical Library (Colson, Whitaker, Marcus, 12 vols., 1929–1962). The standard Greek text is Cohn-Wendland (1896–1930).

### *De Opificio Mundi* 16–25 — the intelligible world as God's thought, with the Logos as architect

The hinge passage of Hellenistic-Jewish Platonism. Philo reads Genesis 1's six-day creation as occurring *in two stages*: first God conceives the *intelligible cosmos* (Plato's world of Forms) within the divine Logos, *then* uses this as the model for the visible cosmos. The Logos is the thought-of-God that *is* the pattern.

> God, being God, assumed that a beautiful copy would never be produced except from a beautiful pattern, and that no object of perception would be irreproachable which was not fashioned with reference to an archetypal idea conceived by the intellect... As therefore the city, when previously shadowed out in the mind of the man of architectural skill, had no external place, but was stamped solely in the mind of the workman, so in the same manner neither can the world which existed in ideas have had any other local position except the divine reason (logos) which made them.
>
> For what other place could there be for his powers sufficient to receive and contain, I do not say all, but even any one of them whatever, in its simple form? ... If any one were to wish to use more undisguised terms, he would not call the world, which is perceptible only by the intellect, anything else but the reason of God already occupied in the creation of the world; for neither is a city, while only perceptible by the intellect, anything else but the reason of the architect, who is already designing to build one perceptible to the external senses, on the model of that one which is so only to the intellect.

Citation: *De Opificio Mundi* 16–24, trans. C. D. Yonge 1854; cf. Colson, *Philo* I, Loeb LCL 226 (1929), pp. 14–21.

### *Legum Allegoriae* III.96 — the Logos as the image of God and the seal

Philo's Logos is at once the *image* of God and the *seal* by which the cosmos is stamped — Platonic Forms language fused with Stoic seminal-reason language and biblical "image of God."

> But the shadow of God is his word, which he used like an instrument when he was making the world. And this shadow, and, as it were, model, is the archetype of other things. For, as God is himself the model of that image which he has now called a shadow, so also that image is the model of other things, as he showed when he commenced giving the law to the Israelites, and said, "And God made man according to the image of God."

Citation: *Legum Allegoriae* III.96, trans. Yonge 1854; cf. Colson, Loeb LCL 226, pp. 364–365.

### *Quis Rerum Divinarum Heres Sit* 205–206 — the Logos between God and creation

A locus classicus for the Logos as *mediator*: standing on the border, separating uncreated from created, and presenting the creature's pleas to the Creator and the Father's promises to the creature. This is the conceptual template Christianity will later use for the divine Word made flesh.

> And to his word, his chief messenger, highest in age and honour, the Father of all has given the special prerogative, to stand on the confines of both, and separate that which had been created from the Creator. And this same word is continually a suppliant to the immortal God on behalf of the mortal race, which is exposed to affliction and misery; and is also the ambassador, sent by the Ruler of all, to the subject race. And the word rejoices in the gift, and, exulting in it, announces it and boasts of it, saying, "And I stood in the midst, between the Lord and you"; neither being uncreate as God, nor yet created as you, but being in the midst between these two extremities, like a hostage, as it were, to both parties...

Citation: *Quis Rerum Divinarum Heres Sit* 205–206, trans. Yonge 1854; cf. Colson, Loeb LCL 261 (1932), pp. 384–385.

### *De Vita Mosis* II.37–40 — the divine inspiration of the Septuagint translators

Philo's defense of the LXX as itself divinely inspired — the conceptual grounding for the Greek text's authority among Hellenistic Jews and the early Christian church.

> ... they all became as it were possessed, and, under inspiration, wrote, not each scribe something different, but the same word for word, as though dictated to each by an invisible prompter... For who is there who does not know that every language, and the Greek language above all others, is rich in a variety of words, and that the same thought can be put in many shapes... ? But this was not the case, as it appears, with this translation of ours; but the very same Greek words were employed by them all, the very same nouns and verbs, as if some unseen prompter had whispered the words into the ear of each.

Citation: *De Vita Mosis* II.37–38, trans. Yonge 1854.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Roman Egypt under the Julio-Claudians. Philo came from one of Alexandria's wealthiest Jewish families (his nephew Tiberius Julius Alexander became Roman prefect of Egypt and prefect of the praetorian guard; his brother Alexander the Alabarch was perhaps the richest Jew in the empire). In +38 the Alexandrian Greeks rioted against the Jewish community; in +40 Philo led a delegation to the emperor Gaius Caligula in Rome — described in *Legatio ad Gaium*.
- **Religious milieu.** Hellenistic Judaism at its full philosophical maturity. Philo writes for *both* Jewish and educated pagan readers; he treats Moses and Plato as concordant, the Torah as containing all philosophy, the LXX as itself inspired (*Life of Moses* 2.25–44).
- **Who was behind it.** Philo himself, a Jewish patrician trained in the full Greek paideia (rhetoric, geometry, philosophy, music) and equally at home in the Alexandrian synagogue. His commitment to Torah practice and to philosophical interpretation is exemplary of the Hellenistic Jewish synthesis.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Philo's works survived because *Christians* preserved them. Eusebius treated Philo as a near-Christian (*Ecclesiastical History* 2.4–18); the Cappadocian Fathers, Ambrose, and Origen relied on him heavily; Byzantine scriptoria continued copying him through the medieval period. Jewish tradition forgot him for over a millennium and rediscovered him in the 16th century.

## Themes and motifs
- [[logos-cosmic-reason]] — Philo's **Logos** is the single most consequential concept in this corpus. It is at once: (a) the Stoic cosmic *logos* (immanent reason); (b) the Platonic world of Forms (the intelligible cosmos); (c) the biblical *Word* of God; (d) God's "first-born son" and image; (e) the high priest who mediates between God and creation. *De Opificio* 16–25 lays out this Logos as the architect's blueprint. **This is the single most important conceptual hinge in the vault for understanding Christian theology of the divine Word.**
- [[allegorical-exegesis]] — Philo systematizes allegorical reading: every biblical narrative has a *literal* meaning (which he generally accepts as historical) and a *higher* / *philosophical* meaning. This becomes the template for Christian exegesis (Origen, Ambrose, Augustine).
- [[wisdom-personified]] — Sophia and Logos are partly merged in Philo; in some passages Sophia is the "mother" of the cosmos and Logos is her "son" by God the "father" (*De Fuga* 109).
- [[ascent-of-the-soul]] — the philosophical-mystical journey: the soul's *epistrophē* (turning) from passions to virtue and ultimately to vision of God. Echoes Plato's *Symposium*-ladder.
- [[demiurge]] — Philo identifies God-the-creator with the Platonic demiurge but elevates the demiurge to the High God (correcting Plato's separation of First Cause and demiurge). Philo's God *is* the demiurge but works through the Logos.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-3-002-plato-dialogues]] — *direct-quote* / *commentary-on*: Philo cites Plato by name; *Opif.* is essentially Genesis-1 read through *Timaeus*; *Leg. All.* deploys the *Phaedrus* charioteer.
- → [[phase-3-005-stoic-foundational-texts]] — *direct-influence*: the Logos doctrine fuses Stoic immanent logos with Platonic transcendent Forms. **This is the philosophical engine of the Stoic→Philo→John pipeline.**
- → [[phase-3-012-wisdom-of-solomon]] — *shared-milieu* / *direct-influence*: Philo's Sophia/Logos pair extends and philosophically formalizes Wisdom of Solomon's personification.
- → [[phase-3-006-septuagint]] — *direct-quote*: Philo's biblical text is the LXX; he holds it to be divinely inspired and works exclusively from its Greek wording.
- → [[phase-3-020-gospel-of-john]] — *direct-influence* (debated mechanism): John 1's Logos prologue presupposes Philonic-Hellenistic-Jewish logos theology. Whether John knew Philo's writings or both drew on shared synagogue discourse is contested.
- → Patristic theology (Clement of Alexandria, Origen, Eusebius, Ambrose — Phase 4) — *direct-influence*: Christian allegorical exegesis is Philonic in structure.
- → Gnostic cosmologies (Sethian, Valentinian — Phase 4) — *direct-influence* (with inversion): the demiurge / Logos / Sophia triad is a Philonic schema. Gnostic systems split the high God from a fallen demiurge, often associating Sophia with a tragic fall — an inversion of Philo's harmony.
- → [[phase-3-021-hermetic-corpus-earliest]] — *parallel-motif*: Hermetic Nous-Logos cosmologies draw on the same Middle Platonist + Egyptian Jewish substrate.

## Disputes
- **Knowledge of rabbinic / Palestinian Jewish tradition.** Older view (Wolfson) held Philo entirely within Hellenistic Judaism; recent work (Niehoff, Birnbaum) finds more contact with proto-rabbinic traditions than previously thought.
- **Direct influence on John.** Hengel and Borgen argue substantial; Dodd, Boyarin argue shared diaspora-synagogue discourse rather than direct literary knowledge. The verbal parallels are strong but not verbatim.
- **Status as philosopher vs. theologian.** Whether Philo is a creative philosophical mind in his own right (Runia, Calabi) or a derivative synthesizer (older view) is a live scholarly question.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **The Logos transmission chain — the vault's most consequential four-step pipeline:** Heraclitus (*logos* as universal reason, ~-500) → Stoics (logos as immanent world-principle holding the cosmos together) → Philo (Logos as God's "first-born Son," mediator, and blueprint of creation — *De Opificio* 16–25) → John's prologue (Logos made flesh, John 1:1–14) → Nicene theology (Logos as second Person of the Trinity). Philo is the pivotal joint where Greek philosophy and Jewish scriptural reading fuse; without this joint, Christian theology of the divine Word has no conceptual framework. This is the most important single transmission node in the vault for understanding how Hellenistic Greek philosophy became Christian doctrine.
- **Allegorical hermeneutic → Origen → medieval four senses of Scripture:** Philo systematizes the move from literal to philosophical meaning in biblical narrative; Origen of Alexandria explicitly credits and extends Philo's method; Jerome's *De Doctrina Christiana* codifies four senses (literal / allegorical / tropological / anagogical); the entire structure of medieval Christian biblical interpretation descends from Philo's technique. This is a fully documented, T1-sourced transmission chain from Alexandrian Judaism into medieval Catholicism (Runia 1993 is the definitive monograph).
- **The survival paradox — a Jewish thinker preserved only by Christians:** Philo is preserved *exclusively* because Christian copyists saved him — Eusebius, Origen, Ambrose, and the Byzantine tradition. Jewish tradition forgot Philo completely for ~1,500 years and rediscovered him in the 16th century. A Jewish philosopher's work surviving only through the tradition it catalyzed is almost unique in intellectual history; it is also direct evidence that early Christianity's philosophical vocabulary was Jewish-Alexandrian in origin, not primarily Greek-pagan.
- **"God is beyond being" (*De Post. Caini* 169) — the Jewish apophatic source node:** Philo writes that God is superior to virtue, knowledge, and even the Platonic Good itself — God is beyond all predication. This pre-dates Plotinus' "the One is beyond being" (Enn. VI.9.4) by ~200 years and is directly channeled through Origen into Pseudo-Dionysius and Eckhart. The Western apophatic tradition does not begin with Plotinus or Pseudo-Dionysius — it begins with a 1st-century Alexandrian Jew reading the Torah through Middle Platonist philosophy.
