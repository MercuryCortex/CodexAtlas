# Samaritan Pentateuch

**The Samaritan Pentateuch** — the Torah as received and transmitted by the Samaritan community, written in the archaic Samaritan Hebrew script (descended from the paleo-Hebrew alphabet used before the Babylonian exile, distinct from the square Aramaic script adopted by Jews post-exile) — is one of three independent textual witnesses to the Hebrew Bible alongside the **Masoretic Text** (MT, the basis of modern Jewish and Protestant Bibles) and the **Septuagint** (LXX, the Greek translation). The Samaritan text differs from the MT in approximately 6,000 places; about 1,900 of these divergences align the SP with the LXX against the MT, indicating they share a common pre-Masoretic Hebrew Vorlage.

## Primary text — selected passages

### Deuteronomy 27:4 — The Gerizim commandment (Samaritan Pentateuch vs. Masoretic Text)
*SP text: "And when you have crossed the Jordan, you shall set up these stones, about which I command you today, **on Mount Gerizim**, and you shall plaster them with plaster." Masoretic Text: "on Mount Ebal."*

*The single most theologically loaded variant between the Samaritan Pentateuch and the Masoretic Text: where the MT has Moses commanding the Israelites to build the altar on Mount Ebal, the SP has Mount Gerizim — the Samaritan community's sacred mountain. This is either the most consequential intentional Samaritan sectarian alteration of the Torah text, or — as the Samaritans argue — the Jews who altered an original Gerizim commandment to Ebal after the schism. The Qumran evidence confirms that the SP preserves genuine pre-Masoretic Hebrew readings in many places, making the Ebal/Gerizim variant the thorniest case in Hebrew Bible textual criticism: we cannot determine which reading is older.*

*Citations: Anderson, R.T. and Giles, T. (2012). The Samaritan Pentateuch. SBL. pp.88–107; Crown, A.D. (ed.) (1993). A Companion to Samaritan Studies. Mohr Siebeck.*

### Exodus 20 (SP) — The Tenth Commandment: Mount Gerizim command added to the Decalogue
*SP text adds after the Decalogue: "And when you cross the Jordan, you shall erect an altar to the LORD your God on Mount Gerizim... for there you shall offer burnt offerings to the LORD your God" [incorporating Deut 27:4–7 and 11:30].*

*The Samaritan Pentateuch adds a commandment at the end of the Decalogue mandating worship at Mount Gerizim. From the Jewish and Christian perspective, this is a sectarian interpolation elevating Gerizim into the commandments themselves. From the Samaritan perspective, the authentic Mosaic command was always there, and the MT's omission of it is the Jewish alteration. The dispute makes the Decalogue itself a site of contested textual authority between two communities who each claim to possess the original Torah.*

*Citations: Anderson, R.T. and Giles, T. (2012). The Samaritan Pentateuch. SBL. pp.107–134; Purvis, J.D. (1968). The Samaritan Pentateuch and the New Testament. Harvard UP.*

### Genesis 5 / 11 — Antediluvian genealogy variants (SP / MT / LXX compared)
*Three-way divergence: Methuselah's lifespan: MT = 969 years; SP = 720 years; LXX = 969. Adam's age at Seth's birth: MT = 130; SP = 130; LXX = 230. The numbers are systematically different across all three traditions.*

*The three-way divergence between MT, SP, and LXX in the biblical genealogies demonstrates that the three textual traditions were not copies of each other but independent representatives of a larger family of Hebrew texts. The genealogies were apparently altered systematically in each tradition for internal-consistency reasons (the LXX numbers were modified to prevent Methuselah from surviving the Flood). This three-way textual divergence at the most arithmetically sensitive passages in the Pentateuch is the clearest proof that multiple independent Hebrew Bibles existed in Second Temple Judaism before the Jamnia standardization.*

*Citations: Anderson, R.T. and Giles, T. (2012). pp.60–87; Tov, E. (2012). Textual Criticism of the Hebrew Bible, 3rd ed. Fortress. pp.80–100.*

## Community and text

The Samaritan community — centered since antiquity on Mount Gerizim near Nablus in the West Bank — traces its lineage to the Israelites of the northern kingdom who were not deported by Assyria in 722 BCE, combined with settlers brought by the Assyrians. They accept only the Pentateuch (Genesis–Deuteronomy) as canonical scripture; they reject the Prophets, Writings, and all post-Mosaic scripture recognized by Rabbinic Judaism. Mount Gerizim (not Jerusalem/Zion) is their sacred mountain — the place designated in the commandments for the central altar, per their version of Deuteronomy. The community numbers approximately 800 today (split between Kiryat Luza on Mount Gerizim and Holon, Israel).

## Textual significance — three witnesses to the Hebrew Bible

The discovery of the Dead Sea Scrolls (1947 onward) revolutionized understanding of the Samaritan Pentateuch. Before Qumran, mainstream scholarship assumed the SP was a sectarian Samaritan *revision* of the proto-Masoretic text. The Qumran finds produced pre-Sectarian Hebrew manuscripts (4QExod-Lev^f, 4QNum^b, 4QSam^a) that align with the SP reading tradition against the MT — proving that the SP preserves an authentic pre-Masoretic textual tradition that was in circulation in Second Temple Judaism, not a sectarian invention. The SP joins the LXX as an independent witness to a *different* Hebrew Bible than the Masoretic — the one standardized by rabbinic scholars at Jamnia (~90 CE).

**Key doctrinal variant**: Deuteronomy 27 in the Samaritan Pentateuch inserts a commandment to build the altar on Mount Gerizim (not Ebal as in MT). This is the Samaritan community's most significant textual intervention — or, per Samaritan tradition, the MT is the Jewish sectarian alteration.

## Cross-tradition significance: the "Which Hebrew Bible?" problem

The Samaritan Pentateuch is the vault's most targeted case study for the problem of **canonical fixation** — the historical moment when a fluid, multiply-attested scriptural tradition gets hardened into a single authoritative text. The rabbinic standardization of the Masoretic text (~1st–2nd c. CE) was not the recovery of an original text but the selection of *one* tradition from a family of competing Hebrew textual traditions (MT, LXX Vorlage, SP, Qumran non-aligned texts). The Samaritan community preserved a different member of that family. Which is "the Bible"? The answer is: neither is prior. Both are ancient. This is the same question that the Septuagint raises from the Greek side — and the two non-Masoretic traditions frequently agree against the MT, pointing to a common pre-Masoretic ancestor.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges

- **The Samaritan Pentateuch + LXX + Masoretic Text = discovery that there was no single "original" Hebrew Bible, only a family of competing textual traditions — the most consequential result of 20th-century biblical textual scholarship**: Before the Dead Sea Scrolls (1947 onward), the Samaritan Pentateuch was widely dismissed as a sectarian revision of the proto-Masoretic text. The Qumran discoveries overturned this: manuscripts like 4QExod-Lev^f and 4QNum^b align with the SP reading tradition against the MT, proving that the SP preserves genuine pre-Masoretic Hebrew readings that circulated in Second Temple Judaism. Combined with the evidence that the LXX translated from Hebrew manuscripts also diverging from the proto-MT, the three-witness picture demonstrates that the Hebrew Bible existed in multiple textual families before the rabbinic standardization at Jamnia (~90 CE). This is among the most consequential results of 20th-century biblical scholarship: the "original Bible" is a modern fiction; there were only multiple ancient Bibles (Tov, E. 2012. *Textual Criticism of the Hebrew Bible*, 3rd ed. Fortress. pp.80–100; Cross, F.M. and Talmon, S. eds. 1975. *Qumran and the History of the Biblical Text*. Harvard UP. pp.1–30; Anderson, R.T. and Giles, T. 2012. *The Samaritan Pentateuch*. SBL. pp.1–30).

- **The Ebal/Gerizim variant in Deuteronomy 27:4 = single most consequential textual divergence in the history of monotheism, generating a competing Israelite community maintaining unbroken continuity from antiquity to the present**: The Samaritan community — currently ~800 people split between Mount Gerizim and Holon, Israel — is the only surviving community outside Judaism and Christianity maintaining continuous practice of the biblical covenant from the Second Temple period to the present day. Their Passover sacrifice on Mount Gerizim is the only surviving sacrificial Passover rite in the world — more ancient in its liturgical form than any Jewish Passover practice, since Jewish Passover was restructured after 70 CE to function without a temple. The Samaritans are thus the most consequential living witness to Second Temple religious practice (Crown, A.D. ed. 1993. *A Companion to Samaritan Studies*. Mohr Siebeck. pp.1–30; Purvis, J.D. 1968. *The Samaritan Pentateuch and the New Testament*. Harvard UP. pp.1–30; Anderson, R.T. and Giles, T. 2012. pp.1–30).

- **The John 4 "Samaritan Woman at the Well" narrative = most consequential NT engagement with the Samaritan/Jewish theological dispute, establishing "true worship" as a category transcending both Mount Gerizim and Jerusalem**: The encounter in John 4:19–24 — "Our ancestors worshiped on this mountain, but you say that the place where people must worship is in Jerusalem... God is spirit, and those who worship him must worship in spirit and truth" — is the NT's direct engagement with the Gerizim/Zion dispute at the heart of the Samaritan Pentateuch. Jesus's answer — "neither on this mountain nor in Jerusalem" — makes the entire competition between the two Torah texts irrelevant in the new worship "in spirit and truth." This passage is the foundational text of Christian anti-localization theology of worship, and it presupposes the Samaritan Pentateuch's Gerizim theology as its foil (Purvis, J.D. 1968. *The Samaritan Pentateuch and the New Testament*. Harvard UP. pp.68–100; Brown, R.E. 1966. *The Gospel According to John I–XII*. Anchor Bible 29. Doubleday. pp.169–185; Coggins, R.J. 1975. *Samaritans and Jews*. Oxford UP. pp.150–170).

- **The Samaritan paleo-Hebrew script = most historically continuous alphabetic writing system in living religious use, maintaining unbroken pre-exilic Hebrew letter forms while Jews adopted Aramaic square script after the Babylonian exile**: The Samaritans write their Torah in Samaritan script — a direct descendant of the paleo-Hebrew script used in the first Temple period, unchanged in its basic letter forms while Jewish scribes adopted the Aramaic square script (the basis of modern Hebrew) after the Babylonian exile (-586 BCE). The Samaritan Pentateuch is thus written in a letter-form system that is the most ancient alphabetic writing tradition in continuous liturgical use — a 2,500-year scribal continuity with the Bronze Age Phoenician-Hebrew alphabet. This makes the Samaritan Pentateuch simultaneously the most conservative and the most ancient continuously-read scripture text in any living religious community (Crown, A.D. ed. 1993. *A Companion to Samaritan Studies*. Mohr Siebeck. pp.31–60; Tov, E. 2012. pp.36–40; Purvis, J.D. 1968. pp.1–30).
