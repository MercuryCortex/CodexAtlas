# Book of Daniel

## What it is
A twelve-chapter book in two halves:
- **Chapters 1–6** — court tales: Daniel and his three companions (Hananiah, Mishael, Azariah / Shadrach, Meshach, Abednego) navigate Babylonian and Persian royal courts. Famous scenes: the fiery furnace (3), Nebuchadnezzar's madness (4), Belshazzar's feast and the writing on the wall (5), Daniel in the lions' den (6). These tales likely circulated as a Jewish diaspora wisdom-cycle from ~-300 onward.
- **Chapters 7–12** — apocalyptic visions: the four beasts and the Son of Man (7); the ram and the goat (8); the seventy weeks (9); the angelic explanation of the Greek wars and the desolating sacrilege (10–12). These were composed during the Antiochene crisis (-167/-164), pseudonymously presented as Daniel's 6th-c. visions but transparently encoding events of the early 2nd century.

The book is bilingual: 2:4b through 7:28 is in Aramaic; the rest (1:1–2:4a; 8–12) in Hebrew. This bilingual sandwich is one of the most studied textual puzzles in the Hebrew Bible.

## Primary text — selected passages

### Daniel 7:13–14 — The Son of Man before the Ancient of Days
*Trans. NRSV; Collins, J.J. (1993). Daniel. Hermeneia, Fortress Press.*
> As I watched in the night visions,
> I saw one like a human being [*bar ʾenāsh*]
>   coming with the clouds of heaven.
> And he came to the Ancient One
>   and was presented before him.
> To him was given dominion and glory and kingship,
>   that all peoples, nations, and languages should serve him.
> His dominion is an everlasting dominion
>   that shall not pass away,
> and his kingship is one
>   that shall never be destroyed.

*The Aramaic *bar ʾenāsh* ("son of man" / "one like a human being") is in deliberate contrast to the four beast-empires that precede it in the vision. This 48-word passage is the primary source-text for Jesus's "Son of Man" self-designation across all four Gospels, used approximately 80 times — more than any other Danielic phrase in the NT tradition.*

### Daniel 12:1–3 — Michael, the resurrection, and the maskilim
*Trans. NRSV*
> At that time Michael, the great prince, the protector of your people, shall arise. There shall be a time of anguish, such as has never occurred since nations first came into existence. But at that time your people shall be delivered, everyone who is found written in the book. Many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt. Those who are wise shall shine like the brightness of the sky, and those who lead many to righteousness, like the stars forever and ever.

*Dan 12:2 is the first unambiguous individual bodily-resurrection text in the Hebrew Bible. The earlier valley-of-dry-bones vision (Ezek 37) is corporate-national metaphor; Daniel 12 describes individual awakening to eternal life or condemnation. This single verse is the direct ancestor of Pharisaic resurrection theology and of Paul's in 1 Cor 15.*

### Daniel 2:31–35 — Nebuchadnezzar's dream: the four-kingdom statue
*Trans. NRSV*
> "You were looking, O king, and lo! there was a great statue. This statue was huge, its brilliance extraordinary; it stood before you, and its appearance was frightening. The head of that statue was of fine gold, its chest and arms of silver, its middle and thighs of bronze, its legs of iron, its feet partly of iron and partly of clay. As you looked on, a stone was cut out, not by human hands, and it struck the statue on its feet of iron and clay and broke them in pieces."

*Daniel 2 (gold-silver-bronze-iron) and Daniel 7 (lion-bear-leopard-ten-horned beast) are the twin foundations of the "four-kingdom schema" that governed all Western and Byzantine universal history from antiquity through the Renaissance and shaped Hegel's philosophy of history.*

*Citations: NRSV; Collins 1993, Hermeneia, Fortress.*

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Antiochus IV Epiphanes, Seleucid king (-175/-164), attempted forced Hellenization of Jerusalem culminating in the **"desolating sacrilege"** of -167 (installation of an altar to Zeus Olympios in the Temple, sacrifice of swine on the altar; described in Dan 9:27, 11:31, 12:11; corroborated in 1 Maccabees and Josephus). The Maccabean revolt (Judah Maccabee, -167/-160) ejected the Seleucids; the Temple was rededicated -164 (Hanukkah).
- **Religious milieu.** Apocalyptic Judaism crystallizing. Daniel and 1 Enoch's Animal Apocalypse are contemporary responses to the same crisis. The book endorses *passive endurance* and divine deliverance over (apparently) the Maccabean military path: 11:34 disparages Maccabean help as "little help".
- **Who was behind it.** The *maskilim* ("wise") of Daniel 11:33–35; 12:3 — a circle that endured persecution and looked for vindication via resurrection and angelic deliverance. Possibly an antecedent to the Qumran community; possibly an apocalyptic-priestly stream parallel to it.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous Jewish canonical transmission (in the *Ketuvim* / Writings, not among the Prophets — a clue to its late date); Greek translation in two competing forms (OG and Theodotion, with the Church preferring Theodotion); confirmed at Qumran from ~-125.

## Themes and motifs
- [[apocalyptic-revelation]] — Daniel is one of the two foundational apocalypses (with 1 Enoch's parts); revelation by angelic interpreter, pseudonymous antiquity, symbolic visions.
- [[four-kingdoms-schema]] — Dan 2 (statue of metals) and Dan 7 (four beasts) lay down the *four-empire* template (Babylon → Media → Persia → Greece; later Christian readings substituted Rome for Greece). This periodization governs medieval and early modern historiography.
- [[son-of-man]] — Dan 7:13–14: "one like a son of man" comes on the clouds of heaven, receives kingship from the Ancient of Days. **This is the Christological *locus classicus*** behind the Gospel "Son of Man" sayings.
- [[resurrection-of-the-dead]] — Dan 12:2: "many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth will awake, some to everlasting life, others to shame and everlasting contempt." This is the **first unambiguous resurrection text in the Hebrew Bible**.
- [[dualism-cosmic]] — Michael as Israel's angelic prince fighting the princes of Persia and Greece (Dan 10) introduces a cosmic dimension of conflict.

- → [[phase-3-016-gospel-of-mark]] — *direct-quote*: Mark 13:14 ("the desolating sacrilege") and Mark 13:26 (the Son of Man coming on the clouds) explicitly invoke Daniel 7 and 9:27 / 11:31.
- → New Testament book of Revelation — *direct-influence* / *commentary-on*: structurally modeled on Daniel; the beast imagery, the kingdoms, the resurrection-judgment all derive.
- → Zoroastrian apocalyptic milieu — *shared-milieu*: world-ages schemata, final battle of light and darkness, resurrection, are paralleled in Avestan and Pahlavi sources; the direction and degree of influence is contested (Hultgård, Boyce).

## Disputes
- **Date.** A near-consensus since Porphyry (3rd c. CE, *Against the Christians*) places the visions chs. 7–12 at -167/-164 because the "prophecies" track Seleucid history with extreme precision down to Antiochus IV and then become inaccurate (Antiochus's death is mis-described in 11:40–45). Traditional / conservative Christian readings retain a 6th-c. Daniel; mainstream critical scholarship since Driver (1900) is unanimous on the Maccabean dating.
- **Bilingual puzzle.** Why is the book half-Hebrew and half-Aramaic? Theories include (a) two separate sources joined; (b) deliberate code-switching (Aramaic for the "international" tales, Hebrew for the Israel-centric visions); (c) an Aramaic original partially Hebraized. No consensus.
- **Influence of Persian eschatology.** Hultgård, Collins argue substantial; others (Barr) more cautious.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges

- **Daniel 7:13 *bar ʾenāsh* — the single most-used Hebrew Bible passage in Jesus's self-presentation, the root of the entire "Son of Man" Christological problem**: The Gospels record Jesus referring to himself as "the Son of Man" approximately 80 times across all four Gospel traditions — the most frequent self-designation in the Gospel record, and the only one without parallel in early Christian preaching about Jesus. Every one of these uses invokes Daniel 7:13 as its scriptural background (whether as allusion or explicit quotation, e.g., Mark 13:26; 14:62). Daniel 7:13 is therefore the single most-used Hebrew Bible text in Jesus's own mode of self-reference, and the foundation of the Christological "Son of Man problem" that has occupied NT scholarship since Weiss (1892). The origin point: a Maccabean-era vision of a human-figured being receiving universal dominion from God — composed to console Jewish resisters in -167 BCE — became the primary scriptural lens through which the first Christians understood the identity and destiny of Jesus (Collins 1993, Hermeneia pp.304–310; Hurtado 2003, *Lord Jesus Christ*, Eerdmans pp.290–306; Allison 2010, *Constructing Jesus*, Baker Academic pp.293–320).

- **Daniel 12:2 — the first unambiguous individual bodily-resurrection text in the Hebrew Bible, ancestral to Pharisaic Judaism, Jesus, and Paul**: Ezekiel 37 (valley of dry bones) is a corporate-restoration metaphor; Isaiah 26:19 may be individual resurrection but is contested. Daniel 12:2 is the text all scholars agree describes individual bodily resurrection to eternal life and eternal condemnation. This verse is the direct textual ancestor of Pharisaic resurrection theology (Pharisees affirm resurrection; Sadducees deny it — because there are no pre-Danielic unambiguous resurrection texts), of Jesus's resurrection debates with the Sadducees (Mark 12:18–27), and of Paul's resurrection theology (1 Cor 15 — "if Christ is not raised, your faith is futile"). The Sadducee-Pharisee-Jesus-Paul fork on the afterlife is traceable to a single verse written during the Antiochene crisis of -167 BCE (Collins 1993 pp.392–398; Wright, N.T. 2003, *The Resurrection of the Son of God*, Fortress pp.109–115; Nickelsburg 2006, *Resurrection, Immortality, and Eternal Life*, Harvard UP pp.23–27).

- **Daniel's four-kingdom schema — the template governing all of Western historiography from antiquity through Hegel**: Daniel 2 (gold-silver-bronze-iron statue) and Daniel 7 (lion-bear-leopard-ten-horned beast) introduce history as a divinely-scheduled succession of world-empires culminating in divine judgment and a final eternal kingdom. Christian interpreters identified the four kingdoms as Babylon, Persia, Greece, Rome — making Daniel the framework for all medieval Christian universal history. Augustine's *City of Two Cities* elaborates a Danielic framework; Orosius's *Seven Books of History Against the Pagans* (~418 CE) is structured around it; it pervades medieval chronicles and maps. Hegel's philosophy of history — progressive world-spirit unfolding through epochs (Oriental, Greek, Roman, Germanic) culminating in rational freedom — is the Danielic schema secularized: history as a purposive sequence toward an absolute end. No other Maccabean-era pseudepigraph has so thoroughly shaped Western historical consciousness (Collins 1993; Swain 1940, "The Theory of the Four Monarchies," *Classical Philology* 35/1; Hartman & Di Lella 1978, AYB 23 pp.165–167).

- **Daniel at Qumran and in the NT — the connective tissue linking Second Temple apocalypticism to early Christianity**: Eight Daniel manuscripts at Qumran make it one of the most-represented biblical books in the sectarian library (alongside Isaiah and Psalms). The War Scroll (*1QM*) is structurally Danielic in its angels-vs-armies eschatology. The NT's apocalyptic chapters explicitly quote Daniel: Mark 13:14 ("the desolating sacrilege" = Dan 9:27 / 11:31), Mark 13:26 (Son of Man on clouds = Dan 7:13), and the entire book of Revelation is structurally modeled on Daniel (beasts, kingdoms, resurrection-judgment). Daniel is the genetic bridge proving institutional continuity between the Qumran sectarian movement and proto-Christianity — both are Danielic communities expecting imminent cosmic deliverance, organized around an elect remnant, convinced of angelic combat in heaven mirroring earthly events. Without Daniel, neither Qumran nor early Christianity looks the same (VanderKam & Flint 2002, *The Meaning of the Dead Sea Scrolls*, HarperCollins pp.165–168; Collins 2016, *The Apocalyptic Imagination*, Eerdmans pp.119–125; Flusser 1988, *Judaism and the Origins of Christianity*, Magnes pp.265–280).
