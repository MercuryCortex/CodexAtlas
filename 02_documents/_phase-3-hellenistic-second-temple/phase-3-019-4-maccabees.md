# 4 Maccabees

## What it is
A philosophical-rhetorical treatise framed as a synagogue homily, arguing that "pious reason is sovereign over the passions" (4 Macc 1:1; 1:7). The author illustrates the thesis with the **Maccabean martyrs** — the priest Eleazar (chs. 5–7), the seven brothers (chs. 8–12), and their mother (chs. 14–17) — who were tortured to death by Antiochus IV Epiphanes for refusing to eat pork. Their endurance proves that *logismos* (reasoned piety) can master the passions of pleasure, pain, and grief. The work fuses **Stoic and Platonic ethics** (passions, virtues, soul-immortality) with **Jewish covenantal piety**, treating Torah-observance as the perfection of philosophy.

Critically, 4 Maccabees develops the martyrs' deaths as **vicarious atonement** for Israel:
- 4 Macc 6:29 — Eleazar's prayer: "Be merciful to your people, and let our punishment suffice for them. Make my blood their purification, and take my life as a substitute (*antipsychon*) for theirs."
- 4 Macc 17:21–22 — "through the blood of those pious ones and their death as an atoning sacrifice (*hilastērion*), divine providence preserved Israel."

This is the most explicit pre-Christian Jewish formulation of substitutionary atonement and is a load-bearing text for understanding NT atonement language.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Roman Empire, early imperial period — most likely under the Julio-Claudians or Flavians. The Antiochene-persecution narrative is two centuries old by the time of composition; the author is using historical exempla.
- **Religious milieu.** Diaspora Hellenistic Judaism in cosmopolitan urban settings (Antioch, Alexandria). The audience is presumed to be Greek-educated and capable of following Stoic-Platonist philosophical argument. Torah-loyal but expressing that loyalty in *philosophical* register.
- **Who was behind it.** Anonymous Hellenistic Jewish author of considerable rhetorical and philosophical skill. The work was sometimes attributed to Josephus in antiquity (e.g., Eusebius *HE* 3.10.6), an attribution now universally rejected.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Preserved in Greek through Christian transmission: the LXX manuscript tradition kept it as an appendix; Eastern Orthodox traditions accord it canonical status (some lists place it in an appendix to the Greek OT); the Latin West did not preserve it. The Maccabean martyrs themselves became *Christian* saints — their bodies were "translated" to Antioch and then to a church in Rome (the *Basilica di San Pietro in Vincoli*) — a remarkable case of Jewish martyrs venerated as Christian saints.

## Themes and motifs
- [[reason-over-passion]] — the entire treatise is structured as a Stoic-style demonstration that *logismos* masters the *pathē*. Vocabulary is technical Stoic (*pathos*, *apatheia*, *eupatheia*).
- [[martyrdom-theology]] — the martyrs die willingly, articulately, with rational composure rather than emotional collapse. This is the **template** for Christian martyr-acts (Polycarp, Perpetua, etc.).
- [[vicarious-atonement]] — Eleazar's *antipsychon* (life-for-life substitute) and the *hilastērion* (atoning sacrifice) language directly parallel NT atonement vocabulary (Rom 3:25 uses *hilastērion* of Christ; Mk 10:45 uses *lytron anti* "ransom for many" with structural parallel).
- [[stoic-virtue]] — the four cardinal virtues (*phronēsis*, *dikaiosynē*, *andreia*, *sōphrosynē*) are repeatedly invoked as the structure of moral life.
- [[soul-immortality]] — the martyrs are explicitly said to enter immortality at death (4 Macc 9:8; 17:18). The doctrine is Platonic-formed.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-3-005-stoic-foundational-texts]] — *direct-influence*: 4 Macc deploys Stoic ethical-philosophical vocabulary thoroughly. The author has likely read or at least absorbed Stoic discourse on the passions.
- → [[phase-3-013-philo-of-alexandria]] — *parallel-motif*: both fuse Hellenistic philosophy with Jewish Torah-loyalty in Greek prose; both treat Torah-observance as the perfection of philosophical virtue.
- → [[phase-3-012-wisdom-of-solomon]] — *parallel-motif*: shared Hellenistic Jewish discourse on righteous-suffering vindication.
- → 2 Maccabees 6–7 — *direct-influence* / *commentary-on*: 4 Maccabees expands and philosophizes the martyr-tales already present in 2 Maccabees.
- → [[phase-3-015-pauline-epistles]] / NT atonement theology — *parallel-motif* / *direct-influence* (debated): the *hilastērion* of Rom 3:25 and the substitutionary-atonement language across the NT have their closest Hellenistic-Jewish precedent in 4 Macc 17. Whether Paul knew 4 Maccabees directly is contested; whether they share a common discourse is certain (van Henten, Williams).
- → Christian martyr-acts (Phase 4 onward) — *direct-influence*: the rational, articulate, defiant Christian martyr (Polycarp, Perpetua) is patterned on the Maccabean martyrs of 2 and 4 Maccabees.

## Disputes
- **Date.** Range from Augustan (~+20) to early Hadrianic (~+135). Most place ~+40/+90. Linguistic and philosophical signals (Stoic vocabulary, Asian-rhetorical style) suggest first century.
- **Place.** Antioch (Hadas, Anderson, deSilva) vs. Alexandria. Antioch is favored because of the local cult of the Maccabean martyrs.
- **Genre.** Synagogue homily, philosophical diatribe, or epideictic encomium of the martyrs?
- **Relationship to Christian atonement.** Whether Paul knew 4 Macc directly (Williams), or both draw on a shared "noble death" topos (van Henten), is debated.
