# Undisputed Pauline Epistles

## What it is
Seven letters written by Paul of Tarsus to early Christian communities in Asia Minor, Macedonia, and Achaia between ~+50 and ~+58, comprising the oldest surviving Christian literature. In likely chronological order:
- **1 Thessalonians** (~+50/+51) — earliest extant Pauline letter; eschatological imminence and the parousia.
- **Galatians** (~+50/+55) — vehement defense of Gentile freedom from Torah-observance against "Judaizing" missionaries; autobiographical narrative of Paul's conversion and his disputes with Peter at Antioch.
- **1 Corinthians** (~+54) — pastoral problem-solving (factionalism, sexuality, lawsuits, communal meals, charismatic gifts, women in worship, resurrection); contains the earliest extant pre-Pauline kerygmatic creed (1 Cor 15:3–5).
- **2 Corinthians** (~+55) — defense of Paul's apostleship; possibly composite of 2–4 letter-fragments.
- **Philippians** (~+55/+62) — composed from prison (Ephesus or Rome); contains the great "Christ hymn" (Phil 2:6–11).
- **Philemon** (~+55/+62) — short personal note about the slave Onesimus.
- **Romans** (~+57/+58) — Paul's mature theological summa, written ahead of his planned visit to a community he did not found.

The other six letters in the NT bearing Paul's name (Ephesians, Colossians, 2 Thessalonians; the three Pastoral Epistles: 1–2 Timothy, Titus) are widely held to be deutero-Pauline — pseudonymous compositions by later admirers. **We split them into a separate node ([deutero-pauline-epistles], Phase 4) to make the distinction visible in the graph.** The split follows mainstream critical consensus (Schnelle, Ehrman, Wright); the disputes are documented in the deutero-Pauline node.

## Primary text — selected excerpts

Excerpts use the Authorized (King James) Version of 1611, public domain and historically the most influential English New Testament. The KJV is now superseded for scholarly use by translations from the NA28/UBS5 critical Greek text (NRSV, ESV, NABRE, NIV); for serious study consult those alongside the Nestle-Aland Greek.

### 1 Corinthians 15:3–8 — the pre-Pauline kerygma

Paul says he "received" and "delivered" this formula — the technical language for traditional material. Scholars regard 15:3–5 as the oldest piece of Christian liturgical/creedal language we possess, likely formulated within years of the crucifixion, perhaps in Antioch or even Jerusalem, and pre-Pauline.

> For I delivered unto you first of all that which I also received, how that Christ died for our sins according to the scriptures; and that he was buried, and that he rose again the third day according to the scriptures: and that he was seen of Cephas, then of the twelve: after that, he was seen of above five hundred brethren at once; of whom the greater part remain unto this present, but some are fallen asleep. After that, he was seen of James; then of all the apostles. And last of all he was seen of me also, as of one born out of due time.

Citation: 1 Corinthians 15:3–8, KJV (1611); cf. NA28 Greek.

### Galatians 2:11–21 — the Antioch incident and justification by faith

Paul's first-person account of his confrontation with Peter at Antioch over table-fellowship, then his sharpest statement of the core doctrine: a person is not justified by works of the Torah but through faith in Christ.

> But when Peter was come to Antioch, I withstood him to the face, because he was to be blamed. For before that certain came from James, he did eat with the Gentiles: but when they were come, he withdrew and separated himself, fearing them which were of the circumcision...
>
> We who are Jews by nature, and not sinners of the Gentiles, knowing that a man is not justified by the works of the law, but by the faith of Jesus Christ, even we have believed in Jesus Christ, that we might be justified by the faith of Christ, and not by the works of the law: for by the works of the law shall no flesh be justified...
>
> I am crucified with Christ: nevertheless I live; yet not I, but Christ liveth in me: and the life which I now live in the flesh I live by the faith of the Son of God, who loved me, and gave himself for me.

Citation: Galatians 2:11–12, 15–16, 20, KJV.

### Philippians 2:5–11 — the Christ-hymn

Almost universally regarded as a pre-Pauline hymn that Paul quotes — one of the earliest Christological pieces extant, plausibly composed within twenty years of the crucifixion. The "kenotic" structure (emptying, descent, exaltation) became the template for all later Incarnational theology.

> Let this mind be in you, which was also in Christ Jesus: who, being in the form of God, thought it not robbery to be equal with God: but made himself of no reputation, and took upon him the form of a servant, and was made in the likeness of men: and being found in fashion as a man, he humbled himself, and became obedient unto death, even the death of the cross. Wherefore God also hath highly exalted him, and given him a name which is above every name: that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow, of things in heaven, and things in earth, and things under the earth; and that every tongue should confess that Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father.

Citation: Philippians 2:5–11, KJV. The line "every knee should bow" deliberately echoes Isaiah 45:23 LXX.

### Romans 7:14–25 — the divided self

The most studied piece of Pauline anthropology: the self split between knowing the good and doing the evil — a passage Augustine, Luther, and modern psychology have each claimed as their own.

> For we know that the law is spiritual: but I am carnal, sold under sin. For that which I do I allow not: for what I would, that do I not; but what I hate, that do I. If then I do that which I would not, I consent unto the law that it is good. Now then it is no more I that do it, but sin that dwelleth in me. For I know that in me (that is, in my flesh,) dwelleth no good thing: for to will is present with me; but how to perform that which is good I find not. For the good that I would I do not: but the evil which I would not, that I do. ... O wretched man that I am! who shall deliver me from the body of this death? I thank God through Jesus Christ our Lord. So then with the mind I myself serve the law of God; but with the flesh the law of sin.

Citation: Romans 7:14–20, 24–25, KJV.

### Romans 8:18–25 — the groaning creation

Paul's vision of the whole creation in birth-pangs, awaiting the revelation of the children of God. The passage was read by Valentinian Gnostics as cosmological allegory and by patristic theologians as eschatological hope; in modern theology it is the seedbed for ecological Christianities.

> For I reckon that the sufferings of this present time are not worthy to be compared with the glory which shall be revealed in us. For the earnest expectation of the creature waiteth for the manifestation of the sons of God. For the creature was made subject to vanity, not willingly, but by reason of him who hath subjected the same in hope, because the creature itself also shall be delivered from the bondage of corruption into the glorious liberty of the children of God. For we know that the whole creation groaneth and travaileth in pain together until now. And not only they, but ourselves also, which have the firstfruits of the Spirit, even we ourselves groan within ourselves, waiting for the adoption, to wit, the redemption of our body.

Citation: Romans 8:18–23, KJV.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Roman Empire under Claudius and Nero. Paul travels Roman roads, uses Roman citizenship for legal protection, addresses urban congregations in the Greek cities of the empire. His mission is shaped by Roman peace, infrastructure, and the Jewish synagogue diaspora that serves as his initial entry-point in each city.
- **Religious milieu.** Pauline communities are *Jewish-Christian-Gentile mixed assemblies*. The conflicts in his letters are precisely about the terms of this mixing: must Gentile converts be circumcised? Keep food laws? Observe the calendar? Paul's answer (Galatians, Romans) is no — they are reconstituted "in Christ" without Torah-observance — and this answer was *not* shared by the Jerusalem leadership (James and others) or by rival missionaries.
- **Who was behind it.** Paul, a Jewish citizen of Tarsus in Cilicia, Pharisaically educated (Phil 3:5), originally a persecutor of the early Jesus-movement, who experienced a christophany (~+34) and reframed his life as apostle to the Gentiles. He worked a craft trade (tentmaker / leather-worker, Acts 18:3) and supported himself rather than depending on his communities (1 Thess 2:9; 1 Cor 9).
- **Discovery / transmission.** The letters were collected, copied, and circulated within a generation. The Marcionite canon (~+140) included ten Pauline letters (rejecting the Pastorals); the proto-orthodox response (Muratorian Fragment, ~+170/+200) included thirteen. P46 (~+200) is our oldest substantial textual witness.

## Themes and motifs
- [[justification-by-faith]] — Gal 2–4, Rom 3–4: God's righteousness comes through faith in Christ, not works of Torah. The locus of all subsequent Protestant theology.
- [[in-christ]] — *en Christō*, the most frequent Pauline locution: union-with-Christ as ontological location of the believer.
- [[crucifixion-theology]] — 1 Cor 1:18–25 the *foolishness* of the cross; Gal 3:13 Christ as cursed under Torah (citing Deut 21:23) to bear the curse.
- [[resurrection-of-the-dead]] — 1 Cor 15 the longest and most influential resurrection chapter in the NT; pre-Pauline kerygma at 15:3–5.
- [[gentile-inclusion]] — Gal 3:28, Rom 11: a single people of God in which Jew and Gentile both have place.
- [[eschatological-imminence]] — 1 Thess 4–5: Christ's return expected within Paul's lifetime ("we who are alive, who are left"); the letters dating from later show partial adjustment as the parousia delays.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-3-014-q-source]] / [[phase-3-016-gospel-of-mark]] — *parallel-motif*: Paul predates the written Gospels; he quotes Jesus-tradition sparingly but knows the kerygma of crucifixion and resurrection (1 Cor 15:3–5) and a few sayings (1 Cor 7:10–11 divorce; 1 Cor 11:23–26 Last Supper). The shared kerygma argues for early oral consolidation.
- → [[phase-3-012-wisdom-of-solomon]] — *direct-influence*: Romans 1:18–32 closely echoes Wisdom 13–15 (idolatry-as-cause-of-moral-collapse).
- → [[phase-3-005-stoic-foundational-texts]] — *parallel-motif* / *direct-influence*: Pauline ethics (virtue/vice lists, *adiaphora*, conscience as natural-law indicator in Rom 2:14–15) shares vocabulary with contemporary Stoic moral discourse. Acts 17:28 has Paul quoting Aratus (Stoic poet).
- → [[phase-3-001-second-third-isaiah]] — *direct-quote*: Paul quotes Isaiah more than any other prophet, especially Isa 40–66 (suffering servant in Rom 4–5; "every knee shall bow" Isa 45:23 in Phil 2:10).
- → [[phase-3-006-septuagint]] — *direct-quote*: Paul's OT is the LXX.
- → Gnostic exegesis (Phase 4, Valentinians) — *direct-influence*: Valentinian Gnostics treated Paul as their primary apostle. Heracleon, Theodotus, and the *Tripartite Tractate* read Paul (esp. 1 Cor 2's "spiritual"/"psychic"/"material" anthropology) as their own. Cf. Pagels, *The Gnostic Paul* (1975).

## Disputes
- **Authorship of Colossians, Ephesians, 2 Thessalonians.** Critical majority (Schnelle, Ehrman): pseudonymous. Conservative scholarship: authentic. We carry the majority view and split off as a separate Phase-4 node.
- **Authorship of Pastoral Epistles (1–2 Timothy, Titus).** Critical near-consensus: pseudonymous (early 2nd c.). We treat these as Phase 4.
- **"New Perspective on Paul" (E.P. Sanders, J.D.G. Dunn, N.T. Wright).** Reframes "justification by faith" as primarily about Jewish-Gentile community boundaries rather than individual salvation-anxiety. Influential but not universally accepted.
- **"Paul within Judaism" (Fredriksen, Eisenbaum, Nanos).** Argues Paul never thought of himself as leaving Judaism; the Gentile mission is *within* a Jewish eschatological framework. Major recent reframing.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **Philippians 2:5–11 "kenotic hymn" — the vault's oldest formal incarnation-descent formula (~+48 CE):** the pre-Pauline Christ-hymn (composed perhaps within 15–20 years of the crucifixion) articulates the divine-self-emptying-into-humanity-then-restored structure. This is structurally identical to Hindu avatāra theology (Vishnu descending into human form, Bhagavad Gītā 4.7–8), the Tibetan *tulku* concept (enlightened being voluntarily reborn to serve beings), and Plotinian *prohodos/epistrophē* (procession and return). The *kenōsis* pattern — voluntary divine descent into limitation, followed by exaltation — is the vault's most widely-distributed divine-descent template, and Phil 2 is its earliest Western attestation.
- **Galatians 2:20 "not I but Christ lives in me" ↔ Hallāj's "Anā'l-Ḥaqq" ↔ Eckhart ↔ Advaita:** Paul's formula (*zō de ouketi egō, zē de en emoi Christos*) is the earliest dateable first-person *unio mystica* claim in the Christian tradition: the ego has been displaced by the divine. The structural parallel with Hallāj's "I am the Real" (executed 922 CE), Eckhart's "I live in God and God lives in me" (tried for heresy 1329), and Advaita's "*Aham Brahmāsmi*" is the vault's most precisely paralleled mystical-identity cluster — same logical form (self-erasure + divine-presence assertion), institutional rejection in three of four cases.
- **Valentinian Gnostics as Paul's first systematic commentators:** Pagels (*The Gnostic Paul*, 1975) demonstrates that Heracleon, Ptolemy, and Theodotus — all Valentinian Gnostics — were the first systematic commentators on the Pauline corpus, reading 1 Corinthians 2's "spiritual/psychic/material" (*pneumatikos/psychikos/sarkikos*) anthropology as a cosmological taxonomy of soul-types. Proto-orthodox Christianity's Pauline exegesis develops partly *in reaction to* this Gnostic reading; the Valentinian Paul predates the orthodox Paul. Both the primary texts and the Gnostic commentaries are vault-resident.
- **Romans 8:18–25 "groaning creation" → modern ecological theology:** Paul's vision of the whole cosmos in birth-pangs awaiting liberation (Rom 8:19–22) is the scriptural seedbed for process theology (Whitehead's creative advance), Teilhard de Chardin's Omega Point, and contemporary ecological Christianity. This is the vault's clearest ancient → modern transmission case for environmental theology, and a rare passage where Pauline soteriology is explicitly cosmic rather than individualistic — structurally closer to the Buddhist doctrine of universal liberation than to the mainstream Western salvation-of-souls framework.
