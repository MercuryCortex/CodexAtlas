# Mahābhārata

The **Mahābhārata** ("the Great [war of the] Bhārata[s]") is the longest poem in world literature — some **100,000 verses**, roughly seven times the combined length of the Iliad and the Odyssey — and, with the Rāmāyaṇa, one of the two great Sanskrit epics (*itihāsa*) at the foundation of Hindu civilization. Traditionally ascribed to the sage **Vyāsa** (who is also a character and progenitor within the story), it is in fact a vast, layered work of the oral **bardic (sūta) tradition**, grown from an older heroic core (the *Jaya*, "Victory") into its received form across roughly **400 BCE – 400 CE**.

## The story

At its center is a dynastic catastrophe: the struggle between two sets of cousins of the Bhārata line — the five righteous **Pāṇḍava** brothers (Yudhiṣṭhira, Bhīma, **Arjuna**, Nakula, Sahadeva, with their shared wife **Draupadī**) and the hundred **Kaurava** brothers led by the jealous **Duryodhana** — for the throne of Hāstinapura. Cheated of their kingdom in a rigged dice-game and exiled, the Pāṇḍavas return to claim their right, and the quarrel erupts into the eighteen-day **war at Kurukṣetra**, a near-total annihilation of the warrior class in which almost every great hero (Bhīṣma, Droṇa, **Karṇa**) falls. The victory is hollow and grief-soaked — the epic's unflinching meditation on the cost of even a just war.

## The central concern: dharma

Above all the Mahābhārata is the great Hindu treatise on **dharma** — righteous duty — and its agonizing ambiguities, where every character is caught between competing obligations and no choice is clean. Its famous self-description claims totality: *"What is found here may be found elsewhere; what is not here is found nowhere."* Embedded at the brink of the battle, in Book 6 (Bhīṣma-parvan), is the **[[phase-2-027-bhagavad-gita|Bhagavad Gītā]]** — **[[krishna|Kṛṣṇa]]**'s discourse to the despairing **Arjuna** on duty, action, and devotion — the single most influential Hindu philosophical text in the world.

## The cross-tradition wire

As a monumental verse-epic remembering a catastrophic dynastic war, the Mahābhārata invites comparison with **Homer's Iliad** — both are the foundational war-epics of their civilizations. Through Kṛṣṇa it carries the **avatar theology** of **[[vishnu|Viṣṇu]]** (the god descending to restore dharma) that the **[[phase-5-050-bhagavata-purana|Bhāgavata Purāṇa]]** would elaborate, while its sister-epic the **[[ramayana|Rāmāyaṇa]]** tells of another Viṣṇu-avatar, Rāma. Its reach is pan-Asian: the **Kurukṣetra battle reliefs of Angkor Wat** carve its war across a Cambodian temple wall. The Mahābhārata is, with the Rāmāyaṇa, the narrative bedrock on which Hindu ethics, devotion, and art have rested for two millennia.
