# Book of Baruch (1 Baruch)

## What it is
A short **deuterocanonical** book of five chapters, transmitted in the Greek Septuagint and ascribed to **Baruch ben Neriah, the scribe of the prophet Jeremiah**, supposedly written in Babylon after the fall of Jerusalem. In fact it is a composite work of the **2nd–1st centuries BCE** with three clearly distinct parts: (1) a narrative introduction and a long **penitential prayer** confessing the exile as deserved judgment (1:1–3:8); (2) a **wisdom poem** identifying divine Wisdom with the Torah given to Israel (3:9–4:4); and (3) a poem of **consolation** in which a personified Jerusalem laments and is then promised her children's return (4:5–5:9). It is canonical for the Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox, and Ethiopian Tewahedo churches, but not in the Jewish or most Protestant canons.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Composed under Hellenistic rule, projecting its concerns back onto the Babylonian-exile setting; a meditation on national sin, repentance, and hoped-for restoration.
- **Religious milieu.** Second-Temple Judaism drawing the Jeremianic legacy, Deuteronomic covenant theology, and the Wisdom-as-Torah identification into a single liturgically-shaped text.
- **Who was behind it.** Anonymous authors writing under the authoritative name of Baruch; the seams between the three sections, and the likely Hebrew origin of the first part (Tov), point to composite growth.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Preserved in the Septuagint and the great Greek codices, and carried into the Latin, Syriac, and Ge'ez Bibles; never part of the Masoretic canon.

## Themes and motifs
- [[wisdom-as-cosmic-order]] — the central poem (3:9–4:4) identifies the elusive cosmic Wisdom with "the book of the commandments of God," the Torah — the same move made in [[phase-3-007-sirach]] 24.
- [[messianism]] — the consolation section's promise of Jerusalem's restoration and the gathering of her scattered children carries the era's restoration hope.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-3-007-sirach]] — *parallel-motif*: the Wisdom-equals-Torah identification is shared with Ben Sira's hymn to Wisdom.
- → [[phase-3-012-wisdom-of-solomon]] — *shared-milieu*: contemporary Hellenistic-Jewish reflection on Wisdom and on Israel's God among the nations.
- → [[phase-3-006-septuagint]] — *manuscript-transmission*: Baruch exists for us as a Septuagint book, placed after Jeremiah.
- → the book of Jeremiah and the Letter of Jeremiah — *direct-development*: the pseudonymous Baruch frame extends the Jeremianic tradition; the Letter of Jeremiah travels with Baruch as its sixth chapter in the Latin tradition.

## Disputes
- **Composite origin and Hebrew Vorlage.** Tov (1976) argues 1:1–3:8 translates a Hebrew original, while the poetic sections may be Greek compositions; the unity and dating of the parts are debated (Steck, Adams).
- **Canonical status.** Its place among the deuterocanonical/"apocryphal" books — canonical for some churches, not for the Tanakh or Protestant Bibles — is itself the subject of the work's reception history.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **A book whose canon-status maps the splitting of the Bible.** Baruch is canonical in the Catholic, Orthodox, and Ethiopian Bibles but absent from the Jewish and Protestant canons — a single text that makes the divergent boundaries of "scripture" visible, exactly the many-to-many `canonical-corpus` axis the Atlas tracks separately from a book's singular origin (Nickelsburg 2005).
- **Wisdom becomes Torah.** Baruch 3–4, with [[phase-3-007-sirach]] 24, is a key witness to the Second-Temple identification of pre-existent cosmic Wisdom with the Mosaic Law — the move that stands behind both rabbinic Torah-theology and the Christian Logos Christology of John's prologue (Adams 2014).
