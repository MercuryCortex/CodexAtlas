# Aristotle — Metaphysics and De Anima

## What it is
Two interlocking treatises from the corpus of Aristotle's school-internal ("esoteric") works.
- **Metaphysics** — fourteen books (Α, α, Β, Γ, Δ, Ε, Ζ, Η, Θ, Ι, Κ, Λ, Μ, Ν) of first philosophy: being qua being, substance, potentiality and actuality, and culminating in Book Λ (Lambda)'s theology of the *unmoved mover* — a self-thinking thought (*noēsis noēseōs*) that moves all things by being the object of cosmic desire without itself moving.
- **De Anima** (*Peri Psychēs*) — three books on the soul as the form of a living body: vegetative (nutrition, growth), sensitive (perception), and rational (intellect, *nous*) capacities. Book III's *active intellect* (Α 5) becomes one of the most contested passages in the history of philosophy.

Together they constitute Aristotle's mature theology and psychology.

## Primary text — selected passages

Aristotle is in the public domain. Translations below are from Jonathan Barnes (ed.), *The Complete Works of Aristotle: The Revised Oxford Translation* (Princeton UP, 1984), used as short fair-use reference citations.

### Metaphysics Λ 7, 1072a26–b4 — the unmoved mover as final cause

The founding passage of philosophical theology: a being of pure actuality that moves all things as their final cause — by being loved — without itself being moved or altered.

> The first mover, then, of necessity exists; and in so far as it is necessary, it is good, and in this sense a first principle... And on such a principle depend the heavens and the world of nature. And it is a life such as the best which we enjoy, and enjoy for but a short time (for it is ever in this state, which we cannot be), since its actuality is also pleasure... If, then, God is always in that good state in which we sometimes are, this compels our wonder; and if in a better this compels it yet more.

Citation: Aristotle, *Metaphysics* Λ 7, 1072b10–24; trans. Barnes. Short fair-use quotation.

### Metaphysics Λ 9, 1074b33–35 — thinking thinking itself

The defining formula of divine intellect: *noēsis noēseōs* — thought thinking itself. Every subsequent "God is pure intellect" theology in the Western tradition runs through this phrase.

> And thought thinks on itself because it shares the nature of the object of thought; for it becomes an object of thought in coming into contact with and thinking its objects, so that thought and object of thought are the same. For that which is capable of receiving the object of thought, i.e. the essence, is thought... And since the object of thought and the act of thinking are not different in the case of things that have not matter, the divine thought and its object will be the same, i.e. the thinking will be one with the object of its thought.

Citation: *Metaphysics* Λ 9, 1074b33–1075a5; trans. Barnes. Short fair-use quotation.

### De Anima III.5, 430a10–25 — the active intellect

The most contested passage in Western philosophical history. The "active intellect" is separable, unmixed, and immortal — but whether this means individual immortality, a shared divine intellect, or neither has generated incompatible readings for 2,500 years.

> And in fact intellect as we have described it is what it is by virtue of becoming all things, while there is another which is what it is by virtue of making all things: this is a sort of positive state like light; for in a sense light makes potential colours into actual colours. And this intellect is separable, unmixed, and immortal, being in its essential nature activity... And without this nothing thinks.

Citation: Aristotle, *De Anima* III.5, 430a14–22; trans. Barnes. Short fair-use quotation.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Aristotle's life spans the rise of Macedon: he was tutor to the young Alexander (~-343) at Pella, then returned to Athens and founded the Lyceum (~-335). After Alexander's death (-323), anti-Macedonian feeling in Athens forced Aristotle to flee ("lest the Athenians sin twice against philosophy" — referencing Socrates); he died at Chalcis the next year.
- **Religious milieu.** Aristotle accepted civic religion outwardly but his theology is philosophical: the *unmoved mover* is impersonal, contemplative, and has no providential care for the world. This is the divinity later Christian and Islamic thinkers had to *modify* heavily to make it congruent with revealed religion.
- **Who was behind it.** Aristotle himself, working from his lecture pulpit at the Lyceum. The treatises read as edited lecture notes, not polished prose; this is one reason the texts were called *esōterika* (inside-the-school) versus the lost polished dialogues (*exōterika*).
- **Discovery / transmission.** Strabo (*Geography* 13.1.54) tells the famous story: after Theophrastus's death, Aristotle's library passed to Neleus, was hidden in a cellar to keep it from the Attalid book-collectors, and rotted there for over a century until being rediscovered, sold, and brought to Rome where Andronicus of Rhodes produced the first proper edition (~-50). Whether literally true or not, the corpus was indeed lost from circulation in the early Hellenistic period and recovered late.

## Themes and motifs
- [[unmoved-mover]] — *Metaphysics* Λ 7–9: a being of pure actuality (*energeia*), pure thinking thinking itself, the final cause toward which the cosmos eternally moves. The first philosophical "God of the philosophers".
- [[forms-and-archetypes]] — Aristotle's *hylomorphism* (form-matter compound) is his answer to Plato's separated Forms; form is internal to substance, not transcendent.
- [[soul-immortality]] — controversial: Aristotle says the *active intellect* "alone is immortal and eternal" (*DA* III.5, 430a23), but this is read variously as personal survival, impersonal divine Nous shared by all minds (Alexander of Aphrodisias's reading; Averroes's), or as something in between.
- [[teleology]] — every natural process is directed toward an end (*telos*); this teleological grammar persists into Christian providence and only collapses with the 17th-c. mechanical revolution.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-3-002-plato-dialogues]] — *commentary-on* / *polemic-against*: *Metaphysics* Α 9 systematically critiques Plato's Forms. Yet the framework (substance, form, soul, divine intellect) is fundamentally Platonic in inheritance.
- → [[phase-3-013-philo-of-alexandria]] — *direct-influence*: Philo absorbs Aristotelian terminology (potency/act, the categories) into his Middle-Platonist Logos theology.
- → [[phase-3-005-stoic-foundational-texts]] — *shared-milieu*: Stoic physics develops in dialogue with both Plato and Aristotle; Aristotle's *nous* and the Stoic *logos* are competing visions of cosmic intelligence.
- → Patristic theology (Origen, Augustine, John of Damascus — Phase 4+) — *direct-influence*: the Christian God-as-Pure-Act formulation is Aristotelian.
- → Islamic philosophy (al-Farabi, Avicenna, Averroes — Phase 5) — *direct-influence*: Aristotle as "the First Teacher"; the *active intellect* doctrine becomes central to falsafa.

## Disputes
- **Date of Metaphysics composition.** Multiple authorial layers and an editorial composite (Andronicus): different books may date from different periods of Aristotle's career (Jaeger's developmental hypothesis vs. unitarian readings).
- **Active intellect.** Personal-individual (Aquinas) vs. shared-divine (Averroes) readings have been live for two millennia; modern scholarship is divided.
- **Theology proper.** Whether Metaphysics Λ's unmoved mover is "the only god" or one of many (the discussion of fifty-five movers in Λ 8 raises this) — and whether Aristotle was *truly* a theist or a philosophical naturalist using theological vocabulary.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges

- **Unmoved Mover pipeline: Aristotle → Philo → Plotinus → Ibn Sīnā — the vault's longest single-concept philosophical transmission chain.** Aristotle's *noēsis noēseōs* (self-thinking divine intellect; Met. Λ 9, 1074b34) is the conceptual origin of a 1,500-year pipeline: (1) Philo's Middle-Platonist *theos agnostos* — God unknowable except through his Logos (*Quaest. Exod.* II.68); (2) Plotinus's *to hen* — the One, beyond intellect and beyond being (*Enn.* V.1); (3) Ibn Sīnā's *Wājib al-Wujūd* — the Necessary Existent whose essence includes its own existence (*Kitāb al-Shifāʾ, Ilāhiyyāt* I.6), which Aquinas translates directly into his *esse subsistens* (*Summa* I.3.4). Each transformation preserves Aristotle's core move (a first principle that is its own activity) while extending it: Philo makes it personal, Plotinus makes it beyond intellect, Ibn Sīnā formalizes the essence-existence distinction. Three Phase-3-to-5 vault documents — Philo, Plotinus's Enneads, and Ibn Sīnā — are stages in this chain; Aristotle is its origin point. Ref: Davidson (1992), *Alfarabi, Avicenna, and Averroes on Intellect*, Oxford UP.

- **Averroes → Aquinas: the Arabic-to-Latin retransmission and the active intellect controversy.** Aristotle's texts entered the Latin West not from Greek manuscripts but through the Baghdad *bayt al-ḥikma* translation movement (Ḥunayn ibn Isḥāq et al., 9th c.) → Averroes's commentaries (~1180) → Toledo Latin translations (12th–13th c.). Averroes read *De Anima* III.5 as asserting a single shared active intellect for all humanity (*monopsychism*) — entailing no individual immortality. Aquinas wrote his own *De Unitate Intellectus* (1270) explicitly to refute this reading, and the 1277 Paris condemnations targeted Averroist propositions by name. This is the vault's strongest documented case of Greek thought surviving only through Muslim intermediaries before re-entering Western thought. The Aristotle corpus that Aquinas and the Scholastics used was the Arabic Aristotle, filtered through 400 years of Islamic philosophical commentary. Ref: Hasse (2016), *Success and Suppression: Arabic Sciences and Philosophy in the Renaissance*, Harvard UP, chs. 1–3.

- **Active intellect (*DA* III.5) — the most contested 100-word passage in Western intellectual history.** Four incompatible readings across 2,500 years: (1) Alexander of Aphrodisias (~200 CE): active intellect = divine Nous itself; no personal immortality. (2) Averroes (~1180): single active intellect shared by all humans; personal immortality impossible. (3) Aquinas (~1270): active intellect is the individual soul's own capacity; personal immortality is preserved. (4) Pomponazzi (1516): active intellect is mortal and individual; no immortality at all. Each reading is internally coherent with the Greek text; the ambiguity is irreducible. Reading 2 generated the 1277 Paris condemnations; reading 4 inaugurated the Renaissance mortality debates that run from Pomponazzi through Locke and Hume to the contemporary philosophy of personal identity literature. No other 100-word passage in the Western tradition has generated this many philosophically sophisticated, mutually exclusive readings over this duration.

- **Teleology as target: Aristotle → Scholastic providence → Darwin's deliberate reversal.** Aristotle's four-cause framework and teleological grammar (*telos*, final cause, *entelecheia*) became the default description of natural processes in medieval Christian theology: "God orders all things to their ends" (*Summa Theologiae* I.103.1) is Aristotelian teleology Christianized. Darwin's theory of natural selection was designed as an explicit *replacement* for this framework: variation + heritable selection + time produces apparent design without purpose or foresight. The *Origin of Species* (1859) is, philosophically, the inversion of *Physics* II.8. Understanding Aristotle's teleology is the prerequisite for understanding what was at stake in Victorian natural theology, why Paley's *Natural Theology* (1802) was the *Origin*'s implicit target, and why Darwinism felt as culturally catastrophic as it did — not merely as a scientific theory but as the elimination of the metaphysical grammar that had described nature for 2,000 years. Ref: Lennox (2001), *Aristotle's Philosophy of Biology*, Cambridge UP, ch. 11.
