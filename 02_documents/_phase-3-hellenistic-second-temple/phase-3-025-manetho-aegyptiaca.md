# Aegyptiaca (fragments)

## What it is
The *Aegyptiaca* is [[manetho]]'s history of Egypt in three books, written in Greek under [[ptolemy-ii-philadelphus]] (c. -280 to -260) by an Egyptian priest of Sebennytos. It is the **earliest surviving Egyptian-priestly history of Egypt in any non-Egyptian language**, and the source of the **30-dynasty schema still used by modern Egyptology**. No continuous text survives — the work is reconstructed from extensive quotations and tabular epitomes in [[josephus]], Africanus, [[eusebius-of-caesarea]], and Syncellus.

## Primary text — selected passages

### Aegyptiaca fr. 1 — The divine-reign prehistory of Egypt
*Trans. Waddell, W.G. (1940). Manetho. Loeb Classical Library 350. Harvard UP.*
> First of all, the gods held the kingship of Egypt for 13,900 years: first Hephaestus [Ptah], who also was called their benefactor... then Helios [Ra] the son of Hephaestus for 77 years... then Kronos and the twelve other gods for 3,984 years; and after these demi-gods, other kings held sway for 1,255 years.

*Manetho's dynastic list begins not with a human king but with the gods: Ptah/Hephaestus, Ra/Helios, and the other Egyptian deities are recorded as the first rulers of Egypt, followed by demigods, before the historical thirty dynasties begin. This "gods reign on earth" prehistory — treating the divine-human boundary as historically permeable — is the foundational category of Egyptian royal theology rendered in Greek dynastic-list form. Structurally, it is the closest parallel in ancient historiography to the Sumerian King List's antediluvian kings with impossibly long reigns.*

*Citations: Waddell, W.G. (1940). Manetho. Loeb 350. Harvard UP; Dillery, J. (2015). Clio's Other Sons: Berossus and Manetho. University of Michigan Press.*

### Aegyptiaca — The Hyksos narrative (preserved in Josephus, Contra Apionem 1.75–87)
*Trans. Thackeray, H.St.J. (1926). Josephus: Against Apion. Loeb Classical Library 186. Harvard UP.*
> There was a king of ours whose name was Timaus. Under him it came to pass, I know not how, that God was displeased with us, and there came, after a surprising manner, from the regions of the East, men of ignoble birth, who had the confidence to invade our country... They set their rulers over all of them, who were desirous of extirpating the Egyptians.

*The Hyksos narrative — the most extensively quoted fragment of the Aegyptiaca — was preserved by Josephus as evidence for a Levantine Semitic presence in Egypt that Josephus identified with the biblical Israelites. Manetho used the term *Hyksos* (which he derives as "shepherd-kings," though modern scholarship reads it as "rulers of foreign lands") to describe the Western Semitic rulers of the 15th–17th Dynasties (~-1650 to -1550 BCE). The Hyksos/Israelite equation has been rejected by modern scholarship, but the passage is the anchor for the entire ancient tradition of Egyptian-Jewish apologetic controversy.*

*Citations: Waddell, W.G. (1940). Manetho. Loeb 350; Josephus, Contra Apionem 1.75–105; Dillery, J. (2015). Clio's Other Sons: Berossus and Manetho. University of Michigan Press.*

### Aegyptiaca — The 30-dynasty schema (Africanus epitome, in Eusebius)
*Trans. Waddell 1940.*
> First Dynasty — eight kings of Thinis. The first was Menes of Thinis, who reigned sixty-two years. He was carried off by a hippopotamus and perished. The second was Athothis, his son, for fifty-seven years...

*The thirty-dynasty framework introduced by Manetho — still used by modern Egyptology — is the most consequential organizational schema in the history of ancient Near Eastern historical study. Manetho grouped all Egyptian rulers from Menes to Alexander the Great into thirty dynasties, each defined by a ruling family or capital city. The schema is not always historically accurate (some dynasties were simultaneous; some transitions were internal), but it provided a framework that modern Egyptology has retained as a working tool. The system is Manetho's most direct contribution to knowledge — a 2,300-year-old organizational device still in active scholarly use.*

*Citations: Waddell, W.G. (1940). Manetho. Loeb 350; Dillery, J. (2015). Clio's Other Sons: Berossus and Manetho. University of Michigan Press.*

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Early Ptolemaic Egypt. [[ptolemy-i-soter]] (r. -323 / -305 to -282) and [[ptolemy-ii-philadelphus]] (r. -282 to -246) are building Alexandria into the intellectual capital of the Hellenistic world: founding the Library, sponsoring the Septuagint ([[phase-3-006-septuagint]]) under Demetrius of Phalerum (tradition), patronizing Greek scholarship across genres. Native Egyptian priestly elites navigate the new Greek-speaking overlord by writing strategically in Greek.
- **Religious milieu.** The Serapis cult ([[serapis]]) is being established as the syncretic Greco-Egyptian state religion under Ptolemy I, with Manetho himself a tradition-named co-architect alongside Timotheus of Eleusis. The Egyptian temple system is intact and well-funded. The Septuagint Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible is underway at the Library of Alexandria. Egypt is the principal site of Jewish-Greek-Egyptian intellectual collision.
- **Who was behind it.** Manetho personally, drawing on Egyptian temple records (king-lists, annal-stelae, sacred narratives in Egyptian); writing for a Ptolemaic court audience that needed a usable history of its new domain. Patron either Ptolemy I or Ptolemy II — most likely a project initiated under one, completed under the other.
- **Discovery / transmission.** **No continuous manuscript exists.** Reconstruction depends on four lines of transmission:
  1. [[josephus]] *Contra Apionem* 1.73–287 — extensive verbatim quotations on the Hyksos, the early dynasties, and on Egyptian polemic against the Jews.
  2. **Africanus** (~+220) — Christian chronographer; his epitome is preserved in Eusebius and Syncellus.
  3. **[[eusebius-of-caesarea]]** *Chronicon* (early 4th c.) — independent epitome partly preserved in the Armenian version and in Jerome's Latin.
  4. **Syncellus** *Ecloga Chronographica* (~+800) — Byzantine compiler, the latest but most extensive single source.

## Themes and motifs
- [[divine-kingship]] — Manetho's dynastic list begins with gods reigning on earth (Hephaestus/Ptah, Helios/Ra, Agathodaimon, Kronos, Osiris, Typhon, Horus), then demigods, then mortal kings. The schema treats the **divine-human boundary as historically permeable** — Egyptian theology of kingship rendered in Greek dynastic-list form.
- [[syncretism-egyptian-greek]] — Manetho systematically uses Greek divine names alongside Egyptian (Hephaestus = Ptah, Helios = Ra, Kronos = Geb or Sokar variously). His dynastic header preserves *interpretatio graeca* as the structural language of his history.
- [[apologetic-historiography]] — Manetho is in part responding to [[herodotus]] and to earlier Greek ethnographies of Egypt that he found inadequate. His own work is then weaponized by [[josephus]] as evidence for a Jewish presence in Egypt (via the Hyksos = Israelites equation, fragments in *Contra Apionem*) and by Christian apologists for the antiquity of Egypt-then-Israel.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-2-028-herodotus-histories-book-2]] — *polemic-against* + *correction-of*: Manetho's tradition includes a lost work *Against Herodotus*; his *Aegyptiaca* implicitly corrects Herodotus from the Egyptian-priestly side. The two are read together throughout antiquity.
- → [[phase-1-015-sumerian-king-list]] — *parallel-form*: structurally similar device — a dynastic king-list combining mythic and historical reigns — though there is no direct manuscript transmission between them. Both record antediluvian-style **gods-reign-on-earth** prehistory plus dynastic succession.
- → [[phase-3-026-diodorus-bibliotheca-book-1]] — *direct-quote* + *commentary-on*: Diodorus's Book 1 on Egypt draws on Manetho (alongside Hecataeus of Abdera and Herodotus).
- → [[phase-4-072-plutarch-de-iside-et-osiride]] — *direct-quote*: Plutarch names Manetho in chapters 9, 28, 49, 62. **Manetho is Plutarch's principal Egyptian-priestly source on the Serapis cult origin and on Egyptian theology.** This is a load-bearing transmission edge — native Egyptian priestly tradition → Greek Ptolemaic intellectual culture → Roman-imperial philosophical reception.
- → [[phase-4-071-josephus-against-apion]] — *direct-quote* + *polemic-against*: Josephus quotes Manetho at extraordinary length both to deploy him as a witness to Egyptian antiquity *and* to attack his account of the Hyksos.
- → [[phase-4-027-eusebius-ecclesiastical-history]] — *manuscript-transmission*: Eusebius's *Chronicon* is the principal channel by which Manetho's dynastic chronology enters Christian universal chronology and from there the medieval European calendar.

## Disputes
- **What did Manetho actually write?** The transmitted text is inconsistent: Africanus, Eusebius, and Syncellus give different dynasty totals, different king-name spellings, different reign lengths. Reconstructing the original is a major source-critical project (Waddell 1940; Dillery 2015).
- **Hyksos = Israelites?** Manetho's Hyksos narrative (in Josephus *Contra Apionem* 1.75–105) is the key text for the ancient identification of the Hyksos rulers of Egypt with the biblical Israelites. Modern scholarship rejects the direct identification (the Hyksos are Western Semitic populations broader than just proto-Israelites, ~-1650 to -1550) but the Hyksos-period reality of Levantine Semites ruling parts of Egypt is genuine. The ancient *use* of Manetho for Jewish-Egyptian apologetic is a load-bearing fact in its own right.
- **Manetho as Egyptian or Hellenizer?** Bickerman (1968) and older scholarship treated Manetho as a Hellenized collaborator. Moyer (2011) and Dillery (2015) treat him as a strategic Egyptian priestly intellectual using Greek as a cross-cultural medium while remaining culturally Egyptian — the current dominant view.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges

- **Manetho's 30-dynasty schema = most consequential single organizational device in the history of ancient Near Eastern studies — still used by modern Egyptology 2,300 years after its invention**: Manetho grouped all Egyptian rulers from Menes to Alexander the Great into thirty dynasties, each identified by ruling family or capital city. The schema is not always historically accurate — some dynasties overlapped, some transitions were administrative rather than dynastic — but it provided the organizational framework that modern Egyptology has retained as its standard working tool. Every textbook chapter on "Dynasty 12" or "Dynasty 18" (Akhenaten's dynasty) uses Manetho's numbering. No other organizational framework invented by a single ancient author has remained the standard scholarly tool in a field of study for 2,300 years (Dillery, J. 2015. *Clio's Other Sons: Berossus and Manetho*. University of Michigan Press. pp.1–40; Waddell, W.G. 1940. *Manetho*. Loeb 350. Harvard UP. pp.1–30; Redford, D.B. 1986. *Pharaonic King-Lists, Annals and Day-Books*. SSEA. pp.1–30).

- **Manetho's Hyksos narrative (Josephus, Contra Apionem 1.75–105) = foundational text of Jewish-Egyptian apologetic controversy and the documentary basis for the ancient "Israelites in Egypt" debate**: Manetho's account of the Hyksos — Western Semitic rulers of Egypt (-1650 to -1550 BCE) — was seized upon by Josephus as evidence that Levantine Semites (identified as proto-Israelites) had ruled Egypt before the Exodus. Josephus quotes Manetho at extraordinary length both to use him as a witness to Egyptian history and to attack his characterization. The Manetho-Josephus exchange is the oldest surviving example of one tradition's priestly historiography being weaponized for and against another tradition's historical claims — the prototype of all subsequent apologetic controversy about the Exodus and the Jewish-Egyptian relationship (Dillery, J. 2015. pp.41–80; Moyer, I.S. 2011. *Egypt and the Limits of Hellenism*. Cambridge UP. pp.84–141; Gruen, E.S. 1998. *Heritage and Hellenism*. UC Press. pp.1–30).

- **Manetho as the co-architect (with Timotheus of Eleusis) of the Serapis cult under Ptolemy I = most consequential single act of Egyptian-Greek syncretic religion-building in the Hellenistic period**: The ancient tradition names Manetho and Timotheus as co-architects whom Ptolemy I commissioned to design the Serapis cult — a syncretic Greco-Egyptian deity fusing Osiris, Apis, Zeus, Hades, and Asclepius into a single figure intended to be religiously authoritative for both Greek and Egyptian populations of Alexandria. If historical (well-attested in Plutarch *De Iside* 28 and Tacitus *Histories* 4.83–84), this is the most deliberate state-sponsored act of syncretic deity-construction in ancient history — a priestly intellectual designing a new god from identified cross-tradition parallels. The Serapis cult became one of the most successful mystery-religion exports of the Roman Empire, spreading from Alexandria to Rome, Athens, and Delos (Dillery, J. 2015. pp.80–120; Witt, R.E. 1997. *Isis in the Ancient World*. Johns Hopkins UP. pp.1–30; Moyer, I.S. 2011. pp.142–207).

- **Manetho's transmission chain (Josephus → Africanus → Eusebius → Syncellus) = most consequential path by which Egyptian dynastic history entered Christian universal chronology and thereby the European calendar and historical consciousness**: No continuous Manetho manuscript exists. The work survives only through four transmission lines: Josephus's quotations (~+90 CE), the Africanus epitome (~+220) preserved in Eusebius, Eusebius's *Chronicon* (early 4th c.) in Armenian and Jerome's Latin, and the Byzantine Syncellus (~+800). Through this chain, Manetho's Egyptian dynastic chronology was incorporated into Christian universal history: Eusebius established the standard Christian world-chronology using Manetho for Egyptian dates, the Hebrew Bible for Jewish dates, and Berossus for Babylonian dates. The resulting framework shaped medieval European historical consciousness — making Manetho's dynastic numbers the invisible backbone of Western historical periodization from Augustine through Scaliger (Dillery, J. 2015. pp.120–180; Grafton, A. 1993. *Joseph Scaliger: A Study in the History of Classical Scholarship*. Oxford UP. vol.2. pp.1–30; Adler, W. 1989. *Time Immemorial*. Dumbarton Oaks. pp.1–40).
