# Stoic Foundational Texts

## What it is
A bundle node for the Stoic tradition's textual base across four centuries:
- **Old Stoa** (~-300/-150) — Zeno (founder), Cleanthes (*Hymn to Zeus*, only complete extant Old-Stoa work), Chrysippus (third head; ~700 lost works; effective systematizer). Survives only as fragments in later quoters.
- **Middle Stoa** (~-150/-50) — Panaetius (introduced Stoicism to Roman elites), Posidonius (cosmologist, anthropologist; Plato-friendlier).
- **Roman Stoa** (~+1/+180) — Seneca (Letters, *De Providentia*, *De Vita Beata*), Musonius Rufus, Epictetus (*Discourses*, *Encheiridion*), Marcus Aurelius (*Meditations*; ~+170, just past Phase 3 endpoint and properly Phase 4).

For Phase 3 we treat the corpus through Epictetus (~+135) as the seed. Marcus Aurelius is flagged here but properly belongs in Phase 4.

## Primary text — selected passages

Old Stoa texts survive only in fragments quoted by later authors. Translations: Cleanthes and Chrysippus fragments from Long & Sedley, *The Hellenistic Philosophers* (Cambridge UP, 1987); Epictetus from Hard/Gill, Oxford 2014. Short fair-use quotations throughout.

### Cleanthes — Hymn to Zeus (c. -250 BCE) — complete

The only complete text surviving from the Old Stoa, preserved in full by Stobaeus. The hymn identifies Zeus with the *logos* — the rational, immanent principle governing all things. This is the theological statement at the head of the vault's Logos transmission chain.

> Most glorious of immortals, many-named, all-powerful forever, Zeus, leader of nature, who governs all things with law — hail. For it is right for all mortals to address you, since we have our origin from you, receiving the lot of an image of god... For so you have fitted together all things into one — the good with the bad — so that there is one eternal logos for all things...
> Lead me, Zeus, and lead me, Destiny, wherever is assigned by you; I will follow without shrinking. And even if I am unwilling and prove base, I will follow nonetheless.

Citation: Cleanthes, *Hymn to Zeus*, lines 1–4, 20–21, 34–35; trans. Long & Sedley (1987, §54I). Short fair-use quotation. Full hymn is 39 lines.

### Chrysippus — on the logos (c. -230 BCE, SVF II.1021)

The systematic Old Stoic definition of the *logos* as the governing rational principle of the cosmos, identified with Zeus, fire, and pneuma — the conceptual foundation of all subsequent Logos-theology.

> God is the intelligent, fiery pneuma — it has no shape but transforms itself into whatever it wishes and is assimilated to everything... The whole cosmos and the heavens are the substance of god... He is called Zeus because he is the cause of life (*zen*) or because he pervades all of life. *Fate* is a continuous cause of existing things, or the logos by which the cosmos is administered.

Citation: Chrysippus, SVF II.1021; preserved in Diogenes Laertius VII.135–136; trans. Long & Sedley (1987, §46A). Short fair-use quotation.

### Epictetus — Encheiridion 1 (~+100 CE)

The opening proposition of Epictetus — the "dichotomy of control" — and the explicit philosophical source of cognitive behavioral therapy. Albert Ellis cites this passage verbatim in *Reason and Emotion in Psychotherapy* (1962).

> Some things are in our control and others are not. In our control are opinion, motivation, desire, aversion, and, in a word, whatever is of our own doing. Not in our control are our body, our reputation, office, and, in a word, whatever is not of our own doing. The things in our control are by nature free, unhindered, and unimpeded; the things not in our control are weak, enslaved, hindered, dependent on others.

Citation: Epictetus, *Encheiridion* 1; trans. Hard/Gill, Oxford 2014. Short fair-use quotation.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** The Old Stoa founded in early Hellenistic Athens (~-300) under the political confusion of Alexander's successor kingdoms. Mid Stoa adapted in late Hellenistic Rhodes and Rome. Roman Stoa flourished under the Julio-Claudian and Flavian emperors, often in opposition (Seneca was Nero's tutor and later forced to suicide; Epictetus was an ex-slave exiled by Domitian).
- **Religious milieu.** Stoicism is a *pantheistic-monotheistic* philosophy: the cosmos is a single, rational, living God whose body is matter and whose soul is the *logos*. Stoics participated in civic religion as a matter of convention but interpreted gods allegorically as aspects of the one *logos*.
- **Who was behind it.** Continuous teaching lineage of the Stoa Poikilē ("Painted Porch") in Athens; later transplanted to Roman elite households and to Epictetus's school at Nicopolis in Epirus.
- **Discovery / transmission.** This is a peculiar transmission story: the *founding* texts are entirely lost — Chrysippus's reputed ~700 books survive in fragments. What we have continuously is *late* Stoicism in Latin (Seneca) and reportage of Epictetus by his student Arrian. The Old Stoa is reconstructed from hostile or sympathetic later authors. Modern critical editions (SVF; Long-Sedley) compile these scattered citations.

## Themes and motifs
- [[logos-cosmic-reason]] — the central Stoic doctrine: the cosmos is structured and animated by a divine, immanent, material *logos* (often identified with Zeus, with fire, with pneuma). **This logos-concept is the load-bearing concept transmitted forward**: Philo Hellenizes it for Judaism, the Johannine prologue Christianizes it, Justin Martyr will speak of *logos spermatikos* (seeds of the Logos in pagan philosophers).
- [[providence-and-fate]] — divine *pronoia* governs all events; nothing happens by accident; freedom is rational assent to what fate has determined.
- [[apatheia-and-virtue]] — the goal of life is virtue, achieved by extirpating passions (*pathē*) and judging only "things in our power" (Epictetus, *Ench.* 1).
- [[cosmopolitanism]] — humans are "citizens of the cosmos" (kosmopolitēs) by virtue of shared participation in logos; ethnic and political boundaries are conventional.
- [[soul-as-spark]] — the human soul is a portion of the cosmic pneuma/logos. This is the doctrine that lets Philo and later Christian writers identify *imago Dei* with *logos*.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-3-013-philo-of-alexandria]] — *direct-influence*: Philo's Logos is the Stoic *logos* (cosmic rational principle) read into the Hebrew Scriptures and personified as God's instrument of creation. **First step of the three-step transmission Stoic → Philo → John.**
- → [[phase-3-020-gospel-of-john]] — *direct-influence* (mediated): John 1:1–18's *logos* draws on Stoic-Philonic logos theology, recast as identified with Christ. **Second step.**
- → [[phase-3-015-pauline-epistles]] — *parallel-motif* / *direct-influence*: Paul's Romans 1–2 natural-law argument ("their conscience also bearing witness") is Stoic in shape; Acts 17:28 has Paul quote Aratus (a Stoic poet) at Athens.
- → [[phase-3-002-plato-dialogues]] — *shared-milieu* + *polemic-against*: Stoic cosmology rejects Platonic dualism (Stoics are materialists) but inherits the *Timaeus* picture of a divine *Nous* ordering matter.
- → [[phase-3-003-aristotle-metaphysics]] — *shared-milieu*: Stoics and Aristotelians share Hellenistic vocabulary; logos vs unmoved mover are rival accounts of cosmic reason.
- → Patristic theology (Justin Martyr — Phase 4) — *direct-influence*: Justin's *logos spermatikos* explicitly Christianizes the Stoic doctrine, claiming Heraclitus and Socrates as proto-Christians.

## Disputes
- **Stoic-Christian dependence.** Whether Paul knew Stoic ethics directly (some commentators), or absorbed common Hellenistic moral discourse, is contested. The Acts 17 quotation is uncontested; deeper structural dependence is debated.
- **Materialism vs theism.** Stoics call the cosmos God and call God material (active *pneuma*). Whether this is genuine theism, pantheism, or "physicalist deism" is debated (Long; Baltzly).
- **Logos transmission to John.** Whether John knew Philo directly, or both drew on shared Hellenistic-Jewish synagogue discourse, is contested (Dodd vs. Hengel vs. Boyarin).

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges

- **Stoic logos = Step 0 of the vault's Logos pipeline: Stoa → Philo → John → Nicaea.** The Stoic *logos* (cosmic rational principle identified with Zeus and pneuma; Chrysippus, SVF II.1021; Cleanthes *Hymn to Zeus* line 20) is the conceptual origin of the vault's most-documented philosophical transmission chain. Step 1: Philo of Alexandria (*Leg. All.* III.96; *De Opificio Mundi* 20–25) absorbs the Stoic logos into Mosaic cosmology as God's instrument of creation. Step 2: John 1:1–18 draws on Philo's Hellenized logos to identify Christ as the divine Logos from before creation. Step 3: the Nicene Creed (325 CE) codifies Christ's eternal pre-existence in the theological vocabulary this pipeline generated. Three separate vault documents — this file, [[phase-3-013-philo-of-alexandria]], and [[phase-3-020-gospel-of-john]] — trace consecutive stages of this chain. The Stoic corpus is the seed from which the central theological claim of Christianity grew. Ref: Dodd (1953), *The Interpretation of the Fourth Gospel*, Cambridge UP, chs. 10–11; Boyarin (2010), *Border Lines*, Pennsylvania UP.

- **Cosmopolitanism: Stoic → Paul → Mahāyāna — three independent articulations of universal human equality.** Zeno's *kosmopolitēs* ("citizen of the cosmos"; Diogenes Laertius VII.33) — the first philosophical assertion that human equality transcends ethnic and political borders — is the intellectual ancestor of Paul's "neither Jew nor Greek, slave nor free, male and female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus" (Gal 3:28), written within the Hellenistic cultural framework the Stoics shaped. The same universalist egalitarianism emerges independently in Mahāyāna Buddhist *karuṇā* (universal compassion) doctrine, developed in India roughly contemporaneously and becoming the foundation of the Bodhisattva ideal. Three vault traditions — Stoicism (Phase 3), Pauline Christianity (Phase 3), Mahāyāna Buddhism (Phase 5 in the vault's arc) — articulate the same universalist ethics by different conceptual routes. The Stoic articulation is the earliest explicit philosophical statement in any of the three. Ref: Engberg-Pedersen (2000), *Paul and the Stoics*, Westminster John Knox.

- **Ekpyrosis ↔ pralaya ↔ frashokereti: Stoic cosmic cyclicism in its cross-tradition cluster.** Stoic *ekpyrosis* (periodic conflagration-dissolution of the cosmos back into primordial fire, followed by *palingenesia* — renewal of an identical cosmos; SVF I.98; II.590–632) is the Western philosophical version of a cross-tradition cosmic-cycle family: Hindu *pralaya* (dissolution at end of a Yuga cycle; *Manusmṛti* 1.51–57) and Zoroastrian *frashokereti* (final world renovation; *Bundahishn* 34) share the temporal structure of cyclical cosmic destruction and renewal. Whether Stoic *ekpyrosis* derives solely from Heraclitean fire-doctrine or also reflects Iranian influence via Achaemenid-Greek contact is debated; West (1997, 289–300) favors Iranian influence on Heraclitus himself, which would make the Stoic-Zoroastrian parallel genetic rather than merely structural. This cluster is one of the vault's most geographically dispersed structural parallels with a plausible transmission route.

- **Epictetus → Albert Ellis: the longest documented direct-use lineage in the vault.** Epictetus *Encheiridion* 1 ("Some things are in our control and others not") is quoted verbatim by Albert Ellis in *Reason and Emotion in Psychotherapy* (1962, p. 35) as the explicit philosophical source of his Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy — which in turn is the foundational precursor of Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT), the most widely practiced psychotherapeutic modality globally, with an estimated 100 million clinical sessions per year. This is a 2,000-year unbroken intellectual citation lineage from a Phrygian ex-slave's classroom (~+100 CE) to 21st-century clinical psychology textbooks. No other ancient text in the vault has a more direct and verifiable impact on current lived human practice: when a therapist tells a patient "you can't control the event but you can control your response," they are teaching Epictetus's opening proposition.
