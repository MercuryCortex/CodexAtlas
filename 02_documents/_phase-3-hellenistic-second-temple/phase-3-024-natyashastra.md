# Nāṭyaśāstra

## What it is
The foundational treatise on classical Indian dramatic, musical, and aesthetic theory, traditionally attributed to the sage [[bharatamuni|Bharata]] but in fact a school-composed layered text dating ~200 BCE – 200 CE in its compositional core, with subsequent additions and recensional variation. 36 chapters (37 in the longer northern recension), ~6000 verses with prose passages. Covers the entire field of classical Sanskrit performance: the mythological origin of drama as a divinely-instituted "fifth Veda" accessible to all four *varṇas* unlike the Sanskrit Vedas, theatre architecture, performance preliminaries (*pūrva-raṅga*), the *rasa* theory of aesthetic experience (the most influential of its doctrines), the *bhāva* psychology of stable and transient emotional states, the four-fold *abhinaya* (modes of expressive performance: bodily / vocal / costume-makeup / inner-emotional), music theory (the basic scales, modes, melodic types — the foundation of all subsequent classical Indian music theory), dance theory (the 108 *karaṇas* later depicted iconographically in medieval temple sculpture), Sanskrit prosody, and dramatic poetics.

## Primary text — selected passages

### Nāṭyaśāstra 1.10–17 — The origin of drama: the Fifth Veda accessible to all four varṇas
*Trans. Ghosh, Manomohan (1951). The Nāṭyaśāstra. Asiatic Society of Bengal.*
> After hearing these words of Indra and the other gods, the wise Brahmā meditated for a while and then called to mind the four Vedas. Reflecting, he thought: "I shall create a fifth Veda on the Nāṭya (drama) with Itihāsa content, which will be consonant with the meanings of poetry and which will give guidance to people... Being related to works of all the sciences, all the crafts, and all the arts, this Nāṭya-Veda will be pleasing to the eyes and ears, and will contain the meaning of every scripture. I shall create a fifth Veda that will be suitable for all castes and all ages."

*The Nāṭyaśāstra's foundational myth: drama is created by Brahmā as a Fifth Veda (*Nāṭya-Veda*) accessible to all four *varṇas* including the Śūdras who were excluded from Vedic recitation. This makes drama the most democratizing religious form in the Sanskrit tradition — a parallel revelatory channel available to those excluded from scripture. The framing positions performance not as entertainment but as a mode of religious instruction equivalent to the Vedas, establishing the theological ground for all subsequent Indian performance tradition and its Southeast Asian exports.*

*Citations: Ghosh, M. (1951). The Nāṭyaśāstra. Asiatic Society of Bengal; Schwartz, S.L. (2004). Rasa. Columbia UP.*

### Nāṭyaśāstra 6.31 — The rasa-sūtra: the foundational definition of aesthetic experience
*Trans. Ghosh 1951.*
> Rasa is produced from the combination of *vibhāvas* [determinants/causes], *anubhāvas* [consequents/responses], and *vyabhicāri-bhāvas* [transient emotional states].

*The rasa-sūtra (NS 6.31) is the most commented-upon single sentence in the history of Sanskrit aesthetics: eight words that generated over a thousand years of aesthetic philosophy. *Rasa* (literally "juice," "taste," "essence") is the aesthetic emotion experienced by the qualified spectator (*rasika*) when the determinants (dramatic situation, character), consequents (physical-gestural responses), and transient states combine to evoke a *sthāyi-bhāva* (stable emotional disposition) in its fully expressed form. Abhinavagupta's 10th-century commentary on this sūtra transformed it from a technical-aesthetic formula into a statement about non-dual consciousness.*

*Citations: Ghosh, M. (1951). The Nāṭyaśāstra. Asiatic Society of Bengal; Gnoli, R. (1956/1968). The Aesthetic Experience According to Abhinavagupta. IsMEO.*

### Nāṭyaśāstra 6.15 — The eight rasas enumerated
*Trans. Ghosh 1951.*
> Śṛṅgāra (love/beauty), Hāsya (humor/comedy), Karuṇā (compassion/pathos), Raudra (fury/wrath), Vīra (heroism/vigor), Bhayānaka (terror/fear), Bībhatsa (disgust/aversion), Adbhuta (wonder/astonishment) — these eight are the rasas. The ninth, Śānta (tranquility/peace), was added to the canonical list by Abhinavagupta.

*The eight-rasa taxonomy is the most influential affective classification system in South and Southeast Asian aesthetic thought. Unlike Western emotion-taxonomies that treat emotions as psychological states, the *rasa-bhāva* system treats them as aesthetic modes — qualities of experience available to the qualified spectator rather than the emotions felt by characters. This reframing makes the Nāṭyaśāstra not just a theory of drama but a phenomenology of aesthetic consciousness, a transformation fully elaborated by Abhinavagupta's commentary.*

*Citations: Ghosh, M. (1951). The Nāṭyaśāstra. Asiatic Society of Bengal; Pollock, S. (2016). A Rasa Reader. Columbia UP.*

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Late-Mauryan / Śuṅga / Kushan / early-Gupta northern India (~200 BCE – 200 CE). The period of Sanskrit's institutional consolidation as the prestige language of court and ritual culture, the development of Indo-Greek and Indo-Scythian / Indo-Parthian / Kushan elite patronage of Sanskrit and Prakrit performance, and the textualization of multiple oral *śāstra* (technical-treatise) traditions in *sūtra* / verse-treatise form.
- **Religious milieu.** Sanskrit drama is positioned by the Nāṭyaśāstra explicitly as a Brahmanical-ritual cultural form — drama is the "fifth Veda," its performance opens with elaborate ritual *pūrva-raṅga* preliminaries, its mythic origin places it in the divine theatre offered by the gods. At the same time, the Nāṭyaśāstra's audience is the broad Sanskrit-using cultural sphere — not narrowly Brahmanical-ritualist — and includes characters of all *varṇas*, including the *vidūṣaka* (Prakrit-speaking jester / clown) who is a Brahmin but whose Prakrit speech ironically displaces the standard Brahmanical-Sanskrit register.
- **Who was behind it.** A Brahmanical-aesthetician school, attributing its product to the legendary sage Bharata. The textual layering shows discernible compositional strata: a core treatment of drama, music, and dance theory; later expansions including the *Nāṭyaveda*-mythological frame, the most-developed *rasa*-theory passages, and additional technical-musical material.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous Sanskrit transmission with two principal recensions. The Kashmir-recension manuscript Abhinavagupta commented on (~1000 CE) is the basis for the longer textual tradition. The Kūṭiyāṭṭam Sanskrit drama of Kerala maintains continuous performance practice. The Indian classical dance traditions in their 20th-c. reconstructed forms (Bharatanatyam, Odissi, etc.) explicitly anchor themselves in the Nāṭyaśāstra's *karaṇa* and *abhinaya* schemata.

## Themes and motifs
- [[mystery-initiation]] — drama is presented in the mythic frame as a divinely-instituted instructional medium for those excluded from Vedic study; the framing makes drama a parallel revelatory channel, accessible to the broader public.
- [[hierophany]] — Abhinavagupta's medieval elaboration of *rasa* as *camatkāra* (aesthetic-mystical astonishment) gives drama-watching an explicit hierophanic structure: the aesthetic experience is a flash of non-dual recognition (*pratyabhijñā*).
- [[sacred-and-profane]] — the Nāṭyaśāstra's drama is sacred in origin and ritual in performance-frame, but profane in much of its content (love, war, satire, the *vidūṣaka*'s social comedy); the sacred-profane interplay is constitutive of the form.
- **Rasa-bhāva theory** — eight (later nine) *rasas* (aesthetic emotions: *śṛṅgāra* love, *hāsya* humor, *karuṇā* compassion, *raudra* anger, *vīra* heroism, *bhayānaka* fear, *bībhatsa* disgust, *adbhuta* wonder, and the late-added *śānta* tranquility) generated when the spectator's stable *sthāyi-bhāva* is fully evoked by the dramatic *vibhāvas* (determinants), *anubhāvas* (consequents), and *vyabhicāri-bhāvas* (transient emotional states).

## Connections (provisional)
- → Aristotle's *Poetics* (mid-4th c. BCE) — *parallel-motif*: structural-comparative parallels are striking and have been periodically noted: both treatises analyze drama in terms of the emotional response of the spectator; both theorize a specifically dramatic emotional psychology (Aristotle's *catharsis* via *eleos* and *phobos* / pity and fear; Bharata's *rasa-bhāva* psychology); both treat plot, character, spectacle, and language as compositional elements. Direct historical influence is unproven; McEvilley 2002 places the parallel within his broader Hellenistic-Indian contact thesis, observing that the post-Alexandrian Bactrian / Gandharan zone (~300 BCE – 200 CE) is the contact zone in which Greek philosophical and aesthetic texts could in principle have reached Sanskrit-using elites. The mainstream Indological position treats the parallels as structurally convergent rather than historically derived. Worth flagging as a live comparative-aesthetic question.
- → [[phase-5-014-abhinavagupta-tantraloka]] — *commentary-on*: Abhinavagupta's *Abhinavabhāratī* (~1000 CE) is the principal medieval commentary on the Nāṭyaśāstra and the principal source for the developed *rasa*-as-*camatkāra* doctrine that integrates Bharata's aesthetic theory with Trika non-dual Tantric metaphysics. Abhinavagupta's reading transforms Bharata from a technical-aesthetic treatise into a *darśana*-level philosophical-mystical document.
- → Sanskrit poetics tradition — *direct-influence*: Bhāmaha (~7th c.), Daṇḍin, Ānandavardhana (*Dhvanyāloka*, 9th c.), Mammaṭa (*Kāvyaprakāśa*, 11th c.), and Viśvanātha (*Sāhityadarpaṇa*, 14th c.) all proceed from Bharata's *rasa-bhāva* foundation.
- → Southeast Asian performance traditions (Wayang shadow-puppetry in Java and Bali; Cambodian Apsara court dance; Thai Khon masked drama; Burmese, Javanese, and Balinese dance-drama generally) — *direct-influence*: the Nāṭyaśāstra and the *Rāmāyaṇa* / *Mahābhārata* repertoire it codified were exported with Sanskrit cultural transmission across Southeast Asia from the 5th c. CE forward; the structural-aesthetic frameworks persist in Southeast Asian performance into the present.
- → Classical Indian dance tradition (Bharatanatyam reconstruction 1930s onward, etc.) — *direct-influence*: 20th-c. Indian classical dance is explicitly anchored in the Nāṭyaśāstra's *karaṇa* / *abhinaya* schemata as authority text.

## Disputes
- **Date and unity**: 200 BCE – 200 CE is the standard range; the text is layered.
- **Bharata as historical**: the historical-vs.-mythic-vs.-school-figure question is unsettled; the consensus is that "Bharata" is a school-composer figure rather than a single historical author.
- **Recensional history**: the textual relationship between northern, southern, and Kashmir recensions is a continuing philological question.
- **The Aristotle-Bharata parallel (McEvilley)**: contested.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges

- **Nāṭyaśāstra rasa theory / Aristotle's *Poetics* catharsis = most precisely documented parallel aesthetic theories in world intellectual history — two independent treatises on how drama transforms spectator emotion, composed within a few centuries of each other**: Aristotle's *Poetics* (~-335 BCE) argues that tragedy produces *catharsis* of *eleos* (pity) and *phobos* (fear) in the spectator. The Nāṭyaśāstra's *rasa-bhāva* theory (~-200 to +200 CE) argues that drama evokes *rasas* in the qualified spectator through combined *vibhāvas*, *anubhāvas*, and *vyabhicāri-bhāvas*. Both treatises: (1) analyze drama in terms of the spectator's emotional transformation, not just plot-structure; (2) treat dramatic emotion as categorically distinct from ordinary emotion; (3) identify pity/compassion (*karuṇā* / *eleos*) and fear (*bhayānaka* / *phobos*) as primary dramatic emotions. No direct transmission is documented; the Gandhāran/Bactrian contact zone is the possible channel. This is the most productive unsolved question in comparative aesthetics (McEvilley, T. 2002. *The Shape of Ancient Thought*. Allworth. pp.1–30; Pollock, S. 2016. *A Rasa Reader*. Columbia UP. pp.1–30; Masson, J.L. and Patwardhan, M.V. 1970. *Śāntarasa and Abhinavagupta's Philosophy of Aesthetics*. Bhandarkar Oriental Research Institute. pp.1–40).

- **The "Fifth Veda accessible to all *varṇas*" = most consequential Indian text for democratizing religious knowledge outside the Brahmanical-Vedic system — the founding charter of Indian performance as a theological medium exported across Southeast Asia**: The Nāṭyaśāstra's mythological claim — drama is a Fifth Veda that Brahmā created specifically to give Śūdras and others excluded from Vedic study access to religious instruction — is the founding theological justification for the entire Indian performance tradition. This charter enabled: the Kūṭiyāṭṭam Kerala Sanskrit drama tradition (still performed; UNESCO 2001 Intangible Cultural Heritage); the Bharatanatyam and Odissi dance traditions as forms of temple liturgy; and the Southeast Asian performance traditions (Cambodian Apsara dance, Javanese Wayang, Thai Khon masked drama) as theological vehicles across Sinicized polities. The "Fifth Veda" claim is the Indian equivalent of the Confucian canon's claim to be the vehicle of cosmic pattern — performance as revelation (Schwartz, S.L. 2004. *Rasa*. Columbia UP. pp.1–30; Ghosh, M. 1951. *The Nāṭyaśāstra*. Asiatic Society of Bengal. pp.1–20; Vatsyayan, K. 1968. *Classical Indian Dance in Literature and the Arts*. Sangeet Natak Akademi. pp.1–30).

- **Abhinavagupta's *Abhinavabhāratī* (~1000 CE) transformation of *rasa* into *camatkāra* (non-dual aesthetic recognition) = most philosophically ambitious commentary on a performance-theory text in any tradition, making drama a mode of Trika Śaiva liberation**: Abhinavagupta's commentary transformed Bharata's technical-aesthetic *rasa* theory into a *darśana*-level philosophical-mystical document: *rasa*-experience is *camatkāra* — a flash of non-dual aesthetic astonishment structurally homologous to *pratyabhijñā* (recognition of one's identity with Śiva). The theater becomes a method of liberation: the qualified spectator achieves a temporary non-dual state. This is the most philosophically ambitious interpretation of a performance-theory text in any tradition — turning a technical treatise on dance and drama into a vehicle for Kashmir Trika Śaiva soteriology (Gnoli, R. 1956/1968. *The Aesthetic Experience According to Abhinavagupta*. IsMEO. pp.1–40; Pollock, S. 2016. *A Rasa Reader*. Columbia UP. pp.30–70; Muller-Ortega, P.E. 1989. *The Triadic Heart of Śiva*. SUNY Press. pp.1–30).

- **The Nāṭyaśāstra's 108 *karaṇas* depicted in medieval temple sculpture = most documented case of a technical performance treatise generating architectural iconography, linking text to stone across 1,000 years — culminating in the CERN Naṭarāja (2004)**: The 108 *karaṇas* (combined hand-foot-body postures) described in Nāṭyaśāstra chapter 4 are depicted in stone relief on the gopurams of major South Indian temples — most completely at the Śiva Naṭarāja temple at Chidambaram (~10th–12th c. CE). The Naṭarāja icon itself — Śiva as cosmic dancer performing the *ānanda-tāṇḍava* — is the visual theology of the Nāṭyaśāstra's claim that dance is a cosmic divine activity. The temple walls function simultaneously as theological statement and technical performance archive. The CERN Naṭarāja (installed 2004 by the Indian government at the European Center for Nuclear Research) extends this iconographic tradition: the physics of particle creation and destruction rendered as Śiva's cosmic dance — a 2,200-year iconographic chain from the Nāṭyaśāstra to particle physics (Schwartz, S.L. 2004. *Rasa*. Columbia UP. pp.30–70; Younger, P. 1995. *The Home of Dancing Śivan*. Oxford UP. pp.1–30; Kavi, M.R. ed. 1926–1964. *Nāṭyaśāstra with Abhinavabhāratī*. Gaekwad. vol.1).
