# Divyāvadāna

## What it is
The **Divyāvadāna** ("Divine Stories" or "Heavenly Exploits") is a Sanskrit anthology of about thirty-eight **avadāna**s — edifying narratives that trace the present fortunes of a person back to the karmic deeds of past lives. Compiled in the early centuries CE within the **Mūlasarvāstivāda** school, much of its material is lifted directly from that school's vast Vinaya (monastic-disciplinary) literature. The collection's most famous component is the **Aśokāvadāna**, the legend of the emperor [[ashoka]] — the great Northern, Sanskrit counterpart to the Pali chronicle tradition's account of the same king. Other celebrated stories include the *Pūrṇāvadāna* and the tale of Rūpāvatī, who gives her own flesh to feed a starving mother. Stylistically the avadāna sits between the canonical *jātaka* (specifically Buddha-birth stories) and the later ornate Sanskrit narrative: each story is a machine for displaying the law of karma across lifetimes.

## Primary text — selected passages

### The structure of an avadāna (frame formula)
*Trans. Rotman, A. (2008). Divine Stories. Wisdom Publications.*
> "What deed did this one do, by the ripening of which...?" The monks, being in doubt, questioned the Blessed One, the cutter-off of all doubts.

*The recurring frame: a present circumstance prompts the question "what past deed caused this?", and the Buddha narrates the karmic backstory. The avadāna genre exists to make the otherwise-invisible mechanism of karma narratable — every fortune and misfortune is the visible ripening of an earlier act.*

### Aśoka's gift of dirt (Aśokāvadāna)
*Trans. Strong, J. (1983). The Legend of King Aśoka. Princeton UP.*
> In a past life, as a small boy, Jaya placed a handful of dirt into the Buddha's begging bowl, imagining it to be food... By the ripening of that gift he was reborn as the great king Aśoka, ruler of Jambudvīpa.

*The Sanskrit legend explains Aśoka's empire as the karmic fruit of a child's innocent offering of dust to the Buddha — and goes on to narrate the violent "Caṇḍāśoka" (Aśoka the Fierce) who becomes the pious "Dharmāśoka" (Aśoka the Righteous) after his conversion by the monk Upagupta. This is a very different Aśoka from the Pali chronicles' steady patron-king.*

## Context
- **Political milieu.** The Mūlasarvāstivāda flourished across Greater Gandhāra and North India under Kushan and post-Kushan patronage — the same milieu that produced the great narrative art of the region.
- **Religious milieu.** A Buddhism organised around merit-making, relic and stūpa cult, and the karmic logic that the avadāna dramatises; the collection is a window onto popular, narrative Buddhist piety rather than abstract doctrine.
- **Who was behind it.** Anonymous monastic compilers, excerpting and arranging older Vinaya narrative material into a freestanding anthology.
- **Transmission.** Survives in Nepalese Sanskrit manuscript tradition; editio princeps Cowell & Neil 1886; standard translation Rotman 2008/2017.

## Themes and motifs
- **Karma across lifetimes** — the entire genre exists to display cause and effect spanning rebirths; the avadāna is the narrative form of the doctrine of *karma-vipāka* (the ripening of action).
- **Merit and the gift** — tiny acts of giving (even a handful of dust) yield vast future fruit; the stories underwrite the economy of donation that built monasteries and stūpas.
- [[divine-kingship]] — the Aśokāvadāna fuses karmic biography with a theory of the righteous Buddhist monarch (the *dharmarāja*).

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[mahavamsa]] — *parallel-tradition / divergent-memory*: the Divyāvadāna's Aśokāvadāna and the Pali Mahāvaṃsa preserve two independent Buddhist memories of [[ashoka]] — the Sanskrit "converted fierce king" versus the Pali "pious chronicle patron." Comparing them is a classic exercise in how textual traditions refract a single historical figure. Ref: Strong 1983.
- → [[jataka-tales]] — *cognate-genre*: avadāna and jātaka are sister narrative forms; the jātaka is the subset whose past-life hero is the Buddha-to-be.
- → [[phase-3-030-asokan-edicts]] — *legend-vs-epigraphy*: the Aśoka of the avadāna (and the chronicles) can be measured against the Aśoka of his own inscriptions — a rare case where a Buddhist legend has a documentary control.

## Disputes
- **Date and unity.** The anthology is a compilation; individual stories are older (excerpted from the Vinaya) than the collection's redaction, which is hard to date precisely (c. 2nd–4th c. CE is the usual range).
- **Relation to the Vinaya.** Scholars debate how much the Divyāvadāna is simply a Vinaya excerpt-anthology versus an independent literary project.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges

- **Two Buddhisms remember one emperor — the Sanskrit "Caṇḍāśoka→Dharmāśoka" vs the Pali patron-king**: The Divyāvadāna's Aśokāvadāna and the Pali [[mahavamsa]] are independent Buddhist传 traditions that remember the historical [[ashoka]] in incompatible ways — the Northern Sanskrit legend casts him as a cruel tyrant ("Aśoka the Fierce") dramatically converted by the monk Upagupta, while the Southern Pali chronicle presents a steadily pious dynastic patron. Set against Aśoka's own [[phase-3-030-asokan-edicts]], the pair is a textbook case of how a single ruler is refracted differently by two scriptural traditions of the same religion (Strong 1983, *The Legend of King Aśoka*, Princeton UP; → [[mahavamsa]]).

- **The avadāna as the narrative engine of karma — and of Buddhist visual art**: By fixing the form "present fortune ← past deed," the avadāna gave Buddhism a portable storytelling machine for the doctrine of karma. These stories supplied the subject matter for the great narrative relief programmes of Buddhist Asia — at Sāñcī, Gandhāra, Ajaṇṭā, and ultimately the galleries of Borobudur — so that the abstract law of cause and effect became something a pilgrim could literally walk past and read (Rotman 2008, *Divine Stories*, Wisdom Publications).
