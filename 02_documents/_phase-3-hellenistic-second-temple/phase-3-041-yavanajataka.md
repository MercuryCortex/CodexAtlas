# Yavanajātaka

## What it is
A Sanskrit verse treatise on **horoscopic (genethlialogical) astrology** whose very name — *Yavana-jātaka*, "the Greeks' [book of] nativities" — announces it as the importation of Greek astral science into India. It is the earliest substantial surviving witness to the moment when the **Hellenistic horoscopic system** (the twelve-sign zodiac, the seven planets as significators, the ascendant and the twelve places, aspect doctrine) was grafted onto the older indigenous **Vedic *nakṣatra*** (lunar-mansion) astronomy — producing the *horā* branch of *jyotiṣa* that remains the basis of Indian astrology to this day. By the membership-vs-wire principle of the Atlas, its **home is India** (it is a Sanskrit composition of the Indian astral-science tradition); its **Greek parentage is a transmission wire**, not a second membership — and that wire is exactly the point.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** The Western Kṣatrapa realm of the early centuries CE — a zone of intense Indo-Greek, Indo-Scythian, and Indo-Roman contact (the Periplus-era trade, Ujjain as an astronomical centre).
- **Religious milieu.** Brahmanical Sanskritic learning absorbing a foreign technical science: the *yavanas* (Ionians/Greeks) are explicitly named as the source, and the text even concedes that the Greeks, though *mleccha* (barbarians), are "honoured like seers" for this science.
- **Who was behind it.** Attributed to **Sphujidhvaja**, said to versify an earlier prose work by **Yavaneśvara** ("Lord of the Greeks", c. 149/150 CE by Pingree's reckoning). Both figures are obscure and known essentially only from this text.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Preserved in a few Sanskrit manuscripts (a key one from Nepal); recovered for scholarship by Pingree (1978) and re-edited by Mak (2013–14).

## Disputes
- **Date and the nature of the "Greek original."** Pingree (1978) dated Sphujidhvaja's versification to **269/270 CE** and reconstructed a single lost Greek source-text. **Bill Mak (2013–2014)**, working from a more complete Kathmandu manuscript, corrected many of Pingree's readings, questioned the colophon-based date, and argued that the work is not the straightforward translation of one Greek treatise that Pingree supposed. What is *not* in dispute is the core fact: the text's **technical vocabulary is saturated with Greek loanwords** — *horā* (< Greek *hōra*), *kendra* (< *kentron*, the angles), *dṛkāṇa/dreṣkāṇa* (< *dekanos*, the decans), *anaphā/sunaphā/durudhurā* (Greek lunar-configuration terms), and the Greek planet-and-sign system itself — which makes the Hellenistic → Indian transmission certain even where the precise textual genealogy is not.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **The Greek-into-India jump made visible.** The *Yavanajātaka* is the single best documented crossing of the astrological tradition from the Mediterranean into South Asia — a transmission proved not by speculation but by the **Greek loanwords embedded in the Sanskrit technical lexicon** (Pingree 1978, 1997; Mak 2013, 2014; Plofker 2009). It is the eastern arm of the same 3,500-year spine ([[phase-1-019-enuma-anu-enlil]] → Hellenistic synthesis → here) whose western arm runs through Sasanian Pahlavi and Arabic into Latin Europe.
- **A model membership-vs-wire case.** The node sits in the **Indian (jyotiṣa) wedge** as its home, while its Greek ancestry is carried as an *influenced-by* wire to [[tradition-western-astrology]] — exactly the architecture the Atlas uses to keep a figure or text in its origin family while making its cross-tradition reach legible as an edge rather than erasing it into a second membership.
