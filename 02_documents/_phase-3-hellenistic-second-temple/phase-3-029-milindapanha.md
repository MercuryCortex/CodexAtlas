# Milindapañha (Questions of King Milinda)

## What it is
The Milindapañha is a Buddhist philosophical-dialogue work in which the Indo-Greek king [[menander-i-soter|Menander I Sōter]] of Bactria-Punjab (Pali *Milinda*) poses questions on doctrine to the Buddhist monk [[nagasena|Nāgasena]], who answers using extended philosophical reasoning, scriptural citation, and a famous series of similes — most famously the **chariot-simile**, the standard Buddhist argument for *anātman* (no-self). The Pali Theravāda recension extends across seven books; the Chinese parallel-recension (*Nāgasena-bhikṣu-sūtra*, T 1670 A and B) preserves only the first three books, generally regarded as the older stratum. **The Milindapañha is the single highest-fidelity Greco-Buddhist primary document in existence** — a literary record of philosophical exchange between a historically-attested Greek king and a Buddhist monk in the Hellenistic-Buddhist contact zone of post-Alexander Bactria-Gandhāra. The text is the canonical reference for Buddhist no-self analysis, the foundational genre-example of Buddhist dialectical philosophy, and a load-bearing piece of evidence for the historical reality of [[tradition-greco-buddhism]].

## Primary text — selected excerpts

The excerpts below use T.W. Rhys Davids's public-domain 1890 *Sacred Books of the East* 35 translation. Rhys Davids is now superseded by Horner 1963-64 (PTS, critical) and Pesala 1991 (Motilal abridged); for serious study consult Horner. References use the standard PTS book/chapter/section numbering (e.g., Mil 2.1.1 = book 2, chapter 1, section 1).

### Mil 2.1.1 — the chariot-simile (THE foundational Buddhist anātman analysis)

The king Milinda has come to test the wise monk. He arrives at his cell:

> The king said: "How is your reverence called? What, Sir, is your name?"
>
> "I am known as Nâgasena, O king, and it is by that name that my brethren in the faith address me. But although parents, O king, give such a name as Nâgasena, or Sûrasena, or Vîrasena, or Sîhasena, yet this, Sire, — Nâgasena, and so on — is only a generally understood term, a designation in common use. For there is no permanent individuality (*puggala*) involved in the matter."
>
> Then Milinda called upon the Yonakas [Greeks] and the brethren to witness: "This Nâgasena says there is no permanent individuality implied in his name. Is it now even possible to approve him in that?"
>
> ...
>
> "Did you, Sire, come on foot, or in a chariot?"
>
> "I did not come, Sir, on foot. I came in a chariot."
>
> "Then if you came, Sire, in a chariot, explain to me what that is. Is it the pole that is the chariot?"
>
> "I did not say that."
>
> "Is it the axle that is the chariot?"
>
> "Certainly not."
>
> "Is it the wheels, or the framework, or the ropes, or the yoke, or the spokes of the wheels, or the goad, that are the chariot?"
>
> And to all these he still answered no.
>
> "Then is it all these parts of it that are the chariot?"
>
> "No, Sir."
>
> "But is there anything outside them that is the chariot?"
>
> And still he answered no.
>
> "Then thus, ask as I may, I can discover no chariot. Chariot is a mere empty sound. What then is the chariot you say you came in?... For 'chariot' is only a way of counting, term, appellation, convenient designation, mere name for pole, axle, wheels, framework, ropes, yoke, spokes, and goad. But in the ultimate sense there is no chariot. — and in exactly the same way, those various designations 'Nâgasena' are a mere name."

Citation: Mil 2.1.1, trans. Rhys Davids 1890, SBE 35.

This passage is **the standard Buddhist anātman analysis** — referenced in virtually every subsequent Theravāda doctrinal exposition and the canonical Buddhist analogue cited by Derek Parfit in *Reasons and Persons* (1984) and by Siderits in *Buddhism as Philosophy* (2007).

### Mil 3.6.1 — Karma without a permanent self

The king's natural follow-up: if there is no permanent self, who is reborn? Nâgasena replies with the flame-simile:

> "If, most reverend Nâgasena, there be no transmigration of souls, and yet there be rebirth — how can this be? Give me an illustration."
>
> "Suppose, Sire, a man were to light a lamp from another lamp, would that lamp have transmigrated from the other lamp?"
>
> "Certainly not, Sir."
>
> "Just so, great king, is rebirth without transmigration."

Citation: Mil 2.2.6 / 3.5.5 depending on edition; trans. Rhys Davids 1890.

### Mil 4.1.1 — the dilemmas (*meṇḍaka-pañha*)

The fourth book opens the famous "dilemmas" — apparent contradictions in canonical scripture that Milinda poses and Nâgasena resolves. The opening exchange:

> The king said: "Are there any laymen, Nâgasena, who have attained to Arahatship while still in the household life and not yet entered into the homeless state?"
>
> "Yes, your Majesty, there are. There are not merely one or two laymen who have attained to Arahatship; but there are hundred, or even thousands... and a thousand thousands."

This pattern — a dilemma that appears to contradict canonical doctrine, resolved by a careful exegesis distinguishing the two cases — characterizes the rest of the work and is the genre's most distinctive contribution to Buddhist dialectical literature.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** The Indo-Greek kingdom of Bactria-Punjab under [[menander-i-soter|Menander I]] (~-165 to -130). Sākala (modern Sialkot) was Menander's capital. The political-cultural milieu is post-Mauryan: Aśoka's empire fragmented after his death (-232), and the northwest passed to the Indo-Greek successor-kingdoms of Bactria, who ruled bilingual Greek-Indian populations.
- **Religious milieu.** Greco-Buddhism in formation. Buddhist communities under Indo-Greek patronage; Greek officers and merchants in Indian cities; bilingual coinage; the [[phase-3-030-asokan-edicts|Aśokan edicts]] only ~80 years in the past with their explicit address to Greek kings. The Heliodorus pillar at Vidiśā (~-110) is a contemporary archaeological witness to Greek officers' engagement with Indian religion (in that case Vaiṣṇava, not Buddhist).
- **Who was behind it.** Anonymous Buddhist redactors of probably Sarvāstivāda or related northern-school provenance for the early stratum (preserved in the Chinese version); Pali Theravāda redactors expanded the work substantially later (books 4-7 of the Pali version are post-3rd c. CE). The historical core — book 2's no-self dialogue with the chariot-simile — is generally dated ~-100 to +100, post-Menander but within living-memory or close-tradition reach of the Menander-Nāgasena encounter.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous transmission in Theravāda Southeast Asia (Burmese, Thai recensions). Chinese parallel-recension preserved in the Buddhist canon. No Sanskrit version survives. The Pali version was first edited critically by Trenckner 1880 (PTS); first translated by Rhys Davids 1890 (SBE 35-36).

## Themes and motifs
- [[anatman-no-self]] — the chariot-simile is the canonical Buddhist anātman analysis.
- [[pratitya-samutpada]] — Nâgasena's flame-simile illustrates rebirth-without-transmigration as dependent origination of a new lamp-flame from an old.
- [[four-noble-truths]] — book 3 includes systematic exposition of the four truths.
- [[emptiness-sunyata]] — the chariot-simile's analysis of "chariot" as "mere designation, name, convention" is the proto-form of Mādhyamika emptiness-analysis. Nāgārjuna's MMK (~+200) develops the same chariot-style dialectic philosophically; the Milindapañha is the pre-philosophical canonical predecessor.
- **The cross-cultural dialogue genre** — the Milindapañha is a *Hellenistic-Buddhist hybrid genre* combining the Greek philosophical-dialogue form (Socratic question-and-answer; Aristotelian dialectic) with the Indian *praśna* (question-and-answer) genre.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[menander-i-soter]] — *frame-figure*: the historical king who interrogates Nāgasena.
- → [[nagasena]] — *frame-figure*: the historical monk who answers.
- → [[tradition-greco-buddhism]] — *core document*: the highest-fidelity primary document of this cross-tradition contact zone.
- → [[phase-3-030-asokan-edicts]] — *shared-milieu*: both are Indo-Greek-frontier primary documents; the Aśokan edicts ~80 years earlier address Greek kings, the Milindapañha records a Greek king's Buddhist conversation.
- → [[nagarjuna]]'s *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* (~+200) — *direct-influence (proposed)*: the chariot-simile and the Mādhyamika emptiness-dialectic share a philosophical structure; whether Nāgārjuna read the Milindapañha or independently developed the analysis is debated; both can be true (shared Indian Buddhist analytical tradition).
- → Plato's *Republic* / *Symposium* / Aristotle's *Topics* — *parallel-form*: the Greek-philosophical dialogue genre. Nāgasena's responses to Milinda's "Did you come on foot or in a chariot?" question is a Greek-style Socratic-elenchus pattern; the parallel is not coincidental in a Hellenistic-Buddhist setting.
- → Modern analytic philosophy (Parfit 1984; Siderits 2007) — *reception-history*: the chariot-simile is invoked as the canonical Buddhist analogue of reductionist personal-identity analysis.

## Disputes
- **Date**: the core dialogue (chariot-simile and immediate context) is ~-100 to +100; the Pali expansion is later. Modern consensus: the Chinese parallel preserves an earlier stratum than the extended Pali version.
- **Historicity of the underlying encounter**: did Menander and Nāgasena actually have such conversations? The Chinese-Pali parallel-recension argues for a real historical core; the elaborated dialogue is literary. Modern consensus (Mairs 2014; Halkias 2014): a historical core is plausible; the *Milindapañha* as we have it is a literary product of the genre.
- **School affiliation**: Sarvāstivāda (Chinese side) or Theravāda (Pali side)? Modern consensus: pre-sectarian original, with the two recensions reflecting the school-divergence of the early Common Era.
- **Canonical status**: the Burmese and Thai recensions of the Pali canon include it in the Khuddaka Nikāya; the Sri Lankan Mahāvihāra recension treats it as authoritative but non-canonical. This is the major scriptural status-disagreement of Theravāda canonicity.
