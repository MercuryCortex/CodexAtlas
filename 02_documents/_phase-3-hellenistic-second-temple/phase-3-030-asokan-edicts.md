# Aśokan Edicts

## What it is
The Aśokan Edicts are a corpus of ~33 inscriptions composed in the first-person voice of the Mauryan emperor [[asoka-maurya|Aśoka the Great]] (*Devānāṃpriya Priyadarśin*, "Beloved of the Gods, Looker-with-Kindness") in the years following his Kaliṅga-war conversion (~-261). They are the earliest dated South Asian historical-religious documents — the Brahmi script in which most are inscribed is the earliest attested fully-developed Indian script — and the only first-person royal voice surviving from pre-Common-Era India. The edicts articulate *dhamma* (Sanskrit *dharma*), Aśoka's program of ethical-religious governance: non-violence to humans and animals, parental respect, religious tolerance, social welfare provision (medicinal trees, wells, hospitals), proselytization through dhamma-missions, and personal moral reflection. The corpus includes the **Major Rock Edicts** (14, inscribed at strategic frontier sites), the **Major Pillar Edicts** (7, on free-standing polished sandstone pillars topped with capitals — the Sarnath Lion Capital is the state emblem of modern India), the **Minor Rock Edicts** (several, more personal in tone, naming Aśoka explicitly), the **Lumbinī Pillar** (commemorating the Buddha's birthplace — independent archaeological confirmation of [[siddhartha-gautama-buddha|the Buddha's]] historicity), the **Kandahar Bilingual Greek-Aramaic Edict** (Aśoka's text in the Hellenistic frontier languages), and a separate **Kandahar Greek Edict** (Greek alone). **The single most consequential cross-tradition transmission edge in this corpus is Major Rock Edict XIII (~-256)**, in which Aśoka names five Hellenistic kings — Antiochus II Theos, Ptolemy II Philadelphus, Antigonus II Gonatas, Magas of Cyrene, and Alexander II of Epirus — as recipients of his dhamma-missions. This is the first attested Indian religious outreach to the Hellenistic West and a load-bearing piece of evidence for [[tradition-greco-buddhism]].

## Primary text — selected edicts

The excerpts below use Romila Thapar's 1961 translation (Appendix V of *Aśoka and the Decline of the Mauryas*) for most edicts, supplemented by N.A. Nikam and Richard McKeon 1958 (*The Edicts of Asoka*, Chicago) for cross-check. References use Hultzsch 1925 numbering.

### Major Rock Edict XIII — THE Greco-Buddhist transmission edge

The most famous and historically consequential of all Aśokan edicts. Aśoka describes his remorse at the Kaliṅga casualties and his subsequent commitment to dhamma — and lists the five Hellenistic kings to whom he has sent dhamma-envoys:

> "The Beloved of the Gods, the king Piyadassi, conquered the Kalingas eight years after his consecration. One hundred and fifty thousand people were deported, one hundred thousand were killed, and many times that number perished. After the Kalingas had been conquered, the Beloved of the Gods came to feel a strong inclination towards the Dhamma... The Beloved of the Gods is pained by the death, slaughter, and deportation of even a single man... Now it is conquest by Dhamma that the Beloved of the Gods considers to be the best conquest.
>
> "And it (conquest by Dhamma) has been won here, on the borders, even six hundred yojanas away, where the Greek king Antiochos rules, and beyond there where the four kings named Ptolemy, Antigonos, Magas and Alexander rule, likewise in the south among the Cholas, the Pandyas, and as far as Tamraparni. Here in the king's domain among the Greeks, the Kambojas, the Nabhakas, the Nabhapamkits, the Bhojas, the Pitinikas, the Andhras and the Palidas, everywhere people are following the Beloved of the Gods' instructions in Dhamma."

Citation: Major Rock Edict XIII, trans. Thapar 1961 Appendix V; cf. Hultzsch 1925 ad loc.

Identification of the five Greek kings (consensus by Bühler 1893, Hultzsch 1925, Tarn 1938, Eggermont 1969):
- **Antiochus II Theos** (Seleucid; r. -261 to -246)
- **Ptolemy II Philadelphus** (Ptolemaic Egypt; r. -283 to -246)
- **Antigonus II Gonatas** (Macedonia; r. -276 to -239)
- **Magas of Cyrene** (Cyrenaica; r. -276 to -250)
- **Alexander II of Epirus** (Epirus; r. -272 to -255) or possibly Alexander of Corinth (r. -252 to -245)

The mutual-reign overlap window narrows the edict's composition to ~-256 (when all five were simultaneously alive and reigning); this is the standard date for Aśoka's regnal-year 12 dhamma-mission corpus.

### Lumbinī Pillar inscription — Buddha-historicity attestation

The shortest and one of the most-cited edicts. Aśoka commemorates his personal pilgrimage to the Buddha's birthplace:

> "When King Devanampriya Priyadarsin had been anointed twenty years, he came in person and worshipped (this spot) because the Buddha Shakyamuni was born here. (He) both caused a stone bearing a horse to be made and a stone pillar to be set up, (in order to show) that the Blessed One was born here. (He) made the village of Lumbini free of taxes and paying (only) an eighth share (of the produce)."

Citation: Lumbinī Pillar (Rummindei Pillar), trans. Hultzsch 1925.

**This is independent epigraphic attestation that the Buddha was historically born at Lumbinī — inscribed by Aśoka in person ~250 years after the parinirvāṇa.** It is one of the strongest individual pieces of evidence for the Buddha's historicity (see [[siddhartha-gautama-buddha]]).

### Kandahar Greek-Aramaic Bilingual Edict (1958 find)

Discovered in 1958 at Old Kandahar (modern Kandahar, Afghanistan); the Greek-Aramaic bilingual presentation of the Aśokan dhamma to the Greek-Aramaic-speaking population of the Mauryan northwest frontier:

(Greek): "Ten years (of reign) having been completed, King Piodasses [Priyadarśin] made known the doctrine of Piety [εὐσέβεια, *eusebeia*] to men. And from this moment he has made men more pious, and everything thrives throughout the whole world. And the king abstains from killing living beings, and other men and those who (are) huntsmen and fishermen of the king have desisted from hunting..."

(Aramaic): Parallel content.

Citation: Schlumberger et al. 1958, *Journal Asiatique* 246:1-48 — editio princeps.

This bilingual edict demonstrates concretely what Major Rock Edict XIII implies abstractly: Aśoka deliberately translated his dhamma-message into the languages of his Hellenistic frontier population, using the Greek philosophical term *eusebeia* ("piety, religious devotion") as the translation-equivalent of *dhamma*. This is the foundational Hellenistic-Buddhist *terminus technicus* translation choice.

### Major Pillar Edict VII — the dhamma-mission infrastructure

Aśoka describes his administrative infrastructure for spreading dhamma:

> "In the past, neither were dhamma-mahāmātras (officers of dhamma) appointed nor was the affair of dhamma reviewed. But by me, when I had been anointed for thirteen years, dhamma-mahāmātras were appointed... They are engaged in all the sects [*pasaṇḍas*] for the establishment of dhamma, the promotion of dhamma, and for the welfare and happiness of those who are devoted to dhamma..."

Citation: Major Pillar Edict VII, trans. Thapar 1961.

### Minor Rock Edict I (Maski version) — Aśoka self-identification

The Maski version of Minor Rock Edict I (discovered 1915 by Beadon) is the only edict explicitly to name "Aśoka" (rather than the standard "Beloved of the Gods, Looker-with-Kindness" title). This was the decisive find that confirmed the Aśokan identification of *Devānāṃpriya Priyadarśin*:

> "It is by the Beloved of the Gods Aśoka thus said... For two and half years and somewhat more, I have been a *upāsaka* (lay-Buddhist), but I had not been very zealous. But it is a year and somewhat more that I have visited the Saṅgha and have been very zealous..."

Citation: Minor Rock Edict I (Maski), trans. Hultzsch 1925.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Mauryan empire under Aśoka (r. -268 to -232); the largest pre-modern Indian polity, extending from Bactria-Gandhāra to Bengal and from the Iranian frontier to the Tamil Nadu border. The empire is post-Alexandrian and post-Seleucid-Mauryan-treaty (Chandragupta-Seleucus, -303): the northwest frontier is bilingual Greek-Aramaic-Indic, and Aśoka actively administers it via the Kandahar-region governorate.
- **Religious milieu.** Mauryan-period India is religiously pluralist: brahminic Vedic religion, śramaṇa movements (Buddhism, Jainism, Ājīvika, several proto-Hindu currents), and the Greek-Iranian frontier traditions of the northwest. Aśoka's *dhamma* is deliberately non-sectarian in its register, addressing "all sects" (*sarva-pāsaṇḍa*); his patronage is nonetheless dominant on Buddhism via specific institutional actions (the [[event-third-buddhist-council-c-250-bce|Third Buddhist Council]] at Pāṭaliputra; the dhamma-missions; the Schism Edict prohibiting saṅgha-schism).
- **Who was behind it.** Aśoka himself in first-person voice, drafted by his secretariat and inscribed by his imperial public-works officials. The first-person voice is unprecedented in the Indian inscriptional record before this point and not paralleled to the same extent until many centuries later.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Edicts were continuously visible at major sites (the Delhi-Topra pillar, Lauriya-Araraj pillar, etc.) throughout the medieval period, but the Brahmi script in which they were written was illegible. Decipherment by **James Prinsep in 1837** opened the corpus. The Maski edict naming Aśoka was found in 1915 (Beadon); the Kandahar bilingual in 1958 (Daniel Schlumberger excavation). Discovery and re-discovery has continued through the 20th-21st c.

## Themes and motifs
- ***Dhamma*** as universal ethical-religious doctrine — non-sectarian framing addressing "all sects." Non-violence to humans and animals; religious tolerance; parental respect; social welfare.
- **Royal proselytization** — the dhamma-mahāmātras (officers of dhamma) and the dhamma-missions to the Hellenistic kings, to Sri Lanka, to Southeast Asia (Suvarnabhūmi).
- **Personal moral reflection** — Aśoka's confession of remorse over Kaliṅga is one of the earliest first-person royal moral statements in world historical record.
- **Cross-tradition transmission infrastructure** — Major Rock Edict XIII + the Kandahar bilingual together establish that Aśoka had a deliberate, multilingual, multi-religious, cross-frontier dhamma-export program.

## Disputes
- **Date of regnal year 12**: depends on Aśoka's accession date, which is reconstructed from Major Rock Edict XIII's Greek-king synchronisms. Hultzsch dates accession to -268; recent scholarship (Falk 1998) refines this.
- **Identity of "Alexander"**: Alexander II of Epirus (r. -272 to -255) is the standard identification; some scholars suggest Alexander of Corinth instead. The discrepancy does not affect the core narrative.
- **"Conversion" timing**: did Aśoka convert to Buddhism in the Kaliṅga-aftermath, or earlier? Minor Rock Edict I (Maski version) places his Buddhist-lay-follower (upāsaka) status at "two and a half years and somewhat more" before that edict's composition (which is dated to ~ year 10-11 of his reign). Modern scholarship (Lahiri 2015, Thapar 1961, Olivelle 2023) generally accepts a multi-stage moral-religious development rather than a single conversion-moment.
- **Sectarian sponsorship**: the edicts' non-sectarian rhetoric vs. the specifically Buddhist patronage acts. See discussion at [[asoka-maurya]].
