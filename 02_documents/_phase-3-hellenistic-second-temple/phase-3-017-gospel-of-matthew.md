# Gospel of Matthew

## What it is
A 28-chapter Greek Gospel built on Mark's narrative skeleton (~90% of Mark's content is taken over) with two major additional sources: Q (sayings tradition shared with Luke) and "M" (Matthean special material — the infancy narratives, the parable cycle of 13, the ecclesiological discourse of 18, the pericope adulterae traditions, much of the Sermon on the Mount's distinctively Matthean material). Organized around **five great discourses**, each closed with the formula "when Jesus had finished these sayings" (7:28; 11:1; 13:53; 19:1; 26:1):
1. The Sermon on the Mount (chs. 5–7)
2. The Mission Discourse (ch. 10)
3. The Parables Discourse (ch. 13)
4. The Community Discourse (ch. 18)
5. The Olivet Discourse / Eschatological Discourse (chs. 23–25)

This five-fold structure is widely read as deliberately echoing the **Five Books of Moses**, presenting Jesus as a new lawgiver. The Gospel is bracketed by the infancy narrative (chs. 1–2) and the resurrection / Great Commission (ch. 28).

## Primary text — selected passages

### Matthew 5:1–12 — The Beatitudes: opening of the Sermon on the Mount
*Trans. NRSV; Luz, U. (2001). Matthew 1–7. Hermeneia, Fortress.*
> When Jesus saw the crowds, he went up the mountain; and after he sat down, his disciples came to him. Then he began to speak, and taught them, saying:
> "Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven.
> Blessed are those who mourn, for they will be comforted.
> Blessed are the meek, for they will inherit the earth.
> Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness, for they will be filled.
> Blessed are the merciful, for they will receive mercy.
> Blessed are the pure in heart, for they will see God.
> Blessed are the peacemakers, for they will be called children of God.
> Blessed are those who are persecuted for righteousness' sake, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven."

*The mountain setting is deliberate new-Moses typology. Matthew's "poor in spirit" (*ptōchoi tō pneumati*) modifies Luke's unqualified "poor" (*ptōchoi*) — the most analyzed single redactional word in NT scholarship.*

### Matthew 5:17, 5:43–45 — The antitheses: fulfillment and love of enemies
*Trans. NRSV*
> "Do not think that I have come to abolish the law or the prophets; I have come not to abolish but to fulfill."
> [...]
> "You have heard that it was said, 'You shall love your neighbor and hate your enemy.' But I say to you, Love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you, so that you may be children of your Father in heaven."

*5:17 is Matthew's most debated programmatic statement. The "antitheses" ("You have heard... But I say") are Jesus's most formally authoritative teaching mode — the "I say" claiming authority parallel to or exceeding Mosaic authority.*

### Matthew 16:18–19 — The Peter/rock declaration: the ecclesiological pivot
*Trans. NRSV*
> "And I tell you, you are Peter [*Petros*], and on this rock [*petra*] I will build my church [*ekklēsia*], and the gates of Hades will not prevail against it. I will give you the keys of the kingdom of heaven, and whatever you bind on earth will be bound in heaven, and whatever you loose on earth will be loosed in heaven."

*Matthew 16:18–19 is the only place in the Gospels that uses *ekklēsia* in the institutional sense and ties it to an apostolic figure. The word "church" (*ekklēsia*) appears only twice in the Gospels — both in Matthew (16:18; 18:17). This verse is the papal proof-text, the founding claim of the Roman primacy.*

### Matthew 28:18–20 — The Great Commission
*Trans. NRSV*
> "All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me. Go therefore and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, and teaching them to obey everything that I have commanded you. And remember, I am with you always, to the end of the age."

*The trinitarian formula ("Father, Son, and Holy Spirit") appears here for the first time in the Gospel tradition. The Great Commission's geographical universalism ("all nations") closes the Jewish-Christian Gospel with an outward thrust that defined Christian missionary strategy for two millennia.*

*Citations: NRSV; Luz 2001, 2007, Hermeneia; France 2007, NICNT.*

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Roman Syria after +70. The Second Temple has fallen; the priestly establishment is gone; Jewish self-understanding is being rebuilt at Yavneh by the proto-rabbinic movement.
- **Religious milieu.** Matthew's community is **Jewish-Christian** in profound tension with the emerging Yavneh rabbinic establishment. The harsh anti-Pharisaic woes of Matthew 23, the unique anti-Judaic verse 27:25 ("his blood be on us and on our children"), and the redactional details show a community recently or currently in painful split with the local synagogue. **The historical tragedy of Christian antisemitism is rooted here**: Matthew's intra-Jewish polemic was inherited by later Gentile-majority Christianity as anti-Jewish theology.
- **Who was behind it.** Anonymous Greek-writing Jewish-Christian author with scribal training (cf. 13:52 the scribe trained for the kingdom). Papias (~+125) reports "Matthew compiled the sayings (*logia*) in the Hebrew dialect (*Hebraïdi dialektō*), and each interpreted them as he was able" — but our Greek Matthew is *not* a translation from a Semitic original (it uses Greek wordplay and quotes the LXX directly). Papias may refer to a lost sayings collection.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Antioch as the place of composition is the dominant view (Streeter, Brown-Meier, Luz) — based on its Jewish-Christian character, its early reception there (Ignatius of Antioch quotes Matthew abundantly ~+110), and its anti-Pharisaic edge. Continuous canonical transmission; Matthew was the most copied and quoted of the four Gospels through the patristic period.

## Themes and motifs
- [[fulfillment-of-prophecy]] — Matthew's signature **"that it might be fulfilled"** formula (~12 explicit citations: 1:22, 2:15, 2:17, 2:23, 4:14, 8:17, 12:17, 13:35, 21:4, 26:54, 27:9, etc.). Often these are creative or loose citations (Mt 2:23 "He shall be called a Nazorean" has no exact OT source; Mt 27:9 attributes Zechariah to Jeremiah). The procedure is **typological** rather than predictive in the modern sense.
- [[new-moses-typology]] — infancy narrative parallels Moses (slaughter of innocents, flight to Egypt, return); five discourses parallel five books of Torah; mountain settings (5:1 Sermon on the Mount, 17:1 Transfiguration, 28:16 Great Commission) recall Sinai.
- [[ecclesiology]] — Matthew uses *ekklēsia* (church) twice (16:18, 18:17) — the only Gospel to do so. Mt 18 provides the most detailed Synoptic instruction for community discipline.
- [[gentile-mission]] — paradoxically present in a Jewish-Christian Gospel: the infancy magi (2), the centurion (8:5–13), the Canaanite woman (15:21–28), and climactically the Great Commission ("make disciples of all nations", 28:19). The Gospel ends *outward* even as it remains internally Jewish-Christian.
- [[apocalyptic-revelation]] — Matthew expands Mark's "Little Apocalypse" into the great Olivet Discourse (24–25), adding the Parables of the Ten Virgins, the Talents, and the Last Judgment ("Sheep and Goats" — Mt 25:31–46).

- → [[phase-3-006-septuagint]] — *direct-quote*: Matthew quotes the LXX (with some independent renderings).
- → [[phase-3-011-dead-sea-scrolls]] — *parallel-motif*: Matthew's community-discipline language (Mt 18:15–17) bears resemblance to 1QS reproof procedures.
- → Later Christian liturgy and lectionary — *direct-influence*: Matthew is by far the most-used Gospel in patristic and medieval Christian life (the Lord's Prayer of liturgy is Matthew's version, not Luke's).

## Disputes
- **Date.** Most place at +80/+90; a minority hold pre-70 (Robinson, Gundry). The post-70 references in the parable of the wedding feast (22:7 "he sent his troops and destroyed those murderers and burned their city") are strongly suggestive of post-Temple composition.
- **Provenance.** Antioch (majority) vs. Galilee (Overman, Saldarini), Caesarea Maritima (Viviano), or even Phoenicia.
- **"Anti-Judaism" vs. "intra-Jewish".** Older scholarship read Mt 23 / 27:25 as anti-Jewish; recent scholarship (Saldarini, Sim, Repschinski) reframes them as intra-Jewish sectarian polemic comparable to DSS internal critiques. The reception-history of these texts as anti-Judaism is its own tragic chapter.
- **The Sermon on the Mount's relation to Torah.** Mt 5:17 "I have not come to abolish but to fulfill" — programmatic but variously interpreted (intensification? transcendence? completion?).

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges

- **The Sermon on the Mount (Matt 5–7) — the most analyzed 3 consecutive chapters of ethical teaching in Western history, the direct source of nonviolence theology from Tolstoy to Gandhi to King**: Matthew 5–7 is the densest concentration of programmatic ethical teaching in the Gospel tradition, and its downstream is incalculable. Tolstoy's *The Kingdom of God Is Within You* (1894) is an extended commentary on the Sermon on the Mount, which then directly shaped Gandhi's *ahimsa* and nonviolent resistance — Gandhi explicitly cites the Sermon (especially "turn the other cheek" and "love your enemies") as one of the three texts (with the Bhagavad Gita and the Quran) that shaped his philosophy. Gandhi's influence on Martin Luther King Jr. completes the chain. The Beatitudes are recited in schools, printed on war memorials, and cited in every Christian social-ethics manifesto; the Sermon's "love your enemies" is the most demanding ethical commandment in any religious scripture (Gandhi 1927, *An Autobiography*; Luz 2001, Hermeneia vol. 1 pp.175–191; Wink 2003, *Jesus and Nonviolence*, Fortress).

- **Matthew 16:18 "on this rock I will build my church" — the single most consequential 12-word sentence in Western ecclesiastical history, generating papal primacy**: Matthew 16:18 ("you are Peter, and on this rock I will build my church, and the gates of Hades will not prevail against it") is the foundational text of the Roman Catholic papacy — the papal claim to Petrine succession and universal jurisdiction derives from this verse. No other 12-word sentence in the New Testament has generated more ecclesiastical, political, and doctrinal consequence: the medieval papacy's claims over kings (Innocent III, Boniface VIII), the Great Schism of 1054 (the Eastern Church rejecting Roman primacy), the Protestant Reformation's explicit rejection of Petrine-Roman authority, and every subsequent ecumenical negotiation about church unity turn on the interpretation of this one verse. The word *ekklēsia* appears only here and in Mt 18:17 in all four Gospels — making Matthew the only Gospel that mentions "church" at all (Luz 2001, vol. 2 pp.356–375; Meier, J.P. 1979, *The Vision of Matthew*, Paulist; Brown, Donfried & Reumann 1973, *Peter in the NT*, Augsburg).

- **Matthew 27:25 ("his blood be on us and on our children") — the single most historically destructive verse in the Bible, the textual root of two millennia of Christian antisemitism**: Matthew 27:25's crowd declaration — unique to Matthew among the four Gospels — was extracted from its intra-Jewish sectarian context by Gentile-majority Christianity and applied collectively to "the Jews" as a people across all generations. This verse was cited by Church Fathers (John Chrysostom, *Adversus Judaeos*) to justify Jewish suffering; it drove medieval Passion play dramatizations that provoked pogroms; and it was standard in Christian preaching through the 20th century. The 1965 *Nostra Aetate* of Vatican II explicitly repudiated the collective-guilt interpretation. This is the single verse with the most directly traceable causal chain from sacred text to historical mass violence — a cross-tradition edge of catastrophic negative consequence (Luz 2001, vol. 3 pp.499–503; Ruether 1974, *Faith and Fratricide*, Seabury Press; Carroll 2001, *Constantine's Sword*, Houghton Mifflin; Nostra Aetate §4, 1965).

- **The Great Commission (Matt 28:19–20) and the trinitarian formula — the founding mandate for two millennia of Christian mission and the first canonical trinitarian formula**: Matthew 28:19's "baptizing in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit" is the first appearance of the trinitarian formula in the Gospel tradition — earlier NT texts use "Father and Son" or list Spirit separately without the baptismal formula. This verse became the basis of all subsequent Christian baptismal liturgy, the Apostles' Creed's trinitarian structure, and the Nicene Creed's elaboration of the three persons. The "make disciples of all nations" mandate drove the entire Catholic and Protestant missionary movement: Jesuit global missions, the Protestant missionary societies of the 18th–19th centuries, and the modern evangelical global mission movement all cite Matthew 28:18–20 as their founding charter (France 2007, NICNT pp.1109–1115; Mortimer 1988, *Mission in the NT*, Eerdmans; Bosch 1991, *Transforming Mission*, Orbis pp.56–73).
