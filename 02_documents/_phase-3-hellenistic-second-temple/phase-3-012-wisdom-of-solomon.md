# Wisdom of Solomon

## What it is
A 19-chapter book composed in elegant Koine Greek by an anonymous Alexandrian Jewish author. The literary persona is King Solomon (Wis 7–9 echoes 1 Kings 3 explicitly) but the language and philosophical vocabulary are Hellenistic Jewish of the early Roman period. Three movements:
- **Chs. 1–6** — exhortation on righteousness: the wicked who deny providence and persecute the righteous will be confounded at the eschaton; the righteous, though they appear to die, are with God (3:1–9). Strong **soul-immortality** doctrine, expressed in Platonic vocabulary.
- **Chs. 7–9** — Solomon's praise of Wisdom (Sophia): she is "a breath of the power of God... a pure emanation of the glory of the Almighty... a reflection of eternal light, a spotless mirror of the working of God" (7:25–26). She *was* with God in creation and is sent to humans.
- **Chs. 10–19** — Wisdom in salvation history: Sophia guides Adam, Noah, Abraham, Jacob, Joseph, Moses; the Exodus is retold; Egyptian idolatry is critiqued at length (chs. 13–15).

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Egypt now under Roman administration after -30; the Alexandrian Jewish community is large, prosperous, culturally bilingual, and politically vulnerable (the Caligula-era pogrom of +38 looms or has just occurred, depending on dating).
- **Religious milieu.** Hellenistic Judaism at full flourish. The author writes Greek as a native, knows Plato and Stoicism, but is committed to Mosaic Torah and Israelite covenant. Hellenistic Jewish synthesis is *the* defining cultural project of Alexandrian Judaism, and this book is one of its highest literary expressions.
- **Who was behind it.** Anonymous. The author was clearly philosophically literate (Plato's *Phaedo*, Stoic vocabulary) and committed to defending Judaism against assimilation and against Egyptian / Greek pagan religion.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuously preserved in LXX manuscript tradition and Christian biblical canons (Catholic and Orthodox, as deuterocanon). Rejected from the rabbinic canon (post-70 CE) because composed in Greek and after the canonical horizon.

## Themes and motifs
- [[wisdom-personified]] — Sophia in chs. 7–9 is the most elaborate development of personified Wisdom in Hellenistic Judaism, lexically and conceptually pre-figuring the Johannine *Logos* and patristic *Sophia*-Christology.
- [[soul-immortality]] — Wis 3:1 "the souls of the righteous are in the hand of God" and 8:19–20 (the pre-existent soul "entering an undefiled body") draw on Platonic immortality and even soul pre-existence — radical for Hebrew biblical anthropology.
- [[logos-cosmic-reason]] — Wis 18:14–16 personifies the "all-powerful word" (*logos*) leaping from heaven to slay the Egyptian firstborn — a remarkable early text where *logos* functions as a quasi-personal divine agent. This bridges to Philo and John.
- [[critique-of-idolatry]] — chs. 13–15 systematically critique nature-worship, anthropomorphic idolatry, and animal-worship. **Wisdom 13:1–9 underlies Paul's natural-theology argument in Romans 1:18–23.**
- [[righteous-suffering-vindication]] — Wis 2:12–20 describes the wicked plotting against "the righteous one" who calls himself God's son; this passage is one of the strongest pre-Christian articulations of righteous-martyr theology, and it is heavily echoed in the Passion narratives (esp. Matt 27:39–43).

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-3-013-philo-of-alexandria]] — *shared-milieu* / *direct-influence*: both are Alexandrian Hellenistic Jewish texts within decades of each other; both elaborate Sophia/Logos with Middle Platonist and Stoic vocabulary. Philo represents the philosophical-allegorical extension of Wisdom's program.
- → [[phase-3-015-pauline-epistles]] — *direct-influence*: Romans 1:18–32 closely parallels Wisdom 13–15 in structure and phrasing (idolatry → moral collapse → divine wrath). Acknowledged across mainstream commentary.
- → [[phase-3-002-plato-dialogues]] — *direct-influence*: soul-immortality vocabulary (Wis 3:1; 8:19) and the contrast between perishable body and immortal soul reflect direct or mediated Platonism.
- → [[phase-3-005-stoic-foundational-texts]] — *direct-influence*: cosmological vocabulary (Wis 7:22–23 lists 21 attributes of Wisdom in Stoic-style philosophical inventory).
- → [[phase-3-007-sirach]] — *parallel-motif* / *direct-influence*: development of Sirach 24's personified Wisdom.
- → [[phase-3-020-gospel-of-john]] — *parallel-motif*: John 1's Logos prologue resonates with Wisdom 7's Sophia (both *pre-existent, agent of creation, sent to humanity, light, glory of God*). Whether John knows Wisdom directly or both draw on shared Alexandrian-Jewish synagogue discourse is the question.

## Disputes
- **Date.** Winston: Roman period, ~+30/+50; some scholars push back into the late 1st c. BCE. Caligula-era (~+38) tensions in Alexandria attractively explain the polemical tone.
- **Unity of authorship.** Most scholars now accept single authorship despite the three rather different sections; older scholarship (Grimm) posited multiple authors.
- **Dependence vs. shared milieu in NT echoes.** Hays (1989) and Watson argue Pauline dependence on Wisdom 13–15 is real and intentional; others (Sanday-Headlam) settled for shared topoi.
