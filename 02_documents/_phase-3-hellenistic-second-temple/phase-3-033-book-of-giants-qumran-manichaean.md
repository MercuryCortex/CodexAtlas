# The Book of Giants (Qumran Aramaic + Manichaean reception)

## What it is
The **Book of Giants** is an Aramaic Second-Temple-Jewish apocalyptic text (~2nd–1st c. BCE) that expands the **Watchers narrative** of 1 Enoch 6–11 by elaborating on the *Nephilim giants* — the offspring of the angelic Watchers (under [[semyaza]]) and human women. Where 1 Enoch focuses on the *Watchers* (the fallen angels) and their teachings, the Book of Giants centers on their *children* (the giants), naming them individually, recounting their dreams of impending judgment, and narrating the giant **Mahaway**'s mission to consult **Enoch** as the antediluvian sage who can interpret their portentous dreams.

The text was preserved in two principal contexts:

1. **Qumran** — Aramaic fragments (~150 BCE) in caves 1, 2, 4, and 6 (especially 4Q203, 4Q530–533, 6Q8). Reconstructed by Milik 1976 and Stuckenbruck 1997.

2. **Manichaeism** — Mani (216–276 CE), the founder of Manichaeism, **incorporated the Book of Giants among the seven canonical Manichaean scriptures** — making it one of the most consequential Second-Temple Jewish texts in late-antique world-religion canon-formation. The Manichaean *Kawan / Kitāb al-Jabābira* is preserved fragmentarily across Middle Persian, Parthian, Sogdian, Uyghur Turkic, Coptic, Arabic, and Latin Manichaean witnesses (Reeves 1992).

**The Book of Giants is NOT part of the Ethiopian-canonical 1 Enoch** — the Ge'ez Mashafa Henok omits this material. But it is the **parallel and expansion** of the Watchers material that *is* Ethiopian-canonical, and it constitutes the documentary attestation for figures the Watchers tradition presupposes but doesn't elaborate.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Second-Temple Jewish apocalyptic, late-Persian to early-Hellenistic Judea. The text is contemporary with or shortly after the original Book of the Watchers (1 Enoch 1–36) and extends its narrative.
- **Religious milieu.** Watchers-tradition Jewish-sectarian milieu (Qumran-adjacent). Subsequently absorbed into Manichaean canon-formation in the 3rd c. CE.
- **Who was behind it.** Anonymous Jewish-Aramaic Second-Temple composition; Manichaean reception is Mani's deliberate canonical-textual choice.
- **Discovery / transmission.** The Qumran Aramaic fragments were discovered 1947+ and identified as a Book of Giants by Milik 1976. The Manichaean reception was reconstructed across decades of Manichaean-studies work (Henning 1943; Reeves 1992; Stuckenbruck 1997).

## Disputes
- **Date.** Most scholarship places composition between 200 and 50 BCE, with the Watchers tradition (1 Enoch 1–36) as antecedent and the Book of Giants as expansion.
- **Whether the Book of Giants is a single text or a tradition-cluster.** The Qumran fragments and the Manichaean reception both attest to an underlying text; minor variations across witnesses suggest some textual fluidity.
- **The Gilgamesh-Nephilim identification.** Milik 1976 first identified the Aramaic *glgmš* in 4Q530 as Gilgamesh; Stuckenbruck 1997 confirms; Reeves 1992 traces the Manichaean reception which independently preserves the Gilgamesh-Nephilim identification. This is now scholarly consensus and constitutes one of the most striking cross-tradition tracing edges in late-Second-Temple-Jewish apocalyptic literature.
