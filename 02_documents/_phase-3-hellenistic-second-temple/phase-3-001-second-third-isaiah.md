# Second Isaiah and Third Isaiah (Isaiah 40–66)

## What it is
A two-part (or three-part) extension of the book of Isaiah, attached to the 8th-c. BCE Isaiah ben Amoz collection (chs. 1–39) but composed two centuries later. Chapters 40–55 ("Second Isaiah" or Deutero-Isaiah) are oracles of consolation addressed to Judean exiles in Babylon on the eve of and during Cyrus's conquest (~545–539 BCE). They proclaim Yahweh's incomparable sovereignty, mock the Babylonian gods Bel and Nebo (Isa 46), name Cyrus as Yahweh's anointed (māshîaḥ, 45:1), and contain the four "Servant Songs" (42:1–4; 49:1–6; 50:4–9; 52:13–53:12). Chapters 56–66 ("Third Isaiah" or Trito-Isaiah) reflect early Persian-period Yehud, mixing eschatological vision with disputes over Temple, Sabbath, and inclusion of foreigners and eunuchs.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Composition straddles the fall of Neo-Babylonia (539 BCE) and the early Persian Achaemenid restoration of Judah as the province of Yehud. Cyrus's Edict (538 BCE; cf. 2 Chr 36; Ezra 1) authorized Temple rebuilding — and Isa 44:28, 45:1 are uniquely positive references to a foreign king in the Hebrew Bible.
- **Religious milieu.** This is the literary moment Israelite religion crystallizes into strict monotheism. Where pre-exilic Yahwism was henotheistic ("among the gods, Yahweh is greatest"), Deutero-Isaiah declares "I am Yahweh, and there is no other; besides me there is no god" (45:5). The mockery of idol-makers (44:9–20) and the elevation of Cyrus to mashiach status reframes geopolitical defeat as Yahweh's universal plan.
- **Who was behind it.** Anonymous prophets of an Isaiah school, likely Levitical or scribal circles in exile and post-exilic Jerusalem. Modern scholarship since Duhm (1892) distinguishes 40–55 from 56–66 on linguistic, theological, and situational grounds.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous Jewish canonical transmission via the Masoretic tradition; partial Greek translation in the Septuagint (~-200); and the spectacular Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsaᵃ) from Qumran Cave 1 — complete, in scribal Hebrew, dated paleographically to ~-125, giving a witness about a millennium older than the previously oldest Hebrew manuscripts.

## Themes and motifs
- [[monotheism-strict]] — Deutero-Isaiah is the textual hinge: explicit denial of any other god (45:5, 46:9).
- [[messianism]] — Cyrus named "mashiach" (45:1) is the earliest extant non-Davidic application of the term; this anomaly drives later messianic theorizing.
- [[suffering-servant]] — the four Servant Songs, particularly Isa 52:13–53:12, become the most heavily reused Old Testament passages in New Testament soteriology.
- [[creation-by-word]] — Yahweh "stretches out the heavens" by speech (Isa 40:22, 44:24); continuity with Genesis 1 P.
- [[divine-kingship]] — universal kingship, not just over Israel: "all the ends of the earth" (45:22).

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-3-006-septuagint]] — *manuscript-transmission* + *direct-quote*: the LXX Isaiah is the form quoted by all NT writers; LXX Isa 7:14's *parthenos* underlies Matthew 1:23's virgin-birth proof-text.
- → [[phase-3-015-pauline-epistles]] — *direct-quote*: Paul cites Isa 53 (Rom 10:16), Isa 45:23 (Phil 2:10), Isa 52:7 (Rom 10:15). Servant Christology is one of the load-bearing OT structures for Paul.
- → [[phase-3-016-gospel-of-mark]] — *direct-quote*: Mark opens (1:2–3) by quoting Isa 40:3 ("voice in the wilderness"), framing John the Baptist with Deutero-Isaiah's herald.
- → [[phase-3-011-dead-sea-scrolls]] — *manuscript-transmission*: 1QIsaᵃ provides one of the most important pre-Masoretic Hebrew witnesses; its readings are now part of every critical apparatus.
- → Persian / Zoroastrian milieu — *shared-milieu*: the new prominence of monotheism, of cosmic Yahweh-vs-idols polemic, and of a salvific king mashiach coincides with Persian Achaemenid contact. Some scholars (Boyce, Yamauchi) argue indirect Zoroastrian shaping; the case is structural rather than verbal.

## Disputes
- **Composition history.** Two-prophet (Duhm: 1–39, 40–66) vs. three-prophet (1–39, 40–55, 56–66) divisions; some recent scholarship (Sweeney, Stromberg) emphasizes redactional unity over multiple authorship.
- **Date of chs. 56–66.** Westermann places these immediately post-return (~520–500); Blenkinsopp pushes later, into Ezra-Nehemiah era (~450–400).
- **Servant identity.** Corporate Israel (Jewish reading), individual prophet, future messianic figure, or Christ (Christian reading) — all defended in modern scholarship. The Hebrew text supports multiple referents.
