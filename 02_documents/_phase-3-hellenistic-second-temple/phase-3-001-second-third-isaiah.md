# Second Isaiah and Third Isaiah (Isaiah 40–66)

## What it is
A two-part (or three-part) extension of the book of Isaiah, attached to the 8th-c. BCE Isaiah ben Amoz collection (chs. 1–39) but composed two centuries later. Chapters 40–55 ("Second Isaiah" or Deutero-Isaiah) are oracles of consolation addressed to Judean exiles in Babylon on the eve of and during Cyrus's conquest (~545–539 BCE). They proclaim Yahweh's incomparable sovereignty, mock the Babylonian gods Bel and Nebo (Isa 46), name Cyrus as Yahweh's anointed (māshîaḥ, 45:1), and contain the four "Servant Songs" (42:1–4; 49:1–6; 50:4–9; 52:13–53:12). Chapters 56–66 ("Third Isaiah" or Trito-Isaiah) reflect early Persian-period Yehud, mixing eschatological vision with disputes over Temple, Sabbath, and inclusion of foreigners and eunuchs.

## Primary text — selected passages

### Isaiah 40:1–5 — The opening consolation: prepare the way
*Trans. NRSV; source: Blenkinsopp, J. (2002). Isaiah 40–55. Anchor Yale Bible 19A. Yale University Press.*
> Comfort, O comfort my people,
>   says your God.
> Speak tenderly to Jerusalem,
>   and cry to her
> that she has served her term, that her penalty is paid,
>   that she has received from the LORD's hand
>   double for all her sins.
> A voice cries out:
>   "In the wilderness prepare the way of the LORD,
>   make straight in the desert a highway for our God.
> Every valley shall be lifted up,
>   and every mountain and hill be made low;
> the uneven ground shall become level,
>   and the rough places a plain."

*The opening word — *naḥamû* ("comfort") — twice repeated — is the exilic prophetic cry of reversal. The Septuagint and New Testament repunctuate 40:3 (see MASSIVE WIN below), generating the entire John-the-Baptist-as-forerunner tradition.*

### Isaiah 45:1–6 — Cyrus named as Yahweh's "anointed"
*Trans. NRSV*
> Thus says the LORD to his anointed, to Cyrus,
>   whose right hand I have grasped
> to subdue nations before him
>   and strip kings of their robes...
> I call you by your name, I surname you, though you do not know me.
> I am the LORD, and there is no other;
>   besides me there is no god.
> I arm you, though you do not know me,
> so that they may know, from the rising of the sun
>   and from the west, that there is no one besides me;
> I am the LORD, and there is no other.

*45:1 is the only verse in the Hebrew Bible calling a non-Israelite *māshîaḥ* (anointed one). 45:5–6 is the most explicit monotheist declaration in the Hebrew Bible — provoked by the theological problem Cyrus's Messiahship created.*

### Isaiah 52:13–53:5 — Fourth Servant Song: the suffering servant
*Trans. NRSV*
> See, my servant shall prosper;
>   he shall be exalted and lifted up, and shall be very high...
> He was despised and rejected by others;
>   a man of suffering and acquainted with infirmity;
> and as one from whom others hide their faces
>   he was despised, and we held him of no account.
> Surely he has borne our infirmities
>   and carried our diseases;
> yet we accounted him stricken,
>   struck down by God, and afflicted.
> But he was wounded for our transgressions,
>   crushed for our iniquities;
> upon him was the punishment that made us whole,
>   and by his bruises we are healed.

*Isa 52:13–53:12 is the most-quoted Old Testament passage in the New Testament. The servant's vicarious suffering — undeserved punishment for the guilt of others, restoration through death — is the template that first-century Christians applied to the death of Jesus within months or years of the crucifixion.*

*Citations: Blenkinsopp 2002, AYB 19A; Blenkinsopp 2003, AYB 19B; NRSV.*

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Composition straddles the fall of Neo-Babylonia (539 BCE) and the early Persian Achaemenid restoration of Judah as the province of Yehud. Cyrus's Edict (538 BCE; cf. 2 Chr 36; Ezra 1) authorized Temple rebuilding — and Isa 44:28, 45:1 are uniquely positive references to a foreign king in the Hebrew Bible.
- **Religious milieu.** This is the literary moment Israelite religion crystallizes into strict monotheism. Where pre-exilic Yahwism was henotheistic ("among the gods, Yahweh is greatest"), Deutero-Isaiah declares "I am Yahweh, and there is no other; besides me there is no god" (45:5). The mockery of idol-makers (44:9–20) and the elevation of Cyrus to mashiach status reframes geopolitical defeat as Yahweh's universal plan.
- **Who was behind it.** Anonymous prophets of an Isaiah school, likely Levitical or scribal circles in exile and post-exilic Jerusalem. Modern scholarship since Duhm (1892) distinguishes 40–55 from 56–66 on linguistic, theological, and situational grounds.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous Jewish canonical transmission via the Masoretic tradition; partial Greek translation in the Septuagint (~-200); and the spectacular Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsaᵃ) from Qumran Cave 1 — complete, in scribal Hebrew, dated paleographically to ~-125, giving a witness about a millennium older than the previously oldest Hebrew manuscripts.

## Themes and motifs
- [[monotheism-strict]] — Deutero-Isaiah is the textual hinge: explicit denial of any other god (45:5, 46:9).
- [[messianism]] — Cyrus named "mashiach" (45:1) is the earliest extant non-Davidic application of the term; this anomaly drives later messianic theorizing.
- [[suffering-servant]] — the four Servant Songs, particularly Isa 52:13–53:12, become the most heavily reused Old Testament passages in New Testament soteriology.
- [[creation-by-word]] — Yahweh "stretches out the heavens" by speech (Isa 40:22, 44:24); continuity with Genesis 1 P.
- [[divine-kingship]] — universal kingship, not just over Israel: "all the ends of the earth" (45:22).

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-3-006-septuagint]] — *manuscript-transmission* + *direct-quote*: the LXX Isaiah is the form quoted by all NT writers; LXX Isa 7:14's *parthenos* underlies Matthew 1:23's virgin-birth proof-text.
- → [[phase-3-015-pauline-epistles]] — *direct-quote*: Paul cites Isa 53 (Rom 10:16), Isa 45:23 (Phil 2:10), Isa 52:7 (Rom 10:15). Servant Christology is one of the load-bearing OT structures for Paul.
- → [[phase-3-016-gospel-of-mark]] — *direct-quote*: Mark opens (1:2–3) by quoting Isa 40:3 ("voice in the wilderness"), framing John the Baptist with Deutero-Isaiah's herald.
- → [[phase-3-011-dead-sea-scrolls]] — *manuscript-transmission*: 1QIsaᵃ provides one of the most important pre-Masoretic Hebrew witnesses; its readings are now part of every critical apparatus.
- → Persian / Zoroastrian milieu — *shared-milieu*: the new prominence of monotheism, of cosmic Yahweh-vs-idols polemic, and of a salvific king mashiach coincides with Persian Achaemenid contact. Some scholars (Boyce, Yamauchi) argue indirect Zoroastrian shaping; the case is structural rather than verbal.

## Disputes
- **Composition history.** Two-prophet (Duhm: 1–39, 40–66) vs. three-prophet (1–39, 40–55, 56–66) divisions; some recent scholarship (Sweeney, Stromberg) emphasizes redactional unity over multiple authorship.
- **Date of chs. 56–66.** Westermann places these immediately post-return (~520–500); Blenkinsopp pushes later, into Ezra-Nehemiah era (~450–400).
- **Servant identity.** Corporate Israel (Jewish reading), individual prophet, future messianic figure, or Christ (Christian reading) — all defended in modern scholarship. The Hebrew text supports multiple referents.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges

- **Isaiah 52:13–53:12 (Fourth Servant Song) — the single most-cited Old Testament passage in New Testament Passion theology**: Isa 53 is the dominant proof-text for NT Passion Christology. "He was wounded for our transgressions, crushed for our iniquities" (53:5) is quoted or alluded to in Mark 10:45 ("give his life as a ransom for many"), Matt 8:17, Luke 22:37, John 12:38, Acts 8:32–33, Rom 10:16, 1 Pet 2:22–25, and almost certainly in the pre-Pauline formula "Christ died for our sins in accordance with the scriptures" (1 Cor 15:3 — the earliest datable Christian creedal statement, Paul citing tradition he received). No other single passage of the Hebrew Bible has done more work in New Testament theology. The cross-tradition wiring is clean: an anonymous exilic poet writing ~545 BCE about a collective Israel-as-servant created the template that within five centuries the first Christians applied to the crucifixion — making Isaiah 53 the most productive single text-to-text connection in the entire Hebrew Bible–New Testament relationship (Janowski & Stuhlmacher 2004, *The Suffering Servant*, Eerdmans; Hengel 1981, *The Atonement*, SCM pp.57–75; Childs 2001, *Isaiah*, Westminster Knox pp.410–434).

- **Isaiah 45:1 naming Cyrus "mashiach" — the literary moment Israelite henotheism becomes strict monotheism**: Isa 45:1 is the only verse in the entire Hebrew Bible calling a non-Israelite *māshîaḥ* (anointed one). This universalist claim — a pagan Persian king is Yahweh's chosen instrument — created a theological problem: if Yahweh controls Cyrus, Yahweh must be the only real deity. The problem generated the answer: "I am the LORD, and there is no other; besides me there is no god" (45:5) — the most explicit strict-monotheist declaration in the Hebrew Bible. Deutero-Isaiah is the literary hinge from henotheism to monotheism, and Cyrus is the irony that drove the prophet to the formulation. This single geopolitical event (Cyrus's 539 BCE conquest of Babylon, authorized by Yahweh who then authorized by the text) produced the theological innovation that underwrites all three Abrahamic monotheisms (Blenkinsopp 2002, AYB 19A pp.245–260; Smith, M.S. 2002, *The Early History of God*, Eerdmans pp.182–196; Morton Smith 1963, "II Isaiah and the Persians," *JAOS* 83/4).

- **Isaiah 40:3 — the most consequential mis-punctuated verse in religious history, generating the John-the-Baptist tradition**: Isa 40:3 in Hebrew punctuates as "A voice cries out: In the wilderness prepare the way of the LORD" — meaning the road-preparation happens in the wilderness. The LXX and all four Gospels (Mark 1:2–3; Matt 3:3; Luke 3:4; John 1:23) repunctuate as "A voice of one crying in the wilderness: Prepare the way of the LORD" — making the *person* (the voice) in the wilderness, creating a forerunner-in-the-desert prophecy. This punctuation shift created the entire tradition of John the Baptist as the prophesied forerunner of the Messiah and structures all four Gospels' opening sequences. An anonymous exilic poet's comma placement, as transmitted through Greek translation, drove the Christological claim that John fulfills Isaianic prophecy (Stendahl 1954, *The School of St. Matthew*, Gleerup pp.47–54; Blenkinsopp 2002 pp.181–183; Evans 1987, *Novum Testamentum* 29/2).

- **Isaiah 61:1–2 — the text Jesus reads at Nazareth, the programmatic self-identification that defines the entire Lukan Christology**: Luke 4:16–21 presents Jesus reading Isa 61:1–2 in the Nazareth synagogue and declaring "Today this scripture has been fulfilled in your hearing" — the most explicit programmatic self-identification statement in the synoptic tradition. Isaiah 61 ("The Spirit of the Lord GOD is upon me; because the LORD has anointed me to bring good news to the poor... to proclaim the year of the LORD's favor") becomes the mission-statement for Luke-Acts' entire portrait of Jesus as the anointed jubilee-bringer. Luke's social-justice program — good news to the poor, release of captives, recovery of sight to the blind — is *Deutero/Trito-Isaianic* eschatology applied to Jesus. No other Hebrew Bible text so directly provides the interpretive key to a complete Gospel (Blenkinsopp 2003, AYB 19B pp.225–233; Fitzmyer 1981, *The Gospel According to Luke*, AYB 28, pp.526–535; Sanders 1985, *Jesus and Judaism*, Fortress pp.122–128).
