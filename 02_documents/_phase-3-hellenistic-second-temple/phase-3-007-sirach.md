# Sirach

## What it is
A book of wisdom instruction in 51 chapters composed in Hebrew around 190–175 BCE by Yeshua ben Eleazar ben Sira, a Jerusalemite sage and scribe. The grandson translated it into Greek in Alexandria a generation later (after -132, dated by the translator's preface mentioning his arrival "in the thirty-eighth year of Euergetes"). The book combines (a) classical Hebrew proverb-style wisdom on family, friendship, prudence, work, and conduct; (b) hymnic passages on the personified figure of Wisdom (24:1–22, identifying Wisdom with the Torah); (c) the famous "Praise of the Ancestors" (chs. 44–50), surveying biblical heroes from Enoch to the contemporary high priest Simon II.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Judea is a Seleucid province; the Tobiad-Oniad rivalry that will erupt under Antiochus IV is already simmering. Ben Sira writes within Jerusalem's priestly-scribal establishment under High Priest Simon II ("Simon the Just"), whom he celebrates in chapter 50 in shining detail — almost certainly from personal observation of him officiating in the Temple.
- **Religious milieu.** Pre-sectarian Second Temple Judaism. Ben Sira represents *establishment* wisdom Judaism: Torah-centered, Temple-loyal, sapiential. He polemicizes against an unnamed group some scholars identify with proto-Enochic apocalypticists (Sir 3:21–24 against "speculating about hidden things") — possibly a counter-current to the contemporary 1 Enoch.
- **Who was behind it.** Ben Sira himself, named in 50:27 — one of the few Second Temple Jewish authors who *signs* his work, as Egyptian and Greek sages did but Hebrew biblical writers generally did not.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Original Hebrew vanished from circulation in Late Antiquity, surviving in Christian translation only (Greek in LXX, Latin in Vulgate). The 1896 Cairo Genizah discovery (Solomon Schechter) recovered the largest run of Hebrew fragments; Qumran and Masada added more — now we have about two-thirds of the original Hebrew text.

## Themes and motifs
- [[wisdom-personified]] — Sir 24 has Wisdom (Hokhmah / Sophia) speak in the first person: "I came forth from the mouth of the Most High... I took root in an honored people, in the portion of the Lord, his heritage." She is identified with the Torah of Moses. This *personified Wisdom* is a critical bridge: from Proverbs 8 → Sirach 24 → Wisdom of Solomon → Philo's Logos → Johannine Christology.
- [[fear-of-the-lord]] — the formal program ("the fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom", inherited from Proverbs) governs the whole.
- [[torah-and-wisdom-identified]] — Sir 24:23 explicitly equates Wisdom with the "book of the covenant of the Most High God, the law that Moses commanded us." This identification becomes axiomatic for Hellenistic and rabbinic Judaism.
- [[honor-of-fathers]] — Praise of the Ancestors (chs. 44–50) is a literary precedent for Hebrews 11 and influences Christian "lives of the saints" hagiography.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-3-012-wisdom-of-solomon]] — *parallel-motif* / *direct-influence*: Wisdom of Solomon expands Ben Sira's personified Wisdom (Sophia) with Stoic and Platonist categories.
- → [[phase-3-006-septuagint]] — *manuscript-transmission*: Sirach is part of the LXX canon (and thus the Christian deuterocanon) though its Hebrew form was eventually excluded from the Jewish canon at Yavneh.
- → [[phase-3-004-1-enoch]] — *polemic-against* (probable): Sir 3:21–24's prohibition of speculation about "what is too hard for you" reads as a critique of Enochic apocalyptic curiosity. Argued by Argall (1995), Boccaccini.
- → New Testament epistle of James — *parallel-motif*: James's practical wisdom in tone and structure closely echoes Sirach (and Proverbs).
- → Synoptic "wisdom-of-Jesus" sayings — *parallel-motif*: the proverbial-form material common to Q and Sirach reflects shared Second Temple wisdom culture, not necessarily literary dependence.

## Disputes
- **Canonical status.** Accepted as scripture in the Septuagint and by Catholic and Orthodox churches (as *Ecclesiasticus*); excluded from the rabbinic Hebrew canon by the late 1st c. CE; quoted as "Ben Sira" in rabbinic sources but with non-scriptural status.
- **Critique-of-Enochic-Judaism reading.** Argall's reading that Sirach polemicizes against Enochic apocalyptic wisdom is influential but contested.
- **Original Hebrew text.** Reconstruction from Genizah + Qumran + Masada is incomplete; ~30% of the Hebrew is still lost, and the Greek/Syriac/Latin versions show some doctrinal expansion (especially Sir 24 in the Greek).
