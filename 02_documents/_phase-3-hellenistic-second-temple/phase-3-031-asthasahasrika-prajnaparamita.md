# Aṣṭasāhasrikā Prajñāpāramitā

## What it is
The Aṣṭasāhasrikā Prajñāpāramitā ("Perfection of Wisdom in 8,000 Lines") is the foundational sūtra of the Prajñāpāramitā corpus and the **earliest extant Mahāyāna sūtra** in any tradition (Conze 1973; Williams 2008; Schopen 2005). It is structured as a teaching delivered by the Buddha at Gṛdhrakūṭa (Vulture Peak) and conveyed through the disciple Subhūti's exposition to Śāriputra and the bodhisattva assembly. The sūtra articulates the central Mahāyāna doctrines of *prajñāpāramitā* (the perfection of wisdom), *śūnyatā* (emptiness), and the *bodhisattva* ideal (the aspirant who delays personal nirvāṇa for the benefit of all beings). Its philosophical project: to systematically negate the *dharmas* of Sarvāstivāda Abhidharma — the irreducible mental and physical event-units that the early-Buddhist analytical tradition treated as ultimately real — by demonstrating that they too lack *svabhāva* (inherent existence). This is the seed-position that [[nagarjuna]] (~+200) develops philosophically in the *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā*, and that the later Prajñāpāramitā sūtras (*Pañcaviṃśatisāhasrikā* 25,000-line; *Śatasāhasrikā* 100,000-line; *Vajracchedikā* Diamond Sūtra; *Hṛdaya* Heart Sūtra) compress, expand, and ritualize.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** The Indo-Greek and early Kushan period of northwest India (~-100 to +100); the Bactrian-Gandhāran cosmopolitan trade-zone in which Mahāyāna Buddhism is generally now thought to have emerged (Schopen 2005 contested-but-leading view).
- **Religious milieu.** A pluralist Buddhist landscape: the early sectarian schools (Sarvāstivāda, Mahāsāṅghika, Vibhajjavāda etc.) were already established with their distinct Abhidharma traditions. Mahāyāna emerges within this landscape — initially probably as a textual-meditative movement within existing schools, only later as a distinct institutional formation. The Aṣṭasāhasrikā is one of the foundational textual moments of this movement.
- **Who was behind it.** Anonymous Mahāyāna redactors — probably within the Mahāsāṅghika tradition (Williams 2008; Lamotte) or within mixed Mahāsāṅghika-Sarvāstivāda milieux (Schopen). The text claims direct Buddha-authority (Buddhavacana); modern critical scholarship treats it as a post-Buddha composition working with pre-existing teaching-material on no-self and emptiness.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous transmission via Sanskrit Buddhist Nepal and via translations to Chinese (from Lokakṣema 179 CE onward) and Tibetan. Gāndhārī fragments recovered in the 1990s-2010s (Schøyen Collection; the Split Manuscript) push the physical-witness chronology back to the 1st-2nd c. CE. The text was first translated into English (in significant part) by Edward Conze in the mid-20th c.

## Themes and motifs
- [[emptiness-sunyata]] — THE central doctrine. The Aṣṭasāhasrikā states it as: "All dharmas have emptiness for their core, emptiness for their character, emptiness for their nature... When this is the case, what then can be born or pass away?" The negation extends to the bodhisattva, the *prajñāpāramitā* itself, the path, and the goal.
- [[bodhisattva-vow]] — the operational soteriological frame: not personal liberation (the arahant-ideal of earlier Buddhism) but the commitment to liberate all sentient beings.
- [[anatman-no-self]] — the early-Buddhist anātman doctrine is preserved but extended: not only the self is empty, but so are all dharmas, all paths, all attainments.
- [[pratitya-samutpada]] — emptiness is reformulated as the universality of dependent origination; nothing arises independently, therefore nothing has *svabhāva*.
- ***Prajñāpāramitā* personified** — the perfection of wisdom is figured as the "Mother of All Buddhas" (see [[prajnaparamita-goddess]]); the sūtra's later reception ritualizes this personification.
- **Skillful means** (*upāya*) — the bodhisattva uses methods adapted to each being's capacity; this is the doctrinal seed of the Lotus Sūtra's full *upāya-kauśalya* elaboration.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-5-002-heart-sutra]] — *redaction-of* (per Nattier 1992): the Chinese Heart Sūtra is a compression assembled from passages of the Chinese Pañcaviṃśatisāhasrikā, which is in turn an expansion of the Aṣṭasāhasrikā.
- → [[phase-5-002b-diamond-sutra]] — *parallel-form*: the Vajracchedikā is the second great compressed Prajñāpāramitā text; it presents the emptiness-doctrine through a different rhetorical strategy (paradox-form rather than systematic negation).
- → [[nagarjuna]]'s *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* — *commentary-on*: Nāgārjuna's MMK is the philosophical systematization of the Aṣṭasāhasrikā's doctrinal position.
- → [[phase-4-101-lotus-sutra]] — *parallel-form*: same Mahāyāna sūtra-genre; Lotus emphasizes Buddhology and skilful means, Aṣṭasāhasrikā emphasizes wisdom-and-emptiness.
- → [[phase-4-102-avatamsaka-sutra]] — *parallel-form*: same Mahāyāna sūtra-genre; Avatamsaka emphasizes cosmic interpenetration and the bodhisattva path.
- → **Lokakṣema's Chinese translation 179 CE** — *manuscript-transmission*: the Aṣṭasāhasrikā is one of the earliest texts in the Chinese Buddhist canon; Lokakṣema's translation establishes the Chinese terminology of Mahāyāna emptiness for centuries to come.

## Disputes
- **Date of composition**: Conze placed the core at ~-100 to +100; Williams 2008 accepts this range; Schopen 2005 argues for a slightly later date (~+1 to +100). The 179 CE Chinese translation by Lokakṣema is the *terminus ante quem* — the text must exist by ~+150 in a form recognizably close to the surviving Sanskrit.
- **Region of origin**: northwest India (Gandhāra) is the modern majority view (Schopen, Conze); Lamotte's older southeast-India hypothesis is now less favored.
- **Mahāyāna emergence**: whether Mahāyāna emerged as an institutional movement separate from the earlier Buddhist schools (Hirakawa 1963; older view) or as a textual-meditative tendency within existing schools (Schopen 2005; current consensus). The Aṣṭasāhasrikā's institutional setting depends on this answer.
- **Authorship of the *Ratnaguṇasaṃcayagāthā*** (the verse summary appended to the Aṣṭasāhasrikā): possibly older than the prose Aṣṭasāhasrikā itself (Conze 1973); some scholars treat it as the original kernel.
