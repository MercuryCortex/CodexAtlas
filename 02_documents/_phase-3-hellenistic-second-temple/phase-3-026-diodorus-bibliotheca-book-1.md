# Bibliotheca Historica, Book 1 (Egypt)

## What it is
Book 1 of [[diodorus-siculus]]'s *Bibliotheca Historica* (composed c. -60 to -30) is a 98-chapter Greek-language ethnography and dynastic history of Egypt. It is the **principal late-Hellenistic / Augustan-era synthesis of Greek knowledge about Egypt** — sitting chronologically between [[herodotus]] (~-440) and [[plutarch]] (~+115) in the transmission chain by which Egyptian religion entered Greek-language literary culture. Book 1 has two halves: chs. 1–41 are mythological / cosmological / theological prehistory (creation, the first gods reigning on earth, the cult-foundations of Osiris and Isis); chs. 42–98 are dynastic history from Menes through the Persian conquest plus extended ethnography (Nile flooding, embalming, laws, civic structure, fauna, animal cults).

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Late Roman Republic / early Augustan period. Diodorus visited Egypt under Ptolemy XII Auletes (c. -59 to -56); the *Bibliotheca* is completed under Octavian/Augustus. Egypt is on the verge of becoming a Roman province (annexed -30 after Actium). Diodorus is a Greek-speaking provincial of the new Roman world synthesizing the Greek intellectual inheritance for Roman readers.
- **Religious milieu.** Isis-cult is well-established across the Mediterranean; the Serapis cult is in mature flourishing; the earliest Hermetic texts are within a century of composition; Roman religion is being reorganized under Augustan ideology. Egyptian temple-religion remains operative but is increasingly mediated to outsiders through Greek-language priestly intellectuals (Manetho's tradition, the Petosiris-and-Nechepso astrological corpus).
- **Who was behind it.** Diodorus personally, drawing on (1) **Hecataeus of Abdera** — a Greek courtier under Ptolemy I, c. -300, whose lost *Aegyptiaca* is Diodorus's principal Egyptian source; (2) **[[manetho]]** for the dynastic chronology; (3) **[[herodotus]]** Book 2, which Diodorus knows and sometimes corrects; (4) probable autopsy from his own Egyptian travel.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous Byzantine manuscript tradition. Read by Photius (9th c., *Bibliotheca* cod. 70). Earliest extant Greek mss c. +900. First printed Greek edition by Henri Estienne (Stephanus) in Geneva 1559. Foundational for Renaissance European Egyptology alongside Herodotus and Plutarch.

## Themes and motifs
- [[syncretism-egyptian-greek]] — Diodorus systematically deploys *interpretatio graeca*: Osiris = Dionysus (1.11, 1.25, 1.96 — Osiris's worldwide civilizing mission is identified with Dionysus's), Isis = Demeter (1.13, 1.96), Horus = Apollo, Typhon = Set, Hermes = Thoth (the inventor of writing, language, religious ritual; 1.16, 1.96), Hephaestus = Ptah (1.13). The chain Herodotus → Diodorus → Plutarch is the **canonical Greek-Egyptian theological equation chain**.
- [[divine-kingship]] — Diodorus 1.13 begins with **gods reigning on earth as kings**: Helios (Ra) first, then Kronos and Rhea, then Zeus and Hera (these are reigning monarchs of the human-world, not Olympians), Osiris, Isis, Horus, etc. The Manethonian dynastic schema is partly visible underneath but Hellenized.
- [[apologetic-historiography]] / **euhemerism** — Diodorus deploys an explicit **euhemerist** reading: the gods are deified mortal benefactors (Osiris invented agriculture and viticulture; Isis civilized humanity; Hermes invented language, writing, music). This is the locus classicus of euhemerism applied to Egyptian theology, and it flows directly into Christian apologetic anti-paganism (Lactantius cites Diodorus repeatedly for this purpose).
- [[civic-religion]] — Diodorus reads Egyptian theology as **civilizing technology**: religion as the means by which culture and law were imposed on pre-civilized humanity. This frame is Hellenistic-philosophical but applied to Egyptian material.

Direct genealogical link from this Diodoran euhemerism to the Hermetic tradition.

## Disputes
- **Hecataeus-of-Abdera versus Diodorus.** How much of Book 1 is Diodorus and how much is Hecataeus? Murray (1970) and Burton (1972) reconstruct Hecataeus's lost text from Diodorus and isolate the Diodoran additions (mostly moralizing reflections and connectives). Sacks (1990) defends greater Diodoran originality.
- **Reliability on Egyptian temple ritual.** Burton 1972 treats the cult-ritual material as substantially reliable Ptolemaic-era Egyptian religion as filtered through Greek priestly informants. The mythological narratives are more synthetic and Hellenizing.
- **Euhemerism's force.** Was Diodorus a doctrinaire euhemerist (the gods are *only* deified benefactors) or a softer one (the gods include such benefactors among other divine principles)? The text reads inconsistently — Sacks 1990 reads him as moderate.
