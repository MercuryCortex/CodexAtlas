# Nergal and Ereshkigal

## What it is
A myth explaining how the war-and-plague god Nergal came to rule the land of the dead. When the upper gods hold a banquet that Ereshkigal, queen of the netherworld, cannot attend, she sends her vizier Namtar in her place; Nergal fails to show him due honor and is summoned below to answer for it. Warned not to eat, drink, or sit in the underworld, Nergal nonetheless ends as Ereshkigal's lover and consort — and so becomes joint sovereign of the dead, binding the upper and lower realms in a marriage.

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** The fixed Mesopotamian cosmos of upper gods and an unyielding netherworld; the myth negotiates a passage between them.
- **Transmission.** A short version was copied at Amarna in Egypt (14th c. BCE) — evidence of Mesopotamian myth circulating in international scribal culture; a longer version comes from first-millennium Assyria.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Cuneiform tablets across a millennium and a thousand miles.

## Themes and motifs
- [[underworld-descent]] — the perilous journey below and its taboos (no eating, drinking, or sitting).
- Sacred marriage — the union that makes Nergal king of the dead.
- The netherworld's rules — the food-of-the-dead motif shared with Persephone's pomegranate.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[document-descent-of-ishtar]] / [[phase-1-017-descent-of-inanna]] — *parallel-motif*: the other great Mesopotamian underworld narratives, sharing the gates, taboos, and the dread queen Ereshkigal.
- → Greek Persephone / Hades — *parallel-motif*: the marriage that joins a deity to the realm of the dead, and the food-taboo that binds the visitor below.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **The food-of-the-dead taboo, from Mesopotamia to Persephone.** Nergal is warned that if he eats the bread, drinks the water, or sits on the throne of the netherworld he can never return — the same logic by which Persephone's eating of pomegranate seeds in Hades binds her to spend part of every year below. This shared "you are what you eat in the land of the dead" motif links Mesopotamian and Greek underworld theology, and the myth's appearance at Amarna shows how such ideas traveled the ancient world's diplomatic and scribal networks (Dalley 2000 pp.163–181; Ponchia & Luukko 2013).
