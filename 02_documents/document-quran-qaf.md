# Sūrat Qāf (Quran 50)

## What it is
A vivid Meccan sura on death, resurrection, and the closeness of God, which the Prophet loved enough to recite at the Friday sermon and the festival prayers. It answers the Meccans' incredulity that they could be raised once they are "dust" by pointing to the dead earth quickened by rain, then turns inward with one of the Qur'an's most quoted lines: God is nearer to a person than his own jugular vein, and two angels sit recording every word. It ends at the deathbed and the trumpet, "the Day of coming forth."

## Primary text

### Quran 50:16
*Trans. Abdel Haleem (2004)*
> We created man — We know what his soul whispers to him: We are closer to him than his jugular vein.

### Quran 50:17–18
*Trans. Abdel Haleem (2004)*
> With two receptors set to record, one on his right side and one on his left: he does not utter a single word without an ever-present watcher.

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** Meccan; the sura concentrates on the resurrection the Meccans most fiercely denied.
- **Tradition.** "Nearer than the jugular vein" became a touchstone verse for Sufi reflection on God's immanence.
- **Discovery / transmission.** The two recording angels (Kirāman Kātibīn) of 50:17 are a fixed feature of Islamic belief about the moral ledger.

## Themes and motifs
- God's nearness — closer than the vein in the neck.
- The recording angels — every word watched and written.
- Resurrection from dust — the revived earth as proof.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **"Nearer than the jugular vein" — the God who is closer than the self.** Sūrat Qāf states divine immanence as starkly as any scripture: God knows what the soul *whispers* and is nearer to a person than his own jugular vein (50:16). The same paradox — a transcendent Creator who is yet more intimate to me than I am to myself — is the burden of Psalm 139: God discerns my thoughts from far off, hems me in behind and before, and there is nowhere I could flee from His presence ("if I ascend to heaven, You are there; if I make my bed in Sheol, You are there"). Augustine will render it *interior intimo meo*, "more inward than my innermost self." The Qur'an pairs this nearness with a moral mechanism the Psalm lacks — the two angels at the shoulders writing every word (50:17) — fusing the comfort of God's closeness with the sobriety of total accountability, a combination that Sufi writers from al-Ḥallāj onward would mine for the doctrine that the heart is the throne on which the near God is found (Reynolds 2010 pp.241–249; McAuliffe 2006 pp.150–161; Nasr 2015 pp.1271–1281).
