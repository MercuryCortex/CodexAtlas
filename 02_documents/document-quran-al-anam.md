# Sūrat al-Anʿām (Quran 6 — Cattle)

## What it is
A long, sustained Meccan argument for the oneness of God, traditionally said to have descended all at once. Its most memorable passage is the young Abraham reasoning his way to the Creator: he sees a star and says "this is my Lord," but when it sets he turns away; then the moon, then the sun, "the greatest" — and as each in turn goes down he renounces it, for "I do not love things that set," and turns his face to the One who made the heavens and earth. The sura also dismantles the Meccans' arbitrary livestock-taboos (hence its title) and rejects every attempt to give God partners, daughters, or sons.

## Primary text

### Quran 6:76–79
*Trans. Abdel Haleem (2004)*
> When the night grew dark over him he saw a star and said, "This is my Lord," but when it set, he said, "I do not like things that set." And when he saw the moon rising he said, "This is my Lord," but when it too set, he said… "I have turned my face as a true believer towards Him who created the heavens and the earth. I am not one of the idolaters."

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** Meccan; the sura answers a polytheism that worshipped celestial bodies by showing their very setting disqualifies them.
- **Tradition.** Abraham here is the *ḥanīf*, the primordial monotheist who finds God by reason before any revealed law.
- **Discovery / transmission.** The star-moon-sun argument elaborates a Jewish legend of the young Abraham reasoning past the idols of his father.

## Themes and motifs
- The God beyond what sets — transience disproves divinity.
- Abraham the ḥanīf — monotheism reached by reason.
- No partners, no offspring — the stripping-away of all that is ascribed to God.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **Abraham finds God by reason — the philosopher patriarch.** Genesis simply has God call Abraham out of Ur (Genesis 12:1); it never says *why* Abraham was ready to be called. Jewish tradition filled that silence with a famous legend — the boy Abraham smashing his father Terah's idols, or reasoning that a sun which sets and a moon which wanes cannot be the true god (Genesis Rabbah 38; the *Apocalypse of Abraham*; later Jubilees). Sūrat al-Anʿām inherits precisely this reasoning and makes it scripture: Abraham tests the star, the moon, and the sun as candidates for Lordship and rejects each at its setting — *"I do not love things that set"* — arriving at the Creator who does not set. The episode turns Abraham into the first *natural theologian*, who reaches monotheism by argument before any prophet instructs him, and it became a cornerstone for Islamic philosophy's confidence that reason and revelation reach the same God (al-Fārābī, Ibn Sīnā). The same patriarch is thus the father of faith for Judaism, the father of reasoned monotheism for Islam's falāsifa, and the model of justifying faith for Paul (Romans 4) (Reynolds 2010 pp.39–48; Tottoli 2002 pp.18–29; Nasr 2015 pp.358–367).
