# The Apocalypse of Adam

## What it is
A Sethian revelation cast as the dying Adam's secret testament to his son Seth — passing on the saving knowledge that was lost at the fall and revealing the future. It retells Genesis from a Gnostic angle: the jealous lower creator tries to destroy Seth's enlightened race by flood and by fire (Sodom), but they are repeatedly rescued by higher powers; and it foretells the descent of a redeeming "Illuminator," followed by thirteen kingdoms each giving a false, mythological account of his origin — capped by the true gnostic understanding. Strikingly, it contains no unmistakably Christian feature, which has made it a centerpiece of debate over Gnosticism's origins.

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** Sethian Gnosticism reworking Jewish scripture and apocalyptic; the lower god of Genesis is the ignorant adversary.
- **The origins question.** Its lack of clear Christian markers fuels arguments that Sethian Gnosticism had non-Christian (perhaps Jewish-apocalyptic) roots.
- **Discovery / transmission.** A single Nag Hammadi copy.

## Themes and motifs
- Hidden knowledge across generations — Adam to Seth.
- The hostile creator — repeated attempts to destroy the gnostic race (flood, fire).
- The descending Illuminator — and the thirteen false versus the one true account of his coming.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **The best candidate for a non-Christian Gnosticism.** Because the Apocalypse of Adam frames its redeemer-myth — a descending Illuminator persecuted and misunderstood — entirely in the idiom of reworked Jewish scripture, with no certain reference to Jesus or the cross, many scholars read it as evidence that the Gnostic redeemer-pattern could arise from Jewish apocalyptic independently of (or prior to) Christianity. That makes this short text a key exhibit in the century-long argument over whether Gnosticism was a Christian heresy or an older, parallel current that Christianity later absorbed and opposed (Layton 1987 pp.52–64; Meyer 2007 pp.343–356).
