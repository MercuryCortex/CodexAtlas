# Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ (Quran 112 — Sincerity / The Oneness)

## What it is
A four-verse sura, among the shortest in the Quran, that states the absolute oneness of God with such concentration that tradition holds reciting it equals a third of the whole Quran. Often called Sūrat al-Tawḥīd ("the sura of divine unity"), it answers — in the form of a divine command to "Say" — the question of who and what God is, and in doing so explicitly denies that God has any offspring.

## Primary text

### Quran 112:1–4
*Trans. Abdel Haleem (2004)*
> Say, "He is God the One, God the eternal. He begot no one nor was He begotten. No one is comparable to Him."

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** Meccan Islam defining its God against both Arabian polytheism (no partners) and Christianity (no son).
- **Theology.** The verse "He begot no one nor was He begotten" (lam yalid wa-lam yūlad) is a precise, deliberate negation of the Christian doctrine of the eternally-begotten Son.
- **Discovery / transmission.** One of the most-memorized and most-recited passages in the Muslim world.

## Themes and motifs
- [[divine-unity]] — God as al-Aḥad, the absolutely One.
- Incomparability — "no one is comparable to Him."
- Negation of offspring — the anti-Trinitarian core.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **The Islamic Shema — and the Quran's sharpest answer to the Trinity.** Al-Ikhlāṣ functions in Islam as the Jewish Shemaʿ ("Hear, O Israel: the LORD our God, the LORD is one," Deut 6:4) functions in Judaism: a terse, supreme statement of divine oneness, recited constantly as the kernel of the faith and even inscribed on the Dome of the Rock (691 CE) — the earliest dated Quranic inscription. But its third verse, "He begot no one nor was He begotten," sharpens the shared monotheism into a polemic: it is a direct, point-by-point negation of the Nicene Creed's "begotten, not made," rejecting the divine sonship of Christ. In four lines the sura both joins the Abrahamic confession of one God and draws the boundary that separates Islam from Christianity (Reynolds 2010 pp.231–235; Nasr 2015 pp.1577–1580).
