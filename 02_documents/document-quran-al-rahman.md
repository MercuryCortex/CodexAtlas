# Sūrat al-Raḥmān (Quran 55 — The Lord of Mercy)

## What it is
One of the most lyrical suras, often called "the beauty of the Qur'an." It opens by naming God as al-Raḥmān, the Lord of Mercy, who "taught the Qur'an, created man, taught him to communicate," then unrolls a panorama of ordered creation — sun and moon to a reckoning, the star and the tree bowing, the heavens raised and the **balance** (mīzān) set, the two seas that meet but do not transgress. Through it runs a single refrain, repeated thirty-one times and addressed to both "heavy" orders of creation, humankind and jinn: *"So which of your Lord's blessings do you both deny?"* It closes with the twinned gardens of paradise.

## Primary text

### Quran 55:1–4
*Trans. Abdel Haleem (2004)*
> It is the Lord of Mercy who taught the Qur'an. He created man and taught him to communicate.

### Quran 55:13 (the refrain)
*Trans. Abdel Haleem (2004)*
> Which, then, of your Lord's blessings do you both deny?

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** Meccan; the sura answers ingratitude not with argument but with a tolling reminder of gift after gift.
- **Tradition.** "Both" of the refrain are humankind and jinn — the sura is unusual in addressing the two responsible creations together.
- **Discovery / transmission.** al-Raḥmān was a divine name already current in pre-Islamic South Arabian monotheism, here made central.

## Themes and motifs
- The litany of blessings — creation itemized as mercy.
- The balance (mīzān) — cosmic and moral equilibrium "set" by God.
- Man and jinn — the two accountable orders, clay and fire.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **The tolling refrain — a shared liturgical architecture.** Sūrat al-Raḥmān is built like a responsorial chant: a clause of blessing, then the bell of the refrain, thirty-one times — *"which of your Lord's blessings do you both deny?"* This is the exact form of the Hebrew "Great Hallel," Psalm 136, where twenty-six verses each name a deed of creation and deliverance and each is answered by the congregation's *kī le-ʿolam ḥasdo*, "for his steadfast love endures forever." Both poems convert theology into rhythm: the worshipper cannot recite them without *participating*, supplying or hearing the refrain again and again until gratitude becomes bodily. The antiphonal psalm was sung in the Second Temple and survives in synagogue and church liturgy; the sura is chanted across the Muslim world and is among the first many children memorize. The parallel is structural rather than borrowed — two scriptures independently discovering that the surest way to teach a people to count their blessings is to make them *answer*, in unison, after every one (Reynolds 2010 pp.251–259; McAuliffe 2006 pp.163–178; Nasr 2015 pp.1331–1345).
