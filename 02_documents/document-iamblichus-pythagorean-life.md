# Iamblichus — On the Pythagorean Way of Life and the Theology of Arithmetic

## What it is
The Pythagorean corpus of Iamblichus of Chalcis, founder of Syrian Neoplatonism — a multi-book reconstruction of the Pythagorean life, ethics, mathematics, and number-mysticism, designed to set the Pythagorean tradition at the head of the philosophical curriculum. Its surviving parts include the *Life of Pythagoras*, the *Protrepticus* (exhortation to philosophy), the works on mathematics, and the related *Theology of Arithmetic*, which expounds the sacred meanings of the numbers one through ten. With his separate *On the Mysteries*, Iamblichus also turned Neoplatonism decisively toward **theurgy** — salvation through divinely-given ritual rather than through reason alone.

## Context
- **Intellectual milieu.** Syrian Neoplatonism, more ritualistic and religious than Plotinus's contemplative mysticism.
- **The theurgic turn.** Against Porphyry, Iamblichus argues that the embodied soul cannot ascend by thought alone but needs the gods' own ritual acts (theurgy).
- **Discovery / transmission.** Greek manuscripts; foundational for Proclus and the Athenian school.

## Themes and motifs
- Theurgy — ritual cooperation with the gods as the path of salvation.
- The Pythagorean way of life — asceticism, community, and number as sacred.
- Number-theology — the metaphysical meanings of the decad.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **The turn from contemplation to ritual — and the survival of sacred number.** Iamblichus changed the course of Neoplatonism: where Plotinus had taught that the soul ascends to the One by pure thought, Iamblichus insisted that ritual — **theurgy**, "god-work" — is necessary, because the gods alone can lift the embodied soul. This sacramental, ritual Neoplatonism became the norm for Proclus and the emperor Julian, and its logic — that material rites can carry divine power — runs parallel to the developing Christian theology of the sacraments. Through his Pythagorean works, Iamblichus also preserved and revived the number-mysticism (the sacred decad, the tetractys) that flowed on into Renaissance esotericism, Christian and Kabbalistic numerology, and Western magic (Dillon & Hershbell 1991; Shaw 1995; Clarke, Dillon & Hershbell 2003).
