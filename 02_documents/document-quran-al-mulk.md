# Sūrat al-Mulk (Quran 67 — Sovereignty)

## What it is
A Meccan sura on the kingship of God: "Exalted is He in whose hand is all control" (67:1). It surveys the cosmos as the work of a single flawless Maker — the seven heavens "in harmonious layers," with the dare to look twice and find any crack; the lowest heaven studded with lamps; the earth made habitable. It presses the contrast between the believer and the denier — who will be cast into Hell hearing it "drawing in its breath" — and asks the unanswerable questions of providence: who holds up the birds? who is the army that could help you against the Lord of Mercy? who would give you water if your streams ran underground? In Muslim devotion al-Mulk is recited every night as **al-munjiya**, "the deliverer," a guard against the torment of the grave.

## Primary text

### Quran 67:3–4
*Trans. Abdel Haleem (2004)*
> who created the seven heavens, one above the other. You will not see any flaw in what the Lord of Mercy creates. Look again! Can you see any flaw? Look again! And again! Your sight will turn back to you, weak and defeated.

### Quran 67:1
*Trans. Abdel Haleem (2004)*
> Exalted is He who holds all control in His hands, who has power over all things.

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** Meccan; the sura answers idolatry by directing the eye upward to a creation too perfect to be the work of many hands.
- **Tradition.** Hadith commends nightly recitation of al-Mulk as intercession in the grave, giving it the names al-munjiya and al-māniʿa ("the protectress").
- **Discovery / transmission.** Its seven-heaven cosmology became the standard frame of Islamic cosmography.

## Themes and motifs
- Sovereignty — all dominion (mulk) in God's hand alone.
- The flawless cosmos — creation as proof, "can you see any flaw?"
- Life and death as God's test — "who created death and life to test which of you is best in deed" (67:2).

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **"Can you see any flaw?" — the perfect cosmos and the seven heavens.** Sūrat al-Mulk stakes God's sovereignty on the *flawlessness* of what He made: stare at the seven-tiered heaven as long as you like, your eye will give out before it finds a crack (67:3–4). This is the Qur'an's version of Genesis 1's refrain that God saw each work and "it was good," climaxing in "very good" (Genesis 1:31) — creation as a deliberately evaluated, approved order, and therefore evidence of one wise King (cf. the enthroned creator-king of Psalm 24). The **seven heavens** themselves are a cosmology the Qur'an shares with the wider Near East: the tiered firmaments of Mesopotamian astronomy, the seven heavens traversed in Jewish apocalyptic ascents (the Hekhalot literature, 2 Enoch) and in Paul's "third heaven" (2 Corinthians 12:2). The sura folds that inherited architecture into a strict monotheism — many layers, one Maker, no flaw — and Muslim piety then turns the chapter into a nightly shield against the grave, so that a cosmological argument becomes a personal prayer for protection (Reynolds 2010 pp.281–289; McAuliffe 2006 pp.150–162; Nasr 2015 pp.1387–1397).
