# Book of Amos

## What it is
The earliest of the Hebrew "writing prophets." A Judahite herdsman and tender of sycamore-figs, Amos was sent north to the royal sanctuary at Bethel to denounce the prosperous kingdom of Jeroboam II for crushing the poor under a veneer of zealous worship. The book opens with a rhetorical sweep of oracles against Israel's neighbors that closes the trap on Israel itself, proceeds through visions of judgment, and ends (in its present form) with a brief restoration hope.

## Primary text

### Amos 5:21–24
*Trans. NRSV*
> I hate, I despise your festivals, and I take no delight in your solemn assemblies... Take away from me the noise of your songs; I will not listen to the melody of your harps. But let justice roll down like waters, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** The reign of Jeroboam II — territorial expansion, wealth, and steep inequality in the northern kingdom, a generation before its destruction by Assyria.
- **Religious milieu.** Amos attacks the cult of Bethel not as idolatry but as moral fraud: ritual without justice. The priest Amaziah expels him (7:10–17).
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous canonical transmission within the Twelve.

## Themes and motifs
- Social justice — the founding prophetic statement that YHWH rejects worship divorced from justice for the poor (5:21–24; 8:4–6).
- Oracles against the nations — the opening pattern (chs. 1–2) that becomes a prophetic genre.
- [[divine-kingship]] — YHWH as judge of all nations, not merely Israel's tribal deity (9:7: "Are you not like the Ethiopians to me, O people of Israel?").

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **Amos 5:24 is the taproot of the religious justice tradition in the modern West.** "Let justice roll down like waters, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream" was inscribed by Martin Luther King Jr. into both the "I Have a Dream" speech (1963) and the "Letter from Birmingham Jail" (1963), and is carved on the Civil Rights Memorial in Montgomery. An 8th-century-BCE shepherd's attack on the cult of a doomed kingdom became the scriptural charter of 20th-century social-gospel and civil-rights activism (Paul 1991, OTL pp.188–195).
