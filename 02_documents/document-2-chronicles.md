# Books of Chronicles (1–2 Chronicles)

## What it is
A single work of the **Writings (Ketuvim)** — one book in the Hebrew Bible, split into 1 and 2 Chronicles only in the Greek and later traditions — that retells the entire sweep of Israel's story from **Adam to the Persian period**. It opens with nine chapters of **genealogies** (1 Chr 1–9) running from the first human through the tribes of Israel, then narrates the reigns of **David and Solomon** at length (1 Chr 10 – 2 Chr 9), and finally the kings of Judah down to the destruction of Jerusalem and the **edict of Cyrus** permitting return (2 Chr 10–36). It is not an independent history but a **theological re-presentation** of the material in Samuel–Kings, reshaped by the Chronicler to foreground the Davidic dynasty, the Jerusalem Temple, and the Levitical clergy.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Persian-period (and possibly early-Hellenistic) Yehud — a small temple-state under imperial rule, with the monarchy long gone and identity centered on the Temple.
- **Religious milieu.** The Chronicler writes to legitimate the Second-Temple cult by anchoring it in David's planning and Solomon's building, and to teach a theology of **immediate divine retribution**: each king prospers or falls according to faithfulness, often diverging pointedly from the parallel account in Kings.
- **Who was behind it.** Anonymous Levitical/temple circles; the close interest in singers, gatekeepers, and cultic personnel points to a Levitical hand.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Preserved at Qumran and in the Masoretic Text; the Greek *Paraleipomena* ("the things left over") gives the two-book division.

## Themes and motifs
- [[divine-kingship]] — the Davidic monarchy as the divinely-chosen vehicle of YHWH's rule, idealized and purified in the retelling.
- [[messianism]] — the unbroken emphasis on the Davidic line and its temple feeds the later hope of a restored Davidic anointed.

- → [[phase-2-034-books-of-kings]] / [[phase-2-019-deuteronomistic-history]] — *redaction-of*: Chronicles rewrites the Deuteronomistic narrative of Samuel–Kings, selecting, omitting (e.g. David's sins), and supplementing to its own theological ends.

- → Ezra–Nehemiah — *shared-milieu*: the standard companion of Second-Temple historiography (whether by the same author is debated).

## Disputes
- **Authorship and unity with Ezra–Nehemiah.** An older view treated Chronicles–Ezra–Nehemiah as one Chronicler's work; many now (Japhet, Williamson) argue Chronicles is a separate composition.
- **Date.** The genealogies' depth and linguistic features place the work c. 400–350 BCE, but a range into the early Hellenistic period is defended.
- **Historical value.** How much the Chronicler preserves independent early tradition versus freely rewrites Samuel–Kings is debated case by case.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **A canonical case-study in how scripture rewrites scripture.** Chronicles is the clearest in-Bible example of *Vorlage* and revision: laid beside Samuel–Kings, it shows an ancient author selecting, harmonizing, and theologizing an earlier authoritative text — the same redactional dynamic the Documentary Hypothesis reconstructs for the Pentateuch, here visible on the surface (Japhet 1993; Knoppers 2003).
- **The genealogical spine of later tradition.** The Chronicler's Adam-to-David genealogy supplies the framework reused in Second-Temple Judaism and in the New Testament genealogies of Jesus, carrying the Davidic line forward as a load-bearing thread of messianic expectation (Knoppers 2003).
