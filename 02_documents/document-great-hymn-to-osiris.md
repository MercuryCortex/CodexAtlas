# The Great Hymn to Osiris (Stela of Amenmose)

## What it is
The most complete connected account, from Egypt's own pen, of the Osiris myth: the good king Osiris murdered and dismembered by his jealous brother Seth; his sister-wife Isis searching the land for his scattered body, reassembling it, and reviving him by her magic long enough to conceive their son Horus; Osiris descending to become king and judge of the dead, while Horus grows up to avenge him and claim the throne of the living. Inscribed on an 18th-Dynasty funerary stela, it is the central scripture of Egypt's resurrection religion.

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** The Abydos cult of Osiris and the universal Egyptian hope of becoming "an Osiris" — justified and resurrected — after death.
- **Genre.** A hymn that narrates as it praises, giving the myth its fullest indigenous form.
- **Discovery / transmission.** The stela of Amenmose (Louvre); the myth's classical retelling is Plutarch's.

## Themes and motifs
- [[dying-rising-god]] — Osiris murdered, revived, and made lord of the resurrected dead.
- The faithful wife — Isis's grief, search, and reviving magic.
- Justification after death — Osiris as the judge before whom every soul is vindicated or condemned.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **Osiris — the ancient world's great dying-and-rising god.** The hymn gives definitive form to the myth at the heart of Egyptian religion: a murdered god revived by love and magic, who conquers death to rule the dead and guarantees resurrection to every justified soul who becomes "an Osiris." Carried into the Greco-Roman world through the Isis-and-Osiris mysteries and Plutarch's *De Iside et Osiride*, the Osirian death-and-resurrection pattern became the most-cited comparand in the long scholarly debate over "dying-and-rising gods" and the religious background against which Hellenistic-era resurrection hopes — Jewish, mystery-cult, and Christian — took shape (Lichtheim 1976 pp.81–86; Assmann 2005).
