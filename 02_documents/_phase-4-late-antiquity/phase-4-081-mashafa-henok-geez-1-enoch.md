# Mashafa Henok (Ge'ez 1 Enoch)

## What it is
**Mashafa Henok** ("Book of Enoch") is the Ge'ez recension of **1 Enoch**, a Second-Temple-Jewish apocalyptic compendium of five originally-distinct books that crystallized into a single corpus by the 1st century CE: the *Book of the Watchers* (chs. 1–36), the *Book of Parables / Similitudes* (chs. 37–71), the *Astronomical Book / Book of the Heavenly Luminaries* (chs. 72–82), the *Book of Dreams / Animal Apocalypse* (chs. 83–90), and the *Epistle of Enoch* (chs. 91–108). Composed in Aramaic between roughly the 3rd century BCE and the 1st century CE (the *Watchers* and *Astronomical Book* are the oldest layers, ~3rd–2nd c. BCE; the *Similitudes* are the latest, ~1st c. BCE to 1st c. CE), translated into Greek by the 1st century CE, translated from Greek into Ge'ez by Ethiopian-monastic translators in the late Aksumite period (likely 5th–7th centuries), and **preserved as canonical scripture in the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Bible** — the only Christian tradition that has continuously held 1 Enoch as scripture.

This node treats the **Ge'ez recension** as a textual artifact. The parent node [[phase-3-004-1-enoch]] treats the original Second-Temple-Jewish composition. The two nodes are linked by *manuscript-transmission*: the Ge'ez Enoch is the *only complete textual witness* to the work outside of fragments — until the 1947+ Qumran Aramaic discoveries, the Ge'ez was the *only* witness to the full corpus, and even now it remains the only complete textual witness (the Aramaic Qumran fragments cover ~20% of the text).

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Aksumite Kingdom, post-Christianization, with active scribal-translational engagement with Greek Christian texts. The translation likely belongs to the Nine-Saints to Kaleb era (~480–540 CE) or shortly thereafter.
- **Religious milieu.** Ethiopian Tewahedo's broad biblical canon was forming in this period. 1 Enoch was retained — partly because it was *already* respected in Greek Christianity (cited by Jude 14–15, by Barnabas, by Tertullian, by Origen), partly because the apocalyptic-cosmological content resonated with Ethiopian Christianity's continuing engagement with Old-Testament observance.
- **Who was behind it.** The Ge'ez translation is anonymous and belongs to the Aksumite-monastic translational project that produced the Ge'ez Bible as a whole. Traditional attribution to the [[the-nine-saints]] is canonical-Ethiopian; the actual translators are unrecoverable. The original *composition* is the work of multiple Second-Temple-Jewish authors writing pseudonymously in the name of the antediluvian patriarch Enoch — the seven-generation descendant of Adam who "walked with God and was not, for God took him" (Gen 5:24).
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous Ethiopian-Christian use across 1500+ years. Brought to European scholarship by [[james-bruce-of-kinnaird]] in 1773 — three Ge'ez manuscripts (Bodleian MSS Bruce 73, 74) became the basis for Laurence's 1821 English translation, Dillmann's 1851 Ge'ez critical edition, and Charles's 1893/1912 English critical edition. The 1947+ Qumran Aramaic Enochic fragments (4Q201–4Q212; the *Book of Giants* fragments 4Q203, 4Q530–4Q533) confirmed the Ge'ez textual tradition as a faithful witness to a Second-Temple Aramaic original.

## Themes and motifs
- [[watchers-and-fallen-angels]] — the *Book of the Watchers* (chs. 1–36) is the foundational text of the "Watchers myth": 200 angelic Watchers descend to earth, take human wives, beget the violent Nephilim giants, teach humanity forbidden arts (metalworking, weapons, cosmetics, sorcery, astrology, abortifacients), and corrupt creation; God commissions Michael, Gabriel, Raphael, and Uriel to bind the Watchers, kill the giants, and prepare creation for the Flood as cleansing.
- [[origin-of-evil]] — 1 Enoch is the load-bearing Second-Temple-Jewish text for the *exogenous* origin-of-evil thesis (evil enters the world through the fallen Watchers, not through Adam's transgression). This thesis stands in tension with the *Adamic* origin-of-evil thesis that dominates later rabbinic and Pauline-Christian theology.
- [[apocalyptic-revelation]] — Enoch's heavenly journeys (chs. 14–36, 72–82) establish the literary form of the *apocalypse* (the ascent-of-the-seer-and-revelation-by-angelic-interpreter genre) that shapes Daniel, 4 Ezra, 2 Baruch, the Apocalypse of Abraham, the Christian Apocalypse of John, and the entire apocalyptic genre across Jewish and Christian literature for half a millennium.
- [[heavenly-tablets]] — the heavenly tablets in 1 Enoch (chs. 81, 93, 103, 106) record the predetermined history of the world; this motif influences [[phase-3-009-jubilees]] and Qumran sectarian eschatology.
- [[son-of-man]] — the *Book of Parables / Similitudes* (chs. 37–71) introduces a heavenly *Son of Man* figure who pre-exists creation and will judge at the end; the influence on the Gospel-Son-of-Man Christology has been debated for over a century (Charles 1912; Vermes 1973; Boyarin 2012 — most modern scholarship sees substantive influence, with Boyarin arguing that the Gospel use of "Son of Man" presupposes the Enochic tradition).
- [[ascent-of-the-soul]] — Enoch's bodily ascent to the throne-room (chs. 14–16) is foundational for the *merkavah* / heavenly-ascent mystical tradition.

## Disputes
- **Date of the *Similitudes* (chs. 37–71).** The latest stratum of the corpus; absent from the Qumran fragments (which suggested to some scholars a Christian-era composition). Most current scholarship dates the Similitudes 1st c. BCE to 1st c. CE; the Christian-composition hypothesis is largely rejected (Nickelsburg-VanderKam 2001).
- **Relationship of the Similitudes' "Son of Man" to NT use.** Disputed for over a century; recent scholarship (Boyarin 2012) argues for substantive influence on Gospel Christology; conservative scholarship has resisted.
- **Status of the Ethiopic translation strata.** Whether the Ge'ez Enoch reflects a single translation event or multiple strata. Knibb 1978 sees a basically-unified translation with subsequent revision.
- **Canonicity in early Christianity.** 1 Enoch was widely accepted by 2nd–3rd c. Greek and Latin fathers; Augustine's *City of God* 15.23 critiques but acknowledges its early circulation; by the 4th–5th c. Greek and Latin Christianity drops it from the canon. Ethiopia retained it as canonical scripture and has done so to the present; it is *the* unique-Ethiopian-canonical text in this regard.
