# Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra

## What it is
A foundational Mahāyāna sūtra synthesizing the Yogācāra ("Mind-Only" / *Cittamātra*) and the Tathāgatagarbha ("Buddha-Womb" / *Tathāgatagarbha*) doctrinal streams. The Sūtra is set as a discourse of the Buddha to the rākṣasa-king Rāvaṇa on Mount Malaya in Laṅkā (the island of Sri Lanka) — a striking choice of setting, putting the cosmic-rākṣasa antagonist of the Rāmāyaṇa in the position of disciple. The interlocutor through most of the text is the bodhisattva Mahāmati, who poses 108 questions; the Buddha's responses present a sustained exposition of: (1) the Yogācāra doctrine of the three natures (*trisvabhāva*) and the eight consciousnesses (*aṣṭa vijñāna*), including the storehouse consciousness (*ālaya-vijñāna*); (2) the Tathāgatagarbha doctrine that all beings possess the buddha-nature; (3) a sustained critique of conceptual proliferation and the language-trap; (4) a doctrine of *self-realization of noble wisdom* (*pratyātmagati-jñāna*) which can only be experienced directly, not communicated. The fourth point made the Laṅkāvatāra the natural canonical anchor for the Chan/Zen tradition's claim to a *separate transmission outside the scriptures*. The text is notoriously difficult — composed in irregular Buddhist Hybrid Sanskrit, with abrupt non-sequiturs, repeated material, and what appear to be unmarked layers of editorial accretion.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Mahāyāna northwest India and Kashmir, 4th-5th c. CE. The Yogācāra school (Asaṅga, ~310-390; Vasubandhu, ~316-396) was developing the *Cittamātra* / Mind-Only philosophy in parallel; the Tathāgatagarbha literature (the *Tathāgatagarbha Sūtra*, the *Śrīmālādevī Siṃhanāda Sūtra*) was emerging from devotional-philosophical communities; the Laṅkāvatāra synthesizes both streams.
- **Religious milieu.** Indian Mahāyāna's classical period, with internal competition between Madhyamaka (Nāgārjuna's emptiness-tradition) and Yogācāra (the Mind-Only tradition). The Laṅkāvatāra is a Yogācāra-Tathāgatagarbha text; the relation of Mind-Only to emptiness is itself a major topic of the Sūtra.
- **Who was behind it.** Anonymous Mahāyāna communities, probably in Kashmir or the northwest. Several editorial layers are evident; the longer Bodhiruci recension preserves material not in the shorter Guṇabhadra recension. Whether these represent later expansion or selective abridgment is debated.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous Mahāyāna manuscript transmission. The decisive event for East Asian reception was Guṇabhadra's 443 CE Chinese translation, the 4-fascicle text that the early Chan tradition took as foundational. The tradition that Bodhidharma transmitted the Laṅkāvatāra to his disciple Huike (the "Two Entrances" doctrine — entrance by principle, entrance by practice) is historically contested but established as Chan-tradition self-narrative.

## Themes and motifs
- **Mind-Only / Cittamātra (*vijñapti-mātra*)** — the doctrine that what we experience as an external world is actually transformations of consciousness; the perceived world is *vijñapti-mātra*, "consciousness-only." This is metaphysical idealism, though Yogācāra interpreters differ on whether the doctrine is ontological (idealist) or epistemological (phenomenological).
- **The eight consciousnesses (*aṣṭa vijñāna*)** — five sense-consciousnesses, the discriminating consciousness (*mano-vijñāna*), the self-affirming consciousness (*manas*), and the storehouse consciousness (*ālaya-vijñāna*). The *ālaya-vijñāna* is the receptacle of karmic seeds that ripen into experiential reality; foundational for all subsequent Mahāyāna and Vajrayāna psychology.
- **Tathāgatagarbha (Buddha-Womb / Buddha-Nature)** — all beings possess the buddha-nature; awakening is not the acquisition of something new but the realization of what is already present. The Laṅkāvatāra harmonizes this seemingly substance-positing doctrine with the emptiness-doctrine: the *tathāgatagarbha* is empty of self-existence but is the basis of the possibility of liberation.
- **Self-realization of noble wisdom (*pratyātmagati-jñāna*)** — the recurrent claim that liberation is *self-realized*, not communicated; the locus classicus of the Chan/Zen "transmission outside the scriptures" doctrine.
- [[idealism-metaphysical]] — the Laṅkāvatāra is the most idealist of the major Mahāyāna sūtras; its closest cross-tradition parallels are Berkeley, Kant, and (in some readings) Hegel.
- [[gnosis-as-salvation]] — direct realization-knowledge, not propositional knowledge or ritual performance, is salvific. Structural parallel to (but doctrinally distinct from) Gnostic *gnōsis* and Sufi *maʿrifa*.
- [[non-duality]] — the dissolution of subject-object duality is the operative liberatory move; the Laṅkāvatāra's negation of "external object" and "internal subject" alike.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-4-061-lotus-sutra]] / [[phase-4-062-avatamsaka-sutra]] / [[phase-4-064-sukhavativyuha-larger]] — *parallel-motif* / *shared-milieu*: the four East Asian Mahāyāna sūtra-corpora.
- → [[phase-5-002-heart-sutra]] — *parallel-motif*: shared Mahāyāna substrate, but Heart Sūtra is Madhyamaka-emptiness; Laṅkāvatāra is Yogācāra-Tathāgatagarbha. The two are doctrinally distinct emphases within a shared Mahāyāna world.
- → [[phase-5-004-platform-sutra-huineng]] — *direct-influence*: the early Chan tradition called itself "Laṅkāvatāra masters" (*Lengqie-shi*); the Sūtra's doctrine of self-realized wisdom is the textual basis of Chan's "separate transmission." The Platform Sutra's own narrative anchors enlightenment in the Diamond Sūtra rather than the Laṅkāvatāra — a substitution that reflects the Southern Chan's strategic shift from the Laṅkāvatāra-anchored Northern School to a more accessible Prajñāpāramitā framing. (McRae 1986 on the Northern School.)
- → **Asaṅga and Vasubandhu's Yogācāra treatises** — *shared-milieu*: the Laṅkāvatāra synthesizes the Yogācāra philosophical apparatus into a sūtra-format. Asaṅga's *Mahāyānasaṃgraha* and Vasubandhu's *Trimśikā* and *Viṃśatikā* are the scholastic-philosophical counterparts.
- → **Tibetan Shentong / "other-empty" school** — *direct-influence*: the Tibetan Jonang school (Dolpopa, 1292-1361) developed a *tathāgatagarbha*-positive reading of Mahāyāna that drew heavily on the Laṅkāvatāra.

## Cross-tradition substrate
- **Metaphysical idealism.** The Laṅkāvatāra's *Cittamātra* doctrine is the closest non-Western parallel to (and a prior independent statement of) the Western idealist tradition: Berkeley's *Principles of Human Knowledge* (1710), Kant's transcendental idealism (1781), Fichte's *Wissenschaftslehre*, Hegel's *Phenomenology* (1807), Schopenhauer's *World as Will and Representation* (1818, with direct Buddhist resonance — Schopenhauer read the Laṅkāvatāra in Latin translation and acknowledged the affinity). Six independent statements of the doctrine that what we experience is consciousness-constituted rather than mind-independent. *Parallel-motif* substrate with Schopenhauer as the explicit historical bridge. Wire to [[idealism-metaphysical]].
- **Gnosis as direct realization.** The Laṅkāvatāra's *pratyātmagati-jñāna* (self-realized wisdom) belongs to a cross-tradition substrate of doctrines of *direct-realization-knowledge*: Gnostic *gnōsis* as direct salvific knowledge (Pagels, Brakke); Sufi *maʿrifa* / *dhawq* as direct tasting of God; Christian Eastern *theōsis* / *theōria* as direct contemplative union; Hindu *jñāna* as direct realization-knowledge in Advaita Vedānta. Substrate; no transmissional claim. (Note: the Gnostic parallel must be cautious — *gnōsis* in second-century Sethian and Valentinian usage is partly cosmological-mythical, partly direct-realization; the Buddhist *pratyātmagati-jñāna* is more purely epistemic-experiential.) Wire to [[gnosis-as-salvation]].
- **Storehouse consciousness and the unconscious.** The Yogācāra *ālaya-vijñāna* (storehouse consciousness containing karmic seeds that ripen into experiential reality) is the closest classical precedent for modern theories of the unconscious — Freud's id-unconscious, Jung's collective unconscious, Lacan's *symbolic order*. Jung explicitly drew the parallel (CW 11, *Psychology and Religion*; his foreword to Suzuki). Substrate; with Jung as a partial historical bridge.

## Disputes
- **Date.** Composition ~300-450 CE; Guṇabhadra's 443 CE translation fixes a terminus. The Bodhiruci recension's additional material is harder to date.
- **Bodhidharma's transmission.** The story that Bodhidharma transmitted the Laṅkāvatāra to Huike is established as Chan-tradition narrative but is historically dubious. Bodhidharma's historicity is itself contested; Faure 1986 reconstructs the traditional Bodhidharma as largely legendary. *[claim: contested]*
- **Suzuki's translation and interpretation.** D.T. Suzuki's 1930-1932 translation and *Studies* shaped 20th-c. Western reception of the Laṅkāvatāra. Suzuki read the text through his Rinzai Zen frame, with characteristic idiosyncrasies (the term "Mind-only," the framing of *tathāgatagarbha* as "Womb of the Tathāgata"). Subsequent scholarship (Faure 1986, Sharf 1993, Williams 2009) has problematized Suzuki's framing as partly modernist projection. The Sūtra itself is more philosophically complex and less Zen-friendly than Suzuki's reading suggests.
- **Mind-Only as ontological or epistemological.** Whether the Yogācāra *vijñapti-mātra* doctrine asserts that nothing exists outside consciousness (idealism) or that we cannot know anything outside consciousness (phenomenology) is a long-running debate within Yogācāra scholarship.
- **Three Chinese recensions.** Why the Guṇabhadra 4-fascicle text is shorter than the Bodhiruci 10-fascicle: did Guṇabhadra translate from a shorter Sanskrit, or did Bodhiruci have an expanded one? Both are possible.
