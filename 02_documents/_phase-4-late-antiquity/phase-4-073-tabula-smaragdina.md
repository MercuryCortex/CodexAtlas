# Tabula Smaragdina (Emerald Tablet)

## What it is
A short pseudepigraphic Hermetic text — fewer than fifteen aphoristic Latin lines in its standard medieval recension — attributed to [[hermes-trismegistus]] and constituting **the single most-cited Hermetic text in Western alchemy**, from the 12th-century Latin translation through Isaac Newton's 17th-century manuscript translation through 20th-century occult and Jungian-psychological reception. The text's compactness — it fits on a single emerald, in the legendary frame, hence the name — made it uniquely transmissible: virtually every major medieval and early-modern Western alchemical text quotes or alludes to it, and its first phrase ("*Quod est inferius est sicut quod est superius*" — "That which is below is as that which is above") is the canonical formulation of the [[as-above-so-below]] / [[microcosm-macrocosm]] Hermetic principle.

The standard medieval Latin text reads, in part (paraphrase of the most-circulated recension):
1. *Verum, sine mendacio, certum et verissimum.* — True, without falsehood, certain and most true.
2. *Quod est inferius est sicut quod est superius* — That which is below is as that which is above…
3. *…et quod est superius est sicut quod est inferius, ad perpetranda miracula rei unius.* — …and that which is above is as that which is below, for accomplishing the wonder of the one thing.
4. (And the development of: all things have their origin in the One; sun is its father, moon its mother, wind has carried it in its belly, earth is its nurse; this is the father of all telesma of the whole world; its power is whole if it is converted into earth.)
5. (And the final claim:) *Complete est quod dixi de operatione solis.* — Complete is what I have said concerning the operation of the Sun.

## Context
- **Linguistic milieu.** Earliest extant attestation is **Arabic**, in the *Kitāb Sirr al-Khalīqa wa-Ṣanʿat al-Ṭabīʿa* (*Book of the Secret of Creation and the Art of Nature*) attributed to **Pseudo-Balīnūs / Pseudo-Apollonius of Tyana**, datable on internal evidence to ~750–800 CE. Whether a lost Greek original lay behind the Arabic is debated (Ruska 1926 argued for a Syriac-or-Arabic original; later scholarship has not produced a Greek antecedent). The text emerges in the Syriac-Arabic intellectual environment of Umayyad and early-Abbasid alchemy and Hermetic philosophy — possibly with connections to the **Harran Sabian community** (a Hermetic-pagan group surviving in Upper Mesopotamia until the 10th–11th c.).
- **Latin transmission.** First Latin translation ~1140, by Hugo of Santalla or his circle (the *Liber de secretis nature* recension). Re-translated into Latin from Arabic in the *Secretum Secretorum* / *Sirr al-asrar* pseudo-Aristotelian compilation (translated by Philip of Tripoli, ~1230s) — the *Secretum* was one of the most-copied medieval Latin books and was the principal carrier of the Tabula into European intellectual life.
- **Religious-philosophical environment.** The text is Hermetic-pagan in shape but acquired heavy Christian-allegorical reading in medieval Latin alchemy ([[albertus-magnus]] cites it in his alchemical works; the *Aurora Consurgens* attributed to Aquinas weaves Tabula phrases through allegorical scripture-exegesis). In the Renaissance the text becomes paradigmatically a *prisca theologia* document — an ancient theology preserved from antediluvian Egyptian wisdom, transmitted through Hermes Trismegistus, harmonious with Christian truth ([[phase-6-001-ficino-pimander|Ficino's Pimander]] framing).
- **Transmission to Newton.** Isaac Newton's autograph English translation of the Tabula, with commentary, survives as **King's College Cambridge Keynes MS 28** — one of the most striking single artifacts of the late-17th-century scientific-alchemical-Hermetic continuity. Newton's alchemical manuscripts were suppressed for 250 years after his death and only rediscovered after Sotheby's 1936 sale of the Portsmouth papers (with John Maynard Keynes purchasing the alchemical lot).

## Themes and motifs
- [[as-above-so-below]] — the canonical formulation. This text *is* the textual locus where this Hermetic principle entered Western philosophical-mystical discourse in its short aphoristic form.
- [[microcosm-macrocosm]] — the broader theoretical principle the Tabula's "above and below" instantiates.
- [[hermetic-correspondences]] — the system of analogical-symbolic correspondences (sun-gold, moon-silver, planets-metals, etc.) that the Tabula's "above and below" justifies.
- [[alchemy-as-spiritual-discipline]] — the Tabula's compact statement of the *operation of the Sun* becomes the canonical Hermetic-alchemical statement of the inseparability of material operation and spiritual transformation.
- [[prisca-theologia]] — the Renaissance reading of the Tabula as a primordial Egyptian wisdom preserved through Hermes, harmonious with biblical revelation.

## Disputes
- **Greek origin?** Ruska 1926 concluded no Greek antecedent could be located; later scholarship has not produced one. The text appears to be of Syriac-Arabic late-antique-to-early-medieval origin, in a milieu where Hermetic Greek material was being translated and recombined.
- **Authorship of the Arabic *Sirr al-Khalīqa*.** Attributed to "Balīnūs" = Apollonius of Tyana, the late-antique Neopythagorean miracle-worker. The attribution is pseudonymous; the actual author is unknown but is plausibly an 8th-c. Syriac-Arabic Hermetic compiler. Stapleton 1933 / Plessner 1954 / Weisser 1979 are the principal philological treatments.
- **The Newton-Hermetism question.** That Newton seriously engaged Hermetic-alchemical literature is no longer disputed (Dobbs 1975 *The Foundations of Newton's Alchemy*; Buchwald-Feingold 2013). Whether his Hermetic engagement was substantively continuous with his scientific work (the strong thesis) or a parallel separate practice (the weak thesis) remains debated.
