# Acts of Paul and Thecla

## What it is
A second-century narrative (~+185) embedded within the larger *Acts of Paul*, recounting the adventures of [[thecla]], a young Iconian woman who hears Paul preach an encratite (sexually renunciatory) gospel through her window, breaks off her engagement to her fiancé Thamyris, is condemned to burn at the stake, is saved by a sudden rainstorm, follows Paul to Antioch in Pisidia, is condemned again to wild beasts, is protected miraculously (a lioness defends her; a pool of seals serves as her self-baptism), survives, dresses as a man, is commissioned by Paul to "go and teach the word of God," and eventually dies (or in some versions, vanishes into a rock) at Seleucia. The text is the foundational hagiographic-novelistic narrative of female Christian discipleship.

## Context
- **Date.** Tertullian's *On Baptism* 17 (~+200) is the *terminus ad quem*; mainstream view ~+185.
- **Author.** Tertullian (followed by Jerome) reports that an Asian presbyter confessed to writing the work "out of love for Paul" and was deposed — possibly *terminus ad quem* corroboration but the identification is hard to verify.
- **Cultural context.** The text emerges from the same milieu as the *Acts of John*, *Acts of Peter*, *Acts of Thomas*, *Acts of Andrew* — a 2nd-c. genre of *Apocryphal Acts* that recasts the apostles in romance/novelistic mode with strongly **encratite** (sexually renunciatory) theological framing. The genre is increasingly understood (MacDonald 1983; Davis 2001) as preserving (under male redaction) traditions of women's oral storytelling.
- **Tertullian's testimony.** *On Baptism* 17 — extant — is hostile: women cite the Thecla text to claim baptismal authority; Tertullian condemns. This shows the text was *operatively* shaping early-3rd-c. Christian practice; not a curiosity.

## Themes and motifs
- [[itinerant-discipleship]] — Thecla as wandering teacher.
- [[martyrdom-theology]] — the survived-not-fully martyrdom: Thecla as *confessor*, repeatedly threatened with death but preserved.
- **Encratism**: sexual renunciation as the operative form of Christian commitment; Paul's preaching summarized as a string of beatitudes blessing celibacy.
- **Self-baptism**: Thecla baptizes herself in the arena (in a pool of seals!) — a striking authority-claim that Tertullian explicitly attacks.
- **Cross-dressing**: she dresses as a man to travel safely as a wandering preacher; the gender-crossing is theologically marked.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-3-015-pauline-epistles]] — *redaction-of* / *pseudepigraphal*: the AP&T elaborates a Pauline preaching summarized largely from 1 Corinthians 7 (the celibacy material), pushing the encratite element further than Paul himself.
- → [[phase-3-018-luke-acts]] — *parallel-genre*: the AP&T is a second-tier apocryphal-Acts narrative paralleling Lukan biography in form but with starkly different theology.
- → [[phase-4-005-gospel-of-mary]] — *parallel-motif* (women's authority): both texts elevate a female disciple whose authority is questioned and partially vindicated.
- → cult of saints (4th–6th c.) — *direct-influence*: the AP&T is the foundation of the *Thecla cult* centered on her shrine at Seleucia.

## Disputes
- **Date.** ~+160/+190 — Tertullian's testimony anchors the *terminus ad quem*; the underlying oral tradition may be earlier.
- **Author.** Tertullian's Asian presbyter is plausible but unidentifiable.
- **Theological character.** Encratite — but how close to Gnostic, Montanist, or simply rigorist ascetic is debated.
- **Relationship to women's authority debates.** Did the text generate or reflect women's baptismal practice? Tertullian's testimony shows the practice existed and the text was its authority.
