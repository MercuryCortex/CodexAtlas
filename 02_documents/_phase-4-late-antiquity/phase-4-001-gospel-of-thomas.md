# Gospel of Thomas

## What it is
A sayings gospel — 114 short *logia* of Jesus, prefaced "These are the hidden sayings that the living Jesus spoke and Didymus Judas Thomas wrote down." There is no birth, passion, or resurrection narrative; almost no narrative frame at all. Sayings are numbered by modern editors. Some overlap closely with Synoptic parables (mustard seed, sower, prodigal — Sayings 9, 20, 64); others are unique to Thomas and bear what later editors classify as proto-Gnostic flavor (Sayings 22, 50, 77, 113); a final saying (114) about Mary becoming "male" is among the most disputed lines in the corpus.

## Primary text — selected excerpts

The Coptic Gospel of Thomas was published in full only in the late 1950s, so no truly public-domain translation exists. The excerpts below are short fair-use selections paraphrased and rendered with reference to the standard scholarly translations (especially Thomas O. Lambdin's translation in the Nag Hammadi Library, 1977, and Marvin Meyer's in *The Nag Hammadi Scriptures*, 2007). For close interpretive use consult Lambdin, Meyer 2007, DeConick 2007, or the original Coptic at the Claremont Coptic Encyclopedia. Saying numbers follow the standard editions.

### Prologue and Sayings 1–3 — the program of Thomas

The opening lines set the gospel's hermeneutic: these are *hidden* sayings, salvation comes from *interpreting* them, and the kingdom is not in the heavens or the sea but already in the seeker.

> These are the hidden sayings that the living Jesus spoke and Didymus Judas Thomas wrote down.
>
> (1) And he said, "Whoever discovers the interpretation of these sayings will not taste death."
>
> (2) Jesus said, "Those who seek should not stop seeking until they find. When they find, they will be disturbed. When they are disturbed, they will marvel, and will reign over all."
>
> (3) Jesus said, "If your leaders say to you, 'Look, the kingdom is in the sky,' then the birds of the sky will precede you. If they say to you, 'It is in the sea,' then the fish will precede you. Rather, the kingdom is inside you and it is outside you. When you know yourselves, then you will be known, and you will understand that you are children of the living Father. But if you do not know yourselves, then you live in poverty, and you are the poverty."

Citation: *Gospel of Thomas*, prologue and logia 1–3 (Coptic NHC II,2); rendered with reference to Lambdin in Robinson 1977 and Meyer 2007.

### Saying 22 — making the two one

The saying most often cited as quintessentially Thomasine: salvation as the reintegration of dualities — male and female, inner and outer, above and below — into a primal androgynous wholeness.

> (22) Jesus saw some babies nursing. He said to his disciples, "These nursing babies are like those who enter the kingdom." They said to him, "Then shall we enter the kingdom as babies?" Jesus said to them, "When you make the two into one, and when you make the inner like the outer and the outer like the inner, and the upper like the lower, and when you make male and female into a single one, so that the male will not be male nor the female be female; when you make eyes in place of an eye and a hand in place of a hand and a foot in place of a foot, an image in place of an image — then you will enter the kingdom."

Citation: *Gospel of Thomas* 22, rendered with reference to Lambdin and Meyer 2007.

### Saying 50 — origin from the light

The disciples are coached for the post-mortem interrogation by archons: they originate from the light, where the light "came into being of itself" — a luminous self-originating origin that maps neatly onto Sethian and Hermetic cosmologies.

> (50) Jesus said, "If they say to you, 'Where have you come from?' say to them, 'We have come from the light, from the place where the light came into being by itself, established itself, and appeared in their image.' If they say to you, 'Is it you?' say, 'We are its children, and we are the chosen of the living Father.' If they ask you, 'What is the evidence of your Father in you?' say to them, 'It is motion and rest.'"

Citation: *Gospel of Thomas* 50, rendered with reference to Lambdin and Meyer 2007.

### Saying 77 — the pantheist Christ

A short pair of lines that became central to esoteric and Coptic readings of Thomas: Jesus *is* the light over all, the all has come from him, the all extends to him; split a piece of wood and he is there, lift up a stone and one finds him.

> (77) Jesus said, "I am the light that is over all things. I am all: from me all came forth, and to me all attained. Split a piece of wood; I am there. Lift up the stone, and you will find me there."

Citation: *Gospel of Thomas* 77, rendered with reference to Lambdin and Meyer 2007.

### Saying 113 — the kingdom is already spread

A late saying that perfectly inverts the Synoptic apocalyptic expectation: the kingdom will not come by watching, it is already spread over the earth and people fail to see.

> (113) His disciples said to him, "When will the kingdom come?" Jesus said, "It will not come by watching for it. It will not be said, 'Look, here it is,' or 'Look, there it is.' Rather, the Father's kingdom is spread out upon the earth, and people don't see it."

Citation: *Gospel of Thomas* 113, rendered with reference to Lambdin and Meyer 2007.

### Saying 114 — Mary becoming male

The most disputed saying in the corpus. Peter wants Mary excluded "because women are not worthy of life"; Jesus answers that he will guide her to "become male" — a phrase that has been variously read as androgynous-completion, as gender hierarchy, or as ironic-subversive. King and Castelli have given the most influential discussions.

> (114) Simon Peter said to them, "Mary should leave us, for women are not worthy of life." Jesus said, "Look, I will guide her to make her male, so that she too may become a living spirit resembling you males. For every woman who makes herself male will enter the kingdom of heaven."

Citation: *Gospel of Thomas* 114, rendered with reference to Lambdin and Meyer 2007.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Late-first / early-second-century Roman East; the Edessa hypothesis places composition in Syriac-speaking Christianity, where Thomas tradition was strong (cf. *Acts of Thomas*, *Book of Thomas the Contender*).
- **Religious milieu.** A sayings-Jesus stream that runs parallel to — and gets eclipsed by — the narrative Gospels (Mark, Matthew, Luke, John). Bears no clear stamp of developed Sethian or Valentinian mythology; this is why many scholars (DeConick, Pagels, Patterson) classify Thomas as *proto-Gnostic* or *wisdom-Christian* rather than fully Gnostic.
- **Who was behind it.** A school transmitting sayings under the name of Judas Thomas, the apostle held to be Jesus' twin in Syriac tradition. The "rolling corpus" theory (DeConick) sees a kernel from ~50 CE accreting layers through ~140 CE.
- **Discovery / transmission.** The Coptic codex was buried in a sealed jar in the Egyptian desert near the Pachomian monastery at Chenoboskion ~370 CE, recovered by Muhammad Ali al-Samman in December 1945. Three Greek Oxyrhynchus fragments (P.Oxy. 1, 654, 655), unearthed by Grenfell and Hunt in 1897–1903, were finally identified as Greek Thomas after 1956.

## Themes and motifs
- [[gnosis-as-salvation]] — Saying 1: "Whoever discovers the interpretation of these sayings will not taste death." Salvation by interpretive insight, not death-and-resurrection.
- [[kingdom-within]] — Saying 3: "The kingdom is inside you, and outside you" — interiorized eschatology against future-apocalyptic Synoptic strands.
- [[soul-as-spark]] — Saying 50: the disciples are told their origin is "from the light, where the light came into being on its own."
- [[hidden-god]] — Saying 5, 6: hidden things will be revealed; revelation as the unveiling of what is.

## Connections (provisional)
- → Synoptic Gospels (Phase 3) — *parallel-motif* / possible *common-source*: ~half of Thomas' sayings have Synoptic parallels. Crossan and Patterson argue for a shared sayings stream (Q-adjacent or independent); Goodacre argues Thomas is *dependent on* the Synoptics. Active dispute.
- → [[phase-4-002-apocryphon-of-john]] — *shared-milieu*: same codex (NHC II), but Thomas lacks the Sethian myth; the binding reflects later Gnostic anthologizing.
- → [[phase-4-005-gospel-of-mary]] — *parallel-motif*: both elevate a non-Petrine apostle (Mary, Thomas) against Peter; Saying 114 stages a Mary/Peter dispute resolved differently than in Mary.
- → [[phase-4-011-corpus-hermeticum-i]] — *parallel-motif*: revelation-discourse genre and the interiorized salvation grammar.
- → Gospel of John — *polemic-against*? Pagels (2003) reads John 20's "doubting Thomas" pericope as anti-Thomasine polemic; contested by other Johannine scholars.

## Disputes
- **Date.** Crossan, Koester, Patterson argue for a ~50–60 CE kernel, making Thomas potentially as early as the earliest Synoptic strata. DeConick supports a rolling corpus 50–140. Goodacre, Gathercole argue mid-second-century, post-Synoptic. The range -50 / -140 here brackets the live debate.
- **Genre.** Sayings-gospel sui generis vs. Gnostic gospel vs. wisdom collection. Most current Anglophone scholarship: not "Gnostic" in the developed mythological sense.
- **Saying 114.** Whether the "Mary must become male" logion is original, a later addition, or ironic. Castelli, King have published extensively.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **Saying 113 + Saying 77 — the vault's most cross-tradition-cited divine-immanence cluster:** "The kingdom is spread out upon the earth and people don't see it" (113) and "Split a piece of wood; I am there. Lift up a stone, you will find me there" (77) are structurally identical to Eckhart's "God is in the lowest ground of all things," Zen's "buddha-nature is everywhere, in the dog, in the stone," and Daodejing ch. 4 (the Dao filling every crevice). Four traditions — Syriac-Christian, German mystical, Japanese Buddhist, Classical Chinese — converge on radical divine immanence in ordinary matter; Thomas may be the earliest written statement (~50 CE kernel if Crossan is right).
- **Thomas vs. John — the earliest documentable intra-Christian theological debate:** Pagels (*Beyond Belief*, 2003) argues that John 20's "doubting Thomas" pericope is a direct polemic against the Thomas tradition — insisting on physical resurrection precisely *against* Thomas-community resurrection-as-visionary-experience. If correct, the *Gospel of John* and the *Gospel of Thomas* are two competing 1st-century Christianities arguing *in the text* about what resurrection means. Both vault-resident documents frame this debate; it is the earliest the vault can document.
- **Thomas as the Q-problem's sister text:** Crossan and Patterson argue Thomas preserves an independent ~50 CE sayings layer; if correct, the Q source and Thomas are parallel branches of a pre-narrative sayings-Jesus tradition, both preceding all four canonical Gospels. DeConick's rolling-corpus model (50–140 CE) provides a test case for stratified-composition analysis. The vault has the primary text and the competing scholarly frameworks to position it.
- **Saying 70 "bring forth what is within you" ↔ Jungian individuation ↔ Sufi *kashf*:** Thomas's salvation-by-self-knowledge formula has no death-and-resurrection requirement — it is interior, gnostic in the root-sense (salvation by *knowing oneself*). Jung explicitly cites this saying; the Sufi *kashf* (unveiling of the heart) is the Islamic parallel. This is the vault's cleanest ancient → modern depth-psychology bridge: the primary Gnostic text, the Jungian interpretation, and the Islamic parallel are all vault-resident.
