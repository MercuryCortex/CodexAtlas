# De Iside et Osiride

## What it is
*De Iside et Osiride* is [[plutarch]]'s essay-treatise on the Egyptian myth of Osiris, Isis, Set/Typhon, and Horus, written c. 100–120 CE and addressed to Clea, a priestess at Delphi and an Isiac initiate. It is the **single most complete surviving narrative of the Osiris myth from antiquity**, and the principal Greek-language interpretation of Egyptian religion read continuously from antiquity through the modern Egyptological tradition. The work has three layers: (1) a connected narrative of the Osiris myth (chs. 12–20); (2) a philological-antiquarian discussion of Egyptian priestly customs, taboos, and etymologies (chs. 2–11, 28–32); (3) a Platonist-philosophical allegoresis identifying Osiris with the intelligible principle, Isis with formless matter receiving form, and Typhon/Set with the irrational disruptive principle (chs. 45–67).

## Context
- **Political milieu.** High Roman Empire, reign of Trajan / early Hadrian. Egypt is a Roman province; the Isis-Serapis cult is widespread across the empire (well-attested in Rome, Pompeii, Britain, Asia Minor).
- **Religious milieu.** Middle Platonism is the dominant philosophical theology among Greek-educated literati; mystery cults (Isis, Mithras, Eleusinian, Dionysian) coexist and partly compete; the earliest Hermetic literature is being composed in Egypt in the same generation ([[phase-3-021-hermetic-corpus-earliest]], [[phase-4-011-corpus-hermeticum-i]]); Christianity is a minority sect not yet on Plutarch's intellectual horizon.
- **Who was behind it.** Plutarch himself — a Delphic priest writing for an Isiac priestess, locating Egyptian theology inside a Platonist universalist framework where every people's gods point toward the same intelligible structures.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous Byzantine transmission as part of the *Moralia* corpus. First printed edition: Aldine *Moralia* (Venice 1509). Read intensively by [[marsilio-ficino]], [[giordano-bruno]], and the Hermetic Renaissance; cited in [[phase-6-024-kircher-oedipus-aegyptiacus]].

## Themes and motifs
- [[syncretism-egyptian-greek]] — Plutarch explicitly identifies Osiris with Dionysus (ch. 35), Isis with Demeter (ch. 60), Horus with Apollo (ch. 12), Thoth with Hermes (ch. 3), the Egyptian Typhon with the Greek Typhon (ch. 30). This is the locus classicus of *interpretatio graeca* applied to Egyptian theology.
- [[allegorical-exegesis]] — myth is read as a coded statement of philosophical truth; the narrative surface is dispensable.
- [[dualism-cosmic]] — Plutarch deploys a strong cosmic dualism: Osiris/Isis represent intelligible order and the receptive principle, Typhon/Set represent irrationality and the disruptive principle. He explicitly invokes Zoroastrian dualism (chs. 46–47), citing Ahura Mazda and Ahriman as parallels — one of antiquity's clearest cross-religious dualist comparisons.
- [[mystery-initiation]] — the work is structured as initiatory instruction for a priestess; Plutarch frames "knowing the truth about the gods" as the ultimate mystery.
- [[dying-rising-god]] — Osiris's dismemberment, Isis's reassembly, his afterlife rule of the dead, and Horus's birth as his posthumous son are the most-quoted ancient narrative of the type.

## Disputes
- **How "Egyptian" is the Osiris narrative?** Plutarch's connected narrative does not match any single Egyptian source; it represents either a Ptolemaic-era priestly synthesis transmitted to Plutarch by Egyptian informants (Griffiths 1970; Quack 2002), or a Plutarchan literary construction from disparate Egyptian fragments (Hornung 1982 leans skeptical), or a faithful echo of a now-lost oral priestly tradition. Most current Egyptologists treat it as substantially Egyptian in core narrative, Greek-philosophical in framing.
- **Plutarch's source(s).** Manetho ([[manetho]]) is the most-cited candidate for Plutarch's Egyptian-historical material; Eudoxus of Cnidus (4th c. BCE) and Hecataeus of Abdera (early Ptolemaic) are also proposed. Plutarch himself names Manetho repeatedly (chs. 9, 28, 49, 62).
- **Is Plutarch sympathetic or polemical toward Egypt?** The text is mostly sympathetic but distances itself from Egyptian zoolatry (the animal-headed gods) by reducing it to symbol. Whether this counts as colonialist condescension, Platonist universalism, or genuine inter-religious engagement is a live argument (Dillon 1996; Frankfurter 1998).
