# Procopius, De Aedificiis (On the Buildings)

## What it is
**De Aedificiis** ("On the Buildings"), written ~554–560 CE by **Procopius of Caesarea**, is a panegyric in (probably) six books cataloguing the vast construction programme of the emperor **[[justinian-i]]** — churches, fortifications, aqueducts, bridges, and cities across the empire. It is the single most important contemporary source for the building of **Hagia Sophia**, the great domed church of Constantinople ([[event-hagia-sophia-construction-537]]), whose architects Anthemius of Tralles and Isidore of Miletus Procopius names, and whose dome he famously describes as seeming "not to rest upon solid masonry, but to cover the space with its golden dome suspended from Heaven." The work stands in pointed contrast to the same author's scabrous *Secret History*: where that text vilifies Justinian, *De Aedificiis* glorifies him as a pious builder-emperor.

## Significance
De Aedificiis is the founding text of Byzantine architectural description and the indispensable witness to Justinian's program of imperial-Christian monumentalism — the fusion of empire and church expressed in stone ([[divine-kingship]]). Procopius's ekphrasis of Hagia Sophia shaped how Byzantium, and later observers, understood sacred architecture as an image of heaven. The standard study is Averil Cameron's.
