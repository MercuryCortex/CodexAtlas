# Coptic Asclepius (NHC VI,8) — including the Hermetic "Egyptian Apocalypse"

## What it is
A Coptic translation of the central section (~§§21–29) of the Greek Hermetic dialogue known in Latin as the *Asclepius* (Greek title: *Logos Teleios* — "Perfect Discourse"). The Greek original is lost. Two daughter-witnesses survive: the **Latin Asclepius** (preserved continuously since late antiquity — see [[phase-4-012-asclepius]]) which gives us the entire dialogue, and this **Coptic Asclepius** at NHC VI,8 which gives us only the central section but in Coptic, providing crucial textual control on the Latin and an early-witness to the Hermetica's circulation in Coptic Christian Egypt.

The most consequential single passage in this section — and perhaps in all of Hermetic literature — is the **Egyptian Apocalypse** at §§24–26 (Latin §§24–26 = Coptic 70.3–73.22): a prophecy attributed to Hermes that warns of the future death of Egyptian religion. The gods will withdraw from Egypt; the sacred temples will be turned into tombs; pious foreigners will inhabit the land but will not honor the old gods; the divinely-ordered cosmos of Egyptian religious civilization will be inverted. The passage is striking because:

1. It is a *pagan apocalypse* (predicting cosmic disorder caused by the abandonment of pagan religion) — a literary register usually associated with Jewish-Christian apocalyptic.
2. It was read by 4th–5th-c. **Christian** authors as a *prophecy of Christianity itself* — the "foreigners" who came to Egypt and disregarded the old gods are the Christians.
3. **Augustine's *City of God* VIII.23–26** polemically engages this exact passage at length, making it one of the most-discussed pagan-religious texts in patristic Christian theology. Augustine takes the passage as Hermes inadvertently prophesying his own religion's defeat.

## Primary text — selected excerpts

The excerpts use Meyer 2007 for the Coptic (English from Coptic), Copenhaver 1992 for the Latin parallel.

### NHC VI,8 70.3–22 (= Latin Asclepius §24) — the Egyptian Apocalypse begins

> Or do you not know, Asclepius, that Egypt is the image of heaven? Or, what is more, that it is the dwelling-place of heaven and all the powers that are in heaven? If it is proper for us to speak the truth, our land has become the temple of the world.
>
> And yet — since it is fitting that wise people know all things in advance — concerning this you must not remain ignorant: there will come a time when it will be apparent that the Egyptians, all in vain, with such piety honored the divine, and all their holy worship will become unfruitful. For all divinity will leave Egypt and will flee upward to heaven. And Egypt will be widowed; it will be abandoned by the gods.

Citation: NHC VI,8 70.3–22, trans. Meyer 2007; cf. Latin Asclepius §24, Copenhaver 1992 pp. 81–82.

### NHC VI,8 71.5–32 (= Latin Asclepius §24–25) — the inversion of the cosmos

> O Egypt, Egypt, of your religions only fables will be left, and even these will be unbelieved by your descendants. Only the words cut into the stones will remain, telling of your pious deeds. And the Scythian or the Indian, or some other neighbor barbarian, will dwell in Egypt.
>
> For divinity will go up to heaven, and Egypt will be widowed. The land that was the seat of religious sanctuaries will be filled with corpses and tombs. O Egypt, Egypt, you who once were the abode of the gods!

Citation: NHC VI,8 71.5–32, trans. Meyer 2007; cf. Latin Asclepius §24–25, Copenhaver 1992 p. 82.

### NHC VI,8 73.23–76.1 — concluding metaphysics

The closing of the Coptic excerpt returns from the apocalyptic to general Hermetic doctrine: God is unbegotten Lord of all; the cosmos is the second god, image of the first; humanity is the third god, image of the second; matter is the substrate. **This is the *deus terrenus* doctrine of the Hermetica — humanity as 'earthly god' — that Renaissance Hermeticism (Pico's *Oration on the Dignity of Man* 1486) later made canonical.**

> God, the master and progenitor or father, who is the source of all... and the cosmos, his offspring, and humanity, the offspring of the cosmos. The ancients have called humanity *cosmos* because of his close kinship with the cosmos.

Citation: NHC VI,8 75.5–18, trans. Meyer 2007.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Composed in late 3rd-c. Roman Egypt; the **Egyptian Apocalypse** passage is widely read as a real Egyptian-priestly lament for the late-Roman / early-Christian dismantling of Egyptian temple religion. Compare the *Demotic Chronicle*, *Oracle of the Lamb*, *Potter's Oracle* — Egyptian pagan-apocalyptic literature reacting to Greco-Roman occupation. Coptic deposit at Nag Hammadi mid-4th c.
- **Religious milieu.** Pagan Greco-Egyptian Hermetic religion under increasing pressure from Christianized Roman state apparatus; the lament at §§24–26 is the Hermetic community's elegiac response to the rapid ascent of Christianity in Egypt (Edict of Theodosius 391; closure of Egyptian temples).
- **Who was behind it.** Egyptian-priestly Hermetic teachers; transmission into Coptic Christian monastic-adjacent context within ~50 years of composition is itself a striking transmission fact.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Two parallel lines: (1) Latin transmission continuous since late antiquity (Apuleian corpus, then medieval Latin philosophical libraries; printed by Lefèvre d'Étaples 1505); (2) Coptic deposit at Nag Hammadi rediscovered 1945; published by Krause-Labib 1971; critical edition Parrott 1979.

## Disputes
- **The Egyptian Apocalypse: late insertion or original to the Greek Vorlage?** Mahé 1978 argues for original; some older scholarship (Reitzenstein) thought it a 3rd-c. addition. Modern consensus: original to the Greek Asclepius.
- **Christian or pagan composition?** Definitively pagan. The Coptic translation is a *Christian-monastic-adjacent transmission* of pagan religious philosophy, not a Christian rewrite. Mahé 1978 establishes this against older Reitzensteinian readings.
- **Why is it in Nag Hammadi VI?** Lundhaug-Jenott 2015 argue Nag Hammadi codices were assembled by Pachomian Christian monks for whom this counted as ascetic-philosophical reading; Khosroyev 1995 etc. argue an outsider-collector hoard. The Hermetic-Christian engagement is real in either case.
