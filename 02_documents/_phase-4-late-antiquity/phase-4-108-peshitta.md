# Peshitta (Syriac Bible)

**Peshitta** (Syriac "simple" or "common") — the standard Bible of Syriac-speaking Christianity — is the oldest Bible translation in continuous liturgical use, predating even the Latin Vulgate. The Old Testament was probably translated in stages by Jewish communities in Edessa (Urfa) and surrounding regions during the 1st–2nd centuries CE, with possible Targum influence; the New Testament was standardized by Rabbula of Edessa (~411–435 CE) as the *Peshitta NT*, which omitted the books of 2 Peter, 2–3 John, Jude, and Revelation (books the Western church considers canonical). The Peshitta remains the liturgical scripture of the Syriac Orthodox Church, Maronite Church, Chaldean Catholic Church, and Church of the East.

## Canon peculiarity: the shorter NT

The Church of the East (*Nestorian*) and Eastern Syriac tradition uses a NT canon of only 22 books — the 27-book Western NT minus 2 Peter, 2–3 John, Jude, and Revelation. This is not a heretical reduction but a preservation of the canon as it stood before Western and Byzantine Christianity added those books to the universally-accepted list. The difference demonstrates that the NT canon was not uniform in antiquity: what counts as "the Bible" varied by region and tradition well into the 5th century.

## Cross-tradition significance: the transmission belt into Asia

The Peshitta is the Bible that travelled the Silk Road. Syriac Christianity — primarily via the Church of the East (historically called "Nestorian," though the label is contested) — was the vehicle for Christianity reaching:
- **Persia** (the Sasanian Empire; the Church of the East had its Catholicosate at Ctesiphon/Baghdad)
- **India** (the Saint Thomas Christians / Nasrani of Kerala, who trace their origin to the apostle Thomas and have used the Peshitta since antiquity — the oldest extra-Palestinian Christian community in the world)
- **Central Asia** (Christian communities along the Silk Road from Antioch to Samarkand to Dunhuang)
- **China** (the Tang-dynasty *Jingjiao* stele, 781 CE, documents Nestorian Christianity in Chang'an; the Syriac missionaries brought Peshitta manuscripts to China in 635 CE)
- **Ethiopia** (Syriac monastic tradition was one vector for early Christianity into Aksum alongside Greek)

The Peshitta-carrying Church of the East was, for several centuries, the geographically largest Christian denomination in the world — from the Mediterranean to Beijing. Its near-total destruction by Tamerlane's Mongol armies in the 14th century is one of the most under-documented religious catastrophes in world history.

## Textual significance: Aramaic and Jesus's language

Syriac is an Eastern Aramaic dialect closely related to the Aramaic that Jesus spoke. The Peshitta NT therefore provides a linguistic bridge between the Greek NT and the spoken language of the earliest Christian community — a point of cross-tradition significance for understanding Aramaic targum influence on early Christianity, the bilingual Jewish-Aramaic environment of 1st-century Palestine, and the transmission from spoken teaching to written Greek text.
