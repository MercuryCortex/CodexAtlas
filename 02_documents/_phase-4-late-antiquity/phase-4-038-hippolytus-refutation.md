# Refutation of All Heresies

## What it is
A ten-book heresiological treatise (~+220) attributed to [[hippolytus-of-rome]]. The text's principal argumentative move is to demonstrate that all Christian heresies derive their doctrines not from divine revelation but from **Greek philosophical schools** — Plato, Aristotle, the Stoics, the Pythagoreans, Heraclitus, and the Mystery cults. Books 1–4 catalogue Greek philosophy, astrology, magic, and the mysteries to set up the comparison; books 5–9 treat the heretics, organized by ascribed philosophical derivation. Book 10 summarizes. The work is **one of the principal sources for reconstructing 2nd-century Gnostic systems** — particularly Sethian (book 5), Valentinian (book 6), Basilidean (book 7), and Modalist (book 9) — and especially valuable because Hippolytus often quotes verbatim from his targets' own writings.

## Context
- **Date.** ~+220, during Hippolytus's schism from Pope Callistus.
- **Author.** Hippolytus is the traditional attribution; the "Hippolytus problem" (Cerrato 2002) raises school-authorship as alternative.
- **Polemical context.** Hippolytus is fighting on two fronts: (a) the older Gnostic-leaning heresies; (b) the Modalist Monarchianism of Noetus, Cleomenes, Epigonus, and (per Hippolytus's polemic) Callistus himself. Book 9 turns to the contemporary Roman Modalist controversy.
- **Discovery history.** Book 1 known throughout the Middle Ages as Origen's *Philosophoumena*. Books 4–10 rediscovered 1842 on Mount Athos by a Greek scholar; published 1851. Books 2–3 lost.

## Themes and motifs
- [[orthodoxy-vs-heresy]] — Hippolytus's central thesis: heresies are *derivative* of Greek philosophy, hence non-apostolic, hence false.
- **Genealogical heresiology**: each heresy is traced to a Greek philosopher (Marcion → Empedocles; Valentinus → Pythagoras + Plato; Basilides → Aristotle; etc.). The connections are often forced but historically valuable.
- **Verbatim quotation**: Hippolytus's method of quoting at length before refuting makes the *Refutation* a critical source for material otherwise lost.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-4-023-irenaeus-against-heresies]] — *commentary-on* / *redaction-of*: Hippolytus uses Irenaeus extensively, but goes further in connecting each heresy to a specific philosophical school.
- → Sethian texts (NHC II,4 *Hypostasis of the Archons*, NHC II,5 *On the Origin of the World*, NHC IX,3 *Testimony of Truth*) — *external-attestation*: book 5 preserves a Sethian Naassene psalm and other material vindicated by Nag Hammadi.
- → [[valentinus]], [[heracleon-valentinian]], [[ptolemaeus-valentinian]] — *external-attestation*: book 6's Valentinian material complements Irenaeus's.
- → [[basilides]] — *external-attestation*: book 7's Basilidean material is closer to (per Löhr 1996) the genuine Basilides than Irenaeus's account.
- → [[noetus-of-smyrna]], [[sabellius]], [[praxeas]] — *external-attestation*: book 9's Modalist material is the principal contemporary source.
- → [[phase-4-011-corpus-hermeticum-i]] — *parallel-tradition*: Hippolytus knows Hermetic material and engages it.

## Disputes
- **Authorship**: the "Hippolytus problem" (one Roman author or multiple) remains unsettled.
- **Reliability**: Hippolytus's identifications of heresy-with-Greek-philosophy are tendentious; his summaries of doctrine are often more reliable than his genealogies.
- **Date of composition**: ~+220 mainstream.
- **Title**: original title likely *Kata pasōn haireseōn elenchos* ("Refutation of All Heresies"); the medieval *Philosophoumena* title attached to book 1 may have been from a separate edition or a Stoic-doxographic reuse.
