# Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra (*Descent into Lanka Sūtra*)

## Overview

The *Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra* (楞伽經, *Léngqié Jīng* — "Descent into Lanka Sūtra") is one of the most philosophically dense and historically pivotal texts of Mahayana Buddhism. Composed in India around the 4th century CE and translated into Chinese multiple times (Gunabhadra, 443 CE; Bodhiruci, 513 CE; Śikṣānanda, ~700 CE), it presents the most comprehensive Indian Buddhist formulation of **consciousness-only (*vijñānavāda* / *cittamātra*)** philosophy: external reality as we experience it is a projection of the *ālayavijñāna* (storehouse consciousness / "the ground of all consciousness"); all apparent phenomena are "mind only" (*cittamātra*).

The text occupies a unique position: it is simultaneously a sophisticated Yogācāra philosophical treatise, a *tathāgatagarbha* ("Buddha-nature" / "Buddha-embryo") text, and the scripture most closely associated with the founding of Chan (Zen) Buddhism in China. The legendary figure [[bodhidharma]] — the Indian monk who is said to have transmitted the "mind-ground" (*xīndì*) teaching to Huike (~520–530 CE) — reportedly entrusted the Laṅkāvatāra as the only scripture necessary: "Four volumes of the Laṅkāvatāra, in which the Buddha-mind is completely expressed — transmit them to the new patriarch."

## Composition

The Laṅkāvatāra resists linear composition analysis because it appears internally composite — different sections employ different philosophical vocabularies (Yogācāra consciousness-analysis; Madhyamaka emptiness-language; *tathāgatagarbha* Buddha-nature). Modern scholars (Suzuki, Lusthaus, Kalupahana) regard it as a compilation assembled c. 4th c. CE, with the *ālayavijñāna* doctrine as its most original systematic contribution. Its dialogue format — the Buddha answering Rāvaṇa's questions on the peak of Lanka — is largely rhetorical scaffolding for dense philosophical exposition.

Gunabhadra's 4-fascicle Chinese translation (443 CE) was the version connected to Bodhidharma's transmission and thus foundational for Chan. Bodhiruci's 10-fascicle version (513 CE) is more complete and is the basis for most scholarly study.

## Contents

The text is organized around Rāvaṇa's extended questioning of the Buddha, covering:

**The Eight Consciousnesses (*aṣṭa-vijñāna*):** The standard Yogācāra analysis of mental operation into eight streams: five sensory consciousnesses, the integrating mind-consciousness (manovijñāna), the ego-constituting self-cognition (manas), and the foundational *ālayavijñāna* (storehouse consciousness). All experienced phenomena — what appears as the "external world" — are projections arising from seeds (*bīja*) stored in the *ālayavijñāna* and maturing as experience.

**Consciousness-only (*cittamātra*):** The sutra's central claim: "The triple world is mind only (*cittamātra*)." No phenomenon has existence independent of the knowing consciousness that perceives it. This is not solipsism (there is no fixed individual self either — the *ālayavijñāna* is itself empty of inherent selfhood) but a rigorous phenomenological claim: the "external world" is a cognitive construction.

***Tathāgatagarbha* (Buddha-embryo / Buddha-nature):** The sutra introduces the doctrine that all beings contain the *tathāgatagarbha* — the embryonic seed of Buddhahood. This teaching is carefully distinguished from the Brahmanical *ātman* (self): the *tathāgatagarbha* is "empty of self" but is the functional ground of enlightenment. The Laṅkāvatāra's treatment became a major reference point for East Asian debates about Buddha-nature.

**The Five Dharmas, Three Self-natures, Eight Consciousnesses, Two Kinds of No-Self:** These four taxonomic systems are repeated throughout as the sutra's analytical framework for liberation.

## Defining contributions

**1. *Ālayavijñāna* (Storehouse Consciousness):** The Laṅkāvatāra gives the most philosophically elaborated early account of the *ālayavijñāna* — the deep underlying mental process that stores karmic seeds, generates experienced phenomena, and underlies personal identity across lifetimes. This concept has no exact parallel in pre-Buddhist Indian philosophy and represents a distinctively Buddhist contribution to the philosophy of mind.

**2. Consciousness-only (*vijñānavāda*) as complete philosophical system:** The text argues that all apparent ontology collapses into epistemology: we never encounter "things in themselves" but only mind-projections. This position was elaborated by Asanga and Vasubandhu into the full Yogācāra school.

**3. The missing link to Chan/Zen:** The Laṅkāvatāra's emphasis on the "turning of the ground-consciousness" (*āśraya-parāvṛtti*) — sudden, non-conceptual realization in which the storehouse consciousness is transformed into mirror-like wisdom — provided the philosophical foundation for Chan's emphasis on sudden enlightenment and the "mind-ground" (*xīndì*) as the locus of practice. Before the Diamond Sutra became the Chan canonical text, the Laṅkāvatāra was the school's primary scripture.

## Relations to neighbors

**MASSIVE-win edge — *vijñānavāda* parallels Berkeley, Kant, and Advaita Vedanta:** The Laṅkāvatāra's consciousness-only claim — that what we perceive as external reality is a projection of storehouse consciousness — parallels three independently developed Western and Hindu metaphysical positions:
- **Berkeley's subjective idealism** (*Principles of Human Knowledge*, 1710): "esse est percipi" (to be is to be perceived); the external world has no existence independent of mind. The Laṅkāvatāra's *cittamātra* is structurally identical, though the Laṅkāvatāra avoids Berkeley's solipsism by denying that the perceiving consciousness is a fixed self.
- **Kant's transcendental idealism** (*Critique of Pure Reason*, 1781): phenomenal reality is mind-structured (space, time, causality are forms of intuition and categories of understanding); the "thing in itself" (*Ding an sich*) is unknowable. The Laṅkāvatāra's *ālayavijñāna*-generated world is structurally similar: the "external" world as we experience it is generated by our cognitive apparatus, not encountered directly.
- **Advaita Vedanta's *māyā* doctrine** ([[phase-5-005-shankara-brahma-sutra-bhasya]] — Shankara, 8th c.): the empirical world is *māyā* (appearance), ultimately reducible to Brahman-consciousness. The structural parallel to *cittamātra* is very close; D.T. Suzuki (1930) noted careful distinctions: the *ālayavijñāna* is not the personal *ātman* and is itself empty, distinguishing it from the Advaita's Brahman-as-permanent-self. See [[idealism-metaphysical]].

**→ [[bodhidharma]]** — *foundational-transmission*: The legendary Chan founder reportedly transmitted this text alone as the "seal of the mind" to his successor; the early Chan school was called the "Laṅkāvatāra school."

**→ [[tradition-zen]]** — *proto-foundational-text*: The Laṅkāvatāra's non-conceptual mind-ground (*xīndì*) teaching directly seeded Chan's radical immediacy before the Diamond Sutra displaced it as Chan's canonical text.

**→ [[emptiness-sunyata]]** — *philosophical-expression*: The sutra integrates Madhyamaka *śūnyatā* with Yogācāra consciousness-analysis, arguing that the emptiness of the *ālayavijñāna*-generated world is the same truth as Nāgārjuna's emptiness of inherent existence.

**→ [[phase-5-005-shankara-brahma-sutra-bhasya]]** — *parallel-form*: Shankara's Advaita and Yogācāra *vijñānavāda* are the two most developed ancient idealist metaphysical systems; whether Shankara engaged with Yogācāra or developed Advaita independently against Buddhist idealism is a long-standing debate (Sengaku Mayeda, Eliot Deutsch).
