# Quran

## What it is
The foundational scripture of Islam — 114 *suras* of revelation traditionally held to have been received by Muhammad ibn Abdullah in Mecca and Medina between 610 and 632 CE, codified under Caliph Uthman ~650. The text is held by Islamic tradition to be uncreated divine speech (kalām Allāh) in Arabic. Content includes: rigorous monotheistic theology and polemic against polytheism and against perceived Christian deviations into tritheism; eschatological warnings about the Last Day and final judgment; narrative re-engagements with biblical and extra-biblical figures (Adam, Noah, Abraham, Joseph, Moses, David, Solomon, Jesus, Mary); ethical and legal injunctions; the foundational ritual obligations.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Late-antique western Arabia between the Byzantine Empire and the Sasanian Empire, both exhausted by the long Roman-Persian wars. The Hijaz had Jewish, Christian, and traditional Arabian polytheist populations.
- **Religious milieu.** Pre-Islamic Hijaz had a high god [[allah|*Allāh*]] known to [[tradition-pre-islamic-arabian|traditional Arabian religion]] ([[allat|al-Lāt]], [[al-uzza|al-ʿUzzā]], [[manat|Manāt]] as major goddesses-and-"daughters of Allāh"; cf. the [[satanic-verses-incident|disputed *gharānīq* / Satanic Verses tradition at Sura 53]]; [[hubal|Hubal]] as principal Kaaba cult-image; the Quran-71:23 antediluvian pantheon — [[wadd|Wadd]] / Suwāʿ / Yaghūth / Yaʿūq / [[nasr-pre-islamic|Nasr]]). The [[hanif-monotheism|*ḥanīf*-tradition]] of late-antique Arabian Abrahamic monotheists (Waraqa ibn Nawfal — Khadīja's Christian-Arab cousin; Zayd ibn ʿAmr ibn Nufayl) is the religious bridge. Jewish (Yathrib/Medina) and Christian (Najran, Yemen, the Syrian frontier) populations were present. Aksumite Christian Yemen ([[event-kaleb-aksum-himyar-invasion-525]]) frames the immediate geopolitical context. Late-antique scholarship (Donner, Hoyland, Reynolds, Crone, Wansbrough) has progressively contextualized the Quran in late-antique biblical and post-biblical religious discourse rather than treating it as a sudden eruption.
- **Who was behind it.** In Islamic tradition, God speaking through Muhammad. In secular historical analysis, the Quran is a textual product of the Hijazi community around Muhammad and the early caliphate, redacted under Uthman.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous Islamic transmission. Early manuscripts (Birmingham, Sanaa) demonstrate the substantial stability of the Uthmanic text from the mid-7th century. *We catalog the Quran as primary historical evidence for the religious life of its time*, in line with the vault's source-integrity policy that applies equal evidentiary respect to all foundational religious texts. **No claim is made here about the theological status of the text.**

## Themes and motifs
- [[hidden-god]] — uncompromising apophatic monotheism: "There is no thing like Him" (42:11).
- [[end-times-judgment]] — the Last Day (yawm al-dīn) is one of the most consistent and emphatic themes, especially in the Meccan suras.
- [[chosen-people]] — the *umma* (Muslim community); Abrahamic continuity is claimed and reframed.
- [[divine-kingship]] — God as *Malik* (King) of the Day of Judgment.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-2-005-hebrew-bible-j-source]] — *parallel-motif* / *direct-engagement*: the Quran engages biblical figures and narratives but reframes them; Mary the mother of Jesus has her own sura (Sura 19); Joseph's story is the longest continuous narrative (Sura 12).
- → [[phase-3-016-gospel-of-mark]] — *parallel-motif* / *partial-polemic*: Jesus (Isa) is honored as a prophet and miracle-worker but his divine sonship and crucifixion are denied (Sura 4:157, 4:171; 5:75).
- → Syriac Christian homiletic tradition — *parallel-motif*: late-antique Syriac homiletic literature (Ephrem, Jacob of Serugh) shares storytelling motifs with Quranic narratives — well documented by Griffith and Reynolds.
- → [[phase-4-018-mandaean-book-of-john]] — *contemporary*: emerges in roughly the same period; Mandaeans (likely the *Sabians* of the Quran 2:62, 5:69, 22:17) were among the protected religious communities (*ahl al-kitāb*).
- → [[event-hegira-622]] — *event-context*: the migration from Mecca to Medina is the canonical Year 1 of the Islamic calendar.

## Disputes
- **Origin and redaction.** Traditional view: revealed 610–632, codified under Uthman ~650. Revisionist views (Wansbrough 1977, Crone-Cook 1977) proposed much later redaction; subsequent manuscript evidence (Birmingham, Sanaa) has constrained the most radical revisions; current critical scholarship (Donner, Sinai, Neuwirth) accepts a 7th-century redaction substantially close to the traditional account, with disagreements about the speed and details.
- **Pre-Islamic Allah.** Whether the pre-Islamic Hijazi *Allāh* was a high god already with monotheist features (Welch, Hawting) or a generic chief deity raised to exclusive monotheism by Muhammad. The vault keeps separate the deity-node [[allah]] with explicit syncretic-edges noting the Semitic linguistic continuity with *El* / *Elah* / *Elohim*.
