# Passio Perpetuae et Felicitatis

## What it is
A martyrological account (~+203/+210) of the imprisonment and execution of [[perpetua-and-felicity|Perpetua, Felicity, and four male companions]] at Carthage on March 7, 203, under [[septimius-severus]]'s persecution. The text is **uniquely valuable** in the early-Christian corpus for embedding **Perpetua's first-person prison diary** (chs 3–10) and **Saturus's first-person vision-narrative** (chs 11–13), making it the earliest extant Christian autobiographical writing by a woman *and* one of the few first-person Christian texts from before Augustine's *Confessions*.

## Context
- **Date.** ~+203 for the underlying events; the framing prologue and conclusion were added shortly after, probably ~+205/+210.
- **Place.** Carthage, the capital of Roman Africa Proconsularis, a major center of 3rd-c. Latin Christianity.
- **Authorship.** Composite: (a) an editor (anonymous; possibly Tertullian or someone in his circle — Heffernan 2012 and Cobb 2017 are cautious on this; mainstream view: probably not Tertullian); (b) Perpetua herself (chs 3–10); (c) Saturus (chs 11–13).
- **Political milieu.** Severan-era persecution targeting catechumens. The North African church under intense pressure; Tertullian active.
- **Theological character.** New-Prophecy / Montanism-friendly: the text accepts continuing revelatory authority (Perpetua's four prison visions) as authoritative; the prologue explicitly cites Acts 2 / Joel 2 on Spirit-poured-out-on-sons-and-daughters as warrant. Trevett 1996 is cautious about reading the text as wholly Montanist; Heine 1989 reads it more strongly so.

## Themes and motifs
- [[martyrdom-theology]] — Christ-imitative suffering; the *passio* / *passion* parallel between Christ and martyr is explicitly developed.
- **Visionary theology**: Perpetua's four visions (Jacob's-ladder ascent; the dead brother Dinocrates suffering then relieved; second Dinocrates vision; the agonal vision of an Egyptian fighter as the devil) — exceptional access to early-Christian prison-vision phenomena.
- **Women's revelatory authority**: Perpetua receives directly from God; she does not defer to her catechist Saturus on the visions.
- **Family / father conflict**: Perpetua's father attempts to dissuade her three times, appealing to his age, her infant, family honor — she rebuffs him each time, the most extended *abandoning-father-for-Christ* scene in early Christian literature.
- **Class and slavery**: Perpetua (noblewoman) and Felicity (enslaved, pregnant) — the *Passio* makes explicit that they walk into the arena hand-in-hand as equals in Christ.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-4-035-acts-of-paul-and-thecla]] — *parallel-genre*: female martyr / women's-authority narrative, but in genuinely autobiographical mode vs. romance.
- → *Martyrdom of Polycarp* (~+156) — *genre-precedent*: structures of arrest, trial, refusal-to-recant, miraculous-detail, communal commemoration are paralleled.
- → *Letter of the Churches of Lyons and Vienne* (177) — *parallel-text*: female martyr at the center ([[blandina]]); North African vs. Gaulish data points on women in 2nd-c. martyrology.
- → Augustine — *commentary-on*: Augustine preached annually on Perpetua's feast day (*Sermons* 280–282), making the *Passio* an authoritative North African text.
- → [[tertullian]] *De anima* 55 — references Perpetua's paradise-vision; provides external attestation.

## Disputes
- **Editor identity.** Tertullian? Heffernan and Cobb caution; style differences argue against. Possibly someone in Tertullian's circle.
- **Date of redaction**: ~+203 for events; ~+205/+210 for the framing.
- **Montanist or proto-orthodox**: the New-Prophecy-friendly features are real but the text was preserved by proto-orthodox North African Christianity.
- **Authenticity of Perpetua's diary**: nearly universally accepted as genuinely first-person. Some recent scholarship (Heffernan) has raised cautions but mainstream is firm.
