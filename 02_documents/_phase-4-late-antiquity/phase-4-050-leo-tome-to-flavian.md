# Tome of Leo (Letter 28 to Flavian of Constantinople)

## What it is
A doctrinal letter sent in June 449 by Leo I, bishop of Rome, to Flavian, archbishop of Constantinople, intervening in the case of the Constantinopolitan archimandrite Eutyches, who had been condemned by Flavian's synod (November 448) for teaching that Christ's humanity was absorbed into a single divine nature after the union. The Tome states in compact Latin a Christology of *two natures in one person*: Christ is *true God and true man*, the divine and human natures preserved entire and unconfused in a single *persona*, each nature performing what is proper to itself in communion with the other. The Tome was suppressed at the so-called *Latrocinium* (Robber Council) of Ephesus August 449, but read aloud and acclaimed at Chalcedon two years later (October 451: "Peter has spoken through Leo!"), where it became one of the two doctrinal pillars (with Cyril's letters) of the Chalcedonian Definition.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** The court of Emperor Theodosius II in Constantinople had backed Eutyches; the second Council of Ephesus 449, presided over by Dioscorus of Alexandria, was the Eastern miaphysite reaction to Nestorius's condemnation at the first Ephesus (431). When Theodosius died in July 450 his sister Pulcheria and her husband Marcian reversed imperial policy and convoked Chalcedon, where the Tome could be ratified.
- **Religious milieu.** The fifth-century Christological controversy is the long aftermath of Nicaea: now that the *consubstantiality* of Father and Son is settled, the question is how the divine Son and the man Jesus are united. Antiochene Christology (Theodore of Mopsuestia, Nestorius) emphasizes the two natures and risks dividing the person; Alexandrian Christology (Cyril, Eutyches, Dioscorus) emphasizes the single subject and risks collapsing the humanity into the divinity. The Tome articulates a Western Latin via media that the Greek East had to translate into its own categories.
- **Who was behind it.** Leo himself, working from his Roman archive (Latin Christology since Tertullian, *Adv. Praxean*: *substantia* / *persona*) and his deacon and successor Prosper of Aquitaine (who is sometimes credited with the literary polish). The Tome is the most theologically focused single document of the early Roman papacy.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous. Read aloud at Chalcedon Session II; entered the conciliar *Acta* (Schwartz, ACO II.2.1); subsequently a foundational text of Western Latin Christology.

## Themes and motifs
- [[trinitarian-formula]] — applied at the Christological level: the *persona* of the Son is the single subject of the two natures; the union does not multiply persons within the Trinity.
- [[orthodoxy-vs-heresy]] — Eutyches is named and condemned; the Tome reads as a heresiological intervention with imperial reach.
- [[ecclesial-authority]] / [[ecclesiology]] — the Tome is also a *political* document: Leo writes as the bishop of Rome claiming doctrinal authority over an Eastern controversy. Chalcedon's acclamation ("Peter has spoken through Leo!") is one of the strongest pre-medieval assertions of Roman primacy from an ecumenical council.
- *Communicatio idiomatum* — the famous formula: "each nature acts in communion with the other what is proper to it; the Word performing what is of the Word, and the flesh accomplishing what is of the flesh" (Ep. 28.4). This becomes the standard Latin Christological grammar.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[event-council-of-chalcedon-451]] — *foundational-text*: the Tome is read aloud at Session II and acclaimed; the Chalcedonian Definition is constructed by harmonizing the Tome with Cyril's *Second Letter to Nestorius* and *Letter to John of Antioch*. Ref: Price & Gaddis 2005 vol. 2.
- → [[phase-4-042-athanasius-on-the-incarnation]] — *theological-continuity*: the Tome explicitly invokes *He became human that we might become divine* logic, locating Leo in continuity with Nicene Alexandrian Christology even as the Tome corrects Cyrillian-Eutychian *one nature* talk.
- → Tertullian, *Against Praxeas* — *substrate-influence*: the Latin technical vocabulary (*substantia* / *persona*, the two-natures grammar) is Tertullian's, 250 years earlier; Leo formalizes it. Norris 1980.
- → Cyril of Alexandria, *Twelve Anathemas* and *Second Letter to Nestorius* — *correction-of* / *harmonization-with*: at Chalcedon, the Tome and Cyril's letters are read together; the Definition emerges from harmonizing them, a delicate operation that the Eastern miaphysite churches (Coptic, Ethiopian, Armenian, Syriac Orthodox, Indian Orthodox) ultimately rejected.
- → The *Latrocinium* of Ephesus 449 — *suppressed-at*: Dioscorus refused to allow the Tome to be read; this rupture motivated Leo's repeated demand for a new council, granted under Marcian.
- → [[phase-4-046-john-of-damascus-exposition-orthodox-faith]] — *downstream-systematization*: John of Damascus's Christology III.3–8 systematizes the two-natures grammar that the Tome introduced into the conciliar tradition.

## Substrate and influence — cross-tradition wiring
- **Latin philosophical substrate.** The *persona* / *natura* terminology is Stoic-juristic Latin (Cicero, *De officiis*; Roman law), repurposed by Tertullian and now by Leo for Christological work. The Tome is one of the clearest moments in which Roman legal-philosophical vocabulary becomes Christian theological vocabulary. Studer 1985, Wessel 2008.
- **The Western-Eastern translation problem.** Cyril and Leo were not, in 449, working in identical conceptual frames: Greek *hypostasis* and Latin *persona* did not yet have isomorphic meanings, and Greek *physis* and Latin *natura* were similarly partial overlaps. Chalcedon's Definition is in substantial part a translation exercise across the Greek-Latin philosophical-theological divide.

## Disputes
- **Reception in the non-Chalcedonian churches.** The Coptic, Ethiopian Tewahedo, Armenian Apostolic, Syriac Orthodox, and Malankara Orthodox churches reject the Tome on the grounds that it crypto-Nestorianizes by speaking of "two natures" rather than Cyril's "one incarnate nature of the divine Word." Modern ecumenical dialogue (the 1989/1990 Anba Bishoy and Chambésy agreed statements) has worked to recover the substantial Christological convergence underneath the terminological divergence.
- **Authorship.** Mostly Leo, with Prosper of Aquitaine credited by some with polishing the Latin. Neil 2009 evaluates the candidates.
- **Papal-authority reading.** Whether the Chalcedonian acclamation "Peter has spoken through Leo" amounts to a 5th-century recognition of universal Roman jurisdiction, or simply an honorific deference to a particularly well-argued doctrinal letter, is the long-running confessional dispute.
