# Corpus Hermeticum I (Poimandres)

## What it is
The first and most famous of the seventeen treatises that constitute the philosophical *Corpus Hermeticum*. A first-person account in which the narrator receives a vision of Poimandres ("Man-Shepherd" or, by an alternate etymology, the Egyptian *p-eime n-rē*, "Knowledge of Re") who discloses the origin of the cosmos and humanity. The treatise narrates: a luminous infinite Nous; the emergence of a darkness from which arises material chaos; the Logos descending; the cosmocrator Demiurge fashioning the seven planetary spheres; the primal anthropos descending from the Father through the spheres and being captured by Nature; awakening through self-knowledge; the ascent of the soul back through the seven spheres, shedding a vice at each, to rejoin the Father. The Greek text is dense, scriptural in cadence (with Septuagint Genesis echoes), and explicitly initiatory.

## Primary text — selected excerpts

The excerpts use G. R. S. Mead's *Thrice-Greatest Hermes* (1906, public domain, available at sacred-texts.com and the Internet Archive). Mead is a Theosophical scholar of considerable learning but his translation is dated; for current scholarly use consult Brian Copenhaver's *Hermetica* (Cambridge UP, 1992) and the Nock-Festugière Budé edition (1945–1954). Section numbering follows the Nock-Festugière paragraphing.

### CH I.1–3 — the appearance of Poimandres

The opening: the narrator, in a state of contemplation, has his senses suspended; an immense Being appears and identifies himself as Poimandres ("Man-Shepherd"), the Nous of the Sovereignty, and offers to teach him.

> It chanced once on a time my mind was meditating on the things that are, my thought was raised to a great height, the senses of my body being held back — just as men who are weighed down with sleep after a fill of food, or from fatigue of body. Methought a Being more than vast, in size beyond all bounds, called out my name and saith: What wouldst thou hear and see, and what hast thou in mind to learn and know?
>
> And I do say: Who art thou? He saith: I am Man-Shepherd (Poimandres), Mind of all-masterhood; I know what thou desirest and I'm with thee everywhere.
>
> [And] I reply: I long to learn the things that are, and comprehend their nature, and know God. This is, I said, what I desire to hear. He answered back to me: Hold in thy mind all thou wouldst know, and I will teach thee.

Citation: *Corpus Hermeticum* I.1–3 (Poimandres), trans. G. R. S. Mead 1906, *Thrice-Greatest Hermes* II; for the standard modern rendering see Copenhaver 1992, pp. 1–2.

### CH I.4–6 — the vision of Light, Darkness, and the Logos

The cosmogonic vision proper. The light of Nous-as-Father is followed by a descending darkness, which becomes a watery chaos; from the light a holy Word (Logos) leaps onto the chaos and orders the elements.

> When he had said these words, forthwith all things changed in aspect before me, and were opened out in a moment. And I beheld a boundless view; all was changed into Light, a mild and joyous Light; and I marveled when I saw it. And in a little while, there had arisen in part a downward-tending darkness, terrible and gloomy, coiling sinuously, so that I likened it to a snake.
>
> And then the darkness changed into some sort of a moist nature, tossed about beyond all power of words, belching out smoke as from a fire, and groaning forth a wailing sound that beggars all description...
>
> And out of the Light... a Holy Word (Logos) descended on that Nature. And upwards to the height from the Moist Nature leaped forth pure Fire; light was it, swift and active too. The Air, too, being light, followed after the Fire; from out of the Earth-and-Water rising up to Fire so that it seemed to hang therefrom. But Earth-and-Water stayed so mingled with each other, that Earth from Water no one could discern. Yet were they moved to hear by reason of the Spirit-Word (logos) pervading them.

Citation: CH I.4–5, trans. Mead 1906; cf. Copenhaver 1992, pp. 1–2.

### CH I.12–15 — the descent of Anthropos

The myth of the primal Anthropos: Man, made in the Father's image, looks down through the heavenly spheres, sees his reflection in the lower waters, falls in love with it, and descends — Nature embraces him; the human being is the result, "double in nature, mortal in body, immortal because of essential Man."

> But All-Father Mind, being Life and Light, did bring forth Man co-equal to Himself, with whom He fell in love, as being His own child; for he was beautiful beyond compare, the Image of his Sire... And Man, who had all authority over the cosmos of things mortal and of animals irrational, bent his face downwards through the Harmony, breaking through its vault, and showed to downward Nature God's fair form...
>
> And when she [Nature] saw that Form of beauty which can never satiate, and him who [now] possessed within himself each single energy of [all seven] Rulers as well as God's own Form, she smiled with love; for it was as though she had seen the image of Man's fairest form upon her water, and his shadow on the earth. And he, in turn, beholding the reflection of himself in her, in her water, loved it and yearned to live in it; and with the will came act, and [so] he vivified the form devoid of reason. And Nature took the object of her love and wound herself completely round him, and they were intermingled, for they were lovers.
>
> And this is why beyond all creatures on the earth man is twofold: mortal because of body, but because of the essential Man immortal.

Citation: CH I.12–15, trans. Mead 1906; cf. Copenhaver 1992, pp. 3–4.

### CH I.24–26 — the ascent of the soul through the seven spheres

The Hermetic ladder. At death, the soul ascends through the seven planetary zones, surrendering to each the vice it took on at descent: appetite to the Moon, intrigue to Mercury, lust to Venus, ambition to the Sun, daring to Mars, greed to Jupiter, falsehood to Saturn. The cleansed soul enters the Ogdoad, the eighth realm, and joins the divine song.

> Tell me again the way; how I ascend to Life. Whereto He saith: When thy material body is to be dissolved, first thou surrenderest the body by itself unto the work of change... Thereafter thou dost rise up through the Harmony.
>
> To the first zone thou givest the energy of Growth and Waning; unto the second [zone], device of evils [now] de-energised; unto the third, the guile of the desires de-energised; unto the fourth, one's domineering arrogance, [also] de-energised; unto the fifth, unholy daring and the rashness of audacity, de-energised; unto the sixth, striving for wealth by evil means, deprived of its aggrandizement; and to the seventh zone, ensnaring falsehood, de-energised.
>
> And then, with all the energisings of the Harmony stript from him, clothed in his proper Power, he cometh to that Nature which belongs unto the Eighth, and there with those-that-are hymneth the Father.

Citation: CH I.24–26, trans. Mead 1906; cf. Copenhaver 1992, pp. 5–6.

### CH I.31–32 — the closing hymn

The treatise closes with a hymn of the initiated narrator — the structure (knowing oneself as immortal light, returning thanks, becoming a guide to others) is the template for the Hermetic mystagogic literature that follows.

> Holy art Thou, O God, the universals' Father.
> Holy art Thou, O God, whose Will perfects itself by means of its own Powers.
> Holy art Thou, O God, who willeth to be known and art known by Thine own.
> Holy art Thou, who didst by Word make to consist the things that are.
> Holy art Thou, of whom All-nature hath been made an image.
> Holy art Thou, whose Form Nature hath never made.
> Holy art Thou, more powerful than all power.
> Holy art Thou, transcending all pre-eminence.
> Holy Thou art, Thou better than all praise.
>
> Accept my reason's offerings pure, from soul and heart for aye stretched up to Thee, O Thou unutterable, unspeakable, Whose Name nought but the Silence can express.

Citation: CH I.31–32 (closing hymn), trans. Mead 1906; cf. Copenhaver 1992, pp. 6–7.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Roman Egypt, second to third century. Alexandria as the great syncretic capital — Egyptian priestly traditions, Greek philosophy, Hellenistic Judaism, and emerging Christianity all in dialogue.
- **Religious milieu.** Greek-Egyptian philosophical religion in which the Egyptian god Thoth was Hellenized as Hermes and apotheosized as Hermes Trismegistus, "thrice-great." The Hermetic literature divides into philosophical / "high" Hermetica (Corpus, Asclepius) and "technical" Hermetica (astrology, alchemy, magic) — the boundary is porous.
- **Who was behind it.** Greek-writing Egyptian priests / philosophers; the underlying religious milieu has been linked by Fowden and by Mahé to actual Egyptian temple settings (Hermopolis Magna) where Thoth-priests transmitted teaching.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous Byzantine transmission until Cosimo de' Medici's 1460 acquisition; Ficino's 1471 Latin translation made Hermes Trismegistus a Renaissance authority, treated as a pre-Mosaic prophet of natural revelation until Casaubon's 1614 *De rebus sacris et ecclesiasticis exercitationes* showed the texts to be Hellenistic.

## Themes and motifs
- [[creation-by-word]] — the Logos descends and structures matter; structural parallel to Genesis 1 and John 1.
- [[hidden-god]] — the Nous-Father is unknowable except through the revealing Poimandres.
- [[soul-as-spark]] — humans contain the primal Anthropos' essence, fallen into matter.
- [[psychopomp]] — the soul ascends through the seven planetary spheres, shedding the vices acquired in descent (echoing both Mithraic and later Gnostic ascent literature).
- [[gnosis-as-salvation]] — explicit: salvation is the recognition that "you are immortal and the embrace of cosmic being is your work."

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-2-005-hebrew-bible-j-source]] (LXX) — *direct-quote* / *parallel-motif*: the Hermetic cosmogony shows clear knowledge of Septuagint Genesis 1 ("Let there be light"; the spirit moving over the waters); Fowden 1986 documents the Hellenistic Jewish substratum.
- → [[phase-3-020-gospel-of-john]] — *parallel-motif*: Logos descending into a darkness that does not comprehend it; the grammar is so close that direct Hermetic-Johannine relationship has been argued (Dodd, *The Bible and the Greeks*).
- → [[phase-4-002-apocryphon-of-john]] — *parallel-motif*: revelation-of-cosmogony genre; soul-ascent through hostile spheres.
- → [[phase-3-002-plato-dialogues]] — *direct-influence*: the Hermetic demiurge is the Platonic craftsman, refunctioned positively.
- → Egyptian theological tradition (Memphite Theology, Hermopolis cosmogony) — *direct-influence* (Fowden's claim): the cosmogony preserves Egyptian priestly material under Greek dress.
- → Renaissance Hermeticism (Phase 6) — *direct-influence*: Ficino, Pico, Bruno, and the entire Renaissance esoteric revival.

## Disputes
- **Egyptian vs. Greek substratum.** Festugière weighted Greek-philosophical; Fowden 1986 restored the Egyptian-priestly substratum; current scholarship sees both. Mahé's *Hermès en Haute-Égypte* documents Egyptian-Coptic Hermetic material (incl. NHC VI,6, our Discourse on the Eighth and Ninth — see [[phase-4-013-discourse-on-the-eighth-and-ninth]]).
- **Date.** Most place CH I at 100–200 CE; some treatises in the Corpus are later, into the 3rd century.
- **Christian or non-Christian.** CH I uses no Christian theological vocabulary; it is contemporary with Christianity, not derivative of it.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **Poimandres cosmogonic vision ↔ John prologue ↔ ApocJohn ↔ Brihadaranyaka — the four-tradition opening-vision cluster:** CH I opens with a light-vision followed by cosmogony delivered by a divine being; John 1:1–14 opens with Logos-as-light descending into darkness; ApocJohn opens with a theophany followed by Barbelo's emanation; BṛhUp 1.1 opens with cosmic-horse meditation on correspondences. Four traditions (Egyptian-Greek syncretist, Syriac-Christian, Sethian Gnostic, Vedic Brahmanical), across four continents and one millennium, begin their foundational documents with the same revelation-of-cosmogony-through-vision structure.
- **Casaubon 1614 — the most productive debunking in Western intellectual history:** Isaac Casaubon's *De rebus sacris et ecclesiasticis exercitationes* (1614) demonstrated from internal linguistic evidence that the Corpus Hermeticum was Greek-Roman-era, not pre-Mosaic. This single philological act destroyed Renaissance *prisca theologia* claims but inaugurated the critical-historical study of religious texts — the same method later used on the Donation of Constantine, the Johannine Comma, the Pentateuchal Documentary Hypothesis, and the Zohar (Scholem 1941). The *Pimander* is the vault's canonical case study in both the construction and critical demolition of claimed ancient transmission.
- **Seven-sphere ascent ↔ Mithraic ladder ↔ Tibetan Bardo seven-day cycles ↔ Kabbalistic *tzimtzum*-levels:** the soul sheds a specific vice at each of seven planetary zones during ascent (CH I.24–26); the Mithraic grade-sequence (Celsus reports it in Origen, *Contra Celsum* 6.22) uses the same planetary-gate structure; the Tibetan Bardo counts seven cycles of peaceful and wrathful deity encounters. Three traditions articulate a post-mortem or initiatory journey through seven graduated zones with specific content at each level — same spatial architecture, different cosmological vocabulary.
- **Ficino 1463 → Pico 1486 → Bruno 1584 → Dee 1582 → Rosicrucianism 1614 → Freemasonry 1717:** the CH I is the launch document of the Western esoteric transmission chain. Ficino's Latin translation circulates; Pico builds a Christian-Kabbalistic-Hermetic synthesis; Bruno radicalizes Hermetic infinite-universe cosmology (and is burned 1600); John Dee's Hermetic-mathematical angelic communication system; Rosicrucian manifestos (1614) cite Hermetic philosophy; Speculative Freemasonry's symbolism inherits it. This is the vault's longest documented esoteric transmission chain, fully traceable from a 2nd-century Alexandrian text to 18th-century institutional esotericism.
