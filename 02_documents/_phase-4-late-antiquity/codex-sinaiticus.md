# Codex Sinaiticus

## What it is
One of the **great 4th-century Greek uncial codices** of the Bible, and the **oldest surviving complete New Testament**. Written on fine parchment in an elegant biblical majuscule arranged in four columns to the page, *Sinaiticus* (designated by the Hebrew letter ℵ, *aleph*, or the number 01) originally contained the entire Greek Old Testament (Septuagint), the complete New Testament, and two further early Christian writings — the *Epistle of Barnabas* and the *Shepherd of Hermas* — a vivid snapshot of what counted as scripture in one 4th-century church. It is, with [[codex-vaticanus]], a foundational witness for the Alexandrian text of the New Testament.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** The Constantinian and post-Constantinian empire, when imperial favour made possible the production of large, expensive parchment Bibles.
- **Religious milieu.** A church in the act of fixing its canon: the inclusion of *Barnabas* and *Hermas* shows the boundary of the New Testament still slightly fluid in the period that produced the codex.
- **Who was behind it.** A team of professional scribes and correctors in a major scriptorium (Milne & Skeat reconstructed the hands); the place of production is debated (Caesarea, Alexandria, or Rome).
- **Discovery / transmission.** Brought to scholarly notice by Constantin von Tischendorf at **St Catherine's Monastery on Sinai** from 1844; now split among four collections and digitally reunited (2009).

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[codex-vaticanus]] — *sibling-witness*: the two greatest 4th-century uncials, together the backbone of the Alexandrian text of the New Testament.
- → [[phase-3-006-septuagint]] — *manuscript-transmission*: Sinaiticus is one of the most important complete witnesses to the Greek Old Testament.
- → [[phase-4-095-new-testament-canon]] — *direct-evidence*: its contents (including Barnabas and Hermas) document the still-forming canon.
- → [[phase-4-108-peshitta]] / [[phase-4-037-diatessaron]] — *parallel-witness*: contemporary witnesses to the text and shape of the New Testament in other traditions (Syriac).

## Disputes
- **Place of origin.** Caesarea, Alexandria, and Rome have each been argued; no consensus.
- **Relation to Constantine's "fifty Bibles."** T.C. Skeat proposed that Sinaiticus (and Vaticanus) may be connected to the fifty parchment Bibles Constantine ordered from Eusebius; this remains a debated hypothesis.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **A photograph of the canon mid-formation.** Because Sinaiticus binds the *Epistle of Barnabas* and the *Shepherd of Hermas* into the New Testament, it preserves direct manuscript evidence that the canon's edge was still soft in the 4th century — a concrete witness to the historical process behind [[phase-4-095-new-testament-canon]] (Parker 2010; Metzger & Ehrman 2005).
- **The manuscript that reopened the text of the New Testament.** Tischendorf's recovery of Sinaiticus ([[event-discovery-of-codex-sinaiticus-1844]]) transformed 19th-century New Testament scholarship, giving critics a 4th-century witness against the much later medieval *textus receptus* and helping launch modern critical editions (Parker 2010).
