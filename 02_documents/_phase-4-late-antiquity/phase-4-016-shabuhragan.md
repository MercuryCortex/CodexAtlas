# Šābuhragān

## What it is
Mani's own summary of his religion, written in Middle Persian and dedicated to Shapur I (whose name it bears — *Šābuhr* + *-agān* = "book composed for Shapur"). Composed early in Mani's career (~240–243) to introduce the system to the new Sasanian king of kings. The text laid out Manichaean cosmogony (Light and Darkness, the Father of Greatness and his emanations), anthropology, and especially eschatology — the Turfan fragments preserve a vivid description of the end-time, the coming of the Great King, the Last Judgment, and the cosmic conflagration. Unique among Mani's writings in being in Middle Persian rather than Aramaic.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Sasanian Iran in its founding decades. Shapur I (r. 240–270) reportedly received Mani favorably; Mani traveled in Shapur's retinue and presented him with the Šābuhragān. Mani's protection ended under Shapur's son Bahram I, leading to his arrest and death ~274–277.
- **Religious milieu.** Mani deliberately used Zoroastrian vocabulary (Ohrmazd for the primal man-emanation; Ahriman for the Prince of Darkness) to make his system intelligible to an Iranian elite. The result is a Manichaean *Iranian* re-clothing of a fundamentally Mesopotamian dualistic-Gnostic system.
- **Who was behind it.** Mani himself, addressing a king. One of the few cases where we can name with high confidence both the author and the addressee of a foundational religious text.
- **Discovery / transmission.** The four German Turfan expeditions (1902–1914) recovered Manichaean fragments in Middle Persian, Parthian, Sogdian, Old Turkic, and Chinese from the oasis sites along the Silk Road; the Šābuhragān fragments are part of this find.
