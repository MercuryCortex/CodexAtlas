# Exposition of the Orthodox Faith

## What it is
The systematic-theological core of John of Damascus's *Pege Gnoseos* (*Fount of Knowledge*) — book III of the three-part work, in 100 numbered chapters (or four "books" in the standard Latin tradition). The first comprehensive systematic theology in the Greek patristic tradition: God's nature and Trinity; creation (visible and invisible); the human creature; the incarnation and its consequences; the sacraments; the resurrection. John presents himself in the prologue as a synthesizer ("I will say nothing of my own") and explicitly draws on Cappadocians, Pseudo-Dionysius, Maximus, and Cyril; but the synthesis and the systematic-Aristotelian framework are his own.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Composed under Muslim rule at the Mar Saba monastery in the Judaean desert. The political distance from Byzantium gave John freedom to oppose the iconoclast policy initiated by Emperor Leo III in 726.
- **Religious milieu.** Byzantine Chalcedonian theology in its late patristic synthesis; immediate engagement with the Iconoclast controversy (the *Three Treatises on the Divine Images* are roughly contemporary); first major Christian theological engagement with Islam (*On Heresies* ch. 100/101, the companion book of the *Pege Gnoseos*).
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous Greek transmission. Latin translation by Burgundio of Pisa (1148–1150) introduced it to the medieval Western theological mainstream; Aquinas cites *Damascenus* hundreds of times.

## Themes and motifs
- [[trinitarian-formula]] — systematic articulation of the three *hypostases* in one *ousia*, defending Cappadocian theology with Aristotelian-logical precision.
- [[iconography-and-aniconism]] — Book IV chapters 16 ("On Images") gives the iconophile defense, paralleling the *Three Treatises*.
- [[chain-of-being]] — the cosmological framework: God → angelic ranks (drawing on Pseudo-Dionysius) → human → animal → inanimate.

## Connections (provisional)
- → Cappadocian Fathers — *direct-precursor*: Basil, the Gregories, especially their Trinitarian formulations.
- → [[phase-4-030-pseudo-dionysius]] — *direct-influence*: Dionysian apophasis and hierarchy structure John's prologue (God beyond names) and his cosmology (the celestial hierarchy).
- → Maximus the Confessor — *direct-influence*: Maximian Christology (two wills, two operations) is incorporated.
- → Aristotle's *Organon* — *substrate-influence*: John's *Dialectica* (Book I of the *Pege Gnoseos*) is a Christian primer in Aristotelian logic and category-theory; the *Exposition* deploys this Aristotelian apparatus on every page. **This is one of the major channels by which Aristotelian logic re-entered Christian systematic theology** — anticipating the medieval scholastic synthesis by 400 years. Louth 2002: 31–82.
- → [[phase-5-024-aquinas-summa-theologiae]] — *direct-influence*: via Burgundio's Latin translation (1148–1150), John is cited by Aquinas as one of his principal patristic sources. The *Damascenus* citations in Aquinas number in the hundreds. **John of Damascus is a major Greek-patristic substrate of high-medieval Latin scholasticism.**
- → [[phase-5-017-peter-lombard-sentences]] — *direct-influence*: similar transmission via Burgundio.

## Disputes
- **Original four-book division vs. 100-chapter division.** The 100-chapter form is John's; the four-book division is a Latin-medieval transmission convention.
- **John's "originality."** Older scholarship (Jugie, Beck) read John as a mere compiler; Louth 2002 vindicates the systematic and innovative character of the work.
