# Viṣṇu Sahasranāma

## What it is
A litany of one thousand epithets-names of the god Viṣṇu, embedded in the Anuśāsana-parvan (Book 13) of the *Mahābhārata*, in which the dying Bhīṣma (lying on his arrow-bed after the Kurukṣetra battle) recites the names to Yudhiṣṭhira at Kṛṣṇa's instruction. The 1000 names are organized into 108 verses in *anuṣṭubh* metre, framed by phala-śruti (recital-benefit) verses, with introductory and concluding sections that situate the recitation. The names range from explicit divine epithets (*Govinda*, *Narāyaṇa*, *Vāsudeva*) through philosophical-cosmological titles (*Sarva* — "All"; *Ananta* — "Endless"; *Brahman*) to obscure mythological-narrative references and grammatical-philosophical playful etymologies. The compositional layer is part of the Mahābhārata's late Vaishnava-theological textualization (~1st c. BCE to ~4th c. CE on standard dating); the text is among the most widely-recited Hindu liturgical compositions globally.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Late-Mauryan / Śuṅga / Kushan / Gupta northern India, the period during which the Mahābhārata's textual core (the *Bhārata* of ~24,000 verses) was elaborated into the full epic (~100,000 verses) including the Bhagavad Gītā (Bhīṣma-parvan), the Anugītā (Aśvamedha-parvan), and the Anuśāsana-parvan instructional materials including the VS.
- **Religious milieu.** The crystallization of Vaishnavism as a distinct theological tradition centered on Viṣṇu-Nārāyaṇa-Vāsudeva-Kṛṣṇa as a single supreme God with multiple personae (the doctrine of *avatāra* is developed in this period). Concurrent crystallization of Shaivism (around Rudra-Śiva) and of early Buddhist Mahāyāna (around the bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara and others). The names-litany genre — collecting epithets of a particular deity for devotional-meditative recitation — is part of a broader pan-South-Asian Vaishnava and Shaiva devotional textualization.
- **Who was behind it.** Anonymous Vaishnava redactors operating within the broader Mahābhārata textual transmission; the Anuśāsana-parvan is widely considered one of the later additions to the epic.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous Sanskrit transmission within Vaishnava liturgical communities. The text became a centerpiece of daily Vaishnava devotion (recited millions of times daily in homes and temples globally) and is among the most-commented Sanskrit texts of medieval Hinduism — Shankara's, Parāśara Bhaṭṭa's, and a Madhva-school commentary are the principal medieval expositions.

## Themes and motifs
- [[divine-name]] — the centerpiece. The thousand names instantiate the doctrine that the Divine has infinite aspects and that recitation of the names is itself a sufficient devotional discipline (the *nāma-japa* tradition).
- [[avatar-doctrine]] — the names include the principal *avatāra* references (Matsya, Kūrma, Varāha, Narasiṃha, Vāmana, Paraśurāma, Rāma, Krishna), grounding the developed *daśāvatāra* (ten-avatāra) doctrine in liturgical recitation.
- [[divine-emanation]] — the names range from concrete-personal (Krishna, Rāma) through cosmological-impersonal (Brahman, Sarva, Vyāpī — All-Pervader) — articulating Viṣṇu as both personal divinity and ultimate metaphysical ground.
- [[bhakti-devotion]] — the recitation itself is a devotional act; the *phala-śruti* verses promise specific benefits (health, prosperity, liberation) to the reciter.
- [[divine-immanence]] / [[apophasis]] — names like *Aprameya* (Immeasurable), *Avyaya* (Imperishable), and *Avyakta* (Unmanifest) articulate the apophatic-immanent register alongside the personal-devotional one.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-2-001-rig-veda-family-books]] — *direct-quote* / *parallel-motif*: the *Puruṣa Sūkta* (RV 10.90) supplies several VS names (*Puruṣa*, *Vāsudeva*, *Viṣṇu*) and conceptually grounds the names-of-God / cosmic-divinity tradition.
- → [[phase-2-017-mahabharata-ramayana-oral-layers]] — *direct-quote* / *commentary-on*: the VS is a passage of the MBh and is part of the MBh's textual elaboration.
- → Sufi *al-asmāʾ al-ḥusnā* (the 99 Most Beautiful Names of Allāh; e.g., the famous Hadith of An-Nasaʾi enumerating the 99) — *parallel-motif*: structurally cognate liturgical-devotional tradition of reciting divine names. The Vaishnava VS and the Sufi 99-names tradition are independently developed in their respective religious cultures (Vaishnava ~1st c. BCE – 4th c. CE; Islamic Hadith-based names list crystallizing ~9th–10th c. CE); the structural parallel is striking. In late-medieval India, Sufi-Hindu cross-pollination (Kabir, Dadu, Sant tradition; Akbar's *sulh-i kull* ideology; the Indo-Persian *bhakti-marfa* literature) made the two devotional-names traditions explicitly comparable to their practitioners — Kabir's verse repeatedly invokes *Rām*, *Hari*, *Allāh*, *Khudā* as alternative names for the same nameless divinity.
- → Christian *Jesus Prayer* and Litany of the Holy Name traditions — *parallel-motif*: the Eastern Christian *Jesus Prayer* ("Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me"), the Catholic Litany of the Holy Name (codified medieval, formalized 1862), the Litany of the Sacred Heart, and broader Christian names-of-Jesus devotional recitation are structurally cognate. Stuart C. Brown 1965, *The Mystery of the Sacred Name in the Old Testament*, and the broader names-of-God comparative-religion literature (R.C. Zaehner 1958, *At Sundry Times*) treat these comparatively.
- → Buddhist *Buddha-nāma* sūtras (the *Bhadrakalpika Sūtra* lists 1000+ Buddha-names; Pure Land *namo Amitābha* recitation) — *parallel-motif*: structurally similar Buddhist names-litany devotional tradition, contemporary with and likely in mutual influence with the Vaishnava VS in late-classical North India.
- → [[phase-5-005-shankara-brahma-sutra-bhasya]], [[phase-5-016-ramanuja-sribhasya]], [[phase-5-022-madhva-brahma-sutra-bhasya]] — *commentary-on*: all three of the great Vedāntic masters wrote commentaries on the VS (or their schools did), making the names-litany a doctrinal-philosophical text in subsequent Vedāntic tradition.

## Disputes
- **Date**: the Anuśāsana-parvan as a whole is late; the VS specifically is generally placed ~1st c. BCE – 4th c. CE within the larger Mahābhārata redaction.
- **The 1000-names list's stability**: the list of names in the canonical Vaishnava text is mostly stable across the recensional tradition, with some variants.
- **Authorship of the Shankara commentary**: the VS-*Bhāṣya* attributed to Shankara is contested by some scholars (some argue for a different author of the same name); the tradition treats it as authentic.
