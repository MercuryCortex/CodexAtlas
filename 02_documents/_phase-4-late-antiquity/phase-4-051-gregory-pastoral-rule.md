# Pastoral Rule

## What it is
A four-part Latin treatise on the duties of the Christian bishop, composed in 590–591 immediately after Gregory's reluctant election to the bishopric of Rome. Part I asks who should accept episcopal office (and who should refuse it); Part II describes the inner life of the bishop (purity of intention, discretion, the balance of action and contemplation); Part III, the longest, gives thirty-six "antithetical" pairs of pastoral situations and how the preacher must adapt his teaching to opposite kinds of hearers (the proud and the humble; the rich and the poor; the cheerful and the sad; the patient and the impatient; the silent and the talkative; the married and the celibate); Part IV is a brief peroration on the bishop's need for self-examination lest pastoral success make him spiritually proud.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Italy in 590 is in catastrophe: the Lombard invasion of 568 has fragmented the peninsula; plague has killed Pope Pelagius II; the Byzantine exarchate of Ravenna is collapsing; Rome itself is starving. Gregory, scion of a senatorial family (the *gens Anicia*), former *praefectus urbi*, then monk, then papal *apocrisiarius* (representative) at Constantinople (579–586), is elected pope September 590 against his will. The *Pastoral Rule* is composed in the first months of his pontificate as both his self-instruction and a programmatic statement of what bishops are to be.
- **Religious milieu.** Latin Christianity at the threshold of the early medieval transformation. The North African church is gone (the Vandal conquest, the Justinianic reconquest's collapse); the Iberian church is recovering from Arian Visigothic rule (Reccared converted 587); the Frankish church is barely emerging from Merovingian disorder; the British church is isolated; the imperial center in Constantinople is becoming linguistically and ecclesially alien. Latin Christianity is forced to organize itself locally, around bishops. The *Pastoral Rule* gives a manual.
- **Who was behind it.** Gregory alone, though he draws on Cassian (the eight principal vices in Part III), on Augustine (the *De doctrina christiana* model), on Benedict (Gregory will compose Benedict's biography in *Dialogues* II), and on his own years as a monk at his family villa-turned-monastery of St. Andrew on the Caelian Hill.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous and remarkable. Gregory sent the work to Anastasius of Antioch (Greek translation made almost immediately, by Anastasius of Antioch's circle); Leander of Seville received it; Augustine of Canterbury carried it to England (Gregory's Anglo-Saxon mission, [[event-augustine-of-canterbury-mission-597]]); Alfred the Great translated it into Old English ~895 and ordered a copy sent to every English bishopric. The Council of Mainz 813 made the *Regula* mandatory reading for bishops.

## Themes and motifs
- [[ecclesiology]] — the bishop as the central figure of the local church; the entire *Rule* is structured around the episcopal office as both pastoral and administrative.
- [[ecclesial-authority]] — the bishop teaches with authority, but the authority is derived from spiritual maturity, not office alone.
- [[monastic-asceticism]] — Gregory's deepest theological commitment: the bishop is fundamentally a monk in active service, the *vita mixta* of action and contemplation.
- [[allegorical-exegesis]] — Gregory's hermeneutical method throughout: the Hebrew Bible read as figurative instruction for the pastor (Ezekiel's watchman as the bishop; the high priest's vestments as the virtues).
- The *vita mixta* — Gregory's signature contribution: the contemplative life is higher in itself, but for the pastor the active life of teaching and care is obligatory, and the genuine pastor is the one who can move between the two without losing either. Aquinas would systematize this in *ST* II-II.179–182.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-4-048-benedict-rule]] — *parallel-genre* / *direct-influence*: both texts emerge from the same Latin monastic reform; Gregory's *Dialogues* II is the principal source for Benedict's life. The *Regula Pastoralis* applies the *Rule of Benedict*'s asceticism to the episcopal office. Markus 1997.
- → [[phase-4-047-cassian-conferences]] — *direct-source*: Cassian's eight principal vices are Gregory's source for the analytical psychology of pastoral situations in Part III. Demacopoulos 2007 intro.
- → [[phase-4-028-augustine-confessions]] and *De doctrina christiana* — *direct-source*: Augustine's framework of the bishop as teacher, the orator-converted-into-preacher, lies behind the structure of Parts I–II.
- → Old English Pastoral Care — *direct-translation*: Alfred's ~895 prose translation, *Hierdebōc*, is one of the first major works of English-language prose; Alfred personally translated the preface and contributed glosses.
- → [[event-augustine-of-canterbury-mission-597]] — *bundled-with*: the *Rule* was the curriculum of the Anglo-Saxon mission Gregory dispatched in 596–597.
- → Carolingian reform and Alcuin — *direct-influence*: Carolingian episcopal capitularies (Council of Aachen 816 *Institutio Canonicorum*; Council of Mainz 813) require bishops to study the *Regula Pastoralis*.
- → [[phase-5-024-aquinas-summa-theologiae]] — *downstream-influence*: *ST* II-II.179–182 on the active and contemplative lives is structured by Gregory's *vita mixta* synthesis. Aquinas cites Gregory in 80+ articles across the *Summa*.

## Substrate and influence — cross-tradition wiring
- **Roman administrative substrate.** Gregory had been *praefectus urbi* before becoming a monk; the *Regula*'s sense of office as a *charge* (the *cura animarum* as parallel to the *cura rei publicae*) is recognizably Roman administrative-juristic. Markus 1997, Straw 1988.
- **The active-contemplative grammar.** Goes back through Cassian to the Egyptian desert (Antony), and ultimately to Hellenistic philosophical schools (the *bios praktikos* / *bios theōrētikos* distinction in Aristotle, *NE* X.7–8; in Plotinus, *Enn.* III.8 on the active and contemplative). Gregory's Christianization of this Greek-philosophical distinction shapes the entire later Latin theology of the religious life.
- **Old Testament prophetic substrate.** Ezekiel 33 (the watchman) and Ezekiel 34 (the bad shepherds) are the load-bearing scriptural foundations; Gregory's reading is allegorical-ecclesial, projecting the Hebrew prophet's role onto the medieval Western bishop.

## Disputes
- **Date.** Most scholars settle on 590–591, the first year of Gregory's pontificate. A small minority puts the Latin original a year or two later.
- **Whether the Greek translation (made almost immediately by Anastasius of Antioch's circle) is by Anastasius himself or a member of his school.** Disputed; Markus 1997 treats it as Anastasius-circle.
- **The relationship to Benedict.** Whether Gregory knew Benedict personally is unclear; the *Dialogues* II portrait is hagiographic and may rely on third-party sources.
