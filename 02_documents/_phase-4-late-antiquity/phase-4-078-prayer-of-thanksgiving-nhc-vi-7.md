# Hermetic Prayer of Thanksgiving (NHC VI,7)

## What it is
A short Hermetic prayer of thanksgiving — not a dialogue or a treatise but a **liturgical formula**. The text of NHC VI,7 occupies one Coptic page (the bottom of fol. 65 of NHC VI). It thanks the supreme God for the gift of *gnōsis*, divinizing knowledge that has made the worshipper "no longer a man" but partakes of the divine nature. The prayer ends with a formal liturgical close ("having received this from your light, we rejoice... we have known you, O Light intelligible to mind") and a scribal note in the Nag Hammadi Coptic version: "When they had said this in their prayer, they embraced one another, and they went to eat their holy and bloodless food" — the only direct evidence that the Hermetic community shared a **communal sacred meal** at the close of liturgical practice. The reference to "bloodless food" (a *cult of vegetable food* — Egyptian-Hermetic mainstream piety; cf. Plutarch *De Iside* on Egyptian priestly diet) implicates this prayer in a Hermetic religious practice analogous to (but not derived from) early Christian eucharistic celebration.

## Primary text — full

The Coptic version is short enough to give in full (trans. Meyer 2007 *Nag Hammadi Scriptures*, with cross-checks against Mahé 1978 and the Latin Asclepius §41 in Copenhaver 1992):

> We thank you, every soul and heart stretches out to you, O ineffable Name, honored with the title "God" and praised with the title "Father." For to everyone and everything come the fatherly love and affection and whatever sweetness and simplicity, granting us mind, word, and knowledge: mind, that we might understand you; word, that we might expound you; knowledge, that we might know you.
>
> We rejoice, having been illuminated by your knowledge. We rejoice, because you have shown us yourself. We rejoice, because while we were in the body you have made us divine through your knowledge.
>
> The thanksgiving of the man who attains to you is one thing only: that we know you. We have known you, O Light intelligible to mind. O Life of life, we have known you. O Womb of every creature, we have known you. O Womb pregnant with the nature of the Father, we have known you. O eternal permanence of the begetting Father, thus have we worshipped your goodness.
>
> One petition only do we ask: that we be preserved in knowledge. And one protection do we desire: that we not stumble in this kind of life.
>
> When they had said this in their prayer, they embraced one another and went to eat their holy and bloodless food.

Citation: NHC VI,7 (full), trans. Meyer 2007; cf. Latin Asclepius §41 in Copenhaver 1992 pp. 92–93; PGM III.591–611 in Preisendanz.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Roman Egypt 1st–4th c. CE; Coptic codex deposited mid-4th c. near Pabau (Pachomian monastic complex region) — the contested question of whether Nag Hammadi VI (with its Hermetic + apocalyptic + ascetical contents) was used by Pachomian Christian monks or by a heterodox community remains live (Goehring 1986; Lundhaug-Jenott 2015 *The Monastic Origins of the Nag Hammadi Codices*).
- **Religious milieu.** Late-antique Egyptian Hermetic religious community with **actual liturgical practice** — not merely literary philosophical reading. The "they embraced one another" and "ate their holy and bloodless food" closing demonstrates communal ritual.
- **Who was behind it.** Hermetic teachers and initiates in late-antique Egypt; later transmitted through (a) the Latin Asclepius into the Latin philosophical-medieval tradition, (b) the Greek magical-papyri tradition (PGM III incorporates the prayer into a pagan-magical handbook), and (c) the Nag Hammadi Coptic monastic codex.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Three transmission lines converging: NHC VI rediscovered 1945; Latin Asclepius continuous since late antiquity; Papyrus Mimaut acquired by the Louvre in the 19th c. and edited by Preisendanz 1928. Mahé 1978 demonstrates that the three witnesses go back to a common Greek Hermetic Vorlage of 1st–3rd c. CE.

## Themes and motifs
- [[hermetic-piety]] — the prayer is the canonical Hermetic *eusebeia* exemplar; explicit thanksgiving as the proper response to revelation.
- [[gnosis-as-salvation]] — "we rejoice because while we were in the body you have made us divine through your knowledge" — explicit gnosis-soteriology.
- [[apotheosis]] — "you have made us divine" (Coptic *anchopen ennout' / nnoute*; Latin *divinos nos esse fecisti*) — divinization in this life.
- [[hermetic-rebirth]] — implicit; the prayer thematically continues the rebirth-doctrine of CH XIII into liturgical practice.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-4-013-discourse-on-the-eighth-and-ninth]] (NHC VI,6) — *shared-codex* + *liturgical-sequel*: directly precedes NHC VI,7 in the same Coptic codex; many scholars (Mahé, Fowden, Meyer) read NHC VI,6 + VI,7 as a paired *initiation + thanksgiving* liturgical sequence.
- → [[phase-4-079-coptic-asclepius-nhc-vi-8]] — *shared-codex*: directly follows NHC VI,7 in the same Coptic codex (NHC VI = 6 + 7 + 8 form a Hermetic mini-library at the end of the codex).
- → [[phase-4-012-asclepius]] — *direct-quote* / *manuscript-transmission*: NHC VI,7 = the same text as Latin Asclepius §41 (the closing prayer of the Latin Asclepius). Direct Greek-to-Coptic / Greek-to-Latin parallel descent.
- → Papyrus Mimaut (PGM III.591–611) — *direct-quote*: the same prayer appears in a Greek magical papyrus, demonstrating that the Hermetic prayer escaped its original liturgical context and entered the broader Egyptian-Greek ritual-magical literature.
- → [[phase-4-075-corpus-hermeticum-xiii-rebirth]] — *parallel-motif*: the closing hymn of CH XIII (the *secret hymn* taught to the reborn Tat) is liturgically cognate to NHC VI,7. Both are Hermetic *thanksgiving / hymn* formulae offered after revelation/initiation.
- → Christian Eucharistic prayer — *parallel-motif (NOT derivation)*: the formal structure (thanksgiving for divine gift + communal embrace + sacred meal) parallels early Christian eucharistic practice as documented in [[phase-4-061-didache]] (Didache 9-10, also a thanksgiving + communal meal). **Both descend from the broader Hellenistic Mediterranean meal-and-prayer religious culture; neither derives from the other.** Cf. McGowan 1999 *Ascetic Eucharists* on bloodless-food sacrificial practices in early Christianity (Marcionite, Encratite, Tatianic) and their Hermetic parallels.
- → [[phase-4-013-discourse-on-the-eighth-and-ninth]] *closing instruction* (NHC VI,6 ends with: "I have given you this discourse, my son, and you have written it on a turquoise stele") — *parallel-form*: NHC VI,7's closing scribal note is structurally cognate.

## Disputes
- **Was Nag Hammadi VI assembled by Christian monks?** The major contemporary debate (Lundhaug-Jenott 2015 vs. Khosroyev 1995 etc.). NHC VI bundles three Hermetica with a *Thunder, Perfect Mind*, an *Authoritative Teaching*, and a *Concept of Our Great Power* — a heterogeneous collection. Whether the Hermetic-prayer practice was performed by the Pachomian-adjacent collectors of NHC VI or whether the codex represents an outsider-collector hoard remains disputed.
- **Liturgical formula or literary set-piece?** Mahé 1978 / Fowden 1986 both argue for actual liturgical use, citing the triple attestation across genres. Older scholarship (early Festugière) leaned literary-only.
- **Date.** All three witnesses point to a Greek original 100–300 CE. Difficult to date more precisely.
