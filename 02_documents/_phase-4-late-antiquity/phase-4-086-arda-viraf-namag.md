# Ardā Wirāz Nāmag (Book of the Righteous Viraf)

## The Document

The *Ardā Wirāz Nāmag* ("Book of the Righteous Viraf") is a Pahlavi (Middle Persian) text narrating the soul-journey of a righteous Zoroastrian named Viraf through the full extent of the afterlife: the judgment, the celestial levels, and the infernal punishments. It is the most complete and literarily ambitious ancient Iranian afterlife narrative and represents the culminating literary expression of Zoroastrian afterlife geography (see [[theme-zoroastrian-afterlife-geography]]).

The text exists in manuscripts ranging from the 13th to 19th centuries CE, but internal evidence, theological content, and cross-references in Pahlavi literature place its core in the **early to middle Sasanian period** (3rd–7th century CE), with possible even earlier Parthian antecedents. Gignoux (1984) argues for a 3rd–4th century CE composition; Vahman (1986) accepts a similar range. The Sasanian context is important: the Zoroastrian clergy (*magi*) were state-sponsored, and the Arda Viraf served partly as a demonstration of orthodox afterlife doctrine — a theological corrective during a period when Manichaean and Christian heterodoxies were competing.

## The Narrative Frame

The text begins with a problem: **the Zoroastrian community is spiritually confused**. The text blames this on Alexander the Great's invasion of Persia (3rd century BCE), which destroyed the Avesta manuscripts and killed many priests who carried the oral tradition — an etiological myth used to explain the uncertainty of the community's practice. To restore certainty, a council of Zoroastrian priests selects a supremely righteous man, Viraf, to be the community's delegate to the divine world.

Viraf drinks wine mixed with *mang* (a psychoactive drug, possibly henbane or a hemp preparation) and falls into a **seven-day trance**. During this trance:

1. His soul departs his body and crosses the **Chinvat Bridge** (see [[theme-zoroastrian-afterlife-geography]])
2. He is welcomed by a beautiful woman representing his own *daēnā* (the personified sum of his righteous deeds)
3. He is guided through the heavens by Sraosha and Adur (fire)
4. He ascends through the celestial stations (stars, moon, sun, Garothman/House of Song)
5. He descends through the punishments of hell (Drujō-dmāna), witnessing 92 chapters of specific sin-punishments

On the seventh day, Viraf returns to his body and dictates his experience to the assembled priests.

## The Hell Topography: Sin-Matched Punishments

The longest section of the text — chapters 11–96 — describes the punishments of the wicked in precise, categorized detail. The operative logic is **moral symmetry**: the punishment physically enacts or reverses the sin. This is the conceptual structure that appears in Dante's *contrapasso* 700-800 years later:

| Sin | Arda Viraf punishment | Dante's Inferno analogue |
|---|---|---|
| False speech / lying | Tongue seized and torn | Various mouth-and-speech punishments |
| Illicit sexual acts | Genitals devoured by serpents and worms | Circle of the lustful, wind-punishment |
| Not giving tithes / withholding charity | Eating frozen ordure | Grafters in boiling pitch |
| Pride before God | Hanging upside down | Dante's inverted sinners |
| Killing innocent animals | Body torn by animals | Various beast-torn sinners |
| Not keeping the sacred fire | Darkness and frozen cold | Ice of Cocytus (the deepest hell) |

The structural parallel does not require direct textual transmission between the Arda Viraf and the *Commedia*; the parallel is mediated by the Islamic mi'raj literature and filtered through the general pattern of graduated afterlife punishment that Zoroastrian tradition bequeathed to late antique religious imagination.

## The Transmission Chain: Arda Viraf → Mi'raj → Dante

This is one of the great transmission chains in comparative religion. Each step is supported by scholarly evidence:

### Step 1: Arda Viraf → Islamic Mi'raj literature
The **Isrā' and Mi'rāj** — the Prophet Muhammad's Night Journey to Jerusalem and Ascent through the Seven Heavens (Quran 17:1, 53:1-18) — generates an enormous body of elaborative tradition in hadith and visionary literature. The most elaborate early collection is the *Kitāb al-Mi'rāj* (Book of the Ladder/Ascent), extant in Arabic, Spanish, and ultimately Latin versions. These mi'raj texts describe:
- Muhammad's ascent through seven heavens (structurally: the Zoroastrian celestial stations)
- The Sirat bridge (structurally: the Chinvat bridge — narrow, spanning hell)
- Paradise and its gardens (structurally: Garothman / pairidaeza)
- Hell's graduated punishments matched to categories of sin (structurally: Drujō-dmāna taxonomy)

The direct Iranian-Islamic transmission is attested by the Persian origin of key Islamic afterlife vocabulary: *firdaws* (paradise, from Avestan *pairidaeza*), the Sirat bridge (Arabic for "path/road" — the image of a narrow bridge above hell is Iranian), and the structural parallels in the mi'raj punishment lists to the Arda Viraf taxonomy. The Persian Muslim scholars who transmitted and elaborated the mi'raj tradition were heirs to pre-Islamic Zoroastrian literary culture in precisely the regions where the Arda Viraf circulated.

### Step 2: Islamic Mi'raj → Dante
Enrico Cerulli (1949) demonstrated that a Latin translation of a Spanish/Arabic mi'raj text — the *Liber Scalae Machometi* (Book of Muhammad's Ladder) — was available in Dante's Florence and that Dante's personal secretary Brunetto Latini had access to related Islamic material. The *Liber Scalae* was translated by Bonaventura da Siena in 1264 CE for Alfonso X of Castile — within Dante's lifetime and in his cultural orbit. Asín Palacios's original (1919) claim that Dante simply copied the *Commedia* from Islamic sources has been moderated: the scholarly consensus now holds that Dante drew on the mi'raj tradition as one source among several (Virgil's *Aeneid*, Cicero's *Somnium Scipionis*, Christian apocalyptic), but that the structural similarities between the *Commedia* and the mi'raj texts — specifically the seven-heaven ascent, the graduated hell with sin-specific punishments, and the guide figure — are too systematic for coincidence.

**The chain is thus:**
*Avestan afterlife doctrine* (~600 BCE?) → *Arda Viraf Namag* (3rd–5th c. CE) → *Islamic Mi'raj literature* (7th–12th c. CE) → *Liber Scalae Machometi* (1264 CE) → *Dante's Commedia* (1308–1321 CE)

Not a straight line of influence — more a set of structural inheritances transmitted through religious contact zones — but the Arda Viraf is the earliest fully elaborated literary expression of the afterlife-journey genre and the sin-matched punishment taxonomy that appears in increasingly elaborate form at each stage.

## Significance

The *Ardā Wirāz Nāmag* stands at the intersection of four major religious-literary traditions:

1. **Zoroastrian eschatology** — its most complete literary expression of the afterlife geography
2. **Apocalyptic literature** — a member of the soul-journey genre that includes *1 Enoch* 14-36, the *Apocalypse of Paul*, the *Book of the Watchers*, and Dante's *Commedia*
3. **Islamic Mi'raj** — structurally: the pre-Islamic Iranian soul-journey in Islamic dress
4. **Dante's Commedia** — the Western literary tradition's highest expression of afterlife geography, structurally downstream of the Arda Viraf chain

For the Codex Atlas, this document is a **MASSIVE-WIN** bridge node: it connects the Zoroastrian tradition to the Islamic and Western literary traditions through a traceable structural chain, demonstrating that the detailed, imaginative geography of heaven and hell that most Western readers associate with Christianity and Islam is in large part a Zoroastrian inheritance.
