# Letter to Flora

## What it is
A short Greek theological letter, preserved by Epiphanius of Salamis in *Panarion* 33.3–7, in which the Valentinian teacher Ptolemy writes to a woman named Flora — a Christian, but not (yet) a Valentinian — instructing her on the proper interpretation of the Mosaic Law. The Letter argues that the Hebrew Bible's Torah is *not* uniformly the work of the supreme good God, nor (against Marcion) is it uniformly the work of an evil rival deity, nor (against the catholic Christians) is it uniformly the work of the good God simpliciter. Rather, the Torah is *three-sourced*: (i) commandments from God himself; (ii) supplements from Moses; (iii) additions from "the elders" of the Jewish people. Within the divinely-originating layer, Ptolemy further distinguishes (a) the *pure law* (the Decalogue, fulfilled by the Saviour), (b) the *law mixed with injustice* (e.g., "eye for an eye," which the Saviour cancelled), and (c) the *symbolic law* (sacrifices and dietary regulations, which the Saviour transposed from sensible to spiritual). The Letter is one of the very few surviving early Christian theological texts addressed to a named female correspondent, and one of the most theologically sophisticated 2nd-c. treatments of the Christian appropriation of the Hebrew Bible.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Mid-to-late 2nd-c. Rome under the Antonines. The Roman Christian community is at the height of its 2nd-c. theological diversification: Valentinus arrives in Rome ~140; Marcion arrives ~140 and is expelled ~144; Justin Martyr active 150s–160s; Tatian a few years later; Ptolemy probably active 155–170. The Letter is composed in this contested Roman theological scene.
- **Religious milieu.** The Letter's primary opponents are two: Marcion (who rejects the Hebrew Bible as the work of an inferior deity) and the catholic Christians (who accept the Hebrew Bible whole as the work of the supreme God). Ptolemy crafts a Valentinian *via media*: the Torah is partly divine, partly Mosaic, partly human; within the divine portion, partly fulfilled, partly cancelled, partly transposed. The framework is a careful theological negotiation of the 2nd-c. Christian problem of the Old Testament.
- **Who was behind it.** Ptolemy, the leading Italian-Valentinian teacher per Irenaeus *Adv. Haer.* 2.4.1. The Letter shows Ptolemy at his most accessible: he is writing to instruct, not to systematize, and the technical Valentinian Pleroma-vocabulary is mostly suppressed in favor of an exegetical-theological argument that Flora can follow as a fairly-educated Christian.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Preserved verbatim by Epiphanius, *Panarion* 33.3–7. Epiphanius quotes the Letter in his anti-heretical encyclopedia (~374) as evidence against the Valentinians; the irony is that Epiphanius's hostile preservation is the *only* reason the Letter survives. No independent manuscript tradition exists.

## Themes and motifs
- [[allegorical-exegesis]] — Ptolemy's method throughout: the threefold division of the Law is performed by allegorical-textual analysis, distinguishing strata within the received Torah.
- *Three-source analysis of the Mosaic Law*: God / Moses / elders. This is a proto-source-critical move; Ptolemy is doing in the 2nd century what Wellhausen and the documentary hypothesis will do in the 19th. Markschies 2000.
- [[demiurge]] — but distinctively Valentinian: the lawgiver-demiurge is *just*, not evil; he is genuinely a god, ranking between the supreme Father and the adversary, and the Torah he gives is mixed but not malevolent.
- [[orthodoxy-vs-heresy]] — Ptolemy positions Valentinian theology against Marcionite extremism and against catholic uncritical-acceptance simultaneously; the Letter is a Valentinian *self-presentation* as theological *via media*.
- *Female theological addressee*. Flora is otherwise unknown but is addressed by Ptolemy as a serious theological interlocutor. The Letter is one of the few surviving 2nd-c. Christian texts addressed to a woman (compare the *Acts of Paul and Thecla*, the *Apocryphon of John* addressed [pseudonymously] to John, several Pauline letters to mixed congregations, Athanasius's letter to Marcellinus on the Psalms). On female religious agency in 2nd-c. Christianity see King 2003 ch. 6.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[ptolemaeus-valentinian]] — *authorship*: the Letter is Ptolemy's most significant surviving direct composition.
- → [[phase-4-023-irenaeus-against-heresies]] — *complementary-source*: Irenaeus's exposition of the Ptolemaic system in *Adv. Haer.* I.1–8 gives the systematic theology Ptolemy is teaching; the *Letter to Flora* gives the entry-level exegetical version. Together they make Ptolemy one of the best-documented 2nd-c. teachers.
- → [[phase-4-024b-tertullian-against-valentinians]] — *parallel-source*: Tertullian's *Adversus Valentinianos* (~+208) gives a generation-later Latin reception of the Ptolemaic system.
- → [[phase-4-059-tripartite-tractate]] — *parallel-Valentinian*: the *Tripartite Tractate* and the *Letter to Flora* are both Valentinian theological prose, but at different registers — the *Tripartite Tractate* is full-systematic, the *Letter to Flora* is exegetical-pastoral.
- → [[marcion-of-sinope]] — *polemical-target*: Ptolemy explicitly rejects the Marcionite move (the Torah as work of an evil god) while sharing some of Marcion's discomfort with the Torah's mixed character.
- → [[phase-3-013-philo-of-alexandria]] — *direct-substrate*: Philo's Alexandrian-Jewish allegorical exegesis of the Torah, distinguishing literal and spiritual senses, is the methodological substrate of Ptolemy's analysis. The Letter is in the broader Hellenistic-Jewish-Christian allegorical-exegetical tradition.
- → [[phase-3-015-pauline-epistles]] — *direct-source*: Paul on the Law (Romans 7; Galatians 3, the *paidagōgos*) provides the Pauline-Christian framework Ptolemy elaborates.
- → JEDP documentary hypothesis (Wellhausen 1878 and following) — *historical-anticipation*: Ptolemy's three-source analysis of the Torah is structurally proto-source-critical, anticipating by 1700 years the modern documentary hypothesis's recognition that the received Pentateuch is composite. The substantive identification of sources differs entirely (Ptolemy's three are God / Moses / elders; the modern J / E / D / P are four scribal traditions), but the *methodological move* — recognizing internal stratification in the Torah and assigning strata to different sources — is the same. Markschies 2000.

## Substrate and influence — cross-tradition wiring
- **Hellenistic-Jewish allegorical substrate.** Ptolemy's exegetical method is Alexandrian-Jewish in its substrate: Philo of Alexandria's allegorical reading of the Torah (the literal commandments as figurative codes for spiritual virtues), Aristobulus's earlier Hellenistic-Jewish allegorical method, the broader Hellenistic-Jewish project of making the Hebrew Bible accessible to Greek-philosophical sensibilities. The Letter applies this Alexandrian-Jewish substrate to a Christian-Valentinian end. Quispel 1966, Markschies 2000.
- **Pauline Law-as-paidagōgos substrate.** Paul's theology of the Law as the *child-attendant* leading to Christ (Galatians 3:24) and the Law as *holy, just, and good* but unable to save (Romans 7:12) is the Pauline-Christian framework Ptolemy elaborates. The Letter is in the Pauline tradition of *partial-affirmation, partial-supersession* of the Torah.
- **Anti-Marcionite positioning.** The Letter is one of the cleanest 2nd-c. statements of a *Valentinian alternative to Marcion*. Where Marcion rejects the Hebrew Bible entirely (and produces his own *Antitheses* contrasting OT and gospel), Ptolemy preserves it with a three-source analysis. Lieu 2015 *Marcion and the Making of a Heretic* places the *Letter to Flora* in this Roman-theological context.
- **The proto-source-critical move.** Ptolemy's distinction of *God's commandments / Moses's supplements / elders' additions* within the received Torah is a recognizably critical-historical move. Whether Ptolemy is the first such mover, or whether he is drawing on an older Jewish or Hellenistic critical tradition, is contested; Markschies 2000 notes parallels in Philo and in the Hellenistic-Jewish *Letter of Aristeas*.
- **Female religious agency in 2nd-c. Christianity.** The Letter's addressee Flora is one of the few 2nd-c. Christian women named in surviving theological literature alongside a male teacher with respect. The pattern (Valentinian male teacher / female student) is also visible in the wider Marcus-the-Magician tradition (Irenaeus 1.13–22 attacks Marcus for what he claims is exploitation of female prophetesses, but the texts show genuine female participation in Valentinian / Marcosian theological practice). King 2003 ch. 6.

## Disputes
- **Identification of Flora.** Otherwise unknown; not the Flora addressed in Roman inscriptions (chronology and content do not fit). Identification with a specific named Roman Christian woman has been attempted but is speculative.
- **Date.** ~155–180; the *terminus ante quem* is Irenaeus's reference to Ptolemy as a contemporary (writing ~180). The *terminus post quem* depends on when Ptolemy's Roman activity began.
- **Authenticity of Epiphanius's quotation.** Generally accepted as verbatim; Epiphanius is hostile but his quotation-practice elsewhere is reliable. Quispel 1966 evaluates.
- **Whether Ptolemy is the Ptolemy martyred under Antoninus Pius (per Justin Martyr *2 Apology* 2).** Proposed by Lampe 2003 as plausible but not certain; contested by other scholars.
