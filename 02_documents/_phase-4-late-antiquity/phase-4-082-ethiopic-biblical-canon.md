# Ethiopic Biblical Canon (the 81-book Ge'ez Bible)

## What it is
The **Ethiopic Biblical Canon** is the Ge'ez-language corpus of canonical scripture of the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church — the broadest and most inclusive biblical canon in the historic Christian world. It contains **81 books**, conventionally divided 46 Old Testament + 35 New Testament. The broader OT includes books retained nowhere else in Christianity as canonical — most consequentially [[phase-4-081-mashafa-henok-geez-1-enoch]] (1 Enoch), [[phase-3-009-jubilees]] (the Book of Jubilees), [[phase-5-040-meqabyan-ethiopian-maccabees]] (the three Ethiopian Books of Meqabyan, *unique to Ethiopia and entirely different from Jewish 1–2 Maccabees*), 1 and 2 Ezra-Apocalypse (= Western 4 Ezra and 3 Ezra), the Prayer of Manasseh, and the Book of Joseph ben Gurion. The broader NT includes the [[phase-4-065-shepherd-of-hermas]] (canonical in Ethiopia long after the West dropped it in the 4th c.), the *Sinodos* (a four-part church-order collection), the *Didascalia*, the *Books of the Covenant* (related to the Testament of Our Lord, *Mashafa Kidan*), and other patristic-era works elevated to scripture in Ethiopian practice.

The exact enumeration varies slightly across Ethiopian scholarly traditions because of (a) variant book-grouping conventions, (b) the "narrow" vs. "broad" canon distinction (the narrow canon corresponds approximately to the LXX-derived canon shared with Eastern Orthodoxy; the broad canon adds the uniquely-Ethiopian additions). The standard total — 81 books — was articulated by Abba Salama III (Andreyas, metropolitan 1841–1867) and is the conventional count.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Initial translation in the Aksumite Kingdom under [[frumentius]]'s episcopate and the [[the-nine-saints]] generations (~4th–6th c. CE), with subsequent medieval revision and textual settlement. The translation process was multi-staged and used multiple Vorlagen: Greek Septuagint as the dominant base, with Syriac Peshitta strata (especially in the NT), some Aramaic/Hebrew traces, and substantial Ethiopian-monastic-redactional adaptation.
- **Religious milieu.** Ethiopian Tewahedo Christianity preserves *broader* canonical boundaries than any other historical Christian tradition: where the Latin Western canon excluded the deuterocanonicals at the Reformation, Eastern Orthodoxy retains a slightly broader LXX canon, and Eastern Catholicism somewhat broader still — the Ethiopian canon goes substantially further, including 1 Enoch, Jubilees, Meqabyan, 4 Ezra, and other Second-Temple Jewish pseudepigrapha and patristic works as full scripture.
- **Who was behind it.** Anonymous monastic-translational tradition centered on the [[the-nine-saints]] generation in the late Aksumite period. The canon as a *boundary* was never formally promulgated by a single council; it crystallized through use, with monastic-liturgical practice as the operative authority.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous Ethiopian-monastic-liturgical use to the present.

## Disputes
- **Exact enumeration.** The "81-book" total depends on the counting convention; alternative traditions count differently (e.g., grouping the Pauline corpus as one book, or counting Sinodos sub-books separately). The functional canon is stable; the *number* is convention-bound.
- **When the broader canon "closed."** No formal council closed it. The medieval Ge'ez monastic-liturgical canon includes substantially what is canonical today; modern post-19th-c. articulations (Abba Salama III) regularize the count.
- **Continuing canonical status of some texts.** Some books (e.g., the Sinodos, the Mashafa Kidan / Testament of Our Lord) are sometimes treated as canonical-deuterocanonical rather than canonical-proper; the boundary is fuzzy.
- **Status of the Octateuch.** The Ethiopian *Octateuch* groups Genesis–Ruth as eight books; some traditions count this differently from the conventional Western Pentateuch + Joshua + Judges + Ruth.
