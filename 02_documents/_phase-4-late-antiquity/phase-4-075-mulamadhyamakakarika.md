# Mūlamadhyamakakārikā (Root Verses on the Middle Way)

## What it is
The Mūlamadhyamakakārikā ("Root Verses on the Middle Way") — universally abbreviated **MMK** — is the foundational philosophical text of the Mādhyamika school of Mahāyāna Buddhism, composed by [[nagarjuna]] (~150-250 CE). It is a sustained dialectical analysis in 27 chapters (449 verses) demonstrating that no phenomenon — including time, motion, causation, the elements, the self, the Tathāgata, the four noble truths, nirvana, and emptiness itself — can be coherently described as possessing *svabhāva* (inherent / intrinsic / self-establishing existence). The work's argumentative method is consistently negative: Nāgārjuna does not propose a positive metaphysical theory but uses dialectical reductio against every available alternative, leaving the conclusion that all phenomena are *śūnya* (empty) — meaning empty of *svabhāva*, not nonexistent. The famous closing of chapter 24 articulates the school's central thesis:

> "Whatever is dependently arisen is what we call emptiness. That is dependent designation; precisely that is the middle way." (MMK 24.18, trans. Garfield 1995)

The MMK is the **single most consequential pre-modern non-Western philosophical text in present analytic-philosophical circulation** — actively engaged in academic philosophy of mind, metaphysics, and logic by Garfield, Priest, Westerhoff, Siderits, Tillemans, and others. It is also the doctrinal foundation of Tibetan Mādhyamika (the central philosophical view of all four Tibetan schools, definitively articulated by [[tsongkhapa]]'s 14th-c. *Lamrim Chenmo* and *Ocean of Reasoning*), Chinese Sanlun (三論), Korean Sŏn, and Japanese Zen via Heart Sūtra and Diamond Sūtra liturgical practice.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Late Sātavāhana / early Ikṣvāku South India (~+150 to +250). Nāgārjuna's traditional patron is "King Satavahana," addressed in his *Suhṛllekha* (*Letter to a Friend*); the Sātavāhana dynasty ruled the Deccan / Andhra Pradesh region in this period. The Andhra-Karnataka region was a major Mahāsāṅghika and Mahāyāna center; the Amaravati and Nāgārjunakoṇḍa archaeological complexes (the latter named after the philosopher in later tradition) preserve the cultural-religious milieu.
- **Religious milieu.** Late sectarian Buddhism in active doctrinal tension: Sarvāstivāda Abhidharma (positing *dharmas* as ultimately real momentary events) vs. proto-Madhyamaka Prajñāpāramitā analysis (denying *dharma*-substantiality). Nāgārjuna writes from within the Mahāyāna stream, treating the Aṣṭasāhasrikā Prajñāpāramitā as authoritative scripture and the Sarvāstivāda dharma-theory as the principal philosophical target.
- **Who was behind it.** [[nagarjuna|Nāgārjuna]] himself, the most influential single Buddhist philosopher after the Buddha. His authentic works include the MMK, *Vigrahavyāvartanī*, *Śūnyatāsaptati*, *Ratnāvalī*, *Suhṛllekha*, and several shorter texts. Substantial legendary accretion attaches to him (the Prajñāpāramitā-recovery legend; the "second Nāgārjuna" tantric/alchemical figure of later tradition), but the philosophical corpus is securely his.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous transmission: in Sanskrit (via Candrakīrti's *Prasannapadā* commentary, which embeds the verses), in Chinese (Kumārajīva's 409 CE *Zhonglun*, the foundational East Asian transmission), and in Tibetan (8th c.). The Vaidya 1960 and Ye Shaoyong 2011 critical Sanskrit editions are current standards.

## Primary text — selected excerpts

The excerpts below use Jay L. Garfield's 1995 translation (*The Fundamental Wisdom of the Middle Way*, Oxford UP), the standard contemporary English version.

### Opening dedicatory verses (MMK 0)

> "I prostrate to the Perfect Buddha, the best of teachers, who taught that whatever is dependently arisen is unceasing, unborn, unannihilated, not permanent, not coming, not going, without distinction, without identity, and free from conceptual construction."

These are the eight negations (*aṣṭa-pratiṣedha*) that frame the entire work.

### Chapter 1 — examination of conditions

> 1.1. "Neither from itself nor from another, nor from both, nor without a cause, does anything whatever, anywhere, arise."

The opening verse rules out the four logically exhaustive accounts of how a thing might arise — establishing that the very notion of inherent arising is incoherent. This is the *catuṣkoṭi* (tetralemma) method: by ruling out all four corners of the logical square, the conclusion is forced.

### Chapter 15 — the central definition of emptiness

> 15.1. "An essence [*svabhāva*] arising from causes and conditions makes no sense. If an essence arose from causes and conditions, then it would be fabricated."
>
> 15.2. "But how could it be appropriate for an essence to be fabricated? For an essence is uncontrived, and is independent of anything else."
>
> 15.7. "Those who see essence and essential difference, and entities and nonentities, do not see the truth taught by the Buddha."

### Chapter 24 — the four noble truths and emptiness; the central thesis

This is the MMK's most-cited chapter. An imagined Sarvāstivāda opponent objects: if everything is empty, then the four noble truths, the Saṅgha, the Buddha, the path, and nirvana are all empty — destroying Buddhism itself. Nāgārjuna's response inverts the charge:

> 24.18. "Whatever is dependently arisen is what we call emptiness. That is dependent designation; precisely that is the middle way."
>
> 24.19. "There does not exist anything that is not dependently arisen. Therefore, there does not exist anything that is not empty."
>
> 24.20. "If all this were nonempty, as in your view, then there would be no arising and ceasing. The four noble truths would become nonexistent."

The famous reversal: the *opponent's* view (that the four noble truths require non-empty dharmas) is the position that destroys the four noble truths; *Nāgārjuna's* view (that emptiness = dependent origination) is the position that *enables* the four noble truths and the entire Buddhist path. Emptiness is not nihilism but the philosophical articulation of the Buddha's core teaching of dependent origination.

### Chapter 25 — examination of nirvana

> 25.19. "There is not the slightest difference between cyclic existence [*saṃsāra*] and nirvana. There is not the slightest difference between nirvana and cyclic existence."
>
> 25.20. "Whatever is the limit of nirvana, that is the limit of cyclic existence. There is not even the slightest difference between them, or even the subtlest thing."

The non-duality of saṃsāra and nirvana — both equally empty, both equally dependent — is the MMK's most consequential single doctrinal claim for subsequent Mahāyāna and Vajrayāna soteriology.

## Themes and motifs
- [[emptiness-sunyata]] — *the* central theme. Articulated as: all phenomena are empty of *svabhāva* (inherent existence); emptiness itself is empty (not a foundational reality); emptiness = dependent designation = the middle way.
- [[pratitya-samutpada]] — dependent origination is reframed not as a temporal causal sequence but as the metaphysical structure that grounds emptiness: nothing arises independently, therefore nothing has *svabhāva*.
- [[anatman-no-self]] — the no-self doctrine is extended from persons to all dharmas (*dharma-nairātmya*); the *Aṣṭasāhasrikā* foreshadows this, Nāgārjuna systematizes it.
- **The two truths** — conventional (*saṃvṛti*) and ultimate (*paramārtha*) truth; conventional truth operates in the everyday domain of cause-and-effect designation, ultimate truth recognizes emptiness; both are needed (MMK 24.10: "Without depending on the conventional, the meaning of the ultimate cannot be taught").
- **The *catuṣkoṭi* (tetralemma) method** — Nāgārjuna's signature logical procedure: refuting all four logically possible positions on a question (it is X / it is not-X / it is both / it is neither), leaving the conclusion that the question's premise was incoherent.

## Disputes
- **Date**: Garfield 1995 accepts ~150-250 CE; modern consensus generally puts Nāgārjuna in this window. Walser 2005 (*Nāgārjuna in Context*) refines the date based on the Sātavāhana-court setting of the *Ratnāvalī* and *Suhṛllekha*.
- **Authentic vs. attributed corpus**: the MMK, *Vigrahavyāvartanī*, *Śūnyatāsaptati*, *Ratnāvalī*, and *Suhṛllekha* are securely authentic; the *Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra* (commentary on the Pañcaviṃśatisāhasrikā) attributed to Nāgārjuna in the Chinese tradition is contested and likely a later compilation; the "second Nāgārjuna" tantric/alchemical corpus is universally regarded as later attribution.
- **Interpretation of *śūnyatā***: nihilist (charged by opponents and explicitly rejected by Nāgārjuna in MMK 24); anti-foundationalist relational pluralism (Garfield 1995); semantic emptiness / no-thesis interpretation (Westerhoff 2009); apophatic realism (Murti 1955, older view, now less favored).
- **Svātantrika vs. Prāsaṅgika**: how should Mādhyamika negation work? Bhāvaviveka (~+500) argued for *svatantra* (independent) syllogisms; Candrakīrti (~+600) argued for *prasaṅga* (consequentialist) reductio only. The dispute defines the central internal division of subsequent Mādhyamika.
