# On the Incarnation

## What it is
Athanasius of Alexandria's systematic exposition of the Christian doctrine of the incarnation, in 57 chapters of Greek. The second half of a two-part work — *Contra Gentes* (rebutting paganism and idolatry) and *De Incarnatione* (positively presenting the Christian doctrine of the Word-made-flesh). Probably composed early in Athanasius's career, c. 328–335 (Anatolios 1998 dates it later than the older consensus, which placed it ~318 prior to the Nicene controversy). The most accessible Athanasian statement of the *logos*-Christology that underwrote Nicene orthodoxy and that grounded the patristic doctrine of *theosis* (deification) — summarized in Athanasius's most famous epigram: "He became human that we might become divine" (*autos gar enēnthrōpēsen, hina hēmeis theopoiēthōmen* — Ch. 54).

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Athanasius is, at the time of composition, either still close to or already bishop of Alexandria. The Arian controversy is the primary intellectual horizon, though *De Incarnatione* engages more with the broader Christian-philosophical project than with the specifics of Arius's position.
- **Religious milieu.** Alexandria of the early 4th c. — Greek philosophical schools, Hellenistic-Jewish exegesis, the Origenist Christian-Platonist tradition (recently divided over Arius), Hermetic and pagan-religious alternatives. Athanasius writes for a sophisticated Greek readership.
- **Who was behind it.** Athanasius personally; the work circulates as part of the larger Athanasian theological corpus.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous Greek manuscript tradition.

## Themes and motifs
- [[logos-cosmic-reason]] — Athanasius's *Logos*-Christology: the divine Word who frames the cosmos becomes incarnate to renew the corrupted human creature.
- [[trinitarian-formula]] — the work assumes and develops the Father-Son-Spirit framework; its full Nicene articulation comes in Athanasius's later anti-Arian works.
- [[apotheosis]] / *theosis* — Christ's incarnation as the basis for human deification. The Athanasian formulation becomes the spine of Eastern Christian soteriology.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-3-013-philo-of-alexandria]] — *direct-influence*: Athanasius's *Logos* concept directly inherits the Philonic-Alexandrian Logos doctrine — the divine reason as God's instrumental presence in creation. Anatolios 1998: 84–93.
- → [[phase-3-020-gospel-of-john]] — *direct-quote* / *commentary-on*: John 1:1–14 is the foundational scriptural text behind the whole work.
- → [[phase-4-026-origen-on-first-principles]] — *partial-redaction-of*: Athanasius is silently revising the Origenist Logos-cosmology toward Nicene non-subordinationism. The substrate is Origenist; the polemical edge is anti-subordinationist.
- → Stoic cosmic *Logos* — *substrate-influence*: the philosophical resonance of *Logos* as cosmic rational principle ([[heraclitus]] → Stoics → Philo → Athanasius) gives the Christological term its persuasive force for a philosophically literate Greek audience.
- → Hellenistic *apotheosis* tradition — *substrate-counterpoint*: Athanasius's *theosis* doctrine engages with and Christianizes a Hellenistic religious-philosophical motif (deification of heroes / emperors / philosophical sages). Russell 2004: 167–88.

## Disputes
- **Date.** Older scholarship dated to ~318; Anatolios 1998 (followed by most recent work) places it after Nicaea, ~328–335.
- **The Athanasian *theosis* doctrine.** Whether it is genuinely a doctrine of human deification or only a vivid metaphor for redemption / adoption is debated; Russell 2004 is the comprehensive treatment, arguing it is genuine deification doctrine.
