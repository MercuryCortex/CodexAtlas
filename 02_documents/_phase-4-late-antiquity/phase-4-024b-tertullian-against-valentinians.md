# Against the Valentinians

## What it is
A 39-chapter Latin polemical treatise by Tertullian, composed at Carthage ~206–208, satirically refuting the Ptolemaean branch of Valentinian Gnostic Christianity. Tertullian opens with a famous theatrical metaphor (Adv. Val. 1): the Valentinian system is "Eleusinian heresy" — its initiates whisper their mysteries, dress their fictions in solemn vocabulary, and lampoon themselves the moment their cosmology is exposed to daylight. The bulk of the work (chapters 7–32) paraphrases and ridicules the Valentinian pleroma: the thirty aeons emanating in syzygies from Bythos and Sige; the fall of Sophia and her abortive offspring; the formation of the demiurge from her passion; the threefold anthropology (pneumatic / psychic / hylic); the redemption-narrative through Christ and Sophia's restoration. Tertullian's method is overtly mocking — he names each aeon as if introducing actors in a comedy — but the underlying data are precise enough that scholars routinely use *Adversus Valentinianos* as a second witness alongside Irenaeus' *Adversus Haereses* 1.1–8 for reconstructing second-century Valentinian theology. The Latin is dense, neologistic, and rhetorically virtuoso; Tertullian coins and stabilizes much of the Latin theological vocabulary that later orthodoxy inherits.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Severan Roman North Africa under Septimius Severus and Caracalla. Carthage was the major Latin Christian center of the empire; its bishopric would soon (under Cyprian) become structurally equal in Latin Christianity to Rome's. The Severan period included the 202–203 persecution under which Perpetua and Felicitas were martyred at Carthage — Tertullian wrote *Ad Martyras* for the prisoners. *Adversus Valentinianos* belongs to the immediately following years of post-persecution consolidation.
- **Religious milieu.** Carthage and the broader Mediterranean were home to a flourishing Valentinian school — Ptolemaeus, Heracleon, Marcus, Theodotus, Florinus — by this date in its second generation after Valentinus's own teaching at Rome (~136–~165). Valentinian texts now recovered from Nag Hammadi (*Gospel of Truth*, *Gospel of Philip*, *Treatise on the Resurrection*, *Tripartite Tractate*) confirm the sophistication of the school's literary and theological output. Tertullian's polemic is aimed precisely at the *Ptolemaean* recension of the Valentinian system — the same recension Irenaeus addressed at length in *Adv. Haer.* 1.1–8 a generation earlier. Tertullian also stood at the threshold of his own turn to the Montanist New Prophecy; *Adv. Val.* is usually placed just before or at the beginning of this transition. The Montanism complicates Tertullian's later orthodox reception but does not affect the polemical accuracy of *Adv. Val.*
- **Who was behind it.** Tertullian himself — Carthaginian, Latin-trained in rhetoric (and possibly law, though Barnes 1985 disputes the older identification of "Tertullianus the jurist" with the theologian), the first major Latin Christian theologian. *Adv. Val.* belongs to the cluster of his anti-heretical works (*De Praescriptione Haereticorum*, *Adversus Marcionem*, *Adversus Hermogenem*, *Scorpiace*, *Adv. Val.*) by which he defines proto-orthodox Latin Christianity against its rivals.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous Latin manuscript transmission through the medieval period. Editio princeps by Beatus Rhenanus, Basel 1521. The text reappeared in scholarly debate in the 19th century with the Migne reprint (PL 2). Modern critical editions: Kroymann (CCSL 2, 1954), Fredouille (SC 280–281, 1980–81), Riley (1971; freely online at Tertullian.org). Riley's edition with parallel-column English is the standard for Anglophone Valentinian-studies citation.

## Themes and motifs
- [[demiurge]] — Tertullian's *Adv. Val.* preserves a particularly detailed witness to the Valentinian demiurge: formed from Sophia's tears (water), laughter (light), grief (the elements), and ignorance, he is *psychic* rather than pneumatic, ignorant of the pleroma above him, and thinks himself supreme — the same anthropomorphic chain Irenaeus describes.
- [[gnosis-as-salvation]] — Tertullian explicitly mocks the Valentinian distinction between pneumatic *gnosis* and psychic faith; chapters 29–32 lampoon the threefold-anthropology.
- [[divine-feminine]] — Sophia, Achamoth, Sige, Aletheia, and the other female aeons get extended (and mocking) treatment; Tertullian's catalog is one of our most detailed second-century sources for the Valentinian feminine pleroma alongside Irenaeus.
- [[anthropos-myth]] — the threefold anthropology (pneumatic, psychic, hylic humans) and the *Anthropos-Ecclesia* syzygy as part of the second tetrad.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-4-023-irenaeus-against-heresies]] — *direct-source* / *redaction-of*: Tertullian's chapters 7–32 are a Latin reworking, often a near-translation, of Irenaeus *Adv. Haer.* 1.1–8 (the Ptolemaean system). Tertullian acknowledges his dependence in *Adv. Val.* 5, naming Irenaeus among his predecessors. Where Irenaeus is forensic, Tertullian is satirical, but the underlying data are the same. Ref: Riley 1971 (commentary, ad loc.); Osborn 2003 ch. 5.
- → [[phase-4-002-apocryphon-of-john]] — *polemic-against* (indirect): Although Tertullian's named target is the Ptolemaean Valentinian system, the broader Sethian mythological grammar shared with the Apocryphon of John — fall of Sophia, ignorant demiurge, archontic creation of humans, redemption through awakening to gnosis — is precisely what Tertullian satirizes. Irenaeus 1.29, the most explicit second-century witness to the Apocryphon's myth, is part of the same heresiological complex Tertullian works in. Ref: Pearson 2007, *Ancient Gnosticism*; King 2003, *What is Gnosticism?*
- → [[phase-4-003-gospel-of-truth]] — *polemic-against*: the *Gospel of Truth*, the most likely candidate for a text by Valentinus himself, articulates the Valentinian soteriology (the Father's revelation through the Son, the awakening from the nightmare of forgetting) that Tertullian is attacking in its school-developed form. Tertullian names Valentinus by name in *Adv. Val.* 4 (and gives our principal datum that Valentinus stood for the Roman bishopric and was passed over). Ref: Thomassen 2006, *The Spiritual Seed*.
- → Heracleon's *Commentary on John* (lost; fragments in Origen) — *parallel-target*: Heracleon was the leading Valentinian exegete of Tertullian's generation; Tertullian and Origen address the same Valentinian school from polemical and (more constructive) Alexandrian angles respectively.
- → [[phase-4-024-tertullian-apology]] — *same-author*: the two works form a complementary diptych of Tertullian's range — the *Apology* defends Christianity outward to Roman pagans, *Adv. Val.* defines Christianity inward against the Gnostic alternatives.
- → Hippolytus *Refutatio Omnium Haeresium* (Book 6 on the Valentinians) — *direct-influence*: Hippolytus draws on both Irenaeus and Tertullian for his Valentinian material.

## Disputes
- **Dating.** Barnes (1985) places *Adv. Val.* in 206–207 on internal stylistic and chronological grounds (after *De Praescriptione*, before the full Montanist works). Some earlier scholarship favored 208–211. The earliest date floor is the publication of Irenaeus' first book in Latin, which Tertullian depends on; the latest is given by Tertullian's first clearly Montanist works.
- **Genre — satire or polemic?** Riley (1971) reads the work as a tightly constructed satirical treatise modelled on Roman *invectiva*; Fredouille (1980–81) and Osborn (2003) treat the satirical surface as ornamenting a serious theological argument. The two readings are largely compatible.
- **Relation to Irenaeus.** Whether Tertullian had Irenaeus' Greek original or a Latin intermediary is debated; Fredouille and Osborn argue for direct dependence on the Greek.
- **Accuracy of the Valentinian portrait.** Williams (1996), King (2003), and Thomassen (2006) caution that heresiological sources homogenize the Valentinian school's internal diversity (Eastern vs. Italian branches; Ptolemaean vs. Heracleonian recensions). Tertullian's portrait is specifically of the *Ptolemaean* Italian recension and should not be over-generalized.
