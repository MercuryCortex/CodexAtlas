# 1 Clement

## What it is
A 65-chapter Greek letter from "the church of God sojourning at Rome" to "the church of God sojourning at Corinth," ~+95/+96, addressing a leadership crisis in which younger members at Corinth had deposed lawfully appointed presbyters. The longest pre-Marcionite extant Christian text and the **earliest surviving Christian writing outside the New Testament**. Its principal historical-doctrinal contribution is the **first articulation of apostolic succession**: the apostles, "having received their orders, appointed the first converts as bishops and deacons of those who would believe; and afterward our apostles laid down a rule that, when these died, other approved men should succeed to their ministry" (42.4–44.3).

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Late Domitian (+95/+96). Domitian's last year of imperial pressure on Roman aristocracy (the Flavius Clemens and Domitilla cases); the letter's opening reference to "the sudden and successive calamities and reverses we have experienced" plausibly alludes to recent Roman trouble.
- **Religious milieu.** Roman Christianity is still a network of household-and-immigrant-network *tituli* (Lampe 2003), governed by college of presbyter-bishops; "bishop" and "presbyter" are still interchangeable terms (44.4–5). The author addresses Corinth as one community-of-presbyters to another — *not* yet as monoepiscopal bishop to bishop.
- **Who was behind it.** Anonymous in text. Ancient attribution to *Clement* — possibly a freedman in the household of T. Flavius Clemens (Lampe's thesis) — is held by Dionysius of Corinth (~+170), Hegesippus, Irenaeus, and Eusebius. Critical scholarship: probably authored by a single learned Christian Roman writer working on behalf of the Roman community-collegium, traditionally identified with Clement.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuously known through Codex Alexandrinus (which appended 1–2 Clement to the NT); fully recovered through Bryennios 1875.

## Themes and motifs
- [[ecclesial-authority]] — load-bearing locus: the document is the earliest articulation of *succession from the apostles* as legitimizing presbyteral office (42, 44). Critically, the Roman church writes to Corinth presuming the *prerogative* to intervene in another community's affairs — read by some (Sullivan, Brown) as proto-Roman-primacy, by others as early-Christian inter-community advisory norm.
- [[ecclesiology]] — the body-and-members metaphor (chs. 37–38) extends Pauline 1 Cor 12 corporate ecclesiology into the post-Pauline period.
- [[stoic-virtue]] — load-bearing **substrate wire**. The letter is saturated with Hellenistic *homonoia* (concord) rhetoric: cosmic harmony (ch. 20, the celestial bodies and seasons in their ordered ranks), military-discipline analogy (ch. 37, the Roman army's ranks and obedience), Stoic-Roman public-virtue vocabulary throughout. Bowe (1988) demonstrates the letter is structurally a Hellenistic *homonoia*-speech in Christian dress.
- [[apocalyptic-revelation]] — the *Phoenix* myth (ch. 25) used as a *natural* sign of resurrection: a striking instance of Greco-Roman material absorbed into Christian apologetic.
- **Civic-religion vocabulary** — the great prayer for civic rulers (chs. 59–61), which deeply influenced subsequent Christian political theology, draws on Jewish-diaspora liturgical prayer-for-empire (cf. *m. Avot* 3.2; *Bar.* 1:11; *1 Tim.* 2:1–2).
- **Mimesis** of OT exemplars (Cain, Abel, Abraham, Moses, David — chs. 4–18): biblical exegesis as moral-paraenetic model.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-3-015-pauline-epistles]] — *direct-quote* / *commentary-on*: 1 Cor is quoted dozens of times; ch. 47 explicitly invokes Paul's previous letter to Corinth ("the epistle of the blessed Paul the apostle"). Critical witness to early Pauline-letter collection at Rome.
- → [[phase-4-063-ignatius-letters]] — *shared-milieu* / *parallel-development*: Ignatius (Antioch, ~+108) advances *monoepiscopal* church order; 1 Clement (Rome, ~+95) still operates with college-of-presbyter-bishops. The two together document the trajectory from collegial to monoepiscopal authority over a decade.
- → [[phase-4-066-polycarp-philippians]] — *direct-quote*: Polycarp's *Phil.* quotes 1 Clement extensively (Hartog 2013 documents this). The Apostolic Fathers form an intertextual web.
- → Hellenistic *homonoia*-rhetoric (esp. Dio Chrysostom's *Orations* 38–41) — *shared-genre* / *substrate-wire*: 1 Clement is structurally a Greek civic concord-speech baptized. Bowe 1988.
- → [[phase-4-027-eusebius-ecclesiastical-history]] — *direct-quote*: Eusebius quotes and discusses 1 Clement at HE 3.16, 3.38, 4.22.
- → [[phase-4-068-2-clement]] — *manuscript-transmission* / *misattribution*: pseudonymously transmitted with 1 Clement, hence the name.

## Disputes
- **Authorship.** Ancient attribution to Clement is the default; nothing internal confirms or denies it. The text is a Roman-community letter regardless.
- **Roman primacy implication.** Sullivan (2001), Brown (1983) read the letter as early evidence of Roman pastoral intervention claiming oversight over other churches. Many Protestant scholars push back: the letter is a fraternal *advisory* rather than a juridical intervention.
- **Date.** ~+95/+96 mainstream (the "calamities" allusion to late-Domitianic pressure). A few scholars (Welborn) argue ~+80s, but this is a minority.
- **Whether "bishop" and "presbyter" are interchangeable in the text.** Mainstream: yes, they are (42–44). Catholic apologetic readings sometimes resist this; the textual evidence is clear.
