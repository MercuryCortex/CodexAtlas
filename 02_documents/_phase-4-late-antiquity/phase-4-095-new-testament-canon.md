# New Testament Canon — Formation

## What it is
The **New Testament canon** — the 27-book list that defines the Christian New Testament — is not a creation of a single council or author but the outcome of a **gradual historical process** spanning roughly 170–419 CE. The process had several catalytic moments:

1. **Marcion of Sinope (~140 CE)**: Marcion created the first known Christian "canon" — a truncated list consisting of a modified Gospel of Luke and ten Pauline letters, rejecting the Hebrew Bible entirely. Marcion's challenge forced proto-orthodox Christianity to articulate its own canonical boundaries.
2. **Muratorian Fragment (~170 CE)**: The oldest surviving canonical list from the Roman church, in fragmentary Latin. Recognizes four Gospels, Acts, 13 Pauline letters, Jude, two Johannine letters, Revelation, and the *Wisdom of Solomon*. Excludes the Shepherd of Hermas as too recent.
3. **Eusebius of Caesarea (~325 CE)**: In *Ecclesiastical History* 3.25, Eusebius classifies books as *homologoumena* (universally accepted), *antilegomena* (disputed but used), and *rejected*. His taxonomy documents the unsettled status of Hebrews, James, 2 Peter, 2–3 John, Jude, and Revelation.
4. **Athanasius' 39th Festal Letter (367 CE)**: Bishop of Alexandria's Easter letter contains the **first list exactly matching the current 27-book New Testament** — the same books in the same number. Athanasius uses the word *kanonizomena* (canonized).
5. **Council of Hippo (393 CE) and Council of Carthage (397 CE)**: African councils ratify the 27-book list; the 419 CE council reconfirms it. These represent the first conciliar endorsements of the canon.

## Contested books
- **Hebrews**: Anonymous; disputed Pauline authorship; accepted in East earlier than West.
- **Revelation (Apocalypse of John)**: Accepted in West; long disputed in East (absent from Syrian canon).
- **James, 2 Peter, 2–3 John, Jude**: The *antilegomena* of Eusebius.
