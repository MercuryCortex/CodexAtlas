# Larger Sukhāvatīvyūha Sūtra (Larger Pure Land Sūtra)

## What it is
The foundational text of the East Asian Pure Land tradition — the most demographically successful single form of Buddhism in human history. The Sūtra narrates the prior career of the cosmic Buddha Amitābha / Amitāyus ("Buddha of Infinite Light / Infinite Life"). In an immeasurably distant past, the bodhisattva Dharmākara, a former king who renounced his throne, made forty-eight vows (forty-seven or forty-eight, varying by recension) before the Buddha Lokeśvararāja, undertaking that if he attained buddhahood he would establish a buddha-field of supreme purity into which beings could be reborn through faith and the calling-to-mind (*nian fo* / *nembutsu*) of his name. Dharmākara then performed inconceivable æons of practice, attained buddhahood as Amitābha, and the buddha-field thereby established — Sukhāvatī ("the Land of Bliss"), located in the western quadrant of the cosmos — is depicted in extensive sensory detail: jewel-trees, lotus-ponds, music-of-itself, no realms of suffering, no women (in some recensions all reborn beings are male, a feature contested in some later readings), continuous Dharma-instruction, and ultimate attainment of buddhahood from this favorable rebirth. The Sūtra's eighteenth vow (the *Original Vow* / *Primal Vow*, *Hongan*) — that any being who calls Amitābha's name with sincere faith ten times at the moment of death will be born in Sukhāvatī — became, in the East Asian Pure Land tradition, the operational core of an entire devotional civilization.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Mahāyāna northwest India / Gandhāra, late Kuṣāṇa period, 1st-2nd c. CE. The cult of Amitābha is one of the earliest attested forms of Mahāyāna devotion. The cosmopolitan Gandhāran context — with Greek, Iranian (Zoroastrian and post-Zoroastrian), and Central Asian cultural materials in active circulation — has prompted scholarly speculation about external influences on Pure Land paradisiacal imagery (see Disputes).
- **Religious milieu.** Indian Mahāyāna's devotional wing, parallel to and competing with the Prajñāpāramitā emptiness-tradition and the Lotus / Avataṃsaka cosmological traditions. Amitābha-devotion is the most explicitly *faith-based* (*śraddhā*) form of Mahāyāna soteriology — the doctrine that the buddha's *Other Power* (*tariki* / *parinipāta*) rather than one's own efforts (*jiriki*) is the operative liberatory force. In East Asia the Pure Land tradition becomes the demographically dominant Buddhism: Hōnen and Shinran's Japanese reformations make *nembutsu* recitation the universal practice; the result is a Buddhism accessible to the illiterate and the lay, structurally analogous to the Protestant Reformation's *sola fide* doctrine.
- **Who was behind it.** Anonymous Mahāyāna devotional communities of the Gandhāran-Kashmirian northwest. The multiple Chinese translations over eight centuries (188 to 1001 CE) attest both the text's importance and the continuing East Asian editorial-interpretive concern with its precise content.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous Mahāyāna manuscript transmission. The Chinese translations are the operational East Asian texts; the Sanskrit Nepalese manuscript tradition is the scholarly reference. The East Asian Pure Land masters — Tanluan, Daochuo, Shandao, Hōnen, Shinran — built an entire theological system on the Sūtra's vows.

## Themes and motifs
- **Dharmākara's vows** — the constitutive event of Sukhāvatī. The eighteenth vow (in Saṃghavarman's standard recension, vow numbering varies) is the operational core: any being who calls Amitābha's name in faith will be born in his Pure Land at death. This vow underwrites the entire devotional system.
- **Pure Land cosmography** — Sukhāvatī in the western quadrant; jewel-trees and lotus-ponds; the Three Bodies of the buddha (especially Amitābha's *Saṃbhogakāya* — the radiant reward-body — as the form by which he appears to those reborn); the attending bodhisattvas Avalokiteśvara and Mahāsthāmaprāpta.
- **Other Power (*tariki*)** — the bodhisattva's vow-power, not the practitioner's effort, accomplishes salvation. Shinran's radicalization of this doctrine in 13th-c. Japan parallels (and is sometimes compared with) Lutheran *sola fide*.
- [[bhakti-devotion]] — the most devotional of the major Mahāyāna traditions; recitation, visualization, faith, and trust in the buddha's saving power as primary practices.
- [[afterlife-judgment]] (Pure Land form) — at the moment of death, sincere recitation effects rebirth in Sukhāvatī rather than in saṃsāra. A radically transformed soteriology compared to Theravāda's stratified meditative-attainment scheme.
- **The cult of the name (*nian fo* / *nembutsu* / *yōmbul*)** — the recitation of "Namu Amida Butsu" / "Namo Amituofo" / "Namo Amitabhaya" — the most-recited single phrase in human religious practice over the last thousand years.
- [[divine-emanation]] (Mahāyāna form) — Amitābha as the cosmic reward-body Buddha, emanating saving power across vast distance.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-4-061-lotus-sutra]] / [[phase-4-062-avatamsaka-sutra]] / [[phase-4-063-lankavatara-sutra]] — *parallel-motif* / *shared-milieu*: the four foundational East Asian Mahāyāna sūtra-corpora. The Pure Land tradition (this text) is the *bhakti* wing; the Lotus is the *upāya* / universalist wing; the Avataṃsaka is the cosmological wing; the Laṅkāvatāra is the philosophical Mind-Only wing.
- → [[amitabha]] — *deity-source*: this is *the* foundational textual source for the Amitābha cult. The Sūtra constitutes the buddha and his Pure Land as objects of devotion.
- → [[avalokitesvara]] / [[guanyin]] — *shared-figure*: Avalokiteśvara is one of the two attending bodhisattvas of Amitābha; Pure Land and Avalokiteśvara/Guanyin devotion are deeply intertwined in East Asian practice.
- → **Chinese Pure Land masters** (Tanluan, Daochuo, Shandao) — *direct-influence*: Shandao's commentaries (7th c.) systematize the Sūtra into the foundation of a self-conscious Pure Land school.
- → **Japanese Pure Land (Hōnen, Shinran)** — *direct-influence*: Hōnen's *Senchakushū* (1198) and Shinran's *Kyōgyōshinshō* (1224) take the Larger Sukhāvatīvyūha as the supreme sūtra and the *nembutsu* as the sole sufficient practice.
- → **Tibetan Buddhism** — *direct-influence*: Sukhāvatī devotion is a major feature of Tibetan practice (especially within Nyingma and Drikung lineages); the Pure Land sūtras are translated and studied alongside the more philosophically prominent Vajrayāna texts.

## Cross-tradition substrate
- **Devotional faith-tradition.** Pure Land Buddhism's faith-based soteriology — salvation through trust in the saving power of a divine figure, accessible to all regardless of intellectual or ritual expertise — is the closest Buddhist parallel to (a) Christian Pietism and the Lutheran *sola fide* doctrine, (b) Hindu *bhakti* traditions (especially Vaiṣṇava bhakti via Rāmānuja and the Vaikuṇṭha doctrine of Viṣṇu's saving grace), (c) Sufi mystical-love poetry and the doctrine of God's mercy as the operative salvific agency. Four traditions independently developing the doctrine that *the divine reaches down and the human reaches up in trust*. The Lutheran-Shinran parallel has been worked out at length (Bloom 1965 *Shinran's Gospel of Pure Grace*; the Christian-Buddhist dialogue tradition centered at Nanzan and Claremont). Substrate; *parallel-motif*, with several independent developments and some demonstrable structural recurrence. Wire to [[bhakti-devotion]].
- **Mystical paradise and the heavenly geography.** Sukhāvatī as the jewel-treed western paradise belongs to a cross-tradition substrate of mystical-paradise geographies: the Zoroastrian *Garōdmān* / House of Song; the Hebrew Bible's Eden and the apocalyptic-tradition's heavenly Jerusalem (Revelation 21); the Quranic *jannāt 'adn* with its rivers and gardens; the Greek Elysium and the Hellenistic Isles of the Blessed. Substrate; the question of *Iranian/Zoroastrian influence* on Pure Land paradise imagery is contested but actively studied (see Disputes). Williams 2009 ch. 10 surveys.
- **Calling the divine name.** The Pure Land *nembutsu* tradition belongs to a cross-tradition substrate of devotional name-recitation: the Christian *Jesus Prayer* and Hesychast tradition (Mount Athos, the *Philokalia*); the Sufi *dhikr* / remembrance of the divine names; the Hindu *japa* of mantra-syllables and the *nāma-saṅkīrtana* of Caitanya-Vaiṣṇava bhakti; the Sikh *Nām Simran*. Five traditions independently developing the practice of repetitive divine-name recitation as the operative spiritual practice. Substrate; functional parallel of deep structural significance for the comparative study of devotional practice.

## Disputes
- **Date.** Composition probably 1st-2nd c. CE; Saṃghavarman's 252 CE translation fixes a terminus. The earliest Chinese translation by Lokakṣema (c. 188 CE) pushes the Indian original earlier.
- **Iranian / Zoroastrian influence on Sukhāvatī imagery.** The hypothesis that Pure Land paradisiacal imagery owes something to Zoroastrian eschatological imagery (the *Garōdmān*, the *frashō-kereti* renovation of the world) has been advanced by various scholars (Pelliot, Lamotte, more recently various authors) and resisted by others. The Gandhāran context makes contact plausible; the doctrinal distinctness of the two systems makes direct borrowing hard to demonstrate. The substrate-overlap is real; *transmissional* claims remain contested. *[claim: contested]*
- **Number of Dharmākara's vows.** Saṃghavarman's standard Chinese text has 48 vows; the Sanskrit has 47; other recensions vary. The numbering of the *Original Vow* therefore varies: Vow 18 in Saṃghavarman; 19 in Bodhiruci; the *content* is consistent across recensions.
- **Pure Land soteriology and the broader Mahāyāna.** Whether the Pure Land tradition's faith-based access constitutes a different soteriology from the bodhisattva-path emphasis of most Mahāyāna, or an *easy entry* into the same path (Shinran's reformulation), is a long-running internal debate.
- **The 252 CE translation's attribution to Saṃghavarman.** Some scholars argue the standard "Saṃghavarman" attribution is unreliable and the translation may be by another hand. The text itself is securely attested.
