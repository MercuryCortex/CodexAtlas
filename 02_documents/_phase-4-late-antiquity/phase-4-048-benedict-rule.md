# Rule of St Benedict

## What it is
A short Latin monastic rule in 73 chapters, composed by Benedict of Nursia at Monte Cassino in the 530s–540s. The work abbreviates and adapts an anonymous longer Latin rule — the *Regula Magistri* ("Rule of the Master") — and incorporates explicit references to John Cassian, Basil, and the *Vitae Patrum*. The constitution of Western monasticism for 1,500 years; the dominant monastic rule in the Latin West from Carolingian times onward.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Ostrogothic and (after 540) Byzantine-reconquering Italy; a period of war, plague, and political collapse. Monte Cassino offered a structured Christian community as a stability-point.
- **Religious milieu.** Egyptian-monastic models had been arriving in the Latin West for two centuries via the *Vita Antonii* (translated ~373), Cassian (~420s), and via Latin transmissions of Basil. The *Regula Magistri* had developed an Italian-Latin synthesis; Benedict's edit is more practical, briefer, and more measured.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous transmission. The original autograph was lost when Monte Cassino was sacked by Lombards in 581; the rule survived in copies, became dominant via Carolingian patronage (the Aachen synod of 817 under Benedict of Aniane's leadership made it the exclusive rule of Frankish monasticism), and has been continuously copied and observed since.

## Themes and motifs
- [[monastic-asceticism]] — the work IS the constitutional text of Western monastic life. Daily office (the seven canonical hours plus the night office), communal labor, communal property, communal meals, hospitality, formation of novices, discipline, governance by an elected abbot.
- *Ora et labora* (pray and work) — the famous tag is not in the Rule literally but summarizes its balance.
- *Stabilitas* (stability of place) — Benedict's distinctive contribution: the monk is bound to one community for life.

## Connections (provisional)
- → ***Regula Magistri*** (anonymous, mid-6th c., Italian) — *direct-source-edited-from*: Benedict's Rule abbreviates the Master's Rule (in some chapters compressing 90% of the Master's text). The textual relationship was definitively established by de Vogüé and others in the 20th c. (reversing the earlier assumption that the Master had expanded Benedict).
- → [[phase-4-047-cassian-conferences]] — *direct-influence*: RB 73.5 explicitly recommends Cassian as required reading.
- → [[phase-4-041-athanasius-life-of-antony]] — *indirect-source*: the *Vita Antonii*'s monastic exemplar through subsequent Latin tradition.
- → Egyptian desert ([[antony-the-great]], [[pachomius]], the *Apophthegmata Patrum*) — *substrate-source*: the practical content of Western monasticism — the daily horarium, the rule of obedience, the eight / seven vices, the discernment-of-thoughts practice — is **Egyptian Coptic monasticism Latin-translated and Italianized**. Benedict is the conduit; the substance comes from the desert. de Vogüé 2001: 47–88.
- → Basil's *Asketikon* (in Rufinus's Latin translation, *Regula Basilii*) — *direct-influence*: Cappadocian-Greek monastic patterns.
- → Pre-Christian Italian rural sacred-site Christianization — *substrate-substitution*: Monte Cassino was founded on the site of a sanctuary of Apollo (per Gregory's *Dialogues* II.8). The Western monastic estate-and-shrine pattern domesticates older rural sacred-site practice into Christian forms.

## Disputes
- **Authorship.** Benedictine; the *Regula Magistri* relationship (Benedict edits the Master, not vice versa) is settled by modern scholarship.
- **Date.** 530s–540s is the consensus range.
- **Survival of Monte Cassino.** The 581 Lombard sack scattered the monks but a copy of the Rule had reached Rome before; the medieval transmission continues from there.
