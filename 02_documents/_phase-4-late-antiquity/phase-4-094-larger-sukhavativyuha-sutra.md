# Larger Sukhāvatīvyūha Sūtra (*Description of the Land of Bliss*)

## Overview

The *Larger Sukhāvatīvyūha Sūtra* ("Description of the Land of Bliss" / *Wuliang Shou Jing* 無量壽經 — "Sutra of Immeasurable Life") is the foundational Pure Land scripture and the primary textual source for the dominant stream of popular Buddhist practice across East Asia. Composed in Sanskrit in Northwest India approximately 1st–3rd century CE and translated into Chinese in five separate versions (the earliest by Saṃghavarman, ~252 CE), it narrates the story of the bodhisattva Dharmākara, who makes 48 vows before the Buddha Lokeśvararāja, creates the Pure Land of Sukhāvatī, and becomes the cosmic Buddha [[amitabha]] (Amituofo / Amida).

The text's soteriological payload is unique in Buddhist history: the 18th of Dharmākara's 48 vows promises that any sentient being who sincerely calls upon Amitabha's name at the moment of death — even just ten times — will be received into Sukhāvatī (the Pure Land) and from there will achieve full enlightenment. This *nianfo* (Chinese) / *nembutsu* (Japanese) practice — recitation of the name "*Namo Amituofo*" / "*Namu Amida Butsu*" — became the most widely practiced Buddhist method across China, Japan, Korea, and Vietnam.

The theological mechanism is *tariki* ("other-power" in Japanese) — salvation by reliance on Amitabha's vow-power (*hongan-riki* — "original vow power"), not by one's own meditation, study, or moral effort (*jiriki* — "self-power"). In Shinran's Jōdo Shinshū (True Pure Land Buddhism, ~1200 CE), this became the most radical statement of grace-soteriology in any Asian tradition.

## Composition

The text exists in five distinct Chinese translations reflecting at least three different Sanskrit recensions — an extraordinarily rare situation that documents the sutra's compositional development over centuries. Scholars (Gómez, Karashima) use comparative analysis of the five versions to reconstruct layers: an early form emphasizing meditative contemplation of the Pure Land; a later expansion of Dharmākara's vows to 48 (from earlier versions with fewer); progressive emphasis on the *nianfo* practice as the primary vehicle.

The Sanskrit originals are almost entirely lost; the sutra is known primarily through its Chinese and Tibetan translations.

## Contents

**Narrative frame:** Śākyamuni Buddha addresses his disciple Ānanda in the presence of 12,000 monks and a vast assembly of bodhisattvas, gods, and dragons.

**Lokeśvararāja section:** Long ages ago, the monk Dharmākara approached the ancient Buddha Lokeśvararāja, announced his aspiration to create a pure Buddha-field, and requested a survey of all existing Buddha-fields and their qualities.

**The 48 Vows of Dharmākara (central section):** Dharmākara articulates 48 vows, each beginning: "If, when I attain Buddhahood, sentient beings in the lands of the ten directions who [X condition] do not [achieve result Y], may I not attain perfect enlightenment." The 18th vow is the heart of Pure Land soteriology:

> *"If, when I attain Buddhahood, sentient beings of the ten directions who, with sincere mind, joyful trust, and aspiration to be born in my land, even ten times calling to mind my name, should not be born there — may I not attain perfect enlightenment."*

This is the *Primal Vow* (*hongan* in Japanese). Amitabha's Buddhahood is pledged as surety for the liberation of all who sincerely call his name.

**Description of Sukhāvatī:** The text describes the Pure Land in elaborate sensory detail — jeweled trees, rivers with perfumed water, lotus flowers bearing newborn bodhisattvas, the absence of women (controversial — women must be reborn as men to enter, in the earlier text; later Pure Land thought complicates this), no suffering, constant Dharma teaching from the Buddha.

**Ānanda's request and the Buddha's confirmation:** The narrative closes with the Buddha confirming that Dharmākara has fulfilled his vows and is now [[amitabha]], that Sukhāvatī exists in the west, and that beings who practice *nianfo* will be born there.

## Defining contributions

**1. Vow-based soteriology (*hongan*):** The 48 vows of Dharmākara — fulfilled vows that constitute Amitabha's ongoing power to receive beings — created the most fully developed "grace-based" soteriology in Asian religious history. The vow is not a past event but an ontologically present power: Amitabha's enlightenment *is* the redemptive availability of the Pure Land.

**2. *Tariki* (other-power):** The explicit rejection of self-power effort as the vehicle of liberation — culminating in Shinran's Jōdo Shinshū — represents the Buddhist tradition's most radical assertion that human effort cannot achieve liberation; only reliance on Amitabha's power can.

**3. *Nianfo* / *nembutsu* (name-recitation) as complete practice:** The simplicity of the practice — recitation of the Name — made Pure Land Buddhism universally accessible regardless of education, social status, gender, or moral history. This is explicitly stated: even those who have committed the five grave offenses can be saved if they sincerely call Amitabha's name ten times before death.

**4. Eschatological pure land as soteriological destination:** The Pure Land is not the final goal (full enlightenment) but an intermediate state: a "training ground" where reborn beings encounter Amitabha personally and achieve enlightenment under ideal conditions. This two-stage structure (birth in Pure Land → enlightenment) influenced subsequent East Asian Buddhist eschatology significantly.

## Relations to neighbors

**MASSIVE-win edge — Pure Land *tariki* soteriology as the closest Buddhist parallel to Protestant *sola fide*:** Jōdo Shinshū (True Pure Land Buddhism, founded by Shinran ~1200 CE) is the closest structural parallel in any Asian tradition to Lutheran/Protestant Christianity's *sola fide* (salvation by faith alone). The four structural parallels are exact:
1. **Human effort cannot achieve liberation:** Shinran taught that in the "Latter Days of the Dharma" (*mappō*), beings are too corrupt to achieve enlightenment by self-power. Luther taught that human will is entirely corrupted (*servum arbitrium*, [[phase-6-007-luther-bondage-of-will]]) — no human effort can merit salvation.
2. **Faith / sincere reliance on a transcendent power is the vehicle:** Shinran: *shinjin* (sincere entrusting to Amitabha's Original Vow). Luther: *sola fide* — faith alone in Christ's atoning work, not human works. Both traditions: the saving action is *entirely* from the transcendent side; the human contribution is receptivity / faith.
3. **Populist devotional movement displacing elite paths:** Pure Land displaced elite meditation (*zazen*, *kōan*) as the dominant popular Buddhist path in East Asia. Protestant Christianity displaced Catholic sacramental-works system as the dominant path for millions. Both produced mass lay religiosity on an unprecedented scale.
4. **Temporal coincidence (~1200 CE):** Shinran's Jōdo Shinshū (~1200 CE Japan) and Francis of Assisi's populist Christianity (~1200 CE) / pre-Lutheran reform movements appear in almost exactly the same century. No historical connection — pure structural parallel. Documented comparison: Alfred Bloom, *Shinran's Gospel of Pure Grace* (1965); Jan Van Bragt, "Pure Land Buddhism and Protestant Christianity" in *Buddhist-Christian Studies* (1991).

**→ [[tradition-bhakti-vaishnavism]]** — *structural-parallel*: Hindu *bhakti* (devotional surrender to Vishnu/Krishna) has the same architecture as Pure Land *tariki*: liberation through loving surrender to a divine person's grace, not through one's own effort. [[phase-2-027-bhagavad-gita]] Chapter 12 on *bhakti-yoga* is the closest Hindu parallel to Dharmākara's 18th Vow. The two traditions appear to develop independently though within overlapping Indian cultural space.

**→ [[amitabha]]** — *defining-locus*: The Larger Sukhāvatīvyūha is the primary scriptural source for Amitabha's character, vows, and Pure Land.

**→ [[avalokitesvara]]** — *co-presence*: Avalokitesvara and Mahāsthāmaprāpta attend Amitabha in Sukhāvatī; Avalokitesvara leads beings westward to the Pure Land at death. The Guanyin devotional cult and Pure Land practice are structurally linked.

**→ [[justification-by-faith]]** — *structural-parallel*: The *tariki* / *sola fide* parallel is explicit in Buddhist-Christian dialogue literature (Van Bragt, Bloom). Both traditions articulate justification / liberation as received, not achieved.

**→ [[tradition-pure-land-buddhism]]** — *foundational-document*: This sutra, together with the *Smaller Sukhāvatīvyūha* and *Amitāyurdhyāna*, constitutes the "Three Pure Land Sutras" which are the canonical basis of the entire Pure Land tradition.
