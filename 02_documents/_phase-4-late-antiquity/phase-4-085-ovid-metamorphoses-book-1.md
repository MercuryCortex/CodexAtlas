# Ovid, Metamorphoses Book 1

## What it is
Book 1 of Ovid's *Metamorphoses* (composed ~2–8 CE, Augustan Rome) contains the principal Latin literary form of the **Deucalion-Pyrrha flood narrative** (lines 260–415) — the Greek-Mediterranean flood-tradition's reception into Latin epic poetry. Book 1 as a whole moves from cosmogony (1.1–88 — the creation of the world from primordial *chaos*; one of the most influential cosmogonic passages in Western literature) through the four ages (1.89–150 — gold, silver, bronze, iron — drawing on Hesiod *Works and Days*) through the Lycaon impiety (1.163–252 — Jupiter's decision to destroy humanity) through the flood (1.253–312 — Jupiter sends the flood with Neptune's help) through the survival of [[deucalion]] and [[pyrrha]] on Mount Parnassus (1.313–415 — the survivors' repopulation of humanity by throwing stones).

The work is **the principal Latin channel** through which Greek mythology reached medieval and Renaissance Europe. Where the Greek-language sources (Pindar, Apollodorus, Hesiod) were largely lost to the Latin West between ~500 and ~1450 CE, Ovid's *Metamorphoses* never ceased circulation. For a millennium it was *the* book through which Western literate culture knew the Greek myths — including the Deucalion-Pyrrha flood — which is why Dante, Petrarch, Chaucer, Shakespeare, and Milton all engage Ovid's flood directly rather than (or alongside) Genesis.

## Context

- **Political milieu.** Augustan Rome (27 BCE – 14 CE). Ovid composed the *Metamorphoses* in the final years of Augustus's reign; in 8 CE he was exiled by Augustus to Tomis on the Black Sea for reasons not fully recoverable (a poem [*Ars Amatoria*] + an unspecified "error"). The *Metamorphoses* was substantially complete by the time of his exile.
- **Religious milieu.** Augustan-Roman religious-cultural synthesis. Ovid's poem treats Greek mythology with a literary-sophisticated voice that is sympathetic but not devotional. The Deucalion-Pyrrha flood is treated as poetic narrative rather than religious truth.
- **Who was behind it.** Publius Ovidius Naso (43 BCE – 17/18 CE), one of the great Augustan poets alongside Virgil and Horace.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous Latin manuscript transmission from late antiquity through medieval Christian Europe; never out of print since the 1471 *editio princeps* at Bologna.

## Themes and motifs

- [[flood-motif]] — Ovid 1.253–415 is the principal Latin literary form of the Mediterranean flood-tradition.
- [[cosmogony]] — Ovid 1.1–88 is one of the most-influential cosmogonic passages in Western literature.
- [[anthropogony]] — the repopulation-by-stones motif (1.395–415) is the Greek-Roman tradition's distinctive post-flood-anthropogony.
- [[golden-age-decline]] — the four-ages structure (1.89–150) integrates the Hesiodic decline-of-the-ages pattern.

## Connections (provisional)

- → Greek sources behind Ovid — *redaction-of*: Ovid draws on Pindar, Apollodorus, Hesiod, and Greek mythographers broadly. The Latin compositional choices (rhetorical elaboration, psychological characterization) are Ovid's own.
- → [[phase-1-006-atrahasis]] / [[phase-1-016-eridu-genesis-flood]] / [[phase-1-004-gilgamesh-old-babylonian]] Tablet XI — *parallel-form* / *cognate-tradition*: Mesopotamian flood-tradition is the deeper Mediterranean substrate (West 1997 *The East Face of Helicon*); direct Mesopotamian → Ovid transmission is unprovable but the structural parallels are extensive.
- → Genesis 6–9 — *parallel-form*: Hebrew flood-tradition contemporaneously circulating in the Mediterranean world via the LXX.
- → [[phase-3-022-plato-timaeus-critias-atlantis]] — *shared-milieu*: Plato's multiple-floods framework (the Egyptian priest to Solon: "you Greeks have had many floods") operates within the broader Greek tradition.
- → Medieval-and-Renaissance Latin reception — Dante, Petrarch, Boccaccio, Chaucer, Shakespeare, Milton all engage Ovid Met. 1 directly.

## Disputes

- **Date of completion.** Conventional dating: substantially complete by 8 CE (the year of Ovid's exile); revisions may have continued in exile until Ovid's death ~17/18 CE.
- **Mesopotamian → Greek → Latin transmission of the flood-tradition.** West 1997 argues for substantial Mesopotamian → Greek transmission; older scholarship treated the Greek flood as independent-parallel; current consensus favors substantial Eastern influence with localized Greek elaboration (Burkert 1992).
