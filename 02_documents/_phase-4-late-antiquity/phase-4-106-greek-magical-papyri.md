# Greek Magical Papyri (Papyri Graecae Magicae)

## What they are

The Greek Magical Papyri (PGM) are a collection of papyri discovered in Egypt and dating from roughly 200 BCE to 500 CE, edited in modern form by Karl Preisendanz (2 vols, 1928–1931) and translated into English by Hans Dieter Betz (1986; 2nd ed. 1992). They constitute the most important surviving evidence of how ordinary religious practice actually functioned in Greco-Roman Egypt — as opposed to the theological treatises produced by educated elites (the Hermetica, Philo, the Gnostic texts of Nag Hammadi). The PGM are street-level, practitioner-facing religious technology.

The collection includes material from multiple Egyptian sites (predominantly the Fayum, Oxyrhynchus, and provenance-unknown caches). Many texts appear to come from professional magical practitioners (*magoi*, *magi*, Coptic *ḥik-personnel*) — scribal or priestly specialists who maintained collections of ritual recipes, hymns, and spells for clients.

## Context: the religious world the PGM inhabit

The PGM emerge from **bilingual, multicultural Roman Egypt** — a world in which the Greek-speaking population (including Hellenized Egyptians, Greek settlers, and a large Jewish diaspora) coexisted with Demotic-speaking Egyptian temple personnel, Coptic-speaking Christian communities, and various Gnostic and Hermetic circles. All of these traditions left traces in the papyri.

This is the same historical environment that produced [[phase-3-021-hermetic-corpus-earliest|the Hermetic Corpus]] and the Nag Hammadi Gnostic texts (see [[tradition-gnosticism]]); the philosophical-theological Hermetica and the practical-magical PGM represent two ends of the same syncretic spectrum — the educated elite synthesis and the popular practical implementation. [[phase-3-013-philo-of-alexandria|Philo of Alexandria]] represents a parallel Jewish attempt to synthesize Greek philosophy and Hebrew scripture in the same milieu; the PGM show what that syncretic ferment looked like at the practitioner level.

## Themes and motifs

### What the papyri contain

A single PGM papyrus might contain: grammatically Greek hymns to Helios (Greek sun god) that identify him with Ra (Egyptian); ritual instructions requiring Egyptian temple ingredients (Nile water, *natron*, specific incense blends) executed in Greek transliteration; spells invoking "Iao" (the Greek rendering of the Hebrew divine name YHWH), "Adonai" (Hebrew divine title *Adonay*), "Sabaoth" (Hebrew divine title *Tzvaot*), "El" (Hebrew/Canaanite divine name); and, in the 3rd–4th century CE texts, invocations of "Iesous" (Jesus) or "the god of Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and Jesus." The same document may invoke [[thoth]], [[hermes-trismegistus]], and Iao in sequence.

This is not syncretism as a philosophical position: these practitioners were not theologians seeking a unified account of the divine. It is **pragmatic syncretic fusion** — the aggregation of maximum divine power through the accumulation of the most potent divine names from every available tradition.

### Jewish divine names in Egyptian magical papyri — MASSIVE WIN

The tetragrammaton (YHWH) renders in Greek transliteration as "Iao" throughout the PGM; "Adonai," "Sabaoth," "El," and "Iao Sabaoth" appear hundreds of times across the corpus. They are invoked by non-Jewish Egyptian practitioners precisely because Jewish divine names were considered the most powerful available in the Greco-Roman magical world.

This is documented cross-tradition evidence for a profound irony: [[tradition-judaism|Jewish monotheism]]'s insistence on divine exclusivity (the Mosaic Distinction — Jan Assmann's term for the revolutionary claim that only YHWH is god and all other gods are false) coexisted, in practice, with Jewish divine names being *borrowed wholesale* by neighboring traditions as magical power-words. The god who could not be named or depicted became, paradoxically, the most-named god in the Greco-Roman magical papyri. See [[jan-assmann]] for the Mosaic Distinction concept.

### Christian divine names in the PGM — the bottom-up Christianity evidence

By the 3rd–4th century CE, Christian divine names appear in the PGM as magical resources: "Iesous" (Jesus), "the god of Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and Jesus," formulas recognizable from early Christian liturgy. In PGM IV (the "Great Magical Papyrus of Paris" — the longest surviving magical papyrus, c. 4th c. CE), Christian elements appear alongside Egyptian, Greek, and Jewish divine names in the same rolls.

This is the most important evidence for what Christianity looked like from *below* — from the perspective of ordinary people who did not read Irenaeus or Origen and were not making theological commitments to doctrinal exclusivism. For many people in Roman Egypt, "Christian" divine names were one tradition's resource among many. The contrast between **elite theological exclusivism** (the developing [[tradition-early-christianity|early Christian]] insistence that Christ is the only way and that magic is diabolical) and **popular practical pluralism** (using Christian divine names alongside Egyptian and Jewish ones) is one of the vault's central cross-tradition tensions.

### The Thoth-Hermes-Iao synthesis in popular form

The philosophical identification of Thoth with Hermes (and both with the divine intellect of the cosmos) is documented in the [[phase-3-021-hermetic-corpus-earliest|Hermetic Corpus]] as an educated-elite synthesis. The PGM show the same identification operating in the popular magical register: spells invoke "Hermes Trismegistus" alongside Thoth (sometimes in Demotic), and both alongside "Iao." [[thoth]] and [[hermes-trismegistus]] appear as functionally interchangeable names for the same divine power across multiple PGM texts — a `syncretic-identification` edge with Tier-1 PGM documentation. See [[tradition-hermeticism]] for the elite synthesis; the PGM are the street-level confirmation.

### Languages and the multi-tradition archive

The papyri are written in three languages: **Greek** (dominant), **Coptic** (Egyptian in the Greek alphabet — the language of Christian Egypt), and **Demotic** (the cursive late form of Egyptian hieratic script). The bilingual and trilingual texts are especially revealing: a practitioner who could write in all three languages was either a Coptic Christian with Egyptian temple training, or a Demotic priest who had learned Greek, or (in one famous bilingual Greek-Demotic papyrus, the *Magical Papyrus of London and Leiden*) a practitioner maintaining parallel recipe-books in multiple scribal traditions.

## Connections (provisional)

- `syncretic-fusion` with [[tradition-egyptian-religion]]: Egyptian divine names, temple procedures, and cosmological concepts provide the substrate.
- `syncretic-fusion` with [[tradition-judaism]]: Jewish divine names (Iao, Adonai, Sabaoth, El) are the most heavily borrowed set of divine names in the corpus.
- `syncretic-fusion` with [[tradition-early-christianity]]: 3rd–4th c. CE Christian divine names incorporated as magical power-words — before, during, and after the institutional church condemned magical practice.
- `syncretic-fusion` with [[tradition-gnosticism]]: Gnostic divine names and cosmological figures (Iao Sabaoth, Yaldabaoth, Barbelo) appear in several PGM texts; the boundary between Gnostic ritual practice and PGM-style magic is porous.
- `parallel-milieu` with [[phase-3-021-hermetic-corpus-earliest]]: Same Greco-Roman Egypt environment; the Hermetica and the PGM represent elite and popular expressions of the same syncretic ferment.
- `shared-milieu` with [[phase-3-013-philo-of-alexandria]]: Same Jewish diaspora Egypt context, roughly same centuries; Philo represents the philosophical integration of Jewish and Greek thought, the PGM the practical-magical integration.
- `syncretic-identification` edge [[thoth]] ↔ [[hermes-trismegistus]]: PGM provide Tier-1 documentation of the popular-register equivalence.
- `syncretic-fusion` with [[tradition-hermeticism]]: PGM texts explicitly invoke Hermes Trismegistus in the same frame as Egyptian and Jewish divine names; Hermetic philosophical synthesis and PGM magical synthesis are two faces of the same Thoth-Hermes identification.
