# Leviticus

**Leviticus** (Hebrew *Wayyiqrāʾ*, "And he called") is the third and most ritually dense book of the **Torah** — the priestly manual at the literary and theological center of the Pentateuch. It sets out the system of **sacrifice** (the burnt, grain, well-being, purification, and reparation offerings), the **ordination of the priesthood** (Aaron and his sons), the laws of **clean and unclean** (dietary law, childbirth, skin-disease, bodily emissions), the great ritual of the **Day of Atonement** (Leviticus 16, with the two goats and the scapegoat sent to Azazel), and the **Holiness Code** (chs. 17–26) with its refrain "You shall be holy, for I, YHWH your God, am holy."

## Composition

Leviticus is the core work of the **Priestly (P)** tradition, framed by the distinct **Holiness Code (H)** — which Milgrom and Knohl argue is not an older block enclosed by P but the **later redactional layer** that supplements and frames it. H is marked by its direct exhortation, its blend of ethical and cultic command (including "love your neighbour as yourself," 19:18), and its sustained holiness-theology. The book reflects the priestly reimagining of Israel's relationship to God around **holiness, purity, and atonement**, crystallized in the exilic and post-exilic period.

## The cross-tradition wire

Leviticus's ritual world is part of the broader **ancient Near Eastern system of purity, sacrifice, and temple cult**: parallels run to Mesopotamian and especially **Hittite/Anatolian elimination rituals** (the **scapegoat** sent into the wilderness has close analogues in Hittite rites transferring impurity or plague onto an animal driven into enemy territory). What distinguishes the Israelite system is its consolidation around a single sanctuary and a single God, and the ethical fusion of the Holiness Code.

## Reach forward

Leviticus is the foundation of the entire later Jewish system of **kashrut, purity, and sacrifice**, of the Yom Kippur liturgy, and — by inversion and fulfilment — of the New Testament theology of atonement, where the **Epistle to the Hebrews** reads Christ as both high priest and offering.
