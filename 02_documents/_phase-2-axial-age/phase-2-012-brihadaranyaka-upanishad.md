# Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad

## What it is
The Bṛhadāraṇyaka ("Great Forest") Upaniṣad is the longest and, by scholarly consensus, the oldest of the principal Upaniṣads. It forms the closing portion of the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa, marking the transition from priestly ritualism to philosophical introspection. The text is organized into six chapters (*adhyāyas*), three pairs (*kāṇḍas*), containing the most famous Upaniṣadic dialogues: Yājñavalkya's debates at King Janaka's court (chs. 3–4), Yājñavalkya's instruction of his wife Maitreyī on the *ātman* and immortality (2.4, 4.5), the great identification of *brahman* and *ātman*, the cosmic-horse meditation, and the doctrine of *karma* and rebirth in its earliest sustained form (3.2, 4.4).

## Primary text — selected excerpts

Excerpts use F. Max Müller's *Sacred Books of the East* vol. XV (1884) translation, which is in the public domain and freely accessible at sacred-texts.com. Müller's renderings are 19th-century and now superseded by Patrick Olivelle's *Early Upaniṣads* (Oxford, 1998), which incorporates a century of philological progress and a critical edition of the Sanskrit; readers should consult Olivelle for any close interpretive use.

### BṛhUp 1.4.1–3 — the cosmogony of the primal Self

The Bṛhadāraṇyaka opens its philosophical section with a cosmogony in which the primal Self (*ātman*) is *alone, afraid, lonely*, and creates a partner by splitting itself into male and female. This is one of the earliest texts in the world to ground the cosmos in self-recognition and desire.

> In the beginning this (world) was Self (Âtman) alone, in the shape of a person (purusha). He looking round saw nothing but his Self. He first said, 'This is I'; therefore he became I by name. Therefore even now, if a man is asked, he first says, 'This is I,' and then pronounces the other name which he may have...
>
> He feared, and therefore any one who is lonely fears. He thought, 'As there is nothing but myself, why should I fear?' Thence his fear passed away. For what should he have feared? Verily fear arises from a second only.
>
> But he felt no delight. Therefore a man who is lonely feels no delight. He wished for a second. He was so large as man and wife together. He then made this his Self to fall in two (pat), and thence arose husband (pati) and wife (patnî). Therefore Yâgñavalkya said: 'We two are thus (each of us) like half a shell.'

Citation: BṛhUp 1.4.1–3, trans. F. Max Müller 1884, *SBE* XV; for current critical edition see Olivelle 1998, pp. 44–47.

### BṛhUp 2.4.5–14 — Yājñavalkya instructs Maitreyī

Yājñavalkya, departing to become a forest-renouncer, instructs his wife Maitreyī. The dialogue contains the foundational Upaniṣadic move: not the things of the world but the *Self* is what is dear; through knowing the Self, all is known.

> 'Verily, a husband is not dear, that you may love the husband; but that you may love the Self, therefore a husband is dear.
>
> 'Verily, a wife is not dear, that you may love the wife; but that you may love the Self, therefore a wife is dear.
>
> 'Verily, sons are not dear, that you may love the sons; but that you may love the Self, therefore sons are dear...
>
> 'Verily, everything is not dear, that you may love everything; but that you may love the Self, therefore everything is dear.
>
> 'Verily, the Self is to be seen, to be heard, to be perceived, to be marked, O Maitreyî! When the Self has been seen, heard, perceived, and known, then all this is known.'

Citation: BṛhUp 2.4.5–6, trans. Müller 1884; cf. Olivelle 1998, pp. 68–71.

### BṛhUp 3.9.26 / 4.5.15 — *neti, neti* (the apophatic formula)

The most famous apophatic phrase in Indian thought. The Self/Brahman is not any predicate one can offer; what remains is the silent Witness. The formula is cited in every later Vedānta school.

> He, the Self, is to be described by No, no! He is incomprehensible, for he cannot be comprehended; he is imperishable, for he cannot perish; he is unattached, for he does not attach himself; he is unbound, he does not suffer, he does not fail.

Citation: BṛhUp 4.5.15 (cf. 3.9.26), trans. Müller 1884; cf. Olivelle 1998, pp. 130–131.

### BṛhUp 4.4.5–6 — the doctrine of karma

One of the earliest fully explicit statements of action shaping rebirth: a person is *made* by desire and action, becomes what one does. This is the doctrinal seed of the entire karma-rebirth complex that becomes shared across Hindu, Buddhist, and Jain traditions.

> And here they say that a person consists of desires. And as is his desire, so is his will; and as is his will, so is his deed; and whatever deed he does, that he will reap.
>
> And here there is this verse: 'To whatever object a man's own mind is attached, to that he goes strenuously together with his deed; and having obtained the end (the last results) of whatever deed he does here on earth, he returns again from that world (which is the temporary reward of his deed) to this world of action.'

Citation: BṛhUp 4.4.5–6, trans. Müller 1884; cf. Olivelle 1998, pp. 120–123.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Iron Age north India (~800–600 BCE). The setting is the kingdoms of Videha (capital Mithilā, King Janaka) and Kuru-Pañcāla. Settled urbanizing society (the Second Urbanization of South Asia); janapadas consolidating into kingdoms; the warrior-aristocracy (kṣatriya) increasingly contesting Brahmanical religious leadership.
- **Religious milieu.** A pivotal moment: the philosophical interior of the Vedic tradition is being articulated against (and within) the elaborate sacrificial ritualism of the Brāhmaṇas. Forest-dwellers, ascetics (*śramaṇas*), and itinerant teachers — Brahmanical and non-Brahmanical — are crystallizing the doctrines of karma, rebirth, and liberation that all three of the great Indian dharmic traditions (Brahmanical Hindu, Buddhist, Jain) will share.
- **Who was behind it.** The dominant voice is Yājñavalkya — a paradigmatic ṛṣi-philosopher whose historicity is generally accepted in some form. The text is a composite redaction within the Vedic schools.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous oral and manuscript transmission within Brahmanical lineages. First translated into a European language by Anquetil-Duperron (Latin, from the Persian Oupnekhat of Dārā Shikoh, 1801), reaching Schopenhauer and German Romanticism.

## Themes and motifs
- **Brahman = Ātman** — the central Upaniṣadic identification: the cosmic ultimate (*brahman*) is identical with the inner self (*ātman*). "*Ayam ātmā brahma*" ("This self is brahman", BṛhUp 2.5.19).
- **Neti, neti** — Yājñavalkya's apophatic formula (BṛhUp 2.3.6, 4.5.15): the ultimate is "not this, not this" — what cannot be grasped by predication.
- **Karma and rebirth** — BṛhUp 3.2.13 (the Jāratkārava-Ārtabhāga dialogue) gives one of the earliest explicit doctrines of *karman* shaping rebirth: "a man becomes good by good action, bad by bad action."
- **The two paths** — devayāna (way of the gods, leading to liberation) and pitṛyāna (way of the fathers, leading to rebirth), BṛhUp 6.2.
- **Cosmic-horse meditation** — BṛhUp 1.1 (the Aśvamedha horse equated with the cosmos): the ritual is internalized as meditation on cosmic correspondences.
- [[afterlife-judgment]] — not in the moralized Zoroastrian-Christian sense, but the soul's transmigration is determined by its actions: the proto-form of karma-cosmology.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-2-006-brahmanas-aranyakas]] — *redaction-of*: the BṛhUp is literally part of the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa's final chapters; it grows organically out of Brahmanic ritual exegesis.
- → [[phase-2-013-chandogya-upanishad]] — *shared-milieu* / *parallel-motif*: the two oldest principal Upaniṣads share doctrines, characters (Uddālaka Āruṇi appears in both), and method. Together they ground all later Vedānta.
- → Early Buddhism (Phase 2–3) — *shared-milieu* / *polemic-against*: Buddhist and Jain doctrines of karma, rebirth, and liberation share intellectual ancestry with the Upaniṣads; the Buddha critiques the *ātman* doctrine specifically (the *anātman* / not-self response). Ref: Bronkhorst 2007, *Greater Magadha*.
- → Orphic / Pythagorean reincarnation doctrines (~6th c. BCE Greece) — *parallel-motif* (independent emergence vs. transmission): the close chronological coincidence of explicit reincarnation doctrine in India and Greece has prompted speculation about contact via Persian intermediaries; direct dependence remains unestablished. Ref: West 1971, *Early Greek Philosophy and the Orient*.
- → Plato — *parallel-motif*: the *ātman* / immortal soul and the body as bondage have structural similarities with Phaedo's soul-doctrine, again with no established direct dependence.

## Disputes
- **Date.** Olivelle (1998) places the prose Upaniṣads (BṛhUp, ChU) at ~700–500 BCE, before the rise of Buddhism (~-500). Witzel argues for a slightly earlier window; some Indian scholars push earlier. The Buddha's known awareness of Upaniṣadic-style doctrine (in the Brahmajāla Sutta etc.) is a key terminus ad quem.
- **Yājñavalkya's historicity.** Most accept a historical kernel; some treat him as a literary persona.
- **Direction of influence with proto-Buddhism/Jainism.** Bronkhorst (2007) controversially argues that the karma-rebirth complex originates in a non-Vedic "Greater Magadha" culture and enters Brahmanism, rather than the other way around. Disputed.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- ***Aham Brahmāsmi* (1.4.10) — the vault's oldest first-person divine-identity claim:** "I am Brahman" is structurally identical to John 8:58's "Before Abraham was, I AM" (divine-identity assertion causing immediate theological controversy), Hallāj's "Anā'l-Ḥaqq" ("I am the Real", executed 922 CE), and Eckhart's "I and the Father are one" (tried for heresy 1329). Four traditions, in four independent institutional contexts, produce the same dangerous first-person claim of identity with the ultimate — with the same institutional rejection in three of the four cases. The BṛhUp statement is the oldest dateable instance in the vault, attested by ~-700 BCE.
- ***Neti, neti* — the oldest apophatic formula in the vault:** "Not this, not this" (BṛhUp 2.3.6, 4.5.15) — Yājñavalkya's refusal of all predicates for the Self — pre-dates Plotinus' "the One is none of the things we name" (Enn. VI.9.4) by ~700 years and is structurally identical to the Daodejing's "the Dao that can be spoken is not the eternal Dao." This is the eastern anchor of the vault's apophatic cluster, and it is also the source from which all later Vedānta schools (Advaita, Viśiṣṭādvaita, Dvaita) derive their metaphysical vocabulary.
- **Karma-rebirth first explicit statement → shared doctrinal substrate:** BṛhUp 3.2.13 is among the oldest explicit karma-rebirth formulations in any surviving text; the same doctrine appears contemporaneously in early Jain Āgamas and in the later Buddhist canon, and in Orphic-Pythagorean metempsychosis in Greece (~6th c. BCE). Whether the Greek reincarnation doctrine was independently generated or reflects Axial-Age contact via Persia is unresolved; the chronological coincidence remains the sharpest puzzle in the comparative study of Axial-Age religion.
- **Anquetil-Duperron → Schopenhauer → 19th-century European philosophy:** The Upaniṣads entered Western modernity via Dārā Shikōh's Persian *Oupnekhat* (1656) → Anquetil-Duperron's Latin translation (1801) → Schopenhauer's *Die Welt als Wille und Vorstellung* (1818, heavily annotated with Upaniṣadic parallels to his "Will"). This is the single most consequential transmission route for Indian philosophy into Western intellectual culture — Schopenhauer's Upaniṣad-influenced pessimism fed Nietzsche, Wagner, Freud, and 20th-century comparative religion. Olivelle 1998 is the scholarly entry point; the transmission history is in Halbfass 1988, *India and Europe*.
