# Doctrine of the Mean (中庸) / Zhongyong

## What it is
The *Zhongyong* (中庸, 'Doctrine of the Mean' or 'Maintaining Perfect Balance') is the metaphysical capstone of [[zhu-xi]]'s Four Books sequence — placed last precisely because it operates at the highest level of abstraction, moving from the self-cultivation program of the [[phase-2-043-great-learning-daxue]] and the ethical detail of the Analects and Mengzi to a cosmological synthesis. The title concept *zhongyong* — often translated 'the Mean' — is better rendered as 'centrality and constancy' or 'the constant middle': *zhong* (中) means centered, not-exceeding, balanced; *yong* (庸) means constant, ordinary, pervading. The Zhongyong is not about psychological moderation (splitting the difference between extremes) but about aligning with the cosmic norm — the way things genuinely are — without deviation or excess in any direction.

## Primary text — selected passages

### Zhongyong — Opening (Chapter 1): Heaven, nature, Way, teaching
*Trans. Gardner, Daniel K. (2007). The Four Books: The Basic Teachings of the Later Confucian Tradition. Hackett.*
> What Heaven has conferred is called human nature. Following this nature is called the Way. Cultivating this Way is called teaching. The Way cannot be departed from for even a moment. That which can be departed from is not the Way.

*The Zhongyong's opening three sentences are among the most philosophically dense in the Confucian corpus: they ground human moral nature (*xing*) in Heaven's endowment, identify the Way (*Dao*) with the actualization of that nature, and define teaching as the cultivation of alignment with the Way. The sequence Heaven → nature → Way → teaching is the metaphysical skeleton of the entire Neo-Confucian self-cultivation tradition: morality is not humanly constructed but cosmically grounded, making moral failure a form of cosmic misalignment.*

*Citations: Gardner, D.K. (2007). The Four Books. Hackett; Chan, W. (1963). A Source Book in Chinese Philosophy. Princeton UP.*

### Zhongyong — Cheng (Sincerity) as cosmic self-completion (Chapter 20)
*Trans. Chan, Wing-tsit (1963). A Source Book in Chinese Philosophy. Princeton UP.*
> Sincerity (*cheng*) is the Way of Heaven. Achieving sincerity is the Way of humanity. One who is sincere is one who, without effort, hits what is right, and without thinking, apprehends. A sage who naturally and easily embodies the Way — this is the sage.

*The Zhongyong's most philosophically distinctive concept: *cheng* (sincerity / self-completion) is not a merely psychological quality but a cosmological mode of being — the mode in which Heaven itself operates. The sage who achieves *cheng* does not merely behave correctly; they become continuous with Heaven's self-completing process. This is the Confucian version of the "alignment with the ground of things" insight that independently characterizes Upaniṣadic Brahman-Ātman identity, Daoist *ziran*, and Stoic *oikeiōsis*.*

*Citations: Chan, W. (1963). A Source Book in Chinese Philosophy. Princeton UP.*

### Zhongyong — The cultivated human as co-creator with Heaven and Earth (Chapter 22)
*Trans. Gardner 2007.*
> Only those in the world who are absolutely sincere are able to realize their nature to the full. Able to realize their nature to the full, they are able to realize the natures of other people. Able to realize the natures of other people, they are able to realize the natures of things. Able to realize the natures of things, they can assist the transforming and nourishing operations of Heaven and Earth. Able to assist the transforming and nourishing operations of Heaven and Earth, they can form a ternion with Heaven and Earth.

*This is among the strongest statements of human cosmological agency in classical Chinese philosophy: the fully cultivated person does not merely align passively with Heaven but becomes an active co-creator — forming "a ternion with Heaven and Earth." The three-element cosmic partnership (Heaven / Earth / Human) is the Confucian parallel to Upaniṣadic Brahman-realization: the fully actualized human is not distinguished from the cosmic ground but continuous with it.*

*Citations: Gardner, D.K. (2007). The Four Books. Hackett.*

## Context
Traditionally attributed to Zisi (Kongji, ~-481 to -402 BCE), Confucius's grandson and the teacher of Mengzi, the Zhongyong is regarded as the textual link in the Confucian transmission line: Confucius → Zengzi → Zisi → Mengzi. Modern scholarship is less certain about Zisi's authorship; the text is composite and may represent a Zisi school tradition rather than a single author. The philosophical level is significantly higher than the Analects or Mengzi, suggesting sustained reflection on the cosmological implications of the Confucian ethical program, likely in dialogue with Daoist and other Warring States cosmological speculation.

[[zhu-xi]] placed the Zhongyong last in the Four Books sequence and wrote his most philosophically ambitious commentary on it, reading it as a cosmological treatise grounding the entire Confucian ethical project in the structure of Heaven and human nature. His commentary transformed the Zhongyong from a relatively minor *Liji* chapter into the metaphysical summit of Neo-Confucian thought.

## Core concepts

**Zhongyong (centrality and constancy).** The opening of the text states: "What Heaven has conferred is called *nature* (*xing*); following that nature is called the *Way* (*Dao*); cultivating that Way is called *teaching*." This three-step sequence — Heaven → nature → Way → teaching — grounds Confucian ethics in cosmological structure. Human moral nature (*xing*) is what Heaven has given; the Way is the actualization of that nature; education cultivates alignment with it.

**Cheng (sincerity / self-completing).** The concept of *cheng* (誠) — variously translated 'sincerity,' 'authenticity,' 'self-completion,' 'self-fulfillment' — is the Zhongyong's most philosophically distinctive contribution. *Cheng* is the mode of being of Heaven itself: Heaven's self-completing activity is the model for human self-cultivation. "Sincerity is the Way of Heaven. Achieving sincerity is the Way of humanity." The sage who achieves *cheng* does not merely behave correctly — they become continuous with Heaven's self-completing process. This is the Confucian version of cosmic immanence: not union with God (theistic) but alignment with the self-completing pattern of Heaven through moral cultivation.

**The fully developed human as co-creator.** The Zhongyong's final chapters assert that the fully cultivated person (*zhicheng* — one who has achieved complete sincerity) can "assist the transforming and nourishing operations of Heaven and Earth" and thereby "form a ternion with Heaven and Earth." This is among the strongest statements of human cosmological agency in the classical Confucian corpus — the human being, through full moral cultivation, becomes a third partner with Heaven and Earth in the ongoing creative process of the cosmos. This framework is the Confucian parallel to Hindu *Brahman* realization and Daoist *ziran* (spontaneous self-so-ness): the fully cultivated human is cosmologically continuous with the ground of things.

## Cross-tradition substrate
The Zhongyong's *cheng* concept — the self-completing sincerity that aligns the human with Heaven's nature — is a structurally significant parallel to:
- **Brahman-Atman identity** ([[phase-2-012-brihadaranyaka-upanishad]], [[phase-2-013-chandogya-upanishad]]): the human self realized as continuous with the cosmic ground — independent Chinese development, same cosmological logic
- **Daoist *ziran*** ([[phase-2-014-daodejing]], [[phase-2-022-zhuangzi]]): the sage who acts in perfect alignment with the Dao without forcing — the Confucian and Daoist versions of the "align with the ground of things" insight, developed in the same Warring States milieu in creative tension
- **Stoic *oikeiosis*** (the Stoic concept of familiarization / appropriation: the process by which the sage internalizes the rational order of the cosmos): structural parallel, independent development (Greece ~-300 BCE, China ~-300 BCE)

All three are independent Axial-Age developments (~-800 to -200 BCE) working out the same insight: there is a cosmic order, and full human actualization consists in aligning with it through cultivation/practice rather than through ritual propitiation or revelation.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges

- **Zhongyong *cheng* (cosmic sincerity) / Upaniṣadic Brahman-Ātman identity / Daoist *ziran* = most precisely mapped Axial Age convergence in metaphysics of "alignment with the cosmic ground"**: The Zhongyong's *cheng* (Heaven's self-completing sincerity as the goal of human cultivation), the Upaniṣadic doctrine of *Ātman = Brahman* (the human self realized as continuous with the cosmic ground; Bṛhadāraṇyaka, Chāndogya), and the Daoist *ziran* (the sage acting in spontaneous alignment with the Dao without forcing) are three independent Axial Age (~-800 to -200 BCE) developments converging on the same insight: there is a cosmic order; full human actualization consists in becoming continuous with it through cultivation/practice, not through ritual propitiation or revelation. Stoic *oikeiōsis* (the sage internalizing the rational order of the cosmos) completes the four-way parallel — all independent, all mapping the same structural move (Hall, D.L. and Ames, R.T. 1987. *Thinking Through Confucius*. SUNY Press. pp.175–238; Chan, W. 1963. *A Source Book in Chinese Philosophy*. Princeton UP. pp.95–114; McEvilley, T. 2002. *The Shape of Ancient Thought*. Allworth. pp.1–30).

- **Zhongyong "human forms a ternion with Heaven and Earth" (ch.22) = most explicit statement of human cosmological co-creativity in the Confucian canon — the primary Chinese parallel to theosis/deification**: The claim that the fully sincere person "can form a ternion with Heaven and Earth" — participating in the cosmic creative process as an equal third partner with the two ultimate cosmic principles — is the most radical statement of human cosmological dignity in the Confucian tradition. It is structurally parallel to: Eastern Orthodox *theōsis* (the human becomes divine by participation); Eckhart's *Gottesgeburt* (the birth of God in the soul makes the human co-creative with the divine process); Sufi *insān al-kāmil* (the Perfect Man as the cosmos's organ of self-awareness). All four posit the fully actualized human as a participant in cosmic creativity, not merely its product (Chan, W. 1963. pp.95–114; de Bary, W.T. 1981. *Neo-Confucian Orthodoxy and the Learning of the Mind-and-Heart*. Columbia UP. pp.1–40; Billioud, S. 2011. *Thinking Through Confucian Modernity*. Brill. pp.1–30).

- **Zhu Xi's placing the Zhongyong last in the Four Books (1190) as the metaphysical "summit" = most consequential sequence decision in Chinese pedagogical history, making cosmology the destination of ethical formation**: By placing the Zhongyong last (after Great Learning → Analects → Mengzi), Zhu Xi encoded a pedagogical conviction: ethical self-cultivation is the gateway to cosmological understanding, not the reverse. The sequence argues that you can only understand the Zhongyong's cosmic *cheng* after having worked through the eight-step program of the Daxue and the ethical-psychological detail of the Analects and Mengzi. This sequencing — ethics first, metaphysics last as the destination rather than the foundation — is Zhu Xi's most philosophically ambitious claim and determined how Neo-Confucianism understood the relationship between practice and theory for 700 years (Gardner, D.K. 2007. *The Four Books*. Hackett. pp.ix–xxv; de Bary, W.T. 1981. pp.40–100; Elman, B. 2000. *A Cultural History of Civil Examinations in Late Imperial China*. UC Press. pp.1–50).

- **Zhongyong Heaven → nature → Way → teaching chain = foundational statement of the naturalistic grounding of ethics in Chinese philosophy, generating all Neo-Confucian li-qi cosmological-ethical argumentation**: The Zhongyong's opening three sentences — "What Heaven has conferred is called human nature; following that nature is called the Way; cultivating that Way is called teaching" — are the foundational statement of the Confucian claim that ethics is cosmologically grounded: moral norms are not human conventions but the actualization of Heaven's endowment in human nature. Zhu Xi's entire *li*-*qi* metaphysics (principle as the moral-structural pattern embedded in Heaven's endowment of human nature) is an elaboration of these three sentences. This chain — Heaven's endowment → human nature (*xing*) → the Way → moral teaching — is the structural basis for all Neo-Confucian arguments that ethics has a metaphysical foundation, not merely a social one (Chan, W. 1963. pp.95–100; Gardner, D.K. 2007. pp.1–30; Ivanhoe, P.J. 2000. *Ethics in the Confucian Tradition*. Hackett. pp.1–40).
