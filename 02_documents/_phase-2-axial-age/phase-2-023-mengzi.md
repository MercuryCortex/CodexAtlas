# Mengzi (Mencius)

## What it is
The second-most-important Confucian text after the Analects, and the text that fixed Confucianism as a positive ethical-anthropological doctrine (human nature is good, and moral cultivation is the unfolding of innate moral seeds). Seven books of dialogues — Mengzi (~-372 to -289) advising the rulers of Liang, Qi, Teng, and Zou, debating with the Mohists (especially Yi Zhi) and the egoist-individualist Yang Zhu, instructing his disciples (Gongsun Chou, Wan Zhang, Gaozi), and most famously theorizing the *four sprouts* (*si duan* 四端) of innate moral capacity: compassion (the seed of *rén* / humaneness), shame (the seed of *yì* / righteousness), deference (the seed of *lǐ* / propriety), and discrimination of right-and-wrong (the seed of *zhì* / wisdom). Mengzi also developed the *mandate of Heaven* doctrine in a sharply populist direction: Heaven sees as the people see; if the people abandon a ruler, Heaven has abandoned him; and tyranicide (the killing of King Zhou of Shang by King Wu of Zhou) is not regicide because a tyrant is no longer a king but a "mere fellow."

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Mid-Warring States, the most violent and fragmented period of Chinese antiquity. Mengzi traveled state to state offering advice to rulers; he influenced none decisively (cf. Confucius's similar career a century earlier) but his published advisories became the moral conscience of the Confucian tradition. The competing schools of Mohism (universal love, utilitarian state) and the proto-individualist Yangism (life-preservation, refusal of state service) were Mengzi's principal philosophical opponents.
- **Religious milieu.** Continuing decline of Zhou ritual religion; rising yin-yang and Five Phases cosmological speculation. Mengzi treats Tian (Heaven) as a moral order that registers and validates human virtue, but does not engage in cosmological speculation; like Confucius, he is reticent about the afterlife and the spirit-world. His Heaven is decidedly moral.
- **Who was behind it.** Mengzi himself and his immediate disciples (Gongsun Chou, Wan Zhang, the unnamed editors). The dialogues are sufficiently consistent in style to suggest a single-authorship core, possibly with disciple-editing.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous transmission through state-Confucian canon, with Zhu Xi's promotion to one of the Four Books (~1190 CE) as the decisive elevation. The Mengzi was foundational to the imperial examination system, 1313 (Yuan) onward.

## Themes and motifs
- **Human nature is good (*xìng shàn* 性善)** — Mengzi's signature doctrine. Humans are born with innate moral capacities (the *four sprouts*); moral cultivation is their nurturing, like a farmer tending sprouts. The thought experiment of the child about to fall into a well (2A:6) is the locus classicus: anyone who sees the child will feel a stab of compassion *not* for instrumental reasons but as an immediate expression of the moral nature.
- **The four sprouts (*si duan* 四端)** — the seeds of the four cardinal Confucian virtues. Mengzi 2A:6.
- **Mandate of Heaven, populist reading** — "Heaven sees as my people see; Heaven hears as my people hear" (5A:5, quoting the Shujing *Tai Shi*). Royal legitimacy is conditional on popular flourishing.
- **The tyrant as "mere fellow"** — King Hui of Liang asks whether King Wu's killing of King Zhou of Shang was regicide; Mengzi: "I have heard of the execution of a mere fellow Zhou; I have not heard of the killing of a ruler" (1B:8). The most radical political-theological statement in pre-modern Chinese thought.
- **Cultivation as nurturing** — repeated agrarian metaphors: tending sprouts, not "pulling them up to help them grow" (2A:2's "ox-mountain" parable and the man of Song who pulls his sprouts).
- **Filial piety as moral foundation** — the parent-child relation as the school in which the moral nature is cultivated.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-2-015-analects-of-confucius]] — *direct-quote* / *commentary-on*: Mengzi quotes Confucius extensively and treats himself as Confucius's heir; the Mengzi systematizes what the Analects only fragmentarily contains.
- → [[phase-2-024-xunzi]] — *polemic-against* (within Confucianism): Xunzi's "human nature is evil" doctrine (*xìng è*) is the direct counter to Mengzi's "human nature is good"; the two are the two great branches of classical Confucianism.
- → [[phase-2-025-mozi]] — *polemic-against*: Mengzi attacks Mohism's "love without distinctions" (*jiān ài*) as destructive of the natural primacy of family ties (3A:5); Mengzi attacks Yang Zhu's egoism as destructive of social bonds. "Yang and Mo's words fill the empire," he complains (3B:9), making his defense of the Confucian middle path a self-conscious project.
- → **Western reception** — Mengzi's "innate moral sense" doctrine was central to 17th-18th c. European Jesuit accounts of China and influenced Enlightenment moral-sense theory (Hutcheson, Hume, Smith — see Jullien 1995 *Founding of Morality* and Israel 2001 *Radical Enlightenment*).

## Cross-tradition substrate
- **Innate moral sense.** Mengzi's doctrine of the *four sprouts* is structurally parallel to the Stoic *oikeiosis* (the innate impulse toward self- and other-care; Cicero *De Finibus* 3; Long & Sedley 57), to the Christian natural-law tradition (Aquinas's *synderesis*, the innate spark of conscience), and to the Western moral-sense theorists (Shaftesbury, Hutcheson, Hume, Adam Smith). All four traditions claim that ethical capacity is built into the human constitution and unfolds with cultivation. *Parallel-motif* substrate; the Western moral-sense theorists were demonstrably influenced by Jesuit reports of Mengzi (Jullien 1995). Cross-tradition wire goes through *both* parallel-motif (Stoic / Christian) and historical-transmission (Mengzi → Jesuits → European Enlightenment).
- **Tyrant deposition.** Mengzi's argument that a tyrant is no longer a king parallels (and pre-dates) Aquinas's defense of conditional resistance to tyrants (*Commentary on the Sentences* II.44; *De Regimine Principum*) and ultimately the early-modern just-resistance and social-contract traditions (Vindiciae contra tyrannos, 1579; Locke). Substrate; no claim of direct transmission, but a structural parallel of foundational importance for the global political-theological tradition of conditional sovereignty. The Confucian-Mencian doctrine functioned in East Asia as the just-resistance tradition functioned in early-modern Europe.

## MASSIVE WIN: Human Nature as Innately Good — The Chinese Rousseau/Hobbes Debate, 2,000 Years Early

Mengzi's most famous doctrine — *xìng shàn* 性善, "human nature is good" — is not a vague optimism but a technical claim about the moral constitution of every human being. The argument turns on the *four sprouts* (*sì duān* 四端), innate germs of virtue present in all humans from birth:

1. **Compassion** (*cè yǐn zhī xīn* 惻隱之心) — the seed of *rén* (humaneness/benevolence)
2. **Shame** (*xiū wù zhī xīn* 羞惡之心) — the seed of *yì* (righteousness)
3. **Deference** (*cí ràng zhī xīn* 辭讓之心) — the seed of *lǐ* (ritual propriety)
4. **Moral discrimination** (*shì fēi zhī xīn* 是非之心) — the seed of *zhì* (wisdom)

The locus classicus is the famous "child at the well" thought experiment (Mengzi 2A:6): "Suppose a person suddenly sees a child about to fall into a well. Anyone in such a situation will have a feeling of alarm and compassion — not because they want to make friends with the child's parents, not because they want fame among their neighbors and friends, not because they hate the sound of the child's cries." The compassion is immediate, pre-reflective, and uncaused by self-interest. This is Mengzi's empirical evidence for the innate moral nature.

Mengzi's great opponent within Confucianism was [[xunzi-person]] (~310–235 BCE), who argued that human nature is *evil* (*xìng è* 性惡): the natural human impulses are toward self-interested desire, and it is ritual (*lǐ*) and education that reshape us toward goodness. This Mengzi/Xunzi debate is the Chinese analogue of the Rousseau/Hobbes debate in Western political philosophy — and it precedes Hobbes by nearly 2,000 years:

| Thinker | Human nature | Mechanism of goodness | Date |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mengzi | Innately good (sprouts of virtue need cultivation) | Cultivation of natural tendency | ~372–289 BCE |
| Xunzi | Innately bad (desires override reason) | Ritual and education reshape character | ~310–235 BCE |
| Pelagius | Naturally capable of good (free will intact after Eden) | Choice; no inherited sin required | ~360–420 CE |
| Augustine | Radically corrupted by original sin; will is bound | Divine grace alone can liberate | ~354–430 CE |
| Rousseau | Noble savage naturally good; *amour de soi* is wholesome | Corrupted by civilization and *amour propre* | 1762 CE |
| Hobbes | State of nature = war of all against all | Sovereign authority alone creates peace | 1651 CE |

The structural mapping is exact: Mengzi:Xunzi :: Rousseau:Hobbes :: Pelagius:Augustine. All three pairs argue about the same question — whether goodness is achieved by *developing* what is already present or by *overcoming* what is naturally given. The Christian version added the theological dimension of grace vs. free will (the Pelagian controversy, 411–418 CE); the Western political version added social-contract theory. But the core anthropological question is identical, and the Chinese debate was first.

The Jesuit missionaries who read Mengzi in the 16th–17th century brought his doctrine of innate moral sense to Europe, feeding directly into the moral-sense tradition: Shaftesbury's "moral sense" (*Characteristics*, 1711), Hutcheson's "benevolence" (*Inquiry*, 1725), and Adam Smith's "sympathy" (*Theory of Moral Sentiments*, 1759) are all structurally indebted to the Mengzian report. Jonathan Israel has documented the Jesuit-to-Enlightenment transmission channel (*Radical Enlightenment*, 2001). This is one of the rare cases where the parallel-motif becomes a historical-transmission story. → [[phase-2-024-xunzi]], [[phase-2-015-analects-of-confucius]], [[tradition-confucianism]].

## MASSIVE WIN: The Mandate of Heaven and the Right of Revolt — Earliest Tyrannicide Doctrine

Mengzi's extension of the "Mandate of Heaven" (*Tiānmìng* 天命) doctrine is the most politically explosive passage in classical Confucian thought — and one of the earliest documented justifications of tyrannicide in world political thought. The exchange occurs in Mengzi 1B:8:

> King Xuan of Qi asked: "Is it true that Tang banished Jie and that King Wu struck down Zhou [the last king of Shang]?"
> Mengzi replied: "It is so recorded."
> "Is it permissible for a minister to kill his lord?"
> Mengzi replied: "He who mutilates benevolence (*rén*) is called a 'mutilator.' He who mutilates righteousness (*yì*) is called a 'destroyer.' A mutilator-and-destroyer is called a 'mere fellow' (*yī fū* 一夫). I have heard of the execution of the mere fellow Zhou; I have not heard of the assassination of a ruler." (Van Norden trans., modified)

The argument strips a tyrant of his royal status *before* the act of deposition — the tyrant is not killed as a ruler but as a "mere fellow," because he has already forfeited royal status by violating the moral conditions of kingship. This is the logical structure that Aquinas will reinvent in the 13th century CE (*Summa Theologiae* IIa-IIae q.42; *De Regimine Principum*), and that the Calvinist resistance theorists (*Vindiciae contra tyrannos*, 1579) and John Locke (*Two Treatises*, 1689) will develop into the modern right of revolution.

The cross-tradition tyrannicide / conditional sovereignty sequence:

| Thinker / Text | Claim | Date |
|---|---|---|
| Mengzi | Tyrant forfeits royal status; "mere fellow" may be executed | ~320–280 BCE |
| Cicero | Tyrant is a beast, outside civic law; may be killed | ~44 BCE (*De Officiis* III.32) |
| John of Salisbury | Tyrannicide is not just permissible but sometimes obligatory | 1159 CE (*Policraticus*) |
| Aquinas | Ruler who governs for self alone is a tyrant; resistance permitted | ~1265 CE |
| Vindiciae contra tyrannos | Magistrates may resist the king on behalf of the people | 1579 CE |
| John Locke | Tyranny dissolves the social compact; right of revolution | 1689 CE |

Mengzi is not merely parallel but prior by more than six centuries to the earliest Western tyrannicide argument. The East Asian doctrine also had political teeth: it was cited to justify dynastic overthrow in Chinese, Korean, and Japanese history throughout the imperial period. → [[tian-heaven]], [[divine-kingship]], [[tradition-confucianism]].

## MASSIVE WIN: The Four Beginnings as Moral Epistemology — Intuitionism Before Its Name

Mengzi's doctrine of the *four sprouts* is not only a claim about human nature — it is a claim about how we *know* moral truths. The sprouts are known through direct, immediate moral perception: when you see the child at the well, you do not *infer* that you should feel compassion; you simply feel it. The moral knowledge is irreducible, non-inferential, and universal.

This makes Mengzi the earliest systematic defender of what Western ethics calls *moral intuitionism* — the doctrine that at least some moral truths are known directly, without derivation from further premises. The Western intuitionist tradition runs from: Francis Hutcheson's "moral sense" (1725) → Richard Price's *Review of the Principal Questions in Morals* (1758) → G.E. Moore's *Principia Ethica* (1903, "the naturalistic fallacy") → W.D. Ross's *prima facie* duties (*The Right and the Good*, 1930) → Derek Parfit's recent defense of non-naturalist moral realism.

All of these thinkers assert, against the utilitarian and contractarian alternatives, that at least some moral facts are *self-evident to moral perception* rather than derived from welfare calculations or social agreements. Mengzi made this argument first, most clearly, and with the "child at the well" thought experiment that remains pedagogically unmatched. The thought experiment is structurally identical to Ross's method of moral reflection: produce a case where the utilitarian calculation would say "do nothing" but the immediate moral perception says "act," and then note that the perception, not the calculation, tracks the moral truth.

## Disputes
- **Authorship of individual books.** Generally regarded as more unified than the Analects; probably composed by Mengzi and his immediate circle. Internal stylistic variation has prompted some scholars to propose layered composition, but the case is weaker than for the Analects.
- **Date of Mengzi himself.** Conventional ~-372 to -289 (a near-contemporary of Aristotle). The Shiji biography is brief and the precise dates are not perfectly secure.
- **"Human nature is good" — what does it mean?** Various interpretations: (a) all humans have innate moral sprouts that need nurturing (Van Norden, mainstream); (b) human nature *tends* toward good in proper environments; (c) the moral capacities are universally present but easily corrupted. The contemporary "Mengzi-Xunzi debate" replays a 2,500-year-old philosophical question.
