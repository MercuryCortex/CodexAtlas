# Mengzi (Mencius)

## What it is
The second-most-important Confucian text after the Analects, and the text that fixed Confucianism as a positive ethical-anthropological doctrine (human nature is good, and moral cultivation is the unfolding of innate moral seeds). Seven books of dialogues — Mengzi (~-372 to -289) advising the rulers of Liang, Qi, Teng, and Zou, debating with the Mohists (especially Yi Zhi) and the egoist-individualist Yang Zhu, instructing his disciples (Gongsun Chou, Wan Zhang, Gaozi), and most famously theorizing the *four sprouts* (*si duan* 四端) of innate moral capacity: compassion (the seed of *rén* / humaneness), shame (the seed of *yì* / righteousness), deference (the seed of *lǐ* / propriety), and discrimination of right-and-wrong (the seed of *zhì* / wisdom). Mengzi also developed the *mandate of Heaven* doctrine in a sharply populist direction: Heaven sees as the people see; if the people abandon a ruler, Heaven has abandoned him; and tyranicide (the killing of King Zhou of Shang by King Wu of Zhou) is not regicide because a tyrant is no longer a king but a "mere fellow."

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Mid-Warring States, the most violent and fragmented period of Chinese antiquity. Mengzi traveled state to state offering advice to rulers; he influenced none decisively (cf. Confucius's similar career a century earlier) but his published advisories became the moral conscience of the Confucian tradition. The competing schools of Mohism (universal love, utilitarian state) and the proto-individualist Yangism (life-preservation, refusal of state service) were Mengzi's principal philosophical opponents.
- **Religious milieu.** Continuing decline of Zhou ritual religion; rising yin-yang and Five Phases cosmological speculation. Mengzi treats Tian (Heaven) as a moral order that registers and validates human virtue, but does not engage in cosmological speculation; like Confucius, he is reticent about the afterlife and the spirit-world. His Heaven is decidedly moral.
- **Who was behind it.** Mengzi himself and his immediate disciples (Gongsun Chou, Wan Zhang, the unnamed editors). The dialogues are sufficiently consistent in style to suggest a single-authorship core, possibly with disciple-editing.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous transmission through state-Confucian canon, with Zhu Xi's promotion to one of the Four Books (~1190 CE) as the decisive elevation. The Mengzi was foundational to the imperial examination system, 1313 (Yuan) onward.

## Themes and motifs
- **Human nature is good (*xìng shàn* 性善)** — Mengzi's signature doctrine. Humans are born with innate moral capacities (the *four sprouts*); moral cultivation is their nurturing, like a farmer tending sprouts. The thought experiment of the child about to fall into a well (2A:6) is the locus classicus: anyone who sees the child will feel a stab of compassion *not* for instrumental reasons but as an immediate expression of the moral nature.
- **The four sprouts (*si duan* 四端)** — the seeds of the four cardinal Confucian virtues. Mengzi 2A:6.
- **Mandate of Heaven, populist reading** — "Heaven sees as my people see; Heaven hears as my people hear" (5A:5, quoting the Shujing *Tai Shi*). Royal legitimacy is conditional on popular flourishing.
- **The tyrant as "mere fellow"** — King Hui of Liang asks whether King Wu's killing of King Zhou of Shang was regicide; Mengzi: "I have heard of the execution of a mere fellow Zhou; I have not heard of the killing of a ruler" (1B:8). The most radical political-theological statement in pre-modern Chinese thought.
- **Cultivation as nurturing** — repeated agrarian metaphors: tending sprouts, not "pulling them up to help them grow" (2A:2's "ox-mountain" parable and the man of Song who pulls his sprouts).
- **Filial piety as moral foundation** — the parent-child relation as the school in which the moral nature is cultivated.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-2-015-analects-of-confucius]] — *direct-quote* / *commentary-on*: Mengzi quotes Confucius extensively and treats himself as Confucius's heir; the Mengzi systematizes what the Analects only fragmentarily contains.
- → [[phase-2-024-xunzi]] — *polemic-against* (within Confucianism): Xunzi's "human nature is evil" doctrine (*xìng è*) is the direct counter to Mengzi's "human nature is good"; the two are the two great branches of classical Confucianism.
- → [[phase-2-025-mozi]] — *polemic-against*: Mengzi attacks Mohism's "love without distinctions" (*jiān ài*) as destructive of the natural primacy of family ties (3A:5); Mengzi attacks Yang Zhu's egoism as destructive of social bonds. "Yang and Mo's words fill the empire," he complains (3B:9), making his defense of the Confucian middle path a self-conscious project.
- → **Western reception** — Mengzi's "innate moral sense" doctrine was central to 17th-18th c. European Jesuit accounts of China and influenced Enlightenment moral-sense theory (Hutcheson, Hume, Smith — see Jullien 1995 *Founding of Morality* and Israel 2001 *Radical Enlightenment*).

## Cross-tradition substrate
- **Innate moral sense.** Mengzi's doctrine of the *four sprouts* is structurally parallel to the Stoic *oikeiosis* (the innate impulse toward self- and other-care; Cicero *De Finibus* 3; Long & Sedley 57), to the Christian natural-law tradition (Aquinas's *synderesis*, the innate spark of conscience), and to the Western moral-sense theorists (Shaftesbury, Hutcheson, Hume, Adam Smith). All four traditions claim that ethical capacity is built into the human constitution and unfolds with cultivation. *Parallel-motif* substrate; the Western moral-sense theorists were demonstrably influenced by Jesuit reports of Mengzi (Jullien 1995). Cross-tradition wire goes through *both* parallel-motif (Stoic / Christian) and historical-transmission (Mengzi → Jesuits → European Enlightenment).
- **Tyrant deposition.** Mengzi's argument that a tyrant is no longer a king parallels (and pre-dates) Aquinas's defense of conditional resistance to tyrants (*Commentary on the Sentences* II.44; *De Regimine Principum*) and ultimately the early-modern just-resistance and social-contract traditions (Vindiciae contra tyrannos, 1579; Locke). Substrate; no claim of direct transmission, but a structural parallel of foundational importance for the global political-theological tradition of conditional sovereignty. The Confucian-Mencian doctrine functioned in East Asia as the just-resistance tradition functioned in early-modern Europe.

## Disputes
- **Authorship of individual books.** Generally regarded as more unified than the Analects; probably composed by Mengzi and his immediate circle. Internal stylistic variation has prompted some scholars to propose layered composition, but the case is weaker than for the Analects.
- **Date of Mengzi himself.** Conventional ~-372 to -289 (a near-contemporary of Aristotle). The Shiji biography is brief and the precise dates are not perfectly secure.
- **"Human nature is good" — what does it mean?** Various interpretations: (a) all humans have innate moral sprouts that need nurturing (Van Norden, mainstream); (b) human nature *tends* toward good in proper environments; (c) the moral capacities are universally present but easily corrupted. The contemporary "Mengzi-Xunzi debate" replays a 2,500-year-old philosophical question.
