# Book of Proverbs

## What it is
A collection of Hebrew wisdom poetry and moral instruction, attributed to Solomon by tradition but composed over several centuries (~950–400 BCE) and likely compiled in post-exilic Jerusalem. The canonical 31-chapter book contains multiple distinct collections: the "Proverbs of Solomon" (chs. 10–22, 25–29), the "Words of the Wise" (22:17–24:22), a section attributed to "Agur son of Jakeh" (ch. 30), and the enigmatic "Capable Wife" acrostic poem (ch. 31). The most theologically consequential section is the prologue (chs. 1–9), which personifies [[hokmah-hebrew]] as a divine feminine figure who was present at creation.

## Context
Proverbs belongs to the broader Ancient Near Eastern wisdom tradition — close parallels exist with the Egyptian *Instruction of Amenemope* (which may have been a direct source for Proverbs 22:17–23:11), the Mesopotamian *Counsels of Wisdom*, and the Aramaic *Words of Ahikar*. The uniquely Israelite contribution is the personification of Wisdom as a female figure — Lady Wisdom — who calls from the streets, builds her house, and was God's delight before creation.

## Proverbs 8 — the locus classicus of Hokmah
The pivotal theological passage: Wisdom speaks in the first person, claiming:
- Pre-existence before creation: "The LORD possessed me at the beginning of his work, the first of his acts of old" (8:22)
- Presence at creation: "When he established the heavens, I was there... when he marked out the foundations of the earth, then I was beside him, like a master workman" (8:27–30)
- Delight in humanity: "rejoicing before him always, rejoicing in his inhabited world and delighting in the children of man" (8:30–31)

This passage is the direct source for both the [[logos-johannine]] prologue ("In the beginning was the Word... through him all things were made") and Philo of Alexandria's [[logos-philonic]] theology. Both later developments are Wisdom-Christology in new philosophical dress.

## Themes and motifs
- The two paths: Wisdom vs. Folly, life vs. death — dualistic moral cosmology
- The "fear of the LORD" as the beginning of wisdom — theodicy and virtue
- [[hokmah-hebrew]] personified as a pre-existent feminine divine principle
