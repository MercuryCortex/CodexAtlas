# Song of Songs (Shir ha-Shirim)

## What it is

The Song of Songs (*Shir ha-Shirim* — "The Song of Songs," a Hebrew superlative meaning "The Greatest of Songs") is a collection of erotic love poems between two speakers — a woman and a man — with occasional interjections from a chorus of "daughters of Jerusalem." It contains no explicit theological content, no mention of God (in the Hebrew), no narrative of historical events, and no didactic wisdom in the conventional biblical sense. It is simply love poetry: sensuous, direct, and rich in agricultural imagery from the landscape of ancient Israel.

The text's presence in the Hebrew canon was debated as late as the 1st century CE — Rabbi Akiva famously declared it "the holiest of holies" and pronounced that "all the ages are not worth the day on which the Song of Songs was given to Israel" (*Mishnah Yadayim* 3:5). The canonical debate itself documents the text's strangeness: the rabbis who admitted it did so by insisting on allegorical reading (God as lover, Israel as beloved). Without that allegorization, the text is erotic poetry.

## Structure and content

The poem has no sustained narrative plot. It consists of alternating speeches in which a woman and man describe each other's physical beauty using extended metaphorical series (*wasf* — the Arabic term for the genre), express desire, describe the beloved's absence and the pain of searching, and celebrate reunion. The woman's voice is remarkably assertive: she speaks more than the man, initiates the search, and describes her own beauty with confidence ("I am dark and beautiful, O daughters of Jerusalem, like the tents of Kedar, like the curtains of Solomon," 1:5). The imagery is agricultural (vineyards, orchards, lilies, ointments), urban (night streets of Jerusalem, watchtowers), and explicitly physical (breasts like clusters of grapes, neck like a tower of David, hair like a flock of goats descending from Gilead).

## Ancient Near Eastern parallels

Marvin Pope (1977) catalogued the extensive parallels with **Egyptian love poetry** from the Chester Beatty papyrus (13th c. BCE) and the Turin Erotic Papyrus — the same poetic conventions, the same *wasf* genre, the same agricultural and sensory imagery. Mesopotamian **sacred marriage** (*hieros gamos*) hymns, particularly those celebrating the union of Inanna/Ishtar with her lover Dumuzi/Tammuz, provide an older structural parallel: the divine-human erotic union celebrated in cult. Whether the Song of Songs preserves echoes of a Canaanite sacred-marriage festival or is simply secular love poetry that borrowed literary conventions from an older sacred genre is debated. The text's eventual canonical inclusion under Solomon's name and the subsequent allegorical tradition may have been the process by which originally profane love poetry was "rescued" for sacred use — or, alternatively, the allegorical reading was the original intention, with the erotic register chosen deliberately as the most powerful available metaphor for divine-human intimacy.

## The Persian loanword: pardes

Song of Songs 4:13 contains one of only three Hebrew Bible occurrences of the Persian loanword *pardes* (alongside Ecclesiastes 2:5 and Nehemiah 2:8): "Your plants are an orchard (*pardes*) of pomegranates with the choicest fruits." The term comes from Old Persian *pairi-daēza* (walled enclosure, the royal garden), entering Hebrew in the post-exilic Persian period. The three *pardes* occurrences are all in late texts — post-exilic compositions showing Persian-period linguistic contact. The word's subsequent career is remarkable: from Persian royal garden (*paridaiza*) → Hebrew *pardēs* → Greek *paradeisos* (Xenophon, Septuagint) → Latin *paradisus* → "paradise" in all Western languages and "firdaws" (*firdaws* from *pairidaēza*) in Arabic. The vault's [[theme-paradise-etymology]] documents this chain. The Song of Songs' *pardes* is a tiny linguistic fossil embedded in an erotic poem that would eventually, through the route of allegorical interpretation, become the most extensively commented text on the soul's union with God in Western mysticism — a union also called "paradise." See also [[persian-period-injection]] for the broader context of Persian-period loanwords in Second Temple literature.

## The allegorization trajectory: MASSIVE WIN

The Song of Songs generated the most sustained allegorical commentary tradition of any text in the Hebrew Bible or Christian Old Testament. Rabbi Akiva's insistence on its holiness established the rabbinic trajectory: God loves Israel as the man loves the woman; the exile is the absence of the beloved; the eschatological reunion is the Song's erotic culmination. The Targum of the Song of Songs (Aramaic, ~700 CE) elaborates this into a full salvation-history allegory.

**Christian allegorization** went further: Origen of Alexandria (184–253 CE), in his *Commentary on the Song of Songs* and *Homilies on the Song of Songs*, established the reading that became normative for Christian mysticism: the Bridegroom is Christ (or the Logos), the Bride is the soul (or the Church). Origen's interpretation was transmitted to Bernard of Clairvaux ([[bernard-of-clairvaux]]), whose *Sermons on the Song of Songs* (86 sermons, 1135–1153 CE) constitute the most extensive medieval reading of the text. Bernard never got past chapter 3 in 86 sermons. The Song became the primary scriptural warrant for **bridal mysticism** — the tradition that the soul's highest union with God is best described in the language of erotic love. This tradition runs through Teresa of Ávila, John of the Cross, Meister Eckhart, and the entire Christian mystical tradition.

**The Sufi parallel** is the vault's MASSIVE WIN: [[rumi]], [[hafez]], [[farid-ud-din-attar]], and the Sufi tradition broadly interpret their own love poetry in precisely the same allegorical register — the human beloved is the divine Beloved, erotic longing is the soul's longing for God, union (*wahdat al-wujūd*) is the mystical culmination. Rumi writes openly that his *Masnavi* is the Quran in Persian dress; his love poetry is simultaneously human and divine. The structural parallel to the Song of Songs allegorization is not incidental: both traditions — Jewish-Christian bridal mysticism and Sufi love-mysticism — use erotic poetry as the supreme available metaphor for the soul's relationship to the divine. Whether this is convergent insight or a shared Semitic cultural inheritance (both traditions ultimately rooted in the ancient Near Eastern sacred-marriage literature) remains an open question. The vault's [[bridal-mysticism]] theme documents this cross-tradition convergence.

**Three Abrahamic traditions, one poetic grammar**: The Song of Songs stands at the origin of a grammatical convention — erotic love as the metaphor for divine-human intimacy — that is subsequently adopted, independently elaborated, and maintained across rabbinic Judaism (Israel as God's bride), Christian mysticism (the soul as Christ's bride), and Sufi Islam (the lover and the divine Beloved). This is one of the longest and most multi-branched transmission chains in the vault.
