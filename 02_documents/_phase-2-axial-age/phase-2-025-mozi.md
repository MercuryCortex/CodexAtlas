# Mozi

## What it is
The foundational text of Mohism, China's first articulated rival to Confucianism and (briefly, in the late Warring States) Confucianism's principal competitor for cultural authority. The Mozi articulates a tightly knit doctrine — universal love (*jiān ài* 兼愛), opposition to offensive war (*fēi gōng* 非攻), promotion of the worthy (*shàng xián* 尚賢) regardless of birth, moderation in use (*jié yòng* 節用) and burial (*jié zàng* 節葬), condemnation of musical entertainment as wasteful (*fēi yuè* 非樂), the active will of Heaven (*tiān zhì* 天志) and ghosts (*míng guǐ* 明鬼) as moral enforcers, and condemnation of Confucian-style fatalism (*fēi mìng* 非命). Mohism was also organized — a quasi-military fraternity of disciplined adherents, under a Grand Master (*jù zǐ* 鉅子), who served as defenders of cities under siege (Mohist defensive military expertise occupies a substantial portion of the text). The Later Mohist branches (~-300 BCE) developed the famous "Mohist Canons," six chapters of dense logical, epistemological, and proto-scientific writing comparable in technical density to Aristotle's *Organon*.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Mozi (~-470 to -390) was born roughly when Confucius died and worked during the early Warring States. His career response to constant inter-state warfare was both intellectual (the anti-aggression doctrine) and practical (the Mohist defensive-warfare guild). His followers — disciplined, frugal, ascetic, in distinctive black garb — formed organized communities that traveled to defend cities under attack. The movement was politically formidable for two centuries; it collapsed during the late-third-century Qin unification and the early Han, perhaps because its organizational form (an autonomous trans-state fraternity) was incompatible with the centralizing imperial state.
- **Religious milieu.** Mohism is more theistic than Confucianism. Tian (Heaven) is treated as an active moral agent who wills universal love and punishes the unrighteous; ghosts and spirits exist and reward the good. Mohism re-activates the older theistic Zhou cult against the more naturalistic / agnostic drift of the Confucians (and later Xunzi, who specifically responds to Mohism). This active-Tian theology gives Mohism its closest cross-cultural parallel: Hebrew-Bible-style ethical theism.
- **Who was behind it.** Mozi (Mo Di), historically attested but with thin biographical detail. His disciples and the school over the next two centuries. Three "Later Mohist" branches diverged after Mozi's death; the surviving triads of essays (with their *shang/zhong/xia* upper-middle-lower versions) reflect this. The Mohist Canons are anonymous Later Mohist work, ~-300 BCE.
- **Discovery / transmission.** The Mozi was preserved in the Han imperial library but was not actively read after Mohism's collapse; it survived as a quiet curio in the Daoist canon (where it was anomalously included). Major modern recovery: Sun Yirang's 1894 *Mozi Jiangu* edition, Hu Shih's 1919 doctoral dissertation rehabilitating Mohism as proto-utilitarian and proto-scientific, and the 20th-c. translations (Mei, Watson, Johnston) brought Mozi back into the philosophical conversation.

## Themes and motifs
- **Universal love (*jiān ài* 兼愛)** — equal concern for all persons, not graded by family proximity. The signature Mohist doctrine and the direct target of Mengzi's polemic (Mengzi: "Mozi's universal love is to be without a father").
- **Anti-aggression (*fēi gōng* 非攻)** — wars of conquest are morally wrong; defensive war is permissible. The Mohist guild organized to make this principle operational.
- **Promotion of the worthy (*shàng xián* 尚賢)** — government office on merit, not birth; one of the most radical anti-aristocratic doctrines in pre-modern thought.
- **Active Heaven (*tiān zhì* 天志)** — Tian has a will, rewards the good, punishes the wicked. Closest classical-Chinese approach to ethical monotheism.
- **Moderation in use, anti-music, anti-elaborate-burial** — Mohism opposes elite consumption, ritual extravagance, and musical performances as wasteful diversion of resources from productive use; Confucians regarded this as philistine and as undermining the cultural foundation of civilization.
- [[ethical-monotheism]] (parallel only) — Mohist Tian is closer to the active moral deity of the Hebrew prophets than any other classical Chinese conception.
- **Anti-fatalism (*fēi mìng* 非命)** — Mohists oppose Confucian (and especially proto-Daoist) ideas of cosmic fate as an excuse for moral inaction.
- **The Mohist Canons** — pre-modern China's most rigorous attempt at systematic logic, epistemology, and proto-science (optics, mechanics, geometry). Graham 1978.

- → [[phase-2-015-analects-of-confucius]] / [[phase-2-023-mengzi]] — *polemic-against*: Mohism's principal philosophical target is Confucianism. Confucians and Mohists waged the first sustained philosophical debate in Chinese history; Mengzi's "Mozi and Yang Zhu's words fill the empire" (3B:9) measures Mohism's success in the late Warring States.
- → [[phase-2-024-xunzi]] — *polemic-against* / *direct-influence*: Xunzi attacks Mohism for its naïve theism (Xunzi's "Discourse on Heaven" partly responds to Mohist active-Tian theology), but absorbs Mohist epistemological rigor.
- → [[phase-2-026-han-feizi]] — *parallel-motif*: Legalism's consequentialism shares structural features with Mohist consequentialism, though Legalism abandoned the universalist-ethical component.
- → **Modern Chinese reception** — Hu Shih (1919), Mao Zedong's selective rehabilitation of Mozi as anti-aristocratic, contemporary Chinese philosophical interest in Mohism as a "Chinese utilitarianism." Mozi has become, in 20th-c. Chinese self-understanding, the lost alternative tradition.

## Cross-tradition substrate
- **Utilitarianism / consequentialism.** Mohism is sometimes called the world's first consequentialism (Fraser 2016, *The Philosophy of the Mòzǐ: The First Consequentialists*). The Mohist criterion for evaluating doctrines is whether their adoption produces *benefit* (*lì* 利) for the empire and the people. This is a clearly different ethical framework from Confucian virtue ethics, and a structural parallel to (and arguably an anticipation of) Bentham-Mill utilitarianism. *Parallel-motif* substrate; no transmissional link. (Though the modern Chinese discovery of Mohism was partly driven by Hu Shih's recognition of the parallel.)
- **Universal love.** Mozi's *jiān ài* parallels the Christian doctrine of *agapē* (universal love including the enemy), the Stoic *philanthrōpia* (love of humanity as such), and the Buddhist *karuṇā* (universal compassion, ungraded). Four traditions independently develop versions of ungraded universal benevolence. The Mohist version is the *most* radical in directly opposing the natural family-graded concern that Confucianism, Christianity, and most lived traditions actually practice. Substrate; no claim of transmission.
- **Ethical theism with active moral deity.** Mohist Tian as moral enforcer parallels the Hebrew prophetic YHWH and Zoroastrian Ahura Mazda — three independent traditions developing the doctrine that the supreme cosmic principle is *morally* engaged with human affairs and rewards/punishes accordingly. Substrate; no transmissional claim. The cross-tradition theme of [[ethical-monotheism]] connects.
- **The disciplined ethical community.** The Mohist *jù zǐ* fraternity — disciplined, ascetic, distinctive dress, organized for ethical action — parallels (in structure, not content) the early Christian community, the Jain *saṅgha*, the Buddhist *saṅgha*, the early Pythagorean brotherhood, and the early Sufi *ṭarīqāt*. Substrate: the genre of the *ethical brotherhood* as a global phenomenon, with characteristic features (distinctive practice, discipline, communal property, anti-elite stance).

## Disputes
- **Date and historicity of Mozi.** Conventional ~-470 to -390. Sima Qian's biography (*Shiji* 74) is brief and uncertain (Sima Qian was uncertain whether Mozi was a contemporary of Confucius or much later); modern scholars accept early Warring States but the precise dates are not perfectly secure.
- **Authorship of the Canons.** Anonymous Later Mohist work, probably late-third-century BCE; Graham 1978's reconstruction is the indispensable basis.
- **Was Mohism "religion" or "philosophy"?** The active-Tian theology, the spirit-belief, and the organized fraternity-form push toward "religion"; the systematic-argumentative form pushes toward "philosophy." The same debate that has surrounded Confucianism applies in sharper form to Mohism.
- **Cause of Mohism's collapse.** Multiple theses: Qin imperial centralization made the trans-state fraternity politically impossible; Mohism's ascetic frugality lost out to the more accommodating Confucianism for state patronage; the organizational *jù zǐ* lineage broke. No definitive answer.
