# Analects of Confucius

## What it is
The *Lúnyǔ* ("Selected Sayings") is a compilation of dialogues, aphorisms, anecdotes, and short narratives attributed to Confucius (Kǒngzǐ, ~551–479 BCE) and his immediate disciples. Twenty books contain ~500 short units, most under fifty characters. The text is the principal source for Confucius's teaching and the founding scripture of Confucianism. Its central concepts: *rén* (仁, humaneness / benevolence), *lǐ* (禮, ritual propriety), *jūnzǐ* (君子, "exemplary person" / "gentleman"), *xiào* (孝, filial piety), the *Way* (*dào*, the proper course of human life and government), and the ideal of moral cultivation through study, ritual practice, and self-examination.

## Primary text — selected passages

### Analects 1.1–1.2 — The opening: learning, friendship, the unrecognized man
*Trans. Slingerland, E. (2003). Confucius: Analects. Hackett.*
> The Master said: "Is it not pleasant to learn and then to practice what you have learned?
> Is it not delightful to have friends coming from afar?
> Is it not the mark of a gentleman that he feels no annoyance
> though men may take no note of him?"

> You Ruo said: "Among those who are filial toward their parents and obedient to their elder brothers,
> those who are inclined to offend against their superiors are few indeed.
> Those who are not inclined to offend against their superiors, and yet are inclined to create disorder:
> there have been none such.
> The exemplary person devotes his efforts to the roots, for the root being established, the Way will grow.
> Filial piety and fraternal submission — are they not the root of all *ren*?"

*The text's opening word is 學 (*xué*, "to learn"). The opening sentence's structure — three rhetorical questions each answered "Is it not...?" — is a model of Confucian rhetorical understatement.*

### Analects 4.15 — The single thread (*yī guàn*)
*Trans. Slingerland 2003*
> The Master said: "Shen! My Way has a single thread running through it."
> Master Zeng replied: "Indeed."
> [After the Master left, the disciples asked:] "What did he mean?"
> Master Zeng said: "The way of the Master is nothing other than *zhong* [doing one's best]
> and *shu* [consideration for others]."

*The "single thread" passage is the Analects' closest equivalent to a doctrinal core. Zhong (loyalty / doing one's best) and shu (reciprocity / consideration) together constitute what the text elsewhere calls ren.*

### Analects 12.1 — *Ren* defined: overcoming self
*Trans. Slingerland 2003*
> Yan Hui asked about *ren* [humaneness].
> The Master said: "To overcome yourself and return to ritual propriety (*lǐ*) is *ren*.
> If for a single day you could overcome yourself and return to ritual propriety,
> the whole world would move toward *ren*.
> The practice of *ren* comes from oneself — how could it come from others?"

### Analects 15.23 — The Golden Rule (*shu*, reciprocity)
*Trans. Slingerland 2003*
> Zigong asked: "Is there one word that can serve as a guide for one's entire life?"
> The Master replied: "*Shu* — do not impose on others what you yourself do not want."

*The earliest preserved written phrasing of the reciprocity principle in the negative form. Compare Hillel's negative form (~-30 BCE: "What is hateful to you, do not do to your neighbor") and Jesus's positive form (Matt 7:12: "Do to others what you would have them do to you").*

### Analects 11.11 — Silence about death and spirits
*Trans. Slingerland 2003*
> [The disciple] asked about serving the ghosts and spirits.
> The Master replied: "You are not yet able to serve people — how could you be able to serve ghosts?"
> "May I ask about death?"
> "You do not yet understand life — how could you understand death?"

*Citation: Slingerland, E. (2003). Confucius: Analects with Selections from Traditional Commentaries. Hackett.*

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Late Spring-and-Autumn period (Confucius's lifetime ~551–479 BCE) in the small state of Lu and other east-Zhou principalities. The Zhou royal house was a ceremonial shell; competing aristocratic clans dominated weakened ducal states. Confucius taught and traveled seeking a ruler willing to put proper governance into practice; he never found one. The compilation of the Analects spans several generations of disciples and second-generation followers, extending into the Warring States.
- **Religious milieu.** Old Zhou cult of *tiān* (Heaven) and the ancestors remained in place; popular religion of nature spirits and divination coexisted. Confucius is largely reticent about cosmological and afterlife questions ("Not yet able to serve the living, how can one serve the dead?", 11.12) but accepts Heaven (*tian*) as a moral order and refers to a divine mandate (*tian ming*).
- **Who was behind it.** Confucius's immediate disciples (Zigong, Zilu, Yan Hui, Zengzi) and second-generation followers (especially the followers of Zengzi and of Confucius's grandson Zisi) compiled and accreted the material. The Brookses (1998) propose a chapter-by-chapter chronological reconstruction; Slingerland is skeptical of the precision but accepts layered composition.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous Chinese transmission. Standardized as one of the Four Books by Zhu Xi (~1190 CE); thereafter foundational to the imperial examination system. The Dingzhou bamboo-slip Analects (excavated 1973 from a Han prince's tomb, sealed -55 BCE) is the oldest physical witness.

## Themes and motifs
- ***Rén*** (仁, humaneness / benevolence) — the central virtue. Difficult to define univocally; rendered variously as benevolence, humaneness, co-humanity. "Overcoming self and returning to ritual is *rén*" (12.1).
- ***Lǐ*** (禮, ritual propriety) — the formal patterns of conduct that, when internalized, shape moral life. Not empty form; the channel through which virtue is cultivated.
- ***Jūnzǐ*** (君子, exemplary person) — the ideal of moral cultivation, contrasted with the *xiǎorén* (the small / petty person).
- **Filial piety (*xiào*)** — the rooting of all virtue in family relations; the parent-child bond as the primary moral arena.
- **The mandate of Heaven (*tian ming*)** — Heaven's moral order; rulers who lose virtue lose the mandate.
- **Self-cultivation through study** — the opening line: "To learn and to apply at the proper time — is this not a pleasure?" (1.1).
- **Skepticism about the supernatural** — Confucius avoids speculation about spirits and afterlife; pragmatic this-worldly ethics.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-2-014-daodejing]] — *polemic-against* / *shared-milieu*: the DDJ ridicules the Confucian project of ritual cultivation; Confucianism counters by treating Daoism as quietist abdication. Both are Warring States responses to the same political-ethical crisis.
- → [[phase-2-025-mozi]] — *polemic-against* (within China): Mozi attacks Confucian particularistic care (the family-first ethic) in favor of universal love. Confucians counter.
- → [[phase-2-023-mengzi]] — *direct-influence* / *commentary-on*: Mencius systematizes Confucian thought, develops the doctrine of innate moral goodness.
- → [[phase-2-024-xunzi]] — *direct-influence* (with internal disagreement): Xunzi develops a more austere Confucianism arguing human nature is bad and must be shaped by ritual.
- → [[phase-2-026-han-feizi]] — *polemic-against*: Han Fei's Legalist tradition treats Confucian moralism as the principal obstacle to functional governance.
- → Greek Socratic / Platonic ethics — *parallel-motif*: roughly contemporaneous emergence of ethics-centered philosophy in two civilizations; the famous Jaspers "Axial Age" thesis treats this convergence as significant. No direct contact.

## Disputes
- **Compositional layers.** Brookses (1998) propose precise chronological strata, attributing different books to different disciple-groups across two centuries. Slingerland and others doubt the precision while accepting that the text is layered.
- **Historical Confucius vs. school Confucius.** What in the Analects goes back to the historical figure vs. what is school accretion remains debated.
- **Religious vs. philosophical character.** Confucianism's status as "religion" or "philosophy" has been disputed for centuries; the *tian* / mandate of Heaven element gives it religious dimensions, while its this-worldly focus gives it philosophical ones.
- **Confucius's view of Heaven.** Whether *tian* in the Analects is a personal moral deity, an impersonal moral order, or both, is debated.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges

- **The Golden Rule in negative form (Analects 15:23) — the most replicated ethical formula in human history, with five independent formulations across four civilizations**: Confucius's *shu* ("Do not impose on others what you yourself do not want") is the earliest written version of the negative Golden Rule, ~-500 BCE. Hillel's "What is hateful to you, do not do to your neighbor" (~-30 BCE, Talmud Shabbat 31a) is independent. Jesus's positive form "Do to others what you would have them do to you" (Matt 7:12, ~+30 CE) is explicitly called "the law and the prophets." The Jain *Acaranga Sutra* 5.101 version (~-400 BCE) and the Hindu Mahabharata 13.113.8 version add two more. The Golden Rule is the single most replicated ethical insight across unconnected civilizations — the Axial Age's clearest convergence on a moral universal (Wattles, J. 1996, *The Golden Rule*, Oxford UP; Slingerland 2003; → [[phase-2-014-daodejing]]).

- **The imperial examination system based on the Analects — largest educational-political program in history built on a single text**: The Analects became the core text of the Chinese imperial examination system (keju) under the Han dynasty, formalized under the Tang, and remained central for 1,300 years (605–1905 CE). Every bureaucratic official in China — millions of men over thirteen centuries — was required to memorize and comment on the Analects. This makes the Confucian examination system the largest single-text-based educational-political institution in world history: no other document (not the Bible, not the Quran, not the Vedas) was the literal prerequisite for political office across an entire civilization for this duration (Elman, B. 2000, *A Cultural History of Civil Examinations in Late Imperial China*, UC Press; Brooks & Brooks 1998, Columbia UP).

- **"Not yet able to serve people, how to serve spirits?" — the most consequential deflection of metaphysics in Axial Age literature**: Analects 11:11's pragmatist silence about death and spirits is structurally identical to Wittgenstein's "Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent" and Buddha's "undetermined questions" (*avyākata*) — refusing to answer cosmological questions on grounds they distract from the actual task of living. These three contemporaneous instances of principled silence-about-metaphysics (Confucius, Buddha, pre-Socratics' skeptical thread) are one of the clearest convergent features of the Axial Age: each tradition, independently, reaches a point where its highest teacher declines to speculate about what cannot be known (Jaspers 1953; Schwartz, B. 1985, *The World of Thought in Ancient China*, Harvard UP).

- **Ren (humaneness) vs. Western virtue ethics — two independent Axial Age systems for grounding ethics in something universal**: Confucius's *ren* (humaneness, co-humanity, the capacity to respond to others as others) and Aristotle's *phronesis* (practical wisdom, the virtue of right action in context) are two Axial Age solutions to the same problem: how does ethics get its content? Ren is relational and developmental (virtue emerges from practicing ritual relationships); phronesis is cognitive and judgmental (virtue is the intelligent perception of what the situation requires). Both are non-rule-based, practice-grounded, and embedded in social life — in contrast to both divine-command ethics and modern utilitarian calculation. Tu Weiming's Confucian humanism was the intellectual foundation for the East Asian engagement with human rights discourse in the late 20th century (Slingerland 2003; Tu Weiming 1985, *Confucian Thought*, SUNY Press; Nussbaum, M. 1990, *Love's Knowledge*, Oxford UP).
