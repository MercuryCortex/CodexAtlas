# The Deuteronomistic History

## What it is
The Deuteronomistic History (DtrH) is the great Israelite-Judahite historiographic compilation identified by Martin Noth in 1943: the four-book retrospective narrative Joshua–Judges–Samuel–Kings, unified by Deuteronomistic theological framing, that tells the history of Israel from the entry into Canaan to the fall of Jerusalem and Babylonian Exile (586 BCE). DtrH is **distinct from** the D source ([[phase-2-011-hebrew-bible-d-source]]) which it presupposes: D is the theological program (covenant, centralization, monolatry); DtrH is the *historical narrative application* of that program to Israelite history, asking — and answering with sustained theological coherence — why a once-elect kingdom was destroyed. Its hallmarks: programmatic speeches at major historical junctures (Joshua's farewell in Joshua 23, Samuel's farewell in 1 Samuel 12, Solomon's Temple dedication in 1 Kings 8, the editorial summary on the fall of Israel in 2 Kings 17); the formulaic king-summary scheme ("did what was right / evil in the sight of YHWH"); the relentless centralization theology (kings judged for tolerating local "high places"); and the salvation-history schema in which divine election, covenant infidelity, prophetic warning, and historical catastrophe form a coherent theological argument.

## Primary text — selected passages

### 2 Samuel 7:12–16 — Nathan's oracle: the eternal Davidic covenant
*Trans. NRSV; McCarter, P.K. (1984). 2 Samuel. Anchor Bible 9.*
> When your days are fulfilled and you lie down with your ancestors, I will raise up your offspring after you, who shall come forth from your body, and I will establish his kingdom. He shall build a house for my name, and I will establish the throne of his kingdom forever. I will be a father to him, and he shall be a son to me... Your house and your kingdom shall be made sure forever before me; your throne shall be established forever.

*The Nathan oracle is the foundational document of Davidic messianism. The unconditional divine promise — "your throne shall be established forever" — is the source text for every subsequent Hebrew prophet who speaks of a future Davidic king, and for the NT's identification of Jesus as the "Son of David." The oracle is the most-cited OT passage in NT Christological proof-texts after the Psalms.*

### 1 Kings 8:27–30 — Solomon's Temple dedication prayer
*Trans. NRSV; Cogan, M. (2001). 1 Kings. Anchor Bible.*
> But will God indeed dwell on the earth? Even heaven and the highest heaven cannot contain you, much less this house that I have built! Regard your servant's prayer and his plea, O LORD my God... that your eyes may be open night and day toward this house... and when you hear, forgive.

*Solomon's prayer encodes the DtrH's central theological paradox: the Temple is dedicated at the moment the prayer insists YHWH cannot be contained within it. The deity who chose to "place his name" in Jerusalem cannot be confined there. This generates the entire tradition of Solomon as builder-sage and the tension between divine transcendence and cultic presence.*

### 2 Kings 17:7–12 — DtrH's theology of the fall of Israel
*Trans. NRSV; Cogan, M. & Tadmor, H. (1988). 2 Kings. Anchor Bible.*
> This occurred because the people of Israel had sinned against the LORD their God, who had brought them up out of the land of Egypt... They had worshiped other gods and walked in the customs of the nations... The people of Israel secretly did things that were not right against the LORD their God. They built for themselves high places at all their towns... They set up for themselves pillars and sacred poles on every high hill and under every green tree.

*2 Kings 17's theological summary of the fall of Samaria (722 BCE) is DtrH's controlling theological statement: covenant-infidelity → divine judgment. Applied retroactively to all Israelite history and prospectively to Judah's fate in 586, it is the founding document of the "sin-exile-return" pattern that shapes all subsequent Jewish and Christian historiography.*

*Citations: NRSV; McCarter, P.K. (1984), AB 9; Cogan, M. (2001), Anchor Bible; Cogan, M. & Tadmor, H. (1988), Anchor Bible.*

## Context
- **Political milieu.** The DtrH is composed during the great crisis of late monarchic and exilic Judah. The fall of the Northern Kingdom (722 BCE) provides the prototype — divine judgment on covenant-breaking. Josiah's Deuteronomic reform (622 BCE) gives the project its theological program — strict Yahwism, centralization, covenant. Josiah's death at Megiddo (609 BCE) and Judah's subsequent vassalage to Babylon and final destruction (586 BCE) require the project's exilic-period completion: explaining how the elect people lost the land.
- **Religious milieu.** The Deuteronomistic worldview is itself a product of the late-monarchic religious revolution. Older Israelite religion was polytheistic / monolatrous, decentralized, and tolerant of local cult sites. The Deuteronomic-Deuteronomistic program — strict monolatry, single legitimate sanctuary, covenant theology in vassal-treaty form — is the institutional victory of the prophetic-Levitical reformers over the older pluralistic Israelite religion. DtrH retrospectively rewrites Israelite history *as if* this strict program had been Israel's normative standard all along, judging earlier kings against it.
- **Who was behind it.** A scribal "Deuteronomistic school" closely related to (perhaps continuous with) the Deuteronomic school behind D. Locating them socially: probably Jerusalem temple-scribal circles in the Josianic phase; almost certainly Judahite scribes in Babylonian exile for the final-form compilation. Friedman 1987 makes the case for Baruch ben Neriah, scribal partner of [[jeremiah]], as a plausible exilic redactor; the connection between Jeremianic prose and DtrH is widely acknowledged.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous Jewish canonical tradition; recognized as the *Former Prophets* (Nevi'im Rishonim) in the Hebrew canon. The compositional unity Noth 1943 identified was a major critical achievement; pre-Noth scholarship treated the four books separately. Post-Noth scholarship has refined his single-author thesis into multi-layered redactional schemes (Cross 1973's two-layer Dtr1/Dtr2; the Smend-school's three-layer DtrG/DtrP/DtrN; Römer 2005's three-stage Neo-Assyrian/Neo-Babylonian/Persian model).

## Themes and motifs
- [[divine-kingship]] — kingship under YHWH; the king's primary obligation is fidelity to the Deuteronomic covenant. David is the type-case of the loyal king (with the Bathsheba episode acknowledged but theologically subordinated); Solomon's apostasy (1 Kings 11) initiates the cycle of judgment; the northern kings fail almost en masse; the Judahite kings split (Hezekiah and Josiah are reformers, most others backslide).
- [[prophet-and-king]] — DtrH gives prophets the structural role of declaring divine judgment on royal apostasy. Samuel, Nathan, Ahijah, Elijah, Elisha, Isaiah, and the unnamed "man of God" of 1 Kings 13 form a sustained prophetic counterweight to kingship; the schema is foundational for Hebrew-Bible *prophet-and-king* theology.
- [[henotheism-monolatry]] — Yahweh-only worship enforced retrospectively as the standard against which all kings are judged.
- [[chosen-people]] — Israel as the elect people of YHWH; the entire historical narrative is structured as covenant-fidelity / covenant-infidelity / divine judgment / occasional restoration.
- [[end-times-judgment]] — the Exile as the culminating divine judgment in history; the historical-eschatological pattern that DtrH establishes (election → infidelity → judgment → exile → potential restoration) shapes all subsequent biblical and Jewish historical-theological thinking.
- [[salvation-history]] — DtrH is the great founding text of *Heilsgeschichte* (salvation-history) as a category. God works through historical events; history is theologically intelligible as the unfolding of divine purpose.
- [[temple-economy]] — the Jerusalem Temple as the legitimate cultic center (1 Kings 8 dedication speech); the destruction of the high places as the central reform-criterion; the burning of the Temple in 586 as the cataclysmic climax.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-2-011-hebrew-bible-d-source]] — *direct-influence* / *commentary-on*: D provides the theological program (covenant, centralization, blessings-and-curses) that DtrH applies retrospectively to Israelite history. The relationship is foundational: there is no DtrH without D. But D and DtrH are distinct compositions and should not be conflated — D is law-program, DtrH is historiographic application. Refs: Noth 1943; Cross 1973; Römer 2005.
- → [[phase-2-010-hebrew-bible-early-prophets]] — *shared-milieu* / *parallel-motif*: DtrH's prophetic episodes (Samuel-Saul, Nathan-David, Elijah-Ahab, Isaiah-Hezekiah) share milieu and theology with the canonical prophetic books. The Jeremiah prose tradition is especially closely intertwined with DtrH; many scholars treat Jeremianic prose as a Deuteronomistic-school product.
- → [[phase-2-018-hebrew-bible-p-source]] — *parallel-redaction* / *polemic-against*: P and DtrH are the two great late-monarchic / exilic theological responses to the crisis. They differ on cult location, ritual emphasis, and the locus of authority (priestly vs. prophetic), but share the project of theological reconstruction. The final-form Pentateuch + Former Prophets results from their integration by Persian-period redactors.
- → 1-2 Chronicles (the Chronicler's History) — *redaction-of*: the Chronicler partly rewrites DtrH from a different theological perspective (priestly-Levitical, Davidic-cult-centered, more optimistic about post-exilic continuation). The Chronicler/DtrH relationship is one of the great Hebrew-Bible compositional puzzles.
- → Neo-Assyrian royal-annal historiography (the inscriptions of Tiglath-Pileser III, Sargon II, Sennacherib, Esarhaddon, Ashurbanipal) — *parallel-motif* / *shared-milieu*: the formal architecture of DtrH (king-by-king annals; theological-political evaluation; victory and disaster narratives) closely parallels Neo-Assyrian royal-historiographic conventions. Some scholars (Van Seters, Cogan-Tadmor) argue direct influence; others see independent ANE convergence.
- → [[phase-3-008-book-of-daniel]] — *parallel-motif*: Daniel's four-kingdoms schema (Daniel 2, 7) inherits DtrH's pattern of divine sovereignty over imperial-political history, abstracted and apocalypticized.

## Disputes
- **Single-author vs. multi-layer.** Noth 1943 argued for a single exilic author working with sources. Cross 1973 split this into Dtr1 (Josianic, optimistic) and Dtr2 (exilic, judgment-finalizing); the Smend / Göttingen school argues for three layers (DtrG / DtrP / DtrN). Römer 2005 proposes three compositional *stages* (Neo-Assyrian, Neo-Babylonian, Persian) which may not correspond to discrete redactional layers. Each scheme has substantial scholarly following.
- **The very existence of DtrH as a unified composition.** A minority (Westermann; some of Schmid's recent work) denies the unity Noth identified, treating the Former Prophets as four books with overlapping but independent redaction. Mainstream consensus retains Noth's basic unity-of-conception.
- **Relation of DtrH to the Tetrateuch / Pentateuch.** Older scholarship (Noth) treated Genesis–Numbers + Joshua–Kings as separate compositions only later combined. Some recent positions (Freedman's "Primary History"; aspects of Schmid's *Erzväter und Exodus*) argue for a more integrated late-redactional unity from Genesis to 2 Kings.
- **Date of final form.** Cross's Dtr1 places a Josianic-optimistic first edition before 609; the Dtr2 / final exilic redaction is consensus ~-560 to -540. Late-dating positions (Persian-period or even Hellenistic; minority view) exist but are not mainstream.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges

- **DtrH invented *Heilsgeschichte* (salvation history) as a theological-historiographic category — foundational to Jewish, Christian, and Islamic philosophy of history**: Martin Noth's 1943 identification of DtrH as the first sustained historiographic work interpreting political events as the unfolding of a single divine purpose across centuries established what von Rad called *Heilsgeschichte*: history as the medium of divine action. The concept is constitutive of Judaism (the Seder retells DtrH's exodus-and-covenant logic), Christianity (Paul's argument in Romans 9–11; Bossuet's *Discours sur l'histoire universelle*; Hegel's *Lectures on the Philosophy of History*), and Islam (the Quranic historical narratives of the prophets). Modern historical theology from Origen through Pannenberg rests on this foundation (Noth, M. 1943/1981. *The Deuteronomistic History*. JSOTSup 15. pp.1–27; von Rad, G. 1962. *Old Testament Theology*. vol. 1. Oliver & Boyd. pp.334–347; Pannenberg, W. 1967. *Revelation as History*. Macmillan. pp.3–21).

- **2 Sam 7 Davidic oracle = most consequential five verses in Jewish and Christian messianism, generating the entire Son of David tradition**: The unconditional divine promise "your throne shall be established forever" generated the entire messianic hope of the Hebrew prophets, Second Temple Judaism, and the NT's identification of Jesus as the "Son of David" (Matthew 1:1; Romans 1:3; Revelation 5:5). The Davidic covenant is the most-cited OT passage in NT Christological proof-texts after the Psalms; it is the textual foundation of both the Christmas liturgy (Luke 1:32–33 quotes it) and the entire messianic-political tradition from Bar Kokhba to Zionism (McCarter, P.K. 1984. *2 Samuel*. AB 9. pp.196–205; Cross, F.M. 1973. *Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic*. Harvard UP. pp.241–265; Wright, N.T. 2003. *The Resurrection of the Son of God*. Fortress. pp.317–322).

- **DtrH's prophet-and-king schema = foundational model for prophetic accountability to power in Western political theology**: The recurring DtrH pattern — king acts; prophet confronts; divine judgment follows (Nathan/David in 2 Sam 12; Elijah/Ahab in 1 Kgs 21; Isaiah/Hezekiah in 2 Kgs 19–20) — creates the political-theological structure that runs through medieval papalist *rex et sacerdos* conflict, Calvinist resistance theory (Knox, Rutherford's *Lex Rex*), Puritan constitutionalism, and the modern Black prophetic tradition from Frederick Douglass to Martin Luther King Jr. Brueggemann argues the prophetic imagination of DtrH is the single most important source for a non-totalitarian politics (Brueggemann, W. 2001. *The Prophetic Imagination*, 2nd ed. Fortress. pp.1–30; Skinner, Q. 1978. *The Foundations of Modern Political Thought*. Cambridge UP. vol. 2 pp.220–230; Walzer, M. 1985. *Exodus and Revolution*. Basic Books. pp.139–149).

- **DtrH's king-evaluation formula = origin of the "decline from a golden age" historiographic pattern that structures all subsequent Western civilizational history**: DtrH evaluates every king of Israel and Judah against a single standard ("did what was right / evil in the eyes of YHWH") and against the template of David. The resulting pattern — golden age (David) → decline → catastrophe → exile — is the historiographic structure that runs through Polybius's anacyclosis, Livy's Rome, Gibbon's *Decline and Fall*, Spengler's *Decline of the West*, and all subsequent civilizational historiography. The DtrH is the most influential origin-text of the Western habit of reading history as moral decline (Noth 1943/1981. pp.88–100; Römer, T. 2005. *The So-Called Deuteronomistic History*. T&T Clark. pp.56–70; Knoppers, G.N. 1993. *Two Nations Under God*. vol. 1. HSM 52. pp.57–90).
