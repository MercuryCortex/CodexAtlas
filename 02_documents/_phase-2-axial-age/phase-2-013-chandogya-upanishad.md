# Chāndogya Upaniṣad

## What it is
The Chāndogya Upaniṣad is the second-oldest principal Upaniṣad (closely paired with the Bṛhadāraṇyaka). Belonging to the Sāmaveda tradition — its name derives from *chandoga*, "singer" of the Sāman melodies — it organizes ~628 verses in eight *prapāṭhakas*. Like the BṛhUp it transitions from ritual exegesis to philosophical meditation: it contains the most famous Upaniṣadic teaching, the great dialogue between Uddālaka Āruṇi and his son Śvetaketu in chapter 6, culminating in the *mahāvākya* "*tat tvam asi*" ("That you are") — the identification of the inner self with cosmic being repeated as refrain.

## Primary text — selected passages

### Chāndogya Upaniṣad 6.2.1–2 — "In the beginning, Being alone" (the cosmogonic premise)
*Trans. Olivelle, P. (1998). The Early Upaniṣads. Oxford UP.*
> "In the beginning, my dear, this was Being alone —
> one only, without a second.
> Some say: 'In the beginning this was Non-being alone, one only, without a second.
> From that Non-being, Being was born.'
> But, my dear, how could Being be born from Non-being?
> On the contrary, my dear, in the beginning this was Being alone,
> one only, without a second."

*Uddālaka Āruṇi corrects a rival cosmogonic view and establishes that Being (sat) is primordial and irreducible. "One only, without a second" — the formula recurs throughout chapter 6 as Uddālaka's refrain.*

### Chāndogya Upaniṣad 6.12–13 — The banyan seed and the salt in water: *tat tvam asi*
*Trans. Olivelle 1998*
> "Break one of these fruits."
> "It is broken, sir."
> "What do you see in it?"
> "These fine seeds, sir."
> "Break one of them."
> "What do you see in it?"
> "Nothing at all, sir."
> "My dear, this finest essence that you cannot perceive —
> it is from this finest essence that this great banyan tree grows.
> Believe me, my dear: the finest essence here —
> that constitutes the self of all this; that is the truth; that is the self.
> And that, Śvetaketu, is what you are — *tat tvam asi*."

> "Throw this salt into water and come back tomorrow morning."
> [Next day:] "Where is the salt?"
> "I don't know where it is, sir."
> "Take a sip. How is it?" "Salty."
> "Take a sip from the middle." "Salty."
> "Take a sip from the far end." "Salty."
> "The salt is always right there, even though you cannot see it.
> In the same way, my dear, you cannot perceive Being here, but it is here.
> This finest essence — that is what you are: *tat tvam asi*, Śvetaketu."

*The mahāvākya is repeated nine times across chapter 6, each time after a different illustration (banyan seed, salt, dye, fire, fig, tree, man). The repetition is pedagogical ritual — the student must hear the identification of ātman with Brahman across multiple registers.*

### Chāndogya Upaniṣad 5.10.3–7 — The five fires: karma and rebirth cosmology
*Trans. Olivelle 1998*
> Those who depart from this world — they all go to the moon.
> By means of their vital breaths the moon becomes full in the first half of the month;
> in the second half it causes them to pass down in birth.
> The moon is King Soma — the food of the gods...
> Those whose conduct has been good here will quickly attain a good birth —
> as a brahmin, a kṣatriya, or a vaiśya.
> But those whose conduct has been bad will quickly attain a bad birth —
> as a dog, a pig, or an outcaste.

*Citation: Olivelle, P. (1998). The Early Upaniṣads: Annotated Text and Translation. Oxford UP. Olivelle, P. (1996). Upaniṣads. Oxford World's Classics.*

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Same Iron Age north-Indian setting as BṛhUp — Kuru-Pañcāla / Videha kingdoms, ~750–550 BCE.
- **Religious milieu.** Brahmanical ritualism becoming inwardly interpreted; ascetic and śramaṇic currents (forest-dwellers, wanderers) intersecting with the priestly tradition. The Buddha and Mahāvīra are about to appear (~-500); the ChU sits at the doorstep of the śramaṇa movement.
- **Who was behind it.** Sāmavedic priestly lineages; the dialogues feature Uddālaka Āruṇi, his son Śvetaketu, the Kshatriya teacher Pravāhaṇa Jaivali (king of Pañcāla — a notable case where a warrior-king instructs a brahmin in deep doctrine).
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous Indian transmission; entered Western awareness through the Persian Oupnekhat and the European-Sanskrit philological tradition (Anquetil-Duperron, Schopenhauer, Max Müller).

## Themes and motifs
- ***Tat tvam asi*** ("That you are") — the *mahāvākya* of chapter 6, taught by Uddālaka to Śvetaketu through nine progressive illustrations (the banyan seed, the salt in water, etc.). The identification of the personal *ātman* with cosmic *sat* (Being).
- ***Sat-chit-ānanda*** core (proto-form) — *sat* (Being, *sad eva somyedam agra āsīt*, "In the beginning, my dear, this was Being alone, one only, without a second", 6.2.1) is the first principle.
- **Karma and rebirth** — ChU 5.10 (the doctrine of the five fires, *pañcāgnividyā*): the post-mortem journey, the moon as transit-stage, return to earth, rebirth as plant → animal → human according to karma. One of the earliest detailed rebirth-cosmologies.
- **Two paths** — *devayāna* and *pitṛyāna* (paralleling BṛhUp 6.2).
- **Om / Udgītha meditation** — ChU 1: the syllable *Om* as the cosmic essence, the Udgītha (the chanted song) as cosmic homology.
- **Antarātman / inner self** — the Dahara-vidyā (8.1): "There is this city of brahman, and in it the palace, the small lotus, and in that the small space — what is within that should be searched out."

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-2-012-brihadaranyaka-upanishad]] — *shared-milieu* / *parallel-motif*: the two oldest Upaniṣads share doctrines (brahman/ātman, karma, rebirth) and personnel (Uddālaka Āruṇi). They ground later Vedānta.
- → [[phase-2-006-brahmanas-aranyakas]] — *redaction-of*: the ChU is the upaniṣadic capstone of the Chāndogya Brāhmaṇa.
- → Early Buddhist suttas (e.g., the Brahmajāla, Pārāyana suttas) — *shared-milieu* / *polemic-against*: the Buddha's *anātman* doctrine reacts to *ātman*-doctrine of the ChU vintage. Ref: Bronkhorst 2007.
- → Bhagavad Gītā (Phase 3) — *commentary-on*: the Gītā explicitly draws Upaniṣadic terminology and concepts into its synthesis.
- → Plato's *Phaedo* and *Symposium* — *parallel-motif*: cosmic self / immortality / unitary Being; possible but unproven mediation via Achaemenid trade routes. Ref: West 1971.

## Disputes
- **Date.** Olivelle (1998) places ChU at ~700–500 BCE, with BṛhUp slightly earlier; not all agree on order.
- **Identity of Uddālaka Āruṇi's teaching.** Whether ChU 6's doctrine is properly "non-dualistic" (Śaṅkara's reading) or "real-cause" monism (Rāmānuja's reading) or something else (modern critical readings) — a 1,200-year philosophical debate.
- **Kshatriya-source thesis.** That key Upaniṣadic doctrines (rebirth, the two paths) were taught *by* warrior-kings *to* brahmins (Pravāhaṇa to Uddālaka, Janaka to Yājñavalkya) has been argued (Deussen, Garbe, more recently Bronkhorst) as evidence the doctrines originate outside the Brahmanical sacrificial mainstream. Contested.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges

- ***Tat tvam asi* — the most-commented sentence in Indian philosophy, generating a 2,500-year interpretive dispute that maps the entire Vedāntic tradition**: "That you are" (ChU 6.8.7, repeated nine times) identifies the individual self (*ātman*) with cosmic Being (*Brahman / sat*). Śaṅkara (8th c. CE) reads it as strict non-duality — *ātman = Brahman* without qualification (Advaita). Rāmānuja (11th c.) reads it as "that which is you is Brahman-qualified-by-finite-selves" (Viśiṣṭādvaita). Madhva (13th c.) reads it as asserting eternal distinction between self and God (Dvaita). Each of the three major Vedāntic schools is a different reading of this one sentence — the most consequential single sentence in Indian philosophy, generating a philosophical tradition as extensive as the entire Western debate over universals (Olivelle 1998, Oxford UP; Deutsch, E. 1969, *Advaita Vedānta*, East-West Press; → [[phase-2-012-brihadaranyaka-upanishad]]).

- **Karma-rebirth cosmology in ChU 5.10 — earliest detailed rebirth-doctrine, establishing the inheritance chain into Buddhism, Jainism, and Greek philosophy**: The *pañcāgnividyā* (five-fires doctrine) is the oldest detailed specification of karma-determined rebirth: the dead travel to the moon, are "rained down" as grain, enter animals and humans, and are reborn according to conduct. This is the template that Buddhist rebirth-cosmology inherits and transforms (dropping the *ātman* that does the transmigrating), that Jain karma-physics systematizes, and whose structural parallel in Plato's *Phaedo* and the Myth of Er (*Republic* 614b–621d) is close enough in time (both ~-400 BCE) and geography (Achaemenid Persian corridor) to be more than coincidence (Bronkhorst, J. 2007, *Greater Magadha*, Brill; West, M.L. 1971, *Early Greek Philosophy and the Orient*, Oxford UP; → [[phase-3-002-plato-dialogues]]).

- **"Being alone, without a second" — Axial Age convergence on non-dual primordial Being across four traditions**: ChU 6.2.1's "In the beginning, Being alone — one only, without a second" is structurally parallel to Genesis 1:1 ("In the beginning, God"), Parmenides's "only Being is" (fr. 6–8, ~-480 BCE), and Islamic *tawḥīd* ("There is no God but God" = only one Being is ultimately real). Four traditions developed independently within overlapping centuries all affirm that ultimate reality is a single irreducible Being — the clearest convergent-philosophical pattern of the Axial Age. Whether this reflects structural constraints on the mind when pushed to metaphysical extremes, or actual transmission via trade routes, is the open question (Jaspers, K. 1953, *The Origin and Goal of History*; West 1971; → [[phase-1-031-rigveda]]).

- **Kshatriya teaching brahmin — the most politically subversive transmission scene in the Upaniṣads**: In ChU 5, the karma-rebirth doctrine is first taught not by a brahmin priest but by Pravāhaṇa Jaivali, a Kshatriya warrior-king, to the brahmin Uddālaka. A warrior teaches the priest what the priest doesn't know. Bronkhorst (2007) reads this as evidence that karma-rebirth originated *outside* the sacrificial Brahmanical tradition — in the *śramaṇa* forest-renouncer culture — and was absorbed into brahminism with the scene reframed to suggest brahmins were its guardians. This pattern (heterodox teaching absorbed and re-attributed to the mainstream) recurs as Buddhism is absorbed into Mahāyāna, Sufism into Sunni Islam, and mystical theology into Catholic scholasticism (Bronkhorst 2007, *Greater Magadha*, Brill, pp. 67–89).
