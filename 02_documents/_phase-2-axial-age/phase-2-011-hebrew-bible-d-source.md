# Hebrew Bible — D (Deuteronomic Core)

## What it is
D, the "Deuteronomic" source, is the third of the four classical reconstructed Pentateuchal sources. Unlike J and E (interwoven throughout Genesis–Numbers), D is essentially confined to the book of Deuteronomy, framed as Moses's farewell speech in the plains of Moab. The core of Deuteronomy — most agree on chapters 12–26 — is widely identified with the "Book of the Law" (*sēfer hattôrāh*) "found" in the Jerusalem Temple in the 18th year of King Josiah (~622 BCE), as narrated in 2 Kings 22–23 and 2 Chronicles 34. Its hallmarks: cult centralization ("the place which YHWH your God shall choose to make his name dwell there"), strict monolatry, covenantal theology framed in vassal-treaty form, the *Shema* (Deut 6:4), the law of the king (17:14–20), the prophet like Moses (18:15–22), and a humanistic ethical revision of earlier law codes.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** The Assyrian empire's collapse (Ashurbanipal dies ~627 BCE; Nineveh falls 612 BCE) opens space for Judahite religious-political assertion. King Josiah (r. ~640–609 BCE) launches a major reform centralizing worship at Jerusalem, destroying outlying sanctuaries (the "high places"), and instituting national Passover (2 Kings 23). The discovery of the law-book in the Temple is integral to this reform; many scholars view this as either the rediscovery of an existing northern legal tradition or a Josianic-period composition presented as ancient.
- **Religious milieu.** The reform represents the institutional victory of strict monolatry — Yahweh-only worship at a single legitimate sanctuary (Jerusalem) — over the popular polytheism and decentralized local cult that the prophets and the Deuteronomic school had been attacking for a century.
- **Who was behind it.** A scribal "Deuteronomic school" likely descended from northern Levitical/Mushite priestly lineages displaced by the fall of Samaria (722 BCE) and resettled in Judah. The school's relation to the temple establishment, the prophetic tradition (especially Hosea, Jeremiah), and the Josianic royal court is intricate. Friedman (1987) makes the case for Jeremiah's scribal partner Baruch ben Neriah as a plausible final redactor; Weinfeld (1972) anchors it in royal scribal circles.
- **Discovery / transmission.** The "book" was reportedly found by Hilkiah the high priest during temple repairs and given to King Josiah, who consulted the prophetess Huldah for authentication (2 Kings 22:14–20). The text's "discovery" is the founding event of canonical Hebrew Bible.

## Themes and motifs
- **Cult centralization** — "The place which YHWH your God shall choose" (Deut 12, recurrent) — only one legitimate altar; local sanctuaries (the "high places") to be destroyed.
- [[henotheism-monolatry]] / strict Yahwism — the *Shema* (Deut 6:4): "Hear, O Israel! YHWH our God, YHWH is one." Whether to read this as monotheistic affirmation or monolatrous demand is debated; in context it functions as a loyalty oath.
- **Covenantal theology in treaty form** — Weinfeld 1972 demonstrated that Deuteronomy reproduces the structural form of Neo-Assyrian vassal treaties (preamble, historical prologue, stipulations, blessings, curses, witnesses). Esarhaddon's Succession Treaty (672 BCE) is the closest formal analogue. Deut 28's curses parallel Assyrian treaty curses sentence-by-sentence in places.
- **Law of the king** (17:14–20) — the king is subordinate to the *torah*, must read it daily, may not multiply horses, wives, or silver. A constitutional limitation on monarchy.
- **Prophet like Moses** (18:15–22) — Moses as the paradigmatic prophet; criteria for true vs. false prophecy.
- **Social-ethical revision** — humanitarian provisions: release of debts in the sabbatical year (15:1–11), provisions for resident aliens and Levites, restrictions on cruelty in warfare. A more humanistic legal sensibility than the older Covenant Code (Exod 21–23).

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-2-007-hebrew-bible-e-source]] — *influences*: D inherits and develops the northern Elohist tradition. The shared theology of covenant, exclusive Yahwism, and prophet-centered religion makes the genealogical relationship widely accepted.
- → [[phase-2-010-hebrew-bible-early-prophets]] — *influences* / *shared-milieu*: Hosea's polemics and demands prefigure Deuteronomic theology; First Isaiah's social ethics resonate. The 8th-century prophets and the Deuteronomic school are arguably two arms of the same religious reform movement.
- → **Esarhaddon's Succession Treaty (Neo-Assyrian, 672 BCE)** — *parallel-motif* / *redaction-of* (formal): Deut 28's curses and the overall treaty form derive from this vassal-treaty tradition. Weinfeld 1972 documents 30+ parallel formulae. The polemic move: Israel is YHWH's vassal, not Assyria's. Ref: Weinfeld 1972; Steymans 1995.
- → **Deuteronomistic History (Joshua–2 Kings)** — *redaction-of*: a large historical narrative composed in Deuteronomic style, evaluating each king of Israel and Judah by D's standards, is widely attributed to a Deuteronomistic Historian (Noth's *DtrH*) writing in the late 7th c. (Cross's Dtr1) and exilic period (Dtr2).
- → [[phase-2-005-hebrew-bible-j-source]] — *parallel-motif* / *polemic-against*: D presupposes and engages the earlier Pentateuchal traditions, sometimes rewriting laws (compare Deut 15:12–18 with Exod 21:2–11 on slave release).

## Disputes
- **Date of the core.** The Josianic-period dating of Deuteronomy's core (de Wette 1805, classical consensus) is the dominant view. Some scholars push it earlier (Hezekian, ~715–687 BCE — Weinfeld in part) or later (mostly exilic — Kaufmann school had a different chronology). The 622 BCE event is anchored by 2 Kings 22, but the date of *composition* is the question.
- **Northern vs. southern origin.** Older view: composed in Judah after the fall of the north, incorporating northern traditions (Alt, von Rad). Some argue more thoroughly southern.
- **Mechanism of "discovery".** Pious fraud (a Josianic composition presented as ancient — late 19th c. consensus); recovered text (substantially existing, recovered during repairs); reform program written *and* presented contemporaneously. All three positions retain advocates.
- **DtrH layers.** Cross's two-edition theory (Dtr1 Josianic, Dtr2 exilic) vs. Smend's three-layer theory (DtrH, DtrP, DtrN) vs. Noth's single-author view. Unresolved.
