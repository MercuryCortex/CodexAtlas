# Iliad and Odyssey (Homeric Epics)

## What it is
Two monumental Greek hexameter epics: the **Iliad** (~15,693 lines, 24 books), narrating the wrath of Achilles and a ~50-day stretch of the ten-year siege of Troy; and the **Odyssey** (~12,109 lines, 24 books), narrating Odysseus's ten-year return from Troy to Ithaca. Both poems present a fully articulated Olympian pantheon — Zeus, Hera, Athena, Apollo, Poseidon, Aphrodite, Ares, Hermes, Hephaestus, Artemis, and others — as active anthropomorphic agents intervening in human affairs. They are simultaneously the literary fountainhead of Greek culture and a primary source for the religious imagination of Archaic Greece. The poems originated in oral-formulaic tradition (Parry/Lord), with the surviving texts representing a written stabilization in roughly the 8th–6th centuries BCE.

## Primary text — selected excerpts

Excerpts use the standard public-domain prose translations: Samuel Butler's *Iliad* (1898) and *Odyssey* (1900), available at sacred-texts.com and Project Gutenberg. Lattimore (1951) and Fagles (1990/1996) render these passages with more philological precision and Homeric rhythm; for serious study they are the better edition.

### Iliad I.1–7 — the invocation: the wrath of Achilles

Western literature opens with a single word, *mēnis* ("wrath"), and a question to the Muse — the first model of the inspired-author trope. Note that the proximate cause assigned for the entire war's misery is *the will of Zeus*: the gods are not merely audience to the heroic action but its frame.

> Sing, O goddess, the anger of Achilles son of Peleus, that brought countless ills upon the Achaeans. Many a brave soul did it send hurrying down to Hades, and many a hero did it yield a prey to dogs and vultures, for so were the counsels of Jove fulfilled from the day on which the son of Atreus, king of men, and great Achilles, first fell out with one another.

Citation: *Iliad* I.1–7, trans. Samuel Butler 1898; cf. Lattimore 1951 ("Sing, goddess, the anger of Peleus' son Achilleus / and its devastation...").

### Iliad XVIII.478–608 — the shield of Achilles (selection)

Hephaestus forges Achilles's new shield, and Homer describes it as a microcosm of the entire ordered world: city at peace, city at war, harvest, dance, the Ocean ringing all. The passage is one of the earliest sustained Greek images of *kosmos* as a layered totality.

> First he shaped the shield so great and strong, adorning it all over and binding it round with a gleaming circuit in three layers; and the baldric was made of silver. He made the shield in five thicknesses, and with many a wonder did his cunning hand enrich it.
>
> He wrought the earth, the heavens, and the sea; the moon also at her full and the untiring sun, with all the signs that glorify the face of heaven — the Pleiads, the Hyads, huge Orion, and the Bear, which men also call the Wain and which turns round ever in one place, facing Orion, and alone never dips into the stream of Oceanus.
>
> And he wrought on the shield two cities, fair to see and busy with the hum of men. In the one were weddings and wedding-feasts, and they were going about the city with brides whom they were escorting by torchlight from their chambers...
>
> Round the outermost rim of the shield he set the mighty stream of the river Oceanus.

Citation: *Iliad* XVIII.478–489, 490–493, 607–608, trans. Butler 1898; for the standard scholarly translation see Lattimore 1951, pp. 388–391.

### Odyssey XI.13–50 — the Nekyia: Odysseus at the gate of the dead

Homer's underworld is the Archaic Greek picture of death: a misty, sunless place where the *psychai* are insubstantial shades who can speak only after drinking sacrificial blood. This is the world Plato, Pythagoras, and the mystery cults will later reform.

> Then, when we had got down to the shore we drew our ship into the water and got her mast and sails into her; we also put the sheep on board and took our places, weeping and in great distress of mind. Circe, that great and cunning goddess, sent us a fair wind that blew dead aft and stayed steadily with us, keeping our sails all the time well filled; so we did whatever wanted doing to the ship's gear and let her go as the wind and helmsman headed her.
>
> Here lie the realm and city of the Cimmerians who live enshrouded in mist and darkness which the rays of the sun never pierce, neither at his rising nor as he goes down again out of the heavens, but the poor wretches live in one long melancholy night.
>
> When we got there we beached the ship, took the sheep out of her, and went along by the waters of Oceanus till we came to the place of which Circe had told us. Here Perimedes and Eurylochus held the victims, while I drew my sword and dug the trench a cubit each way. I made a drink-offering to all the dead — first with honey and milk, then with wine, and thirdly with water — and I sprinkled white barley meal over the whole. The ghosts came trooping up from Erebus — brides, young bachelors, old men worn out with toil, maids who had been crossed in love, and brave men who had been killed in battle...

Citation: *Odyssey* XI.1–41 (selections), trans. Butler 1900; cf. Fagles 1996, pp. 250–252.

### Odyssey XXII.1–6 — Odysseus reveals himself to the suitors

The hinge of the poem: stripped of his beggar's disguise, Odysseus speaks. The moment is the model for all later Western recognition scenes.

> Then Ulysses tore off his rags, and sprang on to the broad pavement with his bow and his quiver full of arrows. He shed the arrows on to the ground at his feet and said, "The mighty contest is at an end. I will now see whether Apollo will vouchsafe it to me to hit another mark which no man has yet hit."

Citation: *Odyssey* XXII.1–6, trans. Butler 1900.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Archaic-period (8th c. BCE) Greek world: rising polis system, increasing literacy with the new alphabet (adapted from Phoenician script), aristocratic warrior culture, expanding Greek presence around the Mediterranean (colonization). The poems' narrated setting is the Bronze Age Mycenaean world (~-1200 BCE), four centuries before composition — a recovered heroic past.
- **Religious milieu.** Pre-philosophical Greek polytheism, with the Olympians as central anthropomorphic deities. The poems offer no systematic theology but enact an Olympian society — divine council on Olympus, gods taking sides among mortals, divine fathering of heroes. They depict (especially Odyssey 11, the Nekyia) the shadowy world of the dead in Hades.
- **Who was behind it.** The poems were transmitted by *aoidoi* (singers) and *rhapsodes* (reciters). "Homer" is the tradition's named author; modern scholarship recognizes this as a complex question — most agree on substantial unity of design for each poem but disagree on whether one person, two, or a tradition of guild-singers produced them. Pisistratus of Athens is credited (Cicero, *De Oratore* 3.137) with ordering the first authoritative written version ~-550 BCE.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous transmission. Hellenistic Alexandrian scholars (Zenodotus, Aristophanes of Byzantium, Aristarchus) edited and commented; Byzantine copyists preserved the text. Modern textual scholarship begins with Wolf's *Prolegomena ad Homerum* (1795). Parry's fieldwork on living South Slavic oral epic (1930s) revolutionized understanding of Homeric composition.

## Themes and motifs
- [[divine-council]] — Olympian assemblies open both poems and recur structurally; Zeus presides; debate, factionalism, and decree are dramatized.
- [[divine-kingship]] — Zeus as "father of gods and men", *patēr andrōn te theōn te*, the cosmic monarch.
- [[afterlife-judgment]] — Odyssey 11 (the Nekyia) gives the most extensive Archaic Greek picture of the dead: shades, sacrificial blood awakening speech, glimpses of figures like Tantalus, Sisyphus, Tityus undergoing punishment, but no fully developed judgment system (that becomes Pythagorean / Eleusinian / Platonic later).
- **Heroic ethics** — *kleos* (renown), *aretē* (excellence), *aidōs* (shame) — the value framework within which gods and men interact.
- [[dying-rising-god]] — not directly an Homeric theme but the Adonis/Persephone vicinity (touched in lyric and later poetry) sits adjacent to the Homeric corpus.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-2-009-hesiod-theogony-works-and-days]] — *shared-milieu*: Hesiod and Homer are the twin foundational poets of archaic Greek religion; together they (per Herodotus 2.53) "made the Greeks' theogony, gave the gods their epithets, distinguished their honors and skills, and indicated their forms."
- → [[phase-1-004-gilgamesh-old-babylonian]] — *parallel-motif*: structural and motif parallels between Gilgamesh and the Iliad/Odyssey have been argued (the hero-and-companion bond Achilles/Patroclus ↔ Gilgamesh/Enkidu; the Odyssey's wandering hero with descent to the dead ↔ Gilgamesh's). Possibility of Bronze Age literary transmission. Ref: West 1997, *East Face of Helicon*; Burkert 1992, *The Orientalizing Revolution*.
- → Hittite Kumarbi cycle / Anatolian myth — *parallel-motif*: succession myths, divine combat, monster-slaying. Direct mediation via Anatolian neighbors of Ionia is now reasonably well-established. Ref: West 1997.
- → All subsequent Greek and Roman epic — *manuscript-transmission* / *direct-influence*: Vergil's *Aeneid* explicitly imitates both, Apollonius's *Argonautica* both, etc.

## Disputes
- **The Homeric Question.** Single author of both poems? Single author of one? Composite traditions? Wolf (1795) opened the question; Analysts and Unitarians fought through the 19th c.; Parry/Lord oralism reframed it; current consensus is uncertain unity within an oral tradition that culminated in monumental versions.
- **Date.** The Iliad is usually placed ~750–700 BCE (slightly earlier than the Odyssey, ~720–650 BCE); some place it later (Janko's linguistic dating ~735 BCE; West argues ~670–660 BCE).
- **Historicity of Troy.** Schliemann's excavation (1870 ff.) identified Hisarlık as Troy; current archaeology (Korfmann) supports a Late Bronze Age city plausibly destroyed by warfare ~-1200 BCE. Whether this corresponds to any "Trojan War" in the Homeric sense is much disputed.
- **Near Eastern influence.** Once dismissed, now firmly established via West (1997) and Burkert. Mechanism: probably Anatolian intermediaries plus Phoenician contact.
