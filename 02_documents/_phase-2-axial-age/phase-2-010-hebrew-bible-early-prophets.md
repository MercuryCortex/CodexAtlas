# Hebrew Bible — Early Prophets (Amos, Hosea, First Isaiah, Micah)

## What it is
The four 8th-century BCE prophetic books — Amos, Hosea, First Isaiah (Isaiah 1–39, redacted core), and Micah — are the earliest substantial corpus of named-author Israelite prophetic literature. They share a historical setting (the Assyrian crisis of ~760–700 BCE, the destruction of the northern kingdom in 722, and Sennacherib's siege of Jerusalem in 701), a sharp social-justice critique of elite oppression, a strict demand for exclusive Yahweh-worship against syncretism, and the inauguration of *eschatological* thinking — the "Day of YHWH" as a coming reckoning.

## Primary text — selected passages

### Amos 5:18–24 — Day of YHWH; let justice roll
*Trans. NRSV; Andersen, F.I. & Freedman, D.N. (1989). Amos. Anchor Bible.*
> Alas for you who desire the day of the LORD! Why do you want the day of the LORD? It is darkness, not light; as if someone fled from a lion, and was met by a bear... Is not the day of the LORD darkness, not light, and gloom with no brightness in it? I hate, I despise your festivals, and I take no delight in your solemn assemblies... But let justice roll down like waters, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream.

*Amos's inversion of the "Day of YHWH" — turning a popular expectation of divine triumph into a warning of judgment against an unjust Israel — is the foundational move of all subsequent Hebrew eschatology. "Let justice roll down like waters" (5:24) is the most-quoted prophetic verse in 20th-century political theology, invoked by Martin Luther King Jr. in the "I Have a Dream" speech (1963) and the Selma marches (1965).*

### Isaiah 6:1–8 — The Throne Vision: the trisagion
*Trans. NRSV; Blenkinsopp, J. (2000). Isaiah 1–39. Anchor Bible.*
> In the year that King Uzziah died, I saw the Lord sitting on a throne, high and lofty; and the hem of his robe filled the temple. Seraphs were in attendance above him; each had six wings... And one called to another and said: "Holy, holy, holy is the LORD of hosts; the whole earth is full of his glory."... And I heard the voice of the Lord saying, "Whom shall I send, and who will go for us?" And I said, "Here am I; send me."

*Isaiah's throne vision (Isa 6) — the *trisagion* "Holy, Holy, Holy" — is the direct structural template for Ezekiel's merkabah vision, the Qumran Songs of the Sabbath Sacrifice, John's Revelation (Rev 4–5), and all Merkabah mysticism. The angelic doxology is embedded in Jewish (*Kedushah*) and Christian (*Sanctus*) liturgy, making it the most universally sung text in the history of Abrahamic worship.*

### Micah 6:6–8 — What does YHWH require?
*Trans. NRSV; Andersen, F.I. & Freedman, D.N. (2000). Micah. Anchor Bible.*
> With what shall I come before the LORD, and bow myself before God on high? Shall I come before him with burnt offerings, with calves a year old? Will the LORD be pleased with thousands of rams, with ten thousands of rivers of oil?... He has told you, O mortal, what is good; and what does the LORD require of you but to do justice, and to love kindness, and to walk humbly with your God?

*Micah 6:8 is the most concentrated expression of ethical monotheism in the Hebrew Bible — three clauses that reduce the entire sacrificial system to a moral program: justice, lovingkindness (*ḥesed*), humble walking with God. It was the epigraph chosen by Abraham Joshua Heschel for his theological masterwork *The Prophets* (1962).*

*Citations: NRSV; Andersen & Freedman 1989/2000, Anchor Bible; Blenkinsopp 2000, Anchor Bible.*

## Context
- **Political milieu.** The Neo-Assyrian Empire's expansion under Tiglath-Pileser III (~745) initiates a generations-long crisis for the small kingdoms of the Levant. Amos and Hosea prophesy in the Northern Kingdom of Israel during its final decades (~760–722); the kingdom falls to Sargon II in 722 BCE and its population is partly deported. Isaiah and Micah prophesy in Judah from the mid-8th into the early 7th century; Sennacherib invades Judah in 701 BCE and besieges (but does not take) Jerusalem.
- **Religious milieu.** Popular Israelite religion was substantially polytheistic (Yahweh + Asherah / Baal cults), as both archaeology (Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions, Khirbet el-Qom) and the prophets' own polemics confirm. The 8th-c. prophets press for exclusive Yahwism (the path toward later monotheism), elevate ethical demands ("let justice roll down like waters", Amos 5:24), and inaugurate the prophetic tradition of speaking against the king and the cult establishment.
- **Who was behind it.** Each prophet is a named historical figure with biographical detail. Amos: a shepherd / sycamore-dresser from Tekoa in Judah, prophesying *at* Bethel in the north. Hosea: northern, marrying Gomer as a prophetic sign. Isaiah ben Amoz: a Jerusalem aristocrat with court access. Micah: a rural Judahite from Moresheth. Their oracles were preserved, organized, and edited by disciples and later scribes; the books in their final form contain significant editorial accretion.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous Jewish transmission; the Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsa^a, late 2nd c. BCE) from Qumran is the oldest essentially complete manuscript of any prophetic book and largely matches the later Masoretic text.

## Themes and motifs
- **Day of YHWH** — Amos 5:18–20 inverts a presumed popular expectation: the Day of YHWH will be darkness, not light, for an unjust Israel. The first articulation of an eschatological reckoning that becomes a major Second Temple and Christian theme.
- **Social justice** — Amos's "they sell the righteous for silver and the needy for a pair of sandals" (2:6); Micah's "what does YHWH require? to do justice, to love mercy, to walk humbly" (6:8).
- [[henotheism-monolatry]] — The prophets demand exclusive Yahweh-worship; Hosea attacks Baal worship directly (the wife metaphor: Israel as adulterous spouse, Hosea 1–3).
- **Covenant breach** — The prophets accuse Israel and Judah of breaking covenant with YHWH; this anchors the later Deuteronomistic theology of curse/blessing.
- **Holiness and transcendence** — Isaiah's vision in Isa 6 ("Holy, holy, holy is YHWH of Hosts; the whole earth is full of his glory") introduces a thoroughly transcendent God of Hosts (*YHWH ṣəbāʾôt*).
- **Remnant** — Isaiah's "Shear-jashub" ("a remnant shall return") inaugurates the remnant theology.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-2-005-hebrew-bible-j-source]] / [[phase-2-007-hebrew-bible-e-source]] — *influences*: The 8th-c. prophets presuppose the patriarchal and exodus traditions known from J and E. Hosea especially invokes the Exodus, the wilderness, and Jacob narratives.
- → [[phase-2-011-hebrew-bible-d-source]] — *influences*: The Deuteronomic theology of covenant, exclusive Yahweh-worship, and curse/blessing emerges in continuity with northern prophetic traditions (Hosea especially) transplanted south after 722. The Deuteronomistic editing of the prophets is a major and accepted phenomenon.
- → [[phase-1-013-baal-cycle]] — *polemic-against*: Hosea's polemic against Baal makes the Canaanite religious vocabulary explicit; the prophets attest to the persistence of Baal religion within Israelite practice.
- → Later prophetic literature (Jeremiah, Ezekiel, Second Isaiah) and Second Temple apocalyptic — *influences*: The "Day of YHWH" and remnant motifs are foundational for later Jewish and Christian eschatology.
- → Neo-Assyrian royal inscriptions — *parallel-motif* (treaty/curse rhetoric): Hillers (1964) and Weinfeld (1972) demonstrated that prophetic covenant-curse vocabulary parallels Neo-Assyrian vassal treaty curses.

## Disputes
- **Editorial layers in Isaiah.** Isaiah 1–39 is widely held to contain Isaianic core material plus extensive 7th–6th-c. and even later editorial expansion. Williamson and Blenkinsopp have detailed reconstructions.
- **Hosea's marriage.** Whether Hosea's marriage to Gomer (Hosea 1, 3) is biographical, metaphorical, or symbolic-vision is debated.
- **Date of Micah 4–7.** Chapters 4–5 are often dated later (post-exilic) than the 8th-c. core of chs. 1–3. Some defend more material as authentic Micah.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges

- **Amos 5:24 "let justice roll down like waters" = most-quoted OT verse in modern civil rights and liberation theology**: Amos's demand that justice replace ritual observance became the defining scriptural anchor of 20th-century political theology. Martin Luther King Jr. quoted it in the "I Have a Dream" speech (1963), at Selma (1965), and in his 1964 Nobel Prize lecture; it became the theological grammar of liberation theology's option for the poor. The journey of an 8th-century BCE shepherd's oracle to a 20th-century global social movement is the most consequential OT-to-modernity prophetic transmission (Heschel, A.J. 1962. *The Prophets*. Harper & Row. pp.27–38; Cone, J.H. 1970. *A Black Theology of Liberation*. Lippincott. pp.62–67; King, M.L. 1964. Nobel Peace Prize lecture, December 10, 1964).

- **Isaiah 6 *trisagion* = structural template for all Jewish and Christian liturgical mysticism**: Isaiah's throne vision — the Seraphim crying "Holy, Holy, Holy is the LORD of Hosts" — is the direct source-text for the Jewish *Kedushah* prayer (recited three times daily), the Christian *Sanctus* (every Eucharistic liturgy), Ezekiel's merkabah vision (Ezek 1), the Qumran Songs of the Sabbath Sacrifice, John's Revelation (Rev 4:8), and the entire Merkabah mystical tradition (*Hekhalot* literature). No other OT passage has generated a comparable liturgical-mystical tradition simultaneously in Judaism and Christianity (Gruenwald, I. 1980. *Apocalyptic and Merkavah Mysticism*. Brill. pp.29–50; Schäfer, P. 2009. *The Origins of Jewish Mysticism*. Mohr Siebeck. pp.74–87; Stuckenbruck, L. 1995. *Angel Veneration and Christology*. Mohr Siebeck. pp.56–71).

- **Micah 6:8 = most concentrated ethical monotheism formula in the Hebrew Bible, generating the prophetic reform tradition**: Three clauses — "do justice, love kindness, walk humbly with your God" — reduce the entire temple-and-sacrifice system to a moral-relational program. This formula became the core text of 19th-century Reform Judaism's prophetic-religion theology (Geiger, Holdheim), Abraham Joshua Heschel's theological anthropology, and the Social Gospel movement's scriptural foundation. It is cited more than any other Micah verse in modern theological and political writing (Heschel, A.J. 1962. pp.iv, 195; Andersen & Freedman 2000. AB. pp.524–537; Sandmel, S. 1978. *Judaism and Christian Beginnings*. Oxford UP. pp.143–148).

- **8th-century prophets' social-justice critique = oldest surviving sustained critique of economic exploitation in world religious literature**: Amos ("they sell the righteous for silver and the needy for a pair of sandals," 2:6; "you who trample the head of the poor into the dust," 2:7) and Isaiah ("adding house to house and field to field," 5:8) produce the oldest surviving sustained literary denunciation of elite economic predation in any religious tradition. The prophets' social critique precedes any comparable sustained literary tradition in Greece, China, or India by at least a century (Wilson, R.R. 1980. *Prophecy and Society in Ancient Israel*. Fortress. pp.270–299; Gottwald, N.K. 1979. *The Tribes of Yahweh*. Orbis. pp.341–357; Blenkinsopp, J. 1996. pp.78–94).
