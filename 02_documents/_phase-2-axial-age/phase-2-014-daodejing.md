# Daodejing (Tao Te Ching)

## What it is
A short Classical Chinese text (~5,000 characters in the received version, ~81 chapters arranged in two halves — *Dao* "Way" and *De* "Virtue"), composed in dense, gnomic, often paradoxical verse-prose. It is the foundational text of philosophical Daoism. Its central concept, *dao* (道, "way"), is the ineffable cosmic principle from which the myriad things arise; its central practical ethic is *wu-wei* (無為, "non-coercive action") and *ziran* (自然, "spontaneity"). The text criticizes Confucian moralism, ritual hierarchy, and active governance, offering instead a model of the sage who governs by yielding, valuing the soft over the hard, the empty over the full.

## Primary text — selected excerpts

The chapter divisions and most of the text below follow the received Wang Bi recension, with English in James Legge's 1891 *Sacred Books of the East* vol. XXXIX translation (public domain, sacred-texts.com). Legge is now superseded by Henricks (1989 Mawangdui; 2000 Guodian) and Ames & Hall (2003); where the Mawangdui or Guodian readings substantively change a chapter, that is noted. Legge's transliteration "Tâo" / "Teh" is preserved as in his text.

### Chapter 1 — the unnameable Dao

The opening chapter is the most quoted single passage in Chinese thought. The Dao is set beyond predication; naming itself is the move that brings the "myriad things" into appearance.

> The Tâo that can be trodden is not the enduring and unchanging Tâo. The name that can be named is not the enduring and unchanging name.
>
> (Conceived of as) having no name, it is the Originator of heaven and earth; (conceived of as) having a name, it is the Mother of all things.
>
> Always without desire we must be found,
> If its deep mystery we would sound;
> But if desire always within us be,
> Its outer fringe is all that we shall see.
>
> Under these two aspects, it is really the same; but as development takes place, it receives the different names. Together we call them the Mystery. Where the Mystery is the deepest is the gate of all that is subtle and wonderful.

Citation: DDJ ch. 1, trans. Legge 1891, *SBE* XXXIX; cf. Henricks 1989, pp. 188–189 (Mawangdui reading), and Ames & Hall 2003, p. 77.

### Chapter 8 — water as image of Dao

The cardinal Daoist image. Water is supreme because it benefits everything and *yields*: it seeks the low place, the place no one else wants. The valley, the female, the empty vessel — the receptive — are the Dao's emblems.

> The highest excellence is like (that of) water. The excellence of water appears in its benefiting all things, and in its occupying, without striving (to the contrary), the low place which all men dislike. Hence (its way) is near to (that of) the Tâo.
>
> The excellence of a residence is in (the suitability of) the place; that of the mind is in abysmal stillness; that of associations is in their being with the virtuous; that of government is in its securing good order; that of (the conduct of) affairs is in its ability; and that of (the initiation of) any movement is in its timeliness.
>
> And when (one with the highest excellence) does not wrangle (about his low position), no one finds fault with him.

Citation: DDJ ch. 8, trans. Legge 1891.

### Chapter 11 — the use of emptiness

The most elegant Daoist demonstration that *non-being* (the empty hub, the empty room, the empty pot) does the actual work. Substance is for support; emptiness is for use.

> The thirty spokes unite in the one nave; but it is on the empty space (for the axle), that the use of the wheel depends. Clay is fashioned into vessels; but it is on their empty hollowness, that their use depends. The door and windows are cut out (from the walls) to form an apartment; but it is on the empty space (within), that its use depends. Therefore, what has a (positive) existence serves for profitable adaptation, and what has not that for (actual) usefulness.

Citation: DDJ ch. 11, trans. Legge 1891.

### Chapter 25 — the Dao before heaven and earth

The DDJ's nearest approach to cosmogony. Something undifferentiated existed before Heaven and Earth; one *names* it "Dao" only by reluctant convention.

> There was something undefined and complete, coming into existence before Heaven and Earth. How still it was and formless, standing alone, and undergoing no change, reaching everywhere and in no danger (of being exhausted)! It may be regarded as the Mother of all things.
>
> I do not know its name, and I give it the designation of the Tâo (the Way or Course). Making an effort (further) to give it a name I call it The Great.
>
> Great, it passes on (in constant flow). Passing on, it becomes remote. Having become remote, it returns. Therefore the Tâo is great; Heaven is great; Earth is great; and the (sage) king is also great. In the universe there are four that are great, and the (sage) king is one of them.
>
> Man takes his law from the Earth; the Earth takes its law from Heaven; Heaven takes its law from the Tâo. The law of the Tâo is its being what it is.

Citation: DDJ ch. 25, trans. Legge 1891; cf. Ames & Hall 2003, p. 115 (their rendering of the final phrase as "*ziran*" / "spontaneously so" is the standard improvement).

### Chapter 48 — learning vs. the Dao; wu-wei

The classic *wu-wei* statement. The pursuit of learning is *additive*; the pursuit of the Dao is *subtractive* — one removes more and more, until non-action remains.

> He who devotes himself to learning (seeks) from day to day to increase (his knowledge); he who devotes himself to the Tâo (seeks) from day to day to diminish (his doing).
>
> He diminishes it and again diminishes it, till he arrives at doing nothing (on purpose). Having arrived at this point of non-action, there is nothing which he does not do.
>
> He who gets as his own all under heaven does so by giving himself no trouble (with that end). If one take trouble (with that end), he is not equal to getting as his own all under heaven.

Citation: DDJ ch. 48, trans. Legge 1891.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Warring States China (475–221 BCE). The Zhou royal house had effectively collapsed; semi-independent regional states fought protracted wars. The Hundred Schools (*zhuzi baijia*) — Confucians, Mohists, Legalists, Logicians, Yin-Yang school, and the proto-Daoists — competed to advise rulers on governance and life.
- **Religious milieu.** Old Zhou ritual religion of Heaven (*tian*) and ancestors was loosening; popular shamanic, mantic, and proto-religious-Daoist practices flourished in the southern Chu region. The DDJ's worldview shows southern / Chu features (water imagery, valley/mother imagery, primordial undifferentiated origin) more than the ritual-classicist northern Zhou heritage.
- **Who was behind it.** "Laozi" ("Old Master") is the traditional author. Sima Qian's *Shiji* (~-90 BCE) records three possibilities for Laozi's identity, indicating the historical record was already uncertain in early Han. Most current scholars treat the DDJ as a composite text, a school anthology or layered composition, with the historical "Laozi" likely an editorial composite or fiction. The Guodian and Mawangdui finds revolutionized the picture by showing the text was still fluid and partly assembling itself around -300 BCE.
- **Discovery / transmission.** The received Wang Bi text (Wang Bi's commentary ~250 CE) dominated the Chinese tradition for ~1,700 years. The Mawangdui silk manuscripts (excavated 1973 from a Han tomb in Changsha sealed in 168 BCE) and the Guodian bamboo slips (excavated 1993 from a tomb sealed ~-300 BCE) have transformed Daodejing studies — the Guodian version represents the earliest accessible layer.

## Themes and motifs
- **Dao (the Way)** — ineffable cosmic origin and principle. "The dao that can be spoken is not the eternal dao" (ch. 1).
- **Wu-wei (non-coercive action)** — the sage acts by not-acting; rulers govern by not-governing.
- **Ziran (spontaneity / self-so)** — things naturally cohere when left to their own way.
- [[primordial-waters]] — water as the supreme image of *dao*: "Highest good is like water; water benefits all things and does not contend" (ch. 8). The valley, the female, the empty vessel — feminine/receptive cosmic principles.
- **Returning** — *fan* / *gui*: the dao moves by reversal; things return to their origin.
- **Critique of Confucianism** — chs. 18, 19, 38: when the great dao is lost, *ren* (benevolence) and *yi* (righteousness) arise; the ritual order is a symptom of decay.
- [[divine-kingship]] — implicit: the sage-king governs by emptiness, the people barely know he exists (ch. 17).

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-2-015-analects-of-confucius]] — *polemic-against* / *shared-milieu*: the DDJ critiques the Confucian project of ritual cultivation and active virtue; both texts are products of the Warring States effort to articulate sage-governance.
- → [[phase-2-022-zhuangzi]] — *direct-influence*: Zhuangzi takes the DDJ's vocabulary (dao, ziran, wu-wei) and develops it through narrative and parable; the two are the joint canon of classical Daoism.
- → [[phase-2-026-han-feizi]] — *commentary-on*: the Han Feizi's *Jie Lao* (ch. 20) and *Yu Lao* (ch. 21) are the earliest extant DDJ commentaries, reading Laozi politically. This Daoist-Legalist hybridization becomes the substrate of Huang-Lao Daoism in early Han.
- → Chinese Buddhism (Chan / Zen, Phase 4–5) — *direct-influence*: Buddhist concepts (emptiness, non-attachment) found Chinese expression through Daoist vocabulary; the "translation" of Indian Buddhism into Chinese (~2nd–6th c. CE) used Daoist terms (*geyi*).
- → Plotinus / Neoplatonism — *parallel-motif*: the ineffable One from which the many emanate, the via negativa. No claim of contact; structural parallel.

## Disputes
- **Date and composition.** Traditional Chinese dating placed Laozi as a contemporary of Confucius (-6th c.) — the "old man" who instructed him. Most modern scholars accept that the *text* in its received form is later (Warring States, -4th to -3rd c.). The Guodian find (~-300 BCE) shows the text existed in partial form by then; the Mawangdui shows substantial coalescence by ~-200 BCE.
- **Authorship.** Whether any historical "Laozi" existed is doubted by most. The text is best treated as a school composition.
- **Order Dao→De vs. De→Dao.** Mawangdui has *De* (chs. 38–81) before *Dao* (chs. 1–37), reversing the received order. Some scholars regard this as the older arrangement.
- **Mystical vs. political reading.** Whether the DDJ is primarily a manual of personal cultivation/mysticism or a manual of statecraft for rulers is debated; both readings have long pedigree (Hanfeizi gave a Legalist political reading already in -3rd c.).
