# Early Buddhist Suttas — Sutta Nipāta (Aṭṭhakavagga and Pārāyanavagga)

## What it is
The Sutta Nipāta ("Group of Discourses") is one of the books of the Pali Khuddaka Nikāya, a verse and verse-prose collection. Within it, the **Aṭṭhakavagga** ("Group of Eights", book 4) and the **Pārāyanavagga** ("Way to the Far Shore", book 5) are by broad scholarly consensus the oldest surviving stratum of Buddhist scripture, on multiple grounds: archaic Middle Indo-Aryan linguistic features, archaic metrical forms, references in other Pali canon texts (the Aṭṭhakavagga is cited by name in other suttas), the existence of an ancient commentary on these books alone (the Niddesa), and presence in Gandhāran parallel manuscripts. They preserve teachings in a sparer, more dialogue-and-debate form than the later prose suttas, addressing brahmins, ascetics, and other interlocutors on the dangers of view-clinging, the path to *anupādiseesa* (no remaining clinging), and liberation from craving.

## Primary text — selected excerpts

The excerpts below use V. Fausböll's public-domain 1881 *Sacred Books of the East* vol. X part 2 translation of the Sutta Nipāta. Fausböll is now superseded by K. R. Norman's *Group of Discourses* (PTS, 1992) and Bhikkhu Bodhi's *Suttanipāta* (Wisdom, 2017), both of which incorporate massive improvements in Pali philology. For serious study consult Norman or Bodhi (and the parallel Sanskrit/Chinese versions at SuttaCentral). Verse numbering follows the standard PTS edition.

### Sn 4.1 (Kāma-sutta) — desire as the source of suffering

The opening sutta of the Aṭṭhakavagga states the basic Buddhist diagnosis in compressed form: one who pursues sensual desire and gains it is delighted; one who pursues and fails is pierced as by an arrow. Both outcomes leave the person bound.

> 766. If a man longs for pleasure and his wish is fulfilled, he, having obtained what mortals wish for, is glad in his mind.
>
> 767. But if those pleasures fail the man who longs for and desires them, he suffers as if pierced by an arrow.
>
> 768. He who avoids sensual passions, as (he would avoid treading upon) the head of a snake with his foot, such a one, being thoughtful (sato), will conquer this desire (visattikā) in the world.
>
> 769. The man who covets — fields, goods, or gold, cows and horses, servants, women, relations, many (sensual) pleasures — sins overpower, dangers crush him, then pain follows him as water (pours into) a broken ship.

Citation: Sutta Nipāta 4.1, verses 766–769, trans. V. Fausböll 1881, *SBE* X.2; cf. Norman 1992, pp. 96–97.

### Sn 4.5 (Paramaṭṭhaka) — the sage who does not cling to views

Among the most important passages of the Aṭṭhakavagga, and the one most pointed to by readers seeking a proto-Madhyamaka position: the genuine sage holds no views *as superior*, does not enter into disputes, has nothing to defend.

> 796. The man who clings to dogmas and views walks about in the world giving offence; let any one shun all dogmas, looking on this advantage; for what reason should the wise man (Brāhmaṇa) embrace what is called 'truth and supreme'?
>
> 797. They have abandoned all means, the body for performance of religious practices, the eye, the ear, the smell, the taste, the touch, all this, they say, is conducive to disputation.
>
> 798. (Such a man) does not by himself form any (philosophical) view either amongst the Sotthiyas (the learned) or amongst the common people; the Brāhmaṇa who wanders about as a free man does not embrace any of the views.

Citation: Sn 4.5 (Paramaṭṭhakasutta), verses 796–798, trans. Fausböll 1881; cf. Norman 1992, pp. 102–103. Bhikkhu Bodhi 2017 translates the title as "On the Supreme."

### Sn 4.9 (Māgandiya) — the dialogue on view-clinging with Māgandiya

The Māgandiya-sutta contains the Buddha's most direct statement that he does not claim any view as supreme, nor seek to establish one. The passage was extensively cited in the later Madhyamaka tradition.

> 837. Mâgandiya said: 'But if a man, after having decided, does not embrace (any of these) opinions, having grasped the words "the same" — what dost thou say is the highest good of the silent fool?'
>
> 838. 'Purity is not (to be obtained) by views, by learning, by knowledge, or by virtue and (holy) works,' — so Bhagavat (said), — 'nor is it (to be obtained) without views, without learning, without knowledge, without virtue and (holy) works; but having given up these, not holding to any of them, calm, independent, one should not desire existence.'
>
> 839. Mâgandiya said: 'If purity is not (to be obtained) by views... then it seems to me, this doctrine of yours is profitless... '
>
> 840. The Bhagavat: 'Whilst inquiring (about something) dependent on (philosophical) views, thou hast fallen into delusion in (respect of) the things grasped; and of this (sublime calm) thou hast not the smallest idea, therefore thou lookest upon it as profitless.'

Citation: Sn 4.9, verses 837–840, trans. Fausböll 1881; cf. Norman 1992, pp. 110–112.

### Sn 5 (Pārāyanavagga, closing) — the way to the far shore

The final verses of the Pārāyanavagga close with the Buddha's promise to Piṅgiya, the aged brahmin who has come a long way to him: the path he has been shown leads beyond death.

> 1136. 'O Pingiya' — so said Bhagavat — 'as people seeing Vakkali, Bhadrāvudha, and Āḷavi-Gotama, possessed of faith, have been delivered, so wilt thou also be delivered, Pingiya, thou wilt go to the further shore of death.'
>
> 1137. 'I am much more pleased having heard the Muni's word; the perfectly Enlightened (sambuddha) has, removing the cover, taught the Dhamma; without barrenness, ready-witted, knowing all worlds, the most excellent...'

Citation: Sn 5, verses 1136–1137 (closing of the Pārāyanavagga, Piṅgiya's final verses), trans. Fausböll 1881; cf. Norman 1992, pp. 167–168.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** The kingdoms of Magadha, Kosala, and other Gangetic-plain janapadas (~-500 BCE); urbanizing society of the Second Urbanization. Magadha is rising under Bimbisāra and Ajātaśatru. The Buddha is a contemporary of these kings; the Pārāyanavagga is set in dialogue with sixteen brahmins sent to question him.
- **Religious milieu.** A boiling intellectual climate of competing teachers (śramaṇas) — Buddha, Mahāvīra (Jain founder), the six "heterodox teachers" of the Pali canon (Ajita Kesakambalin, Pakudha Kaccāyana, etc.), brahmins of the Upaniṣadic stream. All share the karma/rebirth framework but differ on the path. Bronkhorst (2007) argues this "Greater Magadha" culture is partly non-Vedic in origin.
- **Who was behind it.** The Buddha himself (Siddhattha Gotama, ~-563 to -483 traditional, or by some current corrected chronology ~-480 to -400) and his immediate monastic community. The Aṭṭhakavagga and Pārāyanavagga show signs of early oral composition in dialogue form, suggesting close proximity to the Buddha's own teaching milieu.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Oral transmission through specialized reciter (*bhāṇaka*) lineages for ~400 years; first written down on palm leaves in Sri Lanka ~-29 BCE under Vaṭṭagāminī Abhaya. Gandhāran Buddhist Kharosthi-script manuscripts (~-100 to +200 CE) attest to the canonical material in northwestern India.

## Themes and motifs
- **Liberation from craving and clinging** — *taṇhā* (craving), *upādāna* (clinging), and *diṭṭhi* (views) as bondage; their cessation as freedom.
- **Critique of view-clinging** — the Aṭṭhakavagga repeatedly warns against grasping at metaphysical positions ("the sage does not enter into disputes", Suttanipāta 4.13–14). This proto-form is closer to the later Madhyamaka critique of views than to settled doctrinal Buddhism.
- **Renunciation (*nekkhamma*)** — the homeless ascetic life; release from household ties.
- **The Buddha as path-finder, not god** — the suttas treat the Buddha as one who discovered the path, not as a divine savior.
- **Four Noble Truths and dependent origination (in earlier seed form)** — articulated more systematically in later prose suttas; here present in proto-form.
- [[afterlife-judgment]] — karma and rebirth assumed (shared with the Upaniṣads and Jains); liberation is *exit* from the rebirth-cycle.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-2-012-brihadaranyaka-upanishad]] / [[phase-2-013-chandogya-upanishad]] — *shared-milieu* / *polemic-against*: the Buddha's *anattā* (no-self) doctrine answers the Upaniṣadic *ātman*; karma, rebirth, and liberation are shared categories with very different inflections. The Buddha addresses brahmins extensively in the Pārāyanavagga.
- → **Mahāvīra and the Jain Āgamas** — *shared-milieu* / *parallel-motif*: roughly contemporaneous reform movements within the śramaṇa world; rivalry and mutual critique. Both reject brahmin authority of the Veda.
- → **Greek philosophy (Pyrrho)** — *direct-influence (proposed)*: Pyrrho of Elis (in Alexander's army to India, ~-326) was reportedly influenced by Indian "gymnosophists"; Beckwith (2015) controversially argues Pyrrhonist skepticism derives from early Buddhist view-skepticism (Aṭṭhakavagga style). Disputed.
- → Later Buddhist literature (Pali Nikāyas, Mahāyāna sūtras) — *direct-influence*: all later Buddhist literature looks back to this stratum as authoritative.
- → Daoism — *parallel-motif*: contemporary East Asian critique of view-clinging and active striving (DDJ); cross-influence only later via Buddhist transmission to China.

## Disputes
- **Date of the Buddha.** Traditional Theravāda dating: ~-563 to -483. Current "corrected long chronology" (Bechert et al.): ~-480 to -400. Significant for cross-tradition dating; we carry the range.
- **Stratification of the Pali canon.** General consensus that the Aṭṭhakavagga and Pārāyanavagga are oldest (Jayawickrama, Norman, Gombrich, Bronkhorst). Debates over relative dating of other early material.
- **Buddhist-Greek contact.** Beckwith's Pyrrho thesis is controversial but cannot be dismissed; the Indo-Greek contact zone (Bactria, Gandhāra) is real.
- **What the Buddha "really taught".** Schmithausen, Gombrich, Wynne, and Bronkhorst have all attempted reconstructions, with varying degrees of confidence.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- ***Anattā* vs. *Ātman* — the most important philosophical debate in Asian thought:** the Buddha's "no-self" doctrine is a direct philosophical response to the Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad's "*Aham Brahmāsmi*"; where the Upaniṣad claims the permanent inner Self is identical with the cosmic ultimate, the early suttas systematically deny any such permanent self can be found. These two vault documents are in implicit direct dialogue — the most consequential doctrinal dispute in the history of Indian religion, generating two millennia of subsequent argument between Hindu Vedānta and Buddhist Abhidharma/Madhyamaka.
- **Aṭṭhakavagga view-skepticism → Pyrrho of Elis → Western skepticism:** Beckwith (2015) argues that Pyrrho of Elis, who accompanied Alexander's army to India (~-326), encountered Buddhist philosophers and that Pyrrhonist skepticism (suspension of judgment about all views) derives from the Aṭṭhakavagga-style non-clinging to views. If correct, this is the earliest dated direct Indian → Greek philosophical transmission in any text now in the vault. Disputed but not dismissible; the geographical and temporal circumstance is documented (Diogenes Laërtius, *Life of Pyrrho*).
- **Four Noble Truths as therapeutic diagnosis → Stoic cure-of-passions → Sufi psychology:** the First Sermon's structure (suffering → craving as cause → cessation as possibility → eightfold path as treatment) is a medical-diagnostic framework that parallels Stoic *therapeia* of the passions (Chrysippus, Cicero *Tusculan Disputations* III–IV) and later Sufi psychological manuals (al-Ghazālī, *Iḥyāʾ ʿUlūm al-Dīn*, book on the diseases of the heart). Three Axial-Age traditions use the same medical-diagnosis structure for spiritual soteriological purpose — without documented contact.
- **The Buddha and Mahāvīra as simultaneous reformers — the vault's densest Axial-Age religious-emergence cluster:** within one or two generations, the same Gangetic-plain region produced the Buddha (early Buddhism), Mahāvīra (Jain reform), and Yājñavalkya (Upaniṣadic philosophy). All three share karma/rebirth/liberation vocabulary but contest the soul-doctrine, the role of ritual, and the possibility of omniscience. No other region-and-period in the vault shows this density of simultaneous doctrinal innovation; Bronkhorst 2007 proposes "Greater Magadha" as the underlying cultural matrix.
