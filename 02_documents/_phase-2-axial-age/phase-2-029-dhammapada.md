# Dhammapada

## What it is
The Dhammapada is a 423-verse anthology of compressed Buddhist ethical and contemplative aphorisms organized into 26 thematic chapters (*Yamakavagga* "Pairs", *Appamādavagga* "Vigilance", *Cittavagga* "Mind", *Pupphavagga* "Flowers", *Bālavagga* "Fools", *Paṇḍitavagga* "Wise", etc.). The Pali Theravāda recension is canonical (Khuddaka Nikāya). Parallel recensions in Gāndhārī, Sanskrit (Patna Dharmapada; Udānavarga), Tibetan, and Chinese preserve overlapping but distinct verse-selections, demonstrating that the *Dharmapada genre* — verse-anthologies of the Buddha's wisdom-sayings — was a pan-sectarian early-Buddhist form. The text is the most translated, most cited, and most popularly received Buddhist text in modern world history; Max Müller's 1881 Sacred Books of the East translation was a foundational moment in Western Buddhist reception (Halbfass 1988).

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Verse-material likely composed during the Buddha's life and immediate aftermath (~-450 to -400); anthology-redaction during the Mauryan-and-after period (~-300 to -100). The text is broadly contemporaneous with the closing of the Hebrew prophetic canon, the late Upaniṣads, the *Bhagavad-gītā* core, the Mohist and Confucian textual formation in China, and the Pre-Socratic Greek philosophical revolution — the broader Axial-Age frame.
- **Religious milieu.** The wisdom-poetry genre (*subhāṣita*, "well-said") was pan-Indic; the Jain *Sūtrakṛtāṅga* contains structurally parallel verse-anthologies. The Dhammapada is the Buddhist contribution to this genre.
- **Who was behind it.** Anonymous redactors of the *bhāṇaka* reciter-tradition; the verses are attributed to the Buddha (and in some cases to disciples) but the anthology form is post-canonical-emergence. Parallel Gandhari and Sanskrit recensions exist because the *Dharmapada* form was claimed by multiple early schools (Theravāda, Dharmaguptaka, Sarvāstivāda) each producing their own selection.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Pali Dhammapada — continuous transmission via Sri Lankan *bhāṇaka* lineages, first writing-down at Aluvihāra ~-29 BCE. Gandhāran Dharmapada — discovered at Khotan, Xinjiang, in 1892 by Dutreuil de Rhins; critical edition by Brough 1962. Sanskrit Patna Dharmapada — Tibet-recovered birch-bark fragments. Udānavarga — Sanskrit-Sarvāstivāda anthology heavily overlapping with the Dhammapada, the basis of the Tibetan and one of the Chinese recensions.

## Primary text — selected verses

The excerpts below use F. Max Müller's public-domain 1881 *Sacred Books of the East* X.1 translation. Müller is now superseded by Norman 1997 (PTS, critical), Fronsdal 2005 (the standard contemporary English version), and Thanissaro 1997 (Access to Insight, open-access). Verse numbers follow the standard PTS edition.

### Yamaka-vagga (Pairs) 1-2

> 1. All that we are is the result of what we have thought: it is founded on our thoughts, it is made up of our thoughts. If a man speaks or acts with an evil thought, pain follows him, as the wheel follows the foot of the ox that draws the carriage.
>
> 2. All that we are is the result of what we have thought: it is founded on our thoughts, it is made up of our thoughts. If a man speaks or acts with a pure thought, happiness follows him, like a shadow that never leaves him.

Citation: Dhammapada 1-2, trans. Müller 1881 SBE X.1. Fronsdal 2005 renders the opening word *manopubbaṅgamā dhammā* more literally: "All experience is preceded by mind."

### Cittavagga (Mind) 35-36

> 35. As rain breaks through an ill-thatched house, passion will break through an unreflecting mind.
>
> 36. As rain does not break through a well-thatched house, passion will not break through a well-reflecting mind.

### Pupphavagga (Flowers) 50

> 50. Not the perversities of others, not their sins of commission or omission, but his own misdeeds and negligences should a sage take notice of.

### Bālavagga (Fools) 60-64 — the dangers of the fool's company

> 60. Long is the night to him who is awake; long is a mile to him who is tired; long is life to the foolish who do not know the true law.

### Paṇḍitavagga (Wise) 85

> 85. Few are there among men who arrive at the other shore (become arahats); the other people here run up and down the shore.

### Appamādavagga (Vigilance) 21 — the foundational verse

> 21. Earnestness is the path of immortality (Nirvāṇa), thoughtlessness the path of death. Those who are in earnest do not die, those who are thoughtless are as if dead already.

### The Dhammapada's most-quoted verse — verse 5

> 5. For hatred does not cease by hatred at any time: hatred ceases by love, this is an old rule.

Citation: Dhp 5, Müller 1881. This single verse — *na hi verena verāni sammantīdha kudācanaṃ; averena ca sammanti esa dhammo sanantano* — became the most quoted Buddhist verse in modern Western reception; quoted by Martin Luther King Jr., the Dalai Lama in international addresses, the Buddhist response to the Sri Lankan ethnic conflict, and many other contexts.

## Themes and motifs
- [[four-noble-truths]] — implicit framing throughout; the diagnostic structure (suffering / cause / cessation / path) underlies the verses' moral and meditative directives.
- [[noble-eightfold-path]] — referenced in multiple chapters; *sammā-vāyāma* (right effort) is the Appamādavagga's theme.
- [[anatman-no-self]] — the "all that we are is the result of what we have thought" framing presupposes the no-self analysis of personality as a constructed sequence of mental events.
- **Karma and rebirth** — the consequence-pattern (verses 1-2, 71-72) operates inside the standard śramaṇa karma framework.
- **The arahant as goal** — verses 85, 90-99 articulate the arahant figure; foreshadowed but not elaborated into the later bodhisattva-vow ([[bodhisattva-vow]]).

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-2-016-early-buddhist-suttas]] — *shared-milieu* / *parallel-form*: the Sutta-Nipāta's Aṭṭhakavagga + Pārāyanavagga and the Dhammapada are both early Pali verse anthologies of the same broad period; the Aṭṭhakavagga is older but the Dhammapada is more compressed and aphoristic.
- → **Jain *Sūtrakṛtāṅga* + *Uttarādhyayana*** — *parallel-form*: structurally parallel śramaṇa-wisdom verse anthologies of the same period. Both reject Vedic-brahmanical religion and codify ascetic-meditative ethics in compressed verse.
- → [[phase-2-014-daodejing]] — *parallel-motif*: roughly contemporaneous Chinese compressed-wisdom genre; no documented contact in this period; the structural parallel (short verse-aphorisms on practical-spiritual discipline) is a common feature of Axial-Age wisdom literature.
- → [[phase-2-015-analects-of-confucius]] — *parallel-motif*: same Axial-Age compressed-wisdom genre; Confucian.
- → **Gandhāran Dharmapada (Khotan, 1st-2nd c. CE)** — *parallel-form*: the Dharmaguptaka recension; demonstrates the *Dharmapada* genre's pan-sectarian status. The Khotan manuscript is among the oldest extant Buddhist physical mss.
- → **Udānavarga (Sanskrit Sarvāstivāda)** — *parallel-form*: the northern Buddhist recension; heavily overlapping verse-stock; basis of the Tibetan version.
- → **Chinese *Faju jing* (T 210)** — *redaction-of*: the 224 CE Chinese translation by Vighna; one of the earliest Chinese Buddhist translations in the canon.

## Disputes
- **Compositional date**: verse-material may go back to the Buddha himself; the anthology-form is later. Norman 1997 places the redaction at ~-300 to -100.
- **Recension priority**: which recension is closest to the original? Brough 1962 argued the Gandhari Dharmapada preserves features as old as the Pali; the modern consensus is that there is no single "original" — multiple parallel recensions arose from a shared verse-stock with school-specific anthology-decisions.
- **Western reception bias**: Müller 1881 and the early Theosophical-period Western reception emphasized the Dhammapada as "the Buddhist Bible," a framing that has been critiqued (Almond 1988, Lopez 1995) as projecting Christian-canonical models onto Buddhism. The actual Theravāda canonical hierarchy places the Dhammapada in the Khuddaka Nikāya — important but not "the Buddhist Bible."
