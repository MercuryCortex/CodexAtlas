# Yi Jing (易經) / I Ching

## What it is
The *Yi Jing* (易經, 'Classic of Changes') is China's foundational divination and cosmological text — the oldest of the Five Confucian Classics and the only major canonical work claimed simultaneously by Confucianism, Daoism, and Chinese popular religion as their cosmological base. The received text has two distinct historical layers that must be kept apart: (1) the *Zhou Yi* (周易, 'Changes of Zhou'), a divination manual from the early Western Zhou period (~-1050 to -800 BCE), consisting of 64 hexagrams with brief oracle-judgments and line-statements; and (2) the *Ten Wings* (十翼, *Shi Yi*), ten philosophical appendices dating from the Warring States through early Han period (~-300 to -100 BCE), which transform the divination manual into a systematic metaphysical-cosmological treatise. The Western Zhou layer is one of the earliest continuous Chinese texts. The Ten Wings layer is among the most philosophically ambitious works of the Classical period.

## Context

**Political and religious milieu.** The Zhou Yi was composed in the transition from Shang-dynasty oracle-bone divination (pyromancy and scapulimancy — cracks in heated bones interpreted as divine messages) to milfoil-stalk divination (counting yarrow stalks to generate hexagrams), during the early Western Zhou dynasty. The Zhou conquest of Shang (~-1046 BCE) is the political backdrop: the Zhou justified their conquest partly through cosmological claims about Heaven's (Tian) mandate transferring, and the Yi Jing encodes that cosmological framework. The Ten Wings were composed during the Hundred Schools era (Warring States) and early Han, when Confucian scholars were systematizing, philosophizing, and politically promoting the Zhou classical heritage.

**Who was behind it.** The Zhou Yi's hexagram judgments are traditionally attributed to King Wen of Zhou (Wenwang, ~-1099 to -1050) — legendarily composed during his imprisonment by the Shang king. The line-statements are attributed to his son the Duke of Zhou (Zhougong), the great Zhou regent. Modern scholarship treats these attributions as founding myth; the texts are composite, accumulated over the Western Zhou period. The Ten Wings are traditionally attributed to Confucius, who is said to have studied the Yi Jing so intensely that he wore through three sets of leather binding-cords. Modern scholarship dates the Ten Wings to Warring States–Han Confucian scholars. Confucius's own relationship to the Yi Jing is contested: the *Analects* mentions it only once (7.16), obliquely. The Han-dynasty discovery that Confucius had a hand in the Yi Jing was a major legitimating move for the Confucian school.

**Canonization.** Under Emperor Wu of Han (~-136 BCE), the Yi Jing was placed first among the Five Classics (五經: Yi, Shujing, Shijing, Liji, Chunqiu). Being first in the canon — the "root of all classics" (*jing zhi yuan*) — gave it a unique status as the metaphysical foundation of the entire Confucian textual tradition. Wang Bi's (~226-249 CE) philosophical commentary stripped the Han numerological-cosmological elaborations from the text and read the Yi Jing as a philosophical work about the structure of change; his reading shaped all subsequent Confucian and Neo-Confucian engagement.

## The hexagram system
The Yi Jing's core technical apparatus is the hexagram system: 8 trigrams (八卦, *bagua*) × 8 trigrams = 64 hexagrams (六十四卦). Each hexagram consists of 6 stacked lines, each either broken (- -) representing yin, or unbroken (—) representing yang. The 64 hexagrams encode the complete set of possible yin-yang combinations across six registers — a binary combinatorial system covering 2⁶ = 64 states. The 8 trigrams are:
- ☰ Qian (Heaven, creative, yang)
- ☷ Kun (Earth, receptive, yin)
- ☳ Zhen (Thunder, arousing)
- ☴ Xun (Wind/Wood, gentle)
- ☵ Kan (Water, abyssal)
- ☶ Gen (Mountain, still)
- ☲ Li (Fire, clinging)
- ☱ Dui (Lake, joyous)

Each hexagram has a name, a *gua ci* (judgment-text on the whole hexagram), and six *yao ci* (line-statements, one per line, which become operative when a changing line is generated during divination). The divination process — historically by counting yarrow stalks (50 stalks, one set aside, 49 divided repeatedly into groups) — generates a hexagram with or without changing lines; changing lines transform the primary hexagram into a second hexagram, giving the reading its dynamic: not just *where you are* but *where you are moving*.

The Great Commentary (*Xi Ci Zhuan*, one of the Ten Wings) provides the philosophical interpretation of this system: the hexagrams do not merely describe situations, they *are* the situations. The 64 hexagrams map the complete range of cosmic situations because yin and yang — the two fundamental forces — exhaust the combinatorial space of existence.

## Themes and motifs
- [[yin-yang]] — The hexagram system is the foundational systematic expression of yin-yang complementary duality in Chinese thought. Every line is either yin or yang; every hexagram is a configuration of that polarity. The Yi Jing is the cosmological text that gives yin-yang its systematic form.
- [[mixture-cosmology]] — The Yi Jing's cosmology is dynamic: yin and yang are not static opposites but constantly interpenetrating, transforming into each other. Yang at its extreme generates yin; yin at its extreme generates yang. This is the universe as perpetual transformation (*yi*, change).
- [[non-duality]] — The Ten Wings' Great Commentary frames the yin-yang polarity non-dualistically: the two are not enemies but the rhythm of one process. "One yin and one yang: that is the Dao" (*Xi Ci Zhuan* I.5).
- [[divine-immanence]] — Heaven (Tian, [[tian-heaven]]) is present in the Yi Jing not as a transcendent God but as an ordering principle immanent in all change. The sage who understands the hexagrams aligns with Tian's patterns.

## Neo-Confucian reception: cosmological backbone
[[zhu-xi]] (~1130-1200) placed the Yi Jing at the center of Neo-Confucian metaphysics and wrote major commentaries (*Zhouyi benyi* and *Yi xue qimeng*). More important, the cosmological diagram that became the visual center of Neo-Confucian teaching — [[zhou-dunyi]]'s *Taijitu shuo* (Diagram of the Supreme Ultimate, ~1060 CE) — develops directly from Yi Jing cosmology. The Taiji (supreme ultimate), which generates yin-yang, which generates the Five Phases, which generates the myriad things: this is the Yi Jing's *Xi Ci Zhuan* cosmogony made into a pedagogical diagram. The [[yin-yang]] symbol itself (the *taijitu* / "fish" diagram) is the visual representation of the Yi Jing's foundational insight. Zhu Xi's *li-qi* (principle-matter) framework — the backbone of Song-Ming Neo-Confucian metaphysics — is a philosophical elaboration of the yin-yang dynamic the Yi Jing articulates. Every educated person in East Asia from the Song dynasty forward encountered the Yi Jing as the cosmological bedrock underneath ethics, politics, medicine, and self-cultivation.

## MASSIVE WIN 1 — Leibniz and binary arithmetic
Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (~1646-1716) independently developed binary arithmetic — a positional notation system using only 0 and 1 — around 1679, decades before publishing it. He was also in correspondence with Jesuit missionaries in China, including Joachim Bouvet (1656-1730), who had lived at the Kangxi Emperor's court. In 1701 Bouvet sent Leibniz a diagram of the 64 hexagrams arranged in the order that corresponds to binary counting (0-63). Leibniz immediately recognized the correspondence: the broken line (yin) = 0, the unbroken line (yang) = 1; the 64 hexagrams in Bouvet's arrangement were his binary numbers 0-63, discovered in Zhou-dynasty China more than two thousand years earlier. He published this connection in "Explication de l'Arithmétique Binaire" (1703), explicitly stating that the ancient Chinese had anticipated his system. This is one of the vault's most precisely documented cases of parallel independent discovery across isolated traditions: China ~-1050 BCE / Europe 1679 CE, with contemporary correspondence (1701) confirming the connection rather than projection. Leibniz interpreted the hexagram-binary correspondence as evidence of a universal mathesis underlying all traditions — an early form of the perennial philosophy hypothesis, but grounded in mathematical structure rather than mystical experience.

No Leibniz person node exists in the vault yet; this is a gap. The connection is documented: his published 1703 paper names both the hexagrams and binary arithmetic explicitly.

## MASSIVE WIN 2 — Wilhelm, Jung, and Western reception
Richard Wilhelm (1873-1930) was a German Protestant missionary who arrived in Qingdao, China in 1899 and spent 25 years there without ever making a Chinese convert — instead being converted by Chinese culture. He learned classical Chinese and the Yi Jing from Lao Nai-hsuan, a Confucian scholar, and produced the German translation *I Ging: Das Buch der Wandlungen* (1924). Cary Baynes translated Wilhelm's German into English for the 1950 Princeton / Bollingen edition.

[[carl-gustav-jung]] wrote a lengthy preface to the 1950 English edition — one of the most consequential texts in 20th-century East→West religious transmission. In it Jung developed his theory of SYNCHRONICITY: the a-causal connecting principle. His argument was that the Yi Jing works not through causal mechanism but through synchronicity — the meaningful coincidence of an internal psychological state with an external event. The hexagram you cast *at this moment* is connected to your situation not causally but synchronistically. Jung's Yi Jing preface brought the concept of synchronicity before a mass Western audience and offered a philosophical framework for divination that neither dismissed it as superstition nor required supernatural causation. It became one of the foundational texts of the human potential movement and the counterculture's engagement with Asian wisdom.

The Wilhelm-Jung Yi Jing shaped: Herman Hesse's *The Glass Bead Game* (he was a close friend of Wilhelm and directly engaged the Yi Jing), Philip K. Dick's novels (he used the Yi Jing compositionally and thematically), John Cage's *Music of Changes* (1951) — composed by using the Yi Jing's chance-operation procedures to select pitches, durations, and dynamics, one of the foundational works of indeterminacy in Western art music. The 1960s-70s counterculture made the Yi Jing (via the Wilhelm-Jung edition) perhaps the most widely consulted Asian text in the West outside of the Daodejing.

## MASSIVE WIN 3 — yin-yang binary logic across traditions
The Yi Jing's structuring of reality as a dynamic interplay of two complementary forces (yin-yang) is the most systematically developed version of a pattern that recurs independently across traditions:
- **Zoroastrianism**: [[ahura-mazda]] / [[angra-mainyu-ahriman]] — cosmic light vs. darkness, a moral binary (independent of Yi Jing, but structurally similar; both ~-1000 BCE)
- **Manichaeism**: Light-realm vs. Matter-realm — binary cosmic dualism (3rd c. CE, independent development from Zoroastrian and Gnostic sources)
- **Hermetic tradition**: masculine/feminine polarity (Emerald Tablet: "that which is above is like that which is below") — alchemical complementarity (2nd c. CE, Mediterranean)
- **Kabbalistic tradition**: the Sefirot's right-column (masculine-active) vs. left-column (feminine-receptive) polarity (12th c. CE, independent development)
- **Tantric/Shaiva cosmology**: Shiva (pure consciousness) / Shakti (dynamic power) — the supreme complementary polarity (independent, India)

The Yi Jing is not the source of these parallels; all are independent developments. But the Yi Jing is the most systematized pre-modern binary cosmological framework: 64 states exhausting the combinatorial space of complementary duality. The vault's cross-tradition binary-complementarity cluster roots here.

## Disputes
- **Authorship of the Ten Wings.** The traditional attribution to Confucius was accepted through most of Chinese history and was legitimating for the Confucian school. Modern scholarship (from Qing evidential scholars onward, confirmed by Mawangdui evidence) dates the Ten Wings to multiple Warring States–Han authors. The Mawangdui Yi Jing includes some but not all Ten Wings material, confirming their gradual accretion.
- **Original function: divination or philosophy?** The Zhou Yi is clearly a divination manual; the Ten Wings make it into philosophy. The two layers represent genuinely different intellectual enterprises. Sinologist debates persist about whether the Zhou Yi text originally functioned as divination texts at all, or whether it was a philosophical text from the start (minority view; the majority reading is that the hexagram-and-judgment structure is unambiguously divinatory).
- **Leibniz and priority.** Leibniz's claim that the hexagrams confirmed his binary arithmetic was genuine but also historically complex: he needed priority to be clear, and his framing of the Chinese as "already knowing" his system was partly a legitimating move for his own invention. The correspondence with Bouvet (1701) is primary evidence; Leibniz's published account (1703) is the public statement.
- **Jung's Synchronicity and scientific status.** Jung's synchronicity concept — the a-causal connecting principle — was controversial in his own time (Pauli, with whom he co-authored *Synchronicity: An Acausal Connecting Principle* [1952], was a physicist, not a mystic) and remains outside mainstream psychology. Its importance for the vault is not as scientific theory but as a documented major East→West transmission that shaped Western reception of Chinese thought for the second half of the 20th century.
