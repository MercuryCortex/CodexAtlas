# Ācārāṅga Sūtra — First Āṅga

The *Ācārāṅga Sūtra* (Sanskrit) / *Āyāraṃga Sutta* (Ardhamāgadhī) is the first and oldest scripture of the Śvetāmbara Jain canon and the oldest surviving Jain text. It is divided into two books:

**Book I (Śrutaskandha I)** — Mahāvīra's ascetic wanderings. Nine lectures (*adhyāyas*) describing the twelve years of Mahāvīra's extreme asceticism — walking barefoot through thorns and hostile villages, enduring insults and attacks without retaliation, going without food for long stretches, pulling out his own hair (instead of shaving) as an act of renunciation. The text documents *ahiṃsā* as lived practice: care not to crush insects while walking, not to touch plants unnecessarily, not to disturb water or fire. The prose is terse and ritual in character — one of the oldest personal accounts of ascetic experience in world literature.

**Book II (Śrutaskandha II)** — Monastic code. Regulations for monks and nuns: rules for begging food, for the use of material objects, for speech, for the monk's relationship to the lay community. This book is accepted as later in composition (Jacobi dated it to ~2nd-3rd c. CE in its surviving form).

## Significance
The *Ācārāṅga* provides the earliest external evidence (alongside Buddhist Pali sources) for the historical Mahāvīra: the ascetic biography in Book I is plausibly close to oral accounts from his immediate disciples. It also represents the first systematic statement of radical *ahiṃsā* (non-violence) as a cosmological-soteriological principle, not merely an ethical rule. Every act of violence is an act of *karmic bondage* — adding subtle matter to the soul — and liberation is literally impossible without complete non-harm.

## Cross-tradition comparisons
The oldest stratum of the Ācārāṅga Sūtra is roughly contemporary with the Aṭṭhakavagga layer of the Buddhist Sutta Nipāta — both are linguistic/metrical archaisms from the śramaṇa milieu of the eastern Gangetic plain in the 5th-4th c. BCE, preserving the oldest surviving voice of the wandering renouncer tradition. The Buddhist-Jain shared milieu is visible here in the vocabulary, the ascetic practice types, and the polemic against Vedic ritual killing (both traditions oppose Brahmin animal sacrifice as violence).
