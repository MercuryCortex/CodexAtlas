# Books of Kings (1 Kings & 2 Kings)

## What it is
The Books of Kings are the concluding volumes of the **Deuteronomistic History** (Deuteronomy through 2 Kings), a theological-historical narrative compiled and redacted in Judah circa 640–560 BCE, with a final exile-period edition (~560 BCE). The two books cover:

- **1 Kings**: Solomon's construction of the Jerusalem Temple; his wisdom and idolatry; the division of the kingdom after his death (~930 BCE); the northern kingdom of Israel and its kings; the prophet **Elijah** and his confrontation with Baal worship under Ahab and Jezebel.
- **2 Kings**: **Elisha** and his miracles; the fall of the northern kingdom (Israel) to Assyria in **722 BCE**; the reforms of Hezekiah and Josiah in Judah; the fall of Jerusalem to Babylon in **586 BCE** and the beginning of the Babylonian exile.

## Primary text — selected passages

### 1 Kings 18:21–39 — Elijah vs. the prophets of Baal on Mount Carmel
*Trans. New Revised Standard Version (NRSV). National Council of Churches, 1989.*
> Elijah came near to all the people, and said, "How long will you go limping with two different opinions? If the LORD is God, follow him; but if Baal, then follow him." The people did not answer him a word... Then the fire of the LORD fell and consumed the burnt offering, the wood, the stones, and the dust, and even licked up the water that was in the trench. When all the people saw it, they fell on their faces and said, "The LORD indeed is God; the LORD indeed is God."

*The Mount Carmel contest (1 Kings 18) is the definitive narrative of Yahwistic monotheism against Canaanite polytheism. Elijah's challenge — "How long will you go limping with two different opinions?" — is the most dramatic statement of exclusive monotheism in the narrative books, and the fire-from-heaven theophany its vindication. The episode structures the entire subsequent prophetic tradition's theology of divine exclusivity and is the primary narrative template for confrontation between prophet and popular apostasy.*

*Citations: NRSV (1989); Cross, F.M. (1973). Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic. Harvard UP; Brettler, M.Z. (1991). The Book of Kings. JPS.*

### 1 Kings 3:5–9 — Solomon's prayer for wisdom at Gibeon
*Trans. NRSV 1989.*
> At Gibeon the LORD appeared to Solomon in a dream by night; and God said, "Ask what I should give you." And Solomon said... "Give your servant therefore an understanding mind to govern your people, able to discern between good and evil; for who can govern this your great people?"

*Solomon's Gibeon dream and prayer for a "listening heart" (*lev shomea* in Hebrew) is the foundational biblical narrative of wisdom as the supreme royal virtue — superior to wealth, military power, or long life. It is the root text for the Hebrew wisdom tradition (Proverbs, Qohelet, Wisdom of Solomon) and offers a parallel to Platonic philosopher-kings: the ideal ruler is distinguished by cognitive virtue, not political power. This passage generates the entire Jewish-Christian theology of divinely-gifted wisdom.*

*Citations: NRSV (1989); Brettler, M.Z. (1991). The Book of Kings. JPS.*

### 2 Kings 2:9–12 — Elijah's ascent in the chariot of fire
*Trans. NRSV 1989.*
> When they had crossed, Elijah said to Elisha, "Tell me what I may do for you, before I am taken from you." Elisha said, "Please let me inherit a double share of your spirit." ... As they continued walking and talking, a chariot of fire and horses of fire separated the two of them, and Elijah ascended in a whirlwind into heaven.

*Elijah's ascent in the chariot of fire is the most theologically explosive narrative in the Books of Kings: a prophet does not die but is translated directly into Heaven — the only such figure in the Hebrew Bible aside from Enoch. This generates: (1) the Malachi 4:5 prediction of Elijah's return as precursor to the Day of YHWH; (2) the New Testament identification of John the Baptist as Elijah returned (Matt 11:14; 17:10–12); (3) the Elijah-chair at the Passover Seder; (4) the entire category of the "returning precursor" in Jewish-Christian messianic expectation.*

*Citations: NRSV (1989); Cross, F.M. (1973). Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic. Harvard UP; Noth, M. (1981). The Deuteronomistic History. JSOT Press.*

## Context
The Deuteronomistic editors (the "Dtr" school) evaluated each king by a single criterion: faithfulness to Yahweh alone and the rejection of foreign cult. Kings who "did evil in the eyes of the LORD" are contrasted with reformers like Hezekiah and Josiah. This framework was designed to explain how God's chosen people could lose their land — the answer: covenant violation. The history draws on earlier royal annals (explicitly cited but lost) and prophetic source collections.

## Themes and motifs
- **Temple theology**: 1 Kings 5–8 narrates Solomon's construction of the Temple — the primary site for the localization of Yahweh's presence and the theological center of Judahite religion.
- **Prophet vs. king**: [[prophet-and-king]] — the prophetic tradition (Elijah, Elisha, Isaiah) serves as a check on royal power; the prophets speak for Yahweh when kings fail.
- **Elijah typology**: Elijah's ascent in a chariot of fire (2 Kings 2) and his expected return (Malachi 4:5) become foundational for later messianic expectation.
- **Covenant failure**: The double fall (722 BCE and 586 BCE) is the Deuteronomistic theological answer to Israelite/Judahite history.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges

- **Elijah typology → John the Baptist = most documented single OT-to-NT typological identification in the Synoptic Gospels, structuring the entire Synoptic messianic framework**: The identification of John the Baptist with the returning Elijah predicted in Malachi 4:5 is the most explicitly stated OT typological fulfillment in the Synoptic tradition (Matt 11:14; 17:10–12; Mark 9:11–13; Luke 1:17). Jesus's ministry begins only after the "Elijah" figure has prepared the way — the Baptist's desert location, diet, and prophetic style are all coded as Elijanic. The entire Synoptic narrative structure of "precursor prepares for the coming one" rests on 2 Kings 2 and Malachi 4; without the Elijah narrative in Kings, the Synoptic biography of Jesus has no structural template (Fitzmyer, J.A. 1981. *The Gospel According to Luke I–IX*. Anchor Bible 28. Doubleday. pp.313–336; Davies, W.D. and Allison, D.C. 1988. *Matthew 1–7*. ICC. T&T Clark. pp.311–330; Faierstein, M.M. 1981. "Why Do the Scribes Say that Elijah Must Come First?" *JBL* 100/1. pp.75–86).

- **1 Kings 8 Solomon Temple dedication prayer = foundational document of all Jewish, Christian, and Islamic sacred-architecture theology**: Solomon's prayer at the Temple dedication (1 Kings 8:27–30) asks: "But will God indeed dwell on the earth? Even heaven and the highest heaven cannot contain you, much less this house that I have built!" This is the oldest surviving articulation of the theological paradox of divine presence in a built sanctuary — transcendence and immanence in tension. It generates: the Jewish *Shekhinah* theology of divine presence; the Christian theology of church as locus of divine indwelling; the Islamic theology of the mosque as sacred space. Every subsequent Jewish-Christian-Islamic theology of sacred architecture negotiates this verse's paradox (Brettler, M.Z. 1991. *The Book of Kings*. JPS. pp.47–62; Levenson, J.D. 1984. "The Temple and the World." *Journal of Religion* 64/3. pp.275–298; Hayward, C.T.R. 1996. *The Jewish Temple*. Routledge. pp.1–30).

- **1 Kings 10 Queen of Sheba narrative = most consequential single biblical episode for African Christian identity, generating the entire Ethiopian royal ideology**: The Queen of Sheba's visit to Solomon (1 Kings 10:1–13) is the primary biblical anchor for the *Kebra Nagast* ("Glory of Kings," c. 13th-14th c. CE) — the foundational text of Ethiopian Christian royal ideology, which identifies the Queen as Makeda of Ethiopia and her son by Solomon as Menelik I, founder of the Solomonic dynasty. The Solomonic dynasty claimed unbroken descent from this encounter and ruled Ethiopia from 1270 to 1974 CE. The episode also generates the Ethiopian claim to possess the Ark of the Covenant (brought to Aksum by Menelik). No other single biblical narrative has had more concentrated political-theological impact on a single civilization (Ullendorff, E. 1968. *Ethiopia and the Bible*. Oxford UP. pp.131–145; Budge, E.A.W. 1922. *The Queen of Sheba and Her Only Son Menyelek (Kebra Nagast)*. Oxford UP; Cross, F.M. 1973. *Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic*. Harvard UP. pp.1–20).

- **Elijah-Elisha miracle cycles (1 Kings 17–2 Kings 13) = primary narrative source for Gospel miracle typology, establishing the template for prophetic wonder-working in Jewish-Christian tradition**: The Elijah-Elisha cycles contain the closest pre-Gospel parallels to the Gospel miracle narratives: multiplying food for the hungry (1 Kings 17:8–16 / Mark 6:30–44 feeding of the 5,000); raising the dead (1 Kings 17:17–24; 2 Kings 4:18–37 / Mark 5:35–43 raising of Jairus's daughter); healing of a "foreigner" (2 Kings 5:1–14 Naaman / Luke 4:27); and the "double portion of spirit" transferred to a successor (2 Kings 2:9 / Acts 2 Pentecost). Luke 4:25–27 explicitly invokes Elijah and Elisha as precedents for Jesus's ministry. The Elijah-Elisha complex is the narrative DNA of the Synoptic miracle tradition (Cross, F.M. 1973. *Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic*. Harvard UP. pp.223–265; Brodie, T.L. 1983. "Luke 7:36–50 as an Internalization of 2 Kings 4:1–37." *Biblica* 64/4. pp.457–485; Fitzmyer, J.A. 1981. pp.526–537).
