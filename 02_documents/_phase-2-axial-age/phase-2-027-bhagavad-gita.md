# Bhagavad Gītā

## What it is
The 700-verse philosophical-devotional poem embedded in Mahābhārata Book 6 (*Bhīṣmaparvan*, chs. 23-40 in the BORI critical edition), composed ~-200 BCE to +200 CE. It is a dialogue between the warrior Arjuna — paralyzed on the verge of battle by the prospect of killing his kinsmen — and his charioteer Krishna, who progressively reveals himself as the cosmic Lord. Over eighteen chapters Krishna teaches Arjuna a synthesizing path of *yoga* (discipline) that integrates: *karma-yoga* (the path of action performed without attachment to fruits), *jñāna-yoga* (the path of liberating knowledge of the *ātman*-Brahman relation), and *bhakti-yoga* (the path of loving devotion to the personal Lord). Embedded in the dialogue are Krishna's self-disclosure as the avatāra of Viṣṇu, the cosmic theophany of Chapter 11 (Krishna's universal form, the most cosmologically grandiose passage in Hindu scripture), and a systematic doctrine of the *guṇas* (the three cosmic qualities: *sattva*, *rajas*, *tamas*) and their interaction with the indestructible *ātman*. The Gītā is the single most-cited, most-translated, and most-commented-upon Hindu text in the West post-1785 (when Charles Wilkins published the first English translation), and the textual foundation of two and a half millennia of Hindu philosophical and devotional reflection.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Composition spans the late Mauryan period and the post-Mauryan north Indian states, in the broader Mahābhārata compositional window. The narrative setting is the imminent battle of Kurukṣetra. Both the narrative crisis (the propriety of war among kinsmen) and the philosophical solution (action performed in disinterested cosmic-duty service) reflect the political-ethical preoccupations of the era.
- **Religious milieu.** A consolidating Brāhmaṇical Hinduism in dialogue and competition with Buddhism and Jainism. The Gītā harmonizes three previously distinct soteriological streams — Vedic ritual-action (*karma*), Upaniṣadic gnostic-knowledge (*jñāna*), and emerging Vaiṣṇava devotion (*bhakti*) — into a single integrative scripture. The text is a synthetic theological masterstroke: by presenting the Lord himself as endorsing all three paths, it provides a unifying Brāhmaṇical-Vaiṣṇava framework that subsequent Hindu traditions could each draw from differently.
- **Who was behind it.** Anonymous Brāhmaṇical theologians of the Vaiṣṇava devotional milieu. The text's stylistic, doctrinal, and metrical features are not uniform — some scholars have argued for multiple compositional layers within the Gītā itself (Sharma 1986); others maintain substantial unity (van Buitenen 1981). Traditional attribution to Vyāsa (the Mahābhārata's compositor) is theologically meaningful and historically untenable.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous Indian transmission as both an embedded Mahābhārata text and (from the medieval period onward) a freestanding scripture. The Vedānta tradition is built largely through Gītā commentary: Śaṅkara (8th c.) wrote the foundational Advaita commentary; Rāmānuja (11th-12th c.) wrote the foundational Viśiṣṭādvaita commentary; Madhva (13th c.) wrote the foundational Dvaita commentary; all subsequent Vedānta is in dialogue with these three. Charles Wilkins's 1785 English translation — produced under Warren Hastings's patronage with a preface by Hastings explicitly arguing for the cultural worth of Indian scripture — was the first major Hindu text translated directly from Sanskrit into a modern European language. Its reception (Schopenhauer's enthusiasm; Emerson's Transcendentalist absorption; Gandhi's reading-as-allegory; Aurobindo's *Essays on the Gita* 1922-28; Huxley's *Perennial Philosophy* 1945) made the Gītā the operational scripture of modern global Hinduism.

## Themes and motifs
- **Karma-yoga (the discipline of disinterested action)** — Chapter 3, especially 3:19-30. Krishna teaches Arjuna that one should perform one's *svadharma* (one's appointed duty) without attachment to the fruits of action (*phala*); this is the path appropriate to the active life. "Your authority is in action alone, never in its fruits" (2:47) is the locus classicus.
- **Jñāna-yoga (the discipline of liberating knowledge)** — Chapters 2, 13, 14. The *ātman* (true self) is eternal, unborn, undying, immune to the changes that affect the body; recognition of one's true self-as-ātman is liberation.
- **Bhakti-yoga (the discipline of devotion)** — Chapters 9, 10, 11, 12. Loving devotion to the personal Lord is the most direct path to liberation. Chapter 12, the "Yoga of Devotion," is the textual core of the entire bhakti tradition.
- **The Theophany (Chapter 11)** — Krishna grants Arjuna the divine eye and shows him the universal form (*viśvarūpa*): all gods, all worlds, all beings, present and future, contained within Krishna's body. "If a thousand suns were to rise in the sky at once, it would resemble in some measure the radiance of that great being" (11:12). One of the most cosmologically grandiose passages in any scripture. Oppenheimer's 11:32 quotation ("Now I am become Death, the destroyer of worlds") at the Trinity test made the passage globally famous.
- [[avatar-doctrine]] — Krishna's self-disclosure: "Whenever there is a decline of dharma and a rise of adharma, I send myself forth. For the protection of the good and the destruction of the wicked, for the establishment of dharma, I come into being age after age" (4:7-8). The Gītā is the textual foundation of the *avatāra* doctrine in mature Hindu form.
- **The three guṇas (*sattva*, *rajas*, *tamas*)** — Chapters 14, 17, 18. The three cosmic qualities through which Prakṛti (matter / nature) operates; ethical and psychological types are constituted by their interaction.
- **Caste and *svadharma*** — Krishna's argument turns on Arjuna's *kṣatriya* duty as a warrior; the doctrine of *varṇāśrama-dharma* (caste-and-life-stage duty) receives one of its most influential statements (4:13, 18:41-48). This is the most ethically and politically contested feature of the Gītā in modern reception (Gandhi reads the war allegorically to detach the doctrine of *svadharma* from literal caste-warrior application; Ambedkar reads the same passages as the textual root of caste oppression).
- [[non-duality]] (Hindu form) — the Upaniṣadic *ātman-Brahman* identification, integrated with the personal-Lord theology.
- [[bhakti-devotion]] — the Gītā is the textual root of all subsequent Hindu bhakti, including [[event-bhakti-movement-c-700-1700]].

## Cross-tradition substrate
- **Chapter 11 theophany.** The Gītā's cosmic Krishna-theophany — all worlds, all beings, all times present and future contained in the Lord's body — belongs to a cross-tradition substrate of *cosmic theophany* literature. Closest parallels: (a) the Hebrew Bible's Sinai theophany (Exodus 19) and Ezekiel's *merkavah* vision (Ezekiel 1, the divine chariot-throne); (b) the Mesopotamian-Babylonian cosmological imagery of Marduk in *Enuma Elish*, where the high god contains the cosmos; (c) the Hellenistic *epiphany* literature, where a god discloses cosmic identity (Aelius Aristides on Asclepius; the *Hymn to Demeter*'s Demeter-disclosure); (d) the Christian Pauline cosmic-Christ in Colossians 1:15-20 ("in him all things hold together") and the Johannine *Logos* in whom "all things were made." Five traditions independently producing cosmic-theophany literature. The Gītā 11 is among the most rhetorically intense statements of the type. Substrate; *parallel-motif*, with several independent developments and structural recurrence.
- **Karma-yoga and the Stoic *amor fati*.** The Gītā's doctrine of action performed without attachment to fruits — "Your authority is in action alone, never in its fruits" (2:47) — is structurally parallel to the Stoic doctrine of *amor fati* / acceptance of cosmic order, the doctrine that one should align with the *Logos*'s providential pattern and do one's *kathēkon* (proper function) without attachment to outcome (Marcus Aurelius's *Meditations*, especially Books IV and VIII; Epictetus's *Enchiridion*). McEvilley 2002 *The Shape of Ancient Thought* surfaces this as a contested-but-suggestive parallel (Pyrrho's exposure to Indian thought during Alexander's campaign; later Stoic possible diffusion). Standard view: structural parallel, not transmission; the Gandhāran contact zone makes weak diffusion arguments possible but unprovable. Substrate.
- **The disciplined warrior facing death.** The Arjuna-on-the-battlefield scene, the warrior asked to perform his violent duty while interiorly composed, has cross-tradition parallels in (a) the Iliadic Achilles vs. Hector confrontation, (b) the Stoic exemplar of the philosopher-general (Marcus Aurelius), (c) the Bhagavadgita's reception in samurai bushidō (via the early-modern Japanese translations of Hindu scripture), (d) the Christian just-war tradition's treatment of the conscience of the warrior. Substrate of the warrior-disciple type-scene.
- **The Sanskritic-Greek philosophical encounter.** The Gītā's reception by Schopenhauer (who explicitly recognized the affinity with his own metaphysics of Will) and by Emerson/Thoreau (who absorbed Gītā quietism into American Transcendentalism) is the founding event of the modern Western reception of Hindu scripture. Halbfass 1988 *India and Europe* reconstructs.

## Disputes
- **Date.** Standard range -200 BCE to +200 CE; some scholars push earlier or later within this window. The Gītā's relation to the Mahābhārata's compositional layers is part of the broader Mahābhārata dating problem.
- **Unity or layered composition.** Sharma 1986 argues for compositional layers within the Gītā; van Buitenen 1981 argues for substantial unity. The standard view accepts the text as substantially unified, with some debated passages.
- **Was the Gītā composed *as* a Mahābhārata insertion or incorporated from independent circulation?** Both views have defenders. The literary integration is strong enough to suggest the text was at minimum substantially re-worked for its Mahābhārata position.
- **Reading: literal warrior-duty or allegory.** Gandhi famously read the Kurukṣetra battle allegorically — the moral struggle within the soul, not literal war — drawing on a long medieval allegorical reading tradition. The literal-warrior-duty reading sustains the Hindu caste-warrior tradition (Tilak's *Gītārahasya* 1915 emphasizes active engagement); the allegorical reading supports non-violent applications (Gandhi 1929; Aurobindo). Ambedkar's contestation: the Gītā is being read selectively to obscure its endorsement of *varṇāśrama-dharma*.
- **Modern Hindu reception and the Gītā as "national gospel."** The Gītā's modern elevation as the primary Hindu scripture is itself a 19th-20th c. event, shaped by the Brahmo Samaj, Vivekananda, Aurobindo, Gandhi, and the colonial-then-postcolonial need for a single representative Hindu text. Davis 2014 reconstructs this carefully.
