# Sūtrakṛtāṅga — Second Āṅga

The *Sūtrakṛtāṅga* is the second of the twelve Āṅgas of the Śvetāmbara Jain canon — a heterogeneous text combining monastic rules, philosophical polemic, and doctrinal exposition. It is divided into two books (*śrutaskandhas*), the first of which contains some of the earliest surviving inter-school philosophical debates in Indian history.

## Content

**Book I: The Philosophical Debates**
The most historically significant section. Mahāvīra's disciples (in the text's framing) engage in systematic refutation of rival philosophical positions, including:
- **Ājīvika fatalism** — the school of Makkhali Gosāla (Mahāvīra's former companion-turned-rival): the view that liberation is predetermined by fate (*niyati*), not by ascetic effort. Jain response: if liberation were fated, asceticism would be meaningless and ethical choice would be impossible.
- **Buddhist *anātman* and *cetanā*-karma** — the Buddhist denial of a permanent self and the definition of karma as intentional volition rather than physical action. Jain response: if there is no self, who accumulates karma and who is liberated? The Buddhist position collapses the agent of liberation.
- **Brahmin Vedic ritualism** — Vedic sacrifice involves the killing of animals, which generates karma regardless of the sacrificer's intention or the prescribed ritual context.
- **Materialist (*Lokāyata/Cārvāka*) views** — the denial of karma, soul, and liberation altogether.

**Book II: Monastic regulations**
Detailed rules for monks, with emphasis on non-attachment and the proper handling of the body, food, and material objects.

## Significance as cross-tradition source
The *Sūtrakṛtāṅga* is exceptional as evidence of what the rival schools — Ājīvika, Buddhist, Brahmin — actually taught as understood by their *opponents* in the 5th-3rd c. BCE. This makes it, alongside the Buddhist Pali canon's polemical sections (Sāmaññaphala Sutta, etc.), one of the primary sources for the intellectual map of the śramaṇa milieu. The debates recorded here — especially the Jain-Buddhist exchange on karma, self, and liberation — represent some of the oldest surviving philosophical dialogues between living traditions.
