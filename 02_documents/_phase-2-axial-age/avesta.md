The Avesta is the sacred scripture of [[tradition-zoroastrianism]], composed in Avestan — a language closely related to Vedic Sanskrit, both descending from Proto-Indo-Iranian. It is among the oldest extant collections of Indo-European religious literature.

## Structure

The Avesta as it survives is approximately one quarter of the original compilation (much was destroyed during the Macedonian conquest of Persia under Alexander and the subsequent disruption of the Zoroastrian priestly infrastructure):

**The Yasna** (worship liturgy, 72 chapters): The primary liturgical text, recited during the central Zoroastrian ritual. Contains the **Gathas** (Yasna 28–34, 43–51, 53) — the most ancient part of the Avesta, attributed directly to Zarathustra, composed in Old Avestan. The Gathas are theological hymns of extraordinary literary and philosophical depth.

**The Visperad** (additional liturgical sections)

**The Yashts** (hymns to individual deities: [[ahura-mazda]], [[mithra-zoroastrian]], [[anahita]], and others)

**The Vendidad** (the "anti-demon law" — ritual purity laws, creation myth, flood narrative)

**The Khordeh Avesta** (shorter prayers for daily use, still recited by Zoroastrians today)

## The Gathas — Creation-by-Sound

The Gathas are among the oldest texts in the vault in terms of probable composition date. The Ahuna Vairya (*Yatha ahu vairyo*), a brief but cosmologically potent prayer, is described in later Avestan literature as having been uttered by Ahura Mazda at creation — the primordial sound whose vibration brought the cosmos into existence. See [[music-zoroastrian-sacred-sound]] for the full theological account.

## Oral Transmission and Script Creation

The Avesta was transmitted orally for approximately 1,500 years before the Avestan script was created specifically to preserve it (c. 400–600 CE, Sasanian period). See [[alphabet-avestan]] for the complete account of why a new alphabet with the largest phonemic inventory of any traditional script (~53 letters) was engineered to capture every phonological distinction in the Avestan liturgical texts.

The result is that the Avesta is one of the most precisely preserved ancient oral traditions: the oral transmission preserved not just words but exact recitation patterns, which the script then codified.
