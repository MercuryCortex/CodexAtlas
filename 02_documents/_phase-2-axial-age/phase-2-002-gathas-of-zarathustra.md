# Gathas of Zarathustra

## What it is
Seventeen hymns in Old Avestan, attributed to and almost certainly composed by the prophet Zarathustra himself — direct first-person address to Ahura Mazda. They are embedded as the central liturgical core of the Yasna (the principal Zoroastrian ritual), distributed across Yasna 28–34, 43–46, 47–50, 51, and 53, totaling about 240 stanzas. The Gathas inaugurate ethical and cosmic dualism in world religion: a high god (Ahura Mazda) is opposed by a hostile principle (the Lie, *druj*, embodied in Angra Mainyu / Ahriman), with humans called to choose between Truth (*aša*) and the Lie. They are the only stratum of the Avesta in which Zarathustra speaks in his own voice.

## Primary text — selected excerpts

The excerpts below use L. H. Mills's public-domain 1887 *Sacred Books of the East* vol. XXXI translation (Oxford), which is the only English Gathas freely reproducible at length. Mills is heavily superseded by Insler (1975) and Humbach (1991), who corrected hundreds of points of Old Avestan philology; the renderings below should be checked against Insler for any serious scholarly use. Key contested verses are paraphrased with reference to the critical editions.

### Yasna 30.3–6 — the two primordial Spirits

The locus classicus of Zoroastrian cosmic dualism. Zarathustra announces *two* originative Spirits — Spenta Mainyu (the Beneficent) and Angra Mainyu (the Hostile) — who themselves *chose* between life and not-life, between truth and the lie. The doctrine of free moral choice as a structural feature of the cosmos starts here.

> Thus are the primeval Spirits who as a pair (combining their opposite strivings), and (yet each) independent in his action, have been famed (of old). (They are) a better thing, they two, and a worse, as to thought, as to word, and as to deed. And between these two let the wisely acting choose aright. (Choose ye) not (as) the evil-doers!
>
> (Yea) when the two Spirits came together at the first to make life, and life's absence, and to determine how the world at the last shall be ordered, for the wicked (Hell) the worst life, for the holy (Heaven) the Best Mental State,
>
> (Then when they had finished each His part in the deeds of creation, they chose distinctly each His separate realm.) The Evil One choosing the worst deeds, the Bountiful Spirit, clad in the stoutest stone (of the firmament), chose Righteousness; and (his followers) shall (likewise so choose), they who shall satisfy Ahura Mazda continuously with actions truly done.

Citation: Yasna 30.3–5, trans. L. H. Mills, *Sacred Books of the East* XXXI (1887). For the current critical reading see Insler 1975, pp. 32–35.

### Yasna 31.7–8 — Ahura Mazda as first thinker, first creator

Zarathustra's praise of the high god as the originator of *aša* (truth/order) — through his thought, not through combat or sexual generation. The lines are the Iranian Axial-Age move toward a single creating mind.

> He who as first one (thus) thought, "Let the blessed realms be filled with light," he it was who by his understanding created Asha [Righteousness]. (Those holiest realms thou didst increase, O Lord!) which, O Ahura Mazda! even at this day, Thy Spirit doth augment for Thee.
>
> Yea, thus I conceived of Thee, O Mazda, as the first to act for us, as the father of the Good Mind within us, when with mine eyes I saw thee verily existing, the true creator of the Right, the Lord in the deeds of life.

Citation: Yasna 31.7–8, trans. Mills 1887; cf. Insler 1975, pp. 36–37.

### Yasna 43.5–6 — Zarathustra's vision and the appearance of Ahura Mazda

The hymn is structured as eight strophes each opening with "Then I conceived of Thee as the holy One, O Ahura Mazda" — a litany of recognition that constitutes Zarathustra's prophetic call.

> As the holy one I recognised Thee, O Mazda Ahura! when I saw Thee as the first one at the birth of Life, when Thou madest actions and words to have their meed (rewards bad to bad, and a blessing good for the good), through Thy wisdom (to be there each) at the creation's final turning-point.
>
> (And) at which turning-point Thou comest with Thy holy Spirit, O Mazda! and with Thy Sovereign Power, at whose actions the settlements (all) prosper through (Thy) Righteousness. To these Aramaiti [Holy Devotion] proclaims the laws of Thy understanding, the (laws) of Thy Wisdom, which none can deceive.

Citation: Yasna 43.5–6, trans. Mills 1887; cf. Insler 1975, pp. 60–63.

### Yasna 44 — the great question hymn

The most distinctive Gathic literary form: a hymn entirely composed of questions to Ahura Mazda, each strophe opening with "This I ask Thee, O Lord, answer me truly." The text below preserves a few representative strophes.

> This (first) I ask Thee, O Ahura! tell me aright; who was the father of the Righteous Order at the first in the creation? Who established the course of the sun and stars? Who but Thou is it through whom the moon waxes and wanes again? These very things, O Great Creator! would I know, and others likewise still.
>
> This I ask Thee, O Ahura! tell me aright. Who from beneath hath sustained the earth and the clouds above from falling? Who made the waters and the plants? Who yoked (Thy) two steeds to the wind and to the clouds? Who is, O Great Creator! the inspirer of the good thoughts (within our souls)?

Citation: Yasna 44.3–4, trans. Mills 1887; cf. Insler 1975, pp. 64–69, and Humbach 1991, vol. I, pp. 156–161, for the questions' deeper ritual-poetic logic.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Pre-state pastoralist / agrarian society of eastern Iran. The hymns reference cattle, settled "houses" (*nmāna*) and clans, but no kings, no cities, no empire. The social tension is between herders (whom Zarathustra defends) and raiding warrior-priests of the older Indo-Iranian *daēva*-cult.
- **Religious milieu.** A reform / partial inversion of the inherited Indo-Iranian religion shared with the Vedic world. The old *daēvas* (cognate to Vedic *devas*) are reclassified as demonic; the *ahuras* (cognate Vedic *asuras*) become the true divinities; Ahura Mazda alone is supreme. *Yasna* (sacrifice) is cognate with Vedic *yajña*, *zaotar* (priest) with *hotṛ*, *haoma* with *soma*, *aša* with *ṛta*.
- **Who was behind it.** Zarathustra (Greek: Zoroaster), a *zaotar*-priest by training, who experienced what he frames as direct visionary commissioning by Ahura Mazda. The hymns are intensely personal — laments, prayers, polemics, calls for patronage. Transmitted by the priestly *māthrān*/*mowbed* lineages.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous oral-priestly transmission. First entered Western scholarship via Abraham Hyacinthe Anquetil-Duperron (1771 *Zend-Avesta*); critical philological work from Bartholomae (Altiranisches Wörterbuch, 1904) onward.

## Themes and motifs
- [[dualism-cosmic]] — Two primordial Spirits, Spenta Mainyu (Bounteous) and Angra Mainyu (Hostile), choose between Truth and the Lie; the cosmos is the arena of their conflict. Yasna 30 is the locus classicus.
- [[ethical-dualism]] — Each human soul must choose, by "good thoughts, good words, good deeds" (*humata, hūxta, huvarsta*), to ally with Truth or the Lie. The doctrine of individual moral choice is unusually sharp.
- [[end-times-judgment]] — A final reckoning: the *Chinvat Bridge* judges souls; the wicked fall, the righteous cross. Eventually (in the developed system) a *Saoshyant* (savior) appears, the dead are resurrected, the world is renewed (*frashokereti*).
- [[afterlife-judgment]] — Individual post-mortem judgment of the soul, paired with the eventual cosmic judgment. The Gathic seed of what becomes the explicit Zoroastrian eschatology of the later Avesta and Pahlavi books.
- **Choice and freedom** — Y.30.2: "Hear with your ears the highest truths I preach... For decision is to be made by every man before the Great Consummation."

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-2-001-rig-veda-family-books]] — *shared-milieu* / *parallel-motif*: linguistic and ritual cognate system (Avestan/Vedic). Gathic *aša* = Vedic *ṛta*; *haoma* = *soma*; *yasna* = *yajña*. The Gathas *invert* the deva/daēva valence — the strongest evidence that Zoroastrianism is a *reform* of the Indo-Iranian religion rather than an unrelated tradition. Ref: Boyce 1975; Skjærvø 2011.
- → [[phase-2-004-yasna-younger-avesta]] — *redaction-of* / *commentary-on*: the Younger Avesta surrounds the Gathic core and develops the dualistic system into structured angelology (Amesha Spentas, yazatas) and demonology.
- → Second Temple Jewish texts (Phase 3 work) — *shared-milieu*: the appearance of cosmic dualism, named archangels (Michael, Gabriel, Raphael, Uriel), an organized demonology, bodily resurrection, and final judgment in post-exilic and Second Temple Judaism — features absent or marginal pre-exile — is widely argued to reflect Persian-period Zoroastrian influence on the Judean exilic and post-exilic community. Ref: Boyce & Grenet 1991, *A History of Zoroastrianism* III; Barr 1985, JAAR; Shaked 1984, *Studies in Judaism and Zoroastrianism*.
- → Christian eschatology and apocalyptic — *parallel-motif*: Saoshyant, bodily resurrection, last judgment, renovation of the world have strong structural parallels to Christian eschatology, almost certainly mediated through Second Temple Jewish apocalyptic. Ref: Hultgård 2000 in *The Encyclopedia of Apocalypticism*.

## Disputes
- **Date of Zarathustra.** Two main camps:
  - *Linguistic / Boyce camp*: Old Avestan is roughly contemporaneous with Vedic Sanskrit's earliest layer, ~1500–1000 BCE. Boyce, Schmidt, Skjærvø, Kellens argue for a date ~1200 BCE.
  - *Traditional / late date*: Greek and Pahlavi traditions place Zoroaster "258 years before Alexander", i.e., ~600 BCE. Henning (1951) defended this; Gnoli has argued for it. Largely rejected by current scholarship as a back-projection.
  We carry the YAML range -1500 to -1000.
- **Whether Zarathustra is a single historical individual.** Mainstream view: yes, a single visionary author of the Gathas. Some (Molé) have argued for a more diffuse milieu. Kellens has problematized the strict historical-author view but the personal voice of the hymns remains decisive for most.
- **Mechanism and degree of influence on Judaism.** Consensus that Persian period was the matrix; *whether* features like resurrection, Satan, and angelology are direct loans or post-exilic Jewish developments stimulated by the Persian context is debated (Barr 1985 vs. Hultgård vs. Shaked).

## The Gathas as proto-philosophical inquiry — Yasna 44 in depth

Yasna 44 — "the question hymn" — is the most philosophically distinctive text in the Gathas and arguably in all of Old Iranian literature. Every strophe opens with the formula *"This I ask Thee, O Ahura, tell me aright"* — and proceeds with a cosmological question. The hymn thus **inverts the genre expectation of prayer**: instead of praise or petition, Zarathustra interrogates the nature of reality.

The cosmological questions of Yasna 44.3–7 are among the earliest examples of systematic philosophical cosmological inquiry in world literature — genuinely comparable to the earliest Greek pre-Socratics in their structure:

- "Who was the father of Righteousness (*aša*) at the first creation?" (Y.44.3)
- "Who established the course of the sun and stars?" (Y.44.3)
- "Who from beneath hath sustained the earth and the clouds above from falling?" (Y.44.4)
- "Who made the waters and the plants?" (Y.44.4)
- "Who yoked swiftness to wind and the clouds?" (Y.44.4)
- "Who is the inspirer of good thoughts within souls?" (Y.44.4)
- "Who created light and darkness? Who created sleep and waking?" (Y.44.5)

These questions are not rhetorical in the modern sense — they are genuine interrogative-hymnic inquiry, expecting Ahura Mazda to answer through the vision-event the hymn itself constitutes. The format structures a philosophical posture: **truth is to be sought by question and answer, not merely received by tradition**. Skjærvø (*The Spirit of Zoroastrianism*, 2011) notes this as constitutive of the "axial" quality of Zarathustra's theology — a reflexive turn toward questioning the grounds of reality.

The questions then pivot from cosmological to social and biographical: Zarathustra asks for patronage (Y.44.7, 44.9), for revelation about the fate of the *druj*-followers (Y.44.13), and for confirmation of his prophetic mission (Y.44.16). The hymn thus moves from cosmic foundations through social ethics to personal commissioning in a single text — the only instance in world literature where a philosopher-prophet's cosmological inquiry and prophetic self-understanding are woven into a single hymn-sequence.

## The Nietzsche reception — a sustained philosophical misappropriation

Friedrich Nietzsche's *Also Sprach Zarathustra* (1883–85) is the most famous text bearing Zarathustra's name in Western literature. Its relationship to the actual Gathas is one of deliberate inversion rather than ignorance.

In *Ecce Homo* (1888, "Why I Am a Destiny"), Nietzsche explains that he chose Zarathustra precisely because the historical figure was the **inventor of morality** — the first to frame the struggle between good and evil as a cosmic metaphysical principle, to translate ethics into ontology:

> "Zarathustra created this most portentous of all errors — morality; therefore he must also be the first to recognize it." — Nietzsche, *Ecce Homo*

And: "The self-overcoming of morality through truthfulness, the self-overcoming of the moralist into his opposite — into me — that is what the name Zarathustra means in my mouth."

The logic is inverted: precisely because the historical Zarathustra established cosmic moral dualism most clearly, Nietzsche's fictional Zarathustra is uniquely suited to *overcome* it. Nietzsche's Zarathustra is the anti-Zarathustra — a herald of moral nihilism, the Übermensch, the eternal recurrence, and the will to power.

**The actual Zarathustra is Nietzsche's theological opposite in every major respect:**

| Dimension | Historical Zarathustra (Gathas) | Nietzsche's Zarathustra |
|---|---|---|
| Cosmic structure | Truth vs. Lie is irreducibly real and structuring | Good/evil distinction is a human illusion to be overcome |
| Ethics | *Humata, hūkhta, hvarshta* (good thought, word, deed) as cosmic obligation | Beyond good and evil; will to power |
| Eschatology | Cosmic renovation (*frashokereti*) and universal judgment | Eternal recurrence — no linear endpoint |
| Humanity | Every soul accountable to Ahura Mazda | The Übermensch as self-creator beyond accountability |
| God | Ahura Mazda as supreme lord and source of Truth | God is dead; the divine is a human projection |

**Scholarly reception:** Stausberg (*Zarathustra and Zoroastrianism*, 2008) provides the fullest treatment of the Western reception history. The misappropriation is mentioned by Boyce (1975, vol. I, p. 3 n. 1) with characteristic terseness. The philosophical irony is that Nietzsche's text has now shaped the Western cultural idea of "Zarathustra" far more thoroughly than the actual Gathas — making it nearly impossible for a Western-educated reader to encounter the word "Zarathustra" without the Nietzsche association as a primary interference.

**For the vault:** The Nietzsche reception functions as a major **cross-tradition distortion event** — the replacement of a historical figure's identity by a Western philosophical projection. It belongs in the same analytical category as the Western reception of "Buddha" through Schopenhauer's lens, or the 18th-century construction of "Hinduism" as a philosophical monism.
