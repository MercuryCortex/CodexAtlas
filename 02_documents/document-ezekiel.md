# Ezekiel

## What it is
A book of the **Latter Prophets** in the Hebrew Bible, comprising 48 chapters of visions, sign-acts, oracles of judgment, and oracles of restoration delivered by **Ezekiel son of Buzi, a priest deported to Babylonia** in the first wave of the Babylonian exile (597 BCE). Uniquely among the prophets, Ezekiel is precisely dated — his oracles carry day-month-year notices spanning 593–571 BCE — and he writes from *outside* the land, among the exiles by the Chebar canal. The book is famous for its overwhelming visionary imagery: the opening **chariot-throne (merkavah)** borne by four composite living creatures (ch. 1); the departure of the divine Glory (*kavod*) from the defiled Temple and its eventual return (chs. 8–11, 43); the **valley of dry bones** restored to life (ch. 37); the war of **Gog of Magog** (chs. 38–39); and the great blueprint of a **new Temple** and a re-allotted land (chs. 40–48).

## Context
- **Political milieu.** The Neo-Babylonian empire of Nebuchadnezzar II; the two deportations of Judahites (597, 586 BCE) and the destruction of Jerusalem and its Temple in 586.
- **Religious milieu.** A priest forced to theologize catastrophe far from the sanctuary. Ezekiel reworks Priestly holiness theology for a world without a temple, insisting that YHWH's mobile Glory can be present in Babylon and that the covenant relationship can be renewed.
- **Who was behind it.** Ezekiel himself, with subsequent editing by tradents (Zimmerli's "school of Ezekiel") who arranged and expanded the oracles.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Transmitted in the Masoretic Text and in a notably shorter Old Greek edition (Papyrus 967), a key witness to the book's textual development.

## Themes and motifs
- [[divine-kingship]] — the throne-chariot vision and the departing/returning Glory make YHWH's sovereign kingship, unbound to any one place, the book's organizing image.
- [[messianism]] — the promise of a future Davidic "prince" (*nasi*) and the one shepherd over a reunited Israel (chs. 34, 37) feeds later messianic hope.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-2-018-hebrew-bible-p-source]] — *shared-milieu*: Ezekiel's holiness, purity, and temple concerns are closely allied to the Priestly tradition.
- → [[phase-2-019-deuteronomistic-history]] — *parallel-motif*: a shared theology of exile as covenant judgment, framed from a priestly rather than Deuteronomistic angle.
- → [[phase-3-008-book-of-daniel]] — *direct-influence*: Ezekiel's throne-vision and cosmic-war imagery are foundational to the apocalyptic genre Daniel develops.
- → the New Testament Revelation — *direct-influence*: the four living creatures, the measured temple, and Gog–Magog are taken up directly.

## Disputes
- **Unity and redaction.** Greenberg reads the book as a largely coherent composition; Zimmerli and others reconstruct layered growth around an authentic core.
- **The two editions.** The relationship between the longer Masoretic text and the shorter Old Greek (P967) — which is more original — is unresolved and central to Ezekiel textual criticism.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **The seedbed of apocalyptic and of merkavah mysticism.** Ezekiel's chariot-throne is among the most generative images in this strand of religious history: it stands behind the apocalyptic visions of [[phase-3-008-book-of-daniel]] and Revelation, and it became the central object of contemplation in Jewish **Merkavah ("chariot") mysticism** — the esoteric tradition the Mishnah later restricts from public exposition (Greenberg 1983; Zimmerli 1979).
- **Babylon written into the vision.** The composite throne-bearers and the cosmic imagery register the exilic encounter with Babylonian art and cosmology under [[nebuchadnezzar-ii]] — catastrophe and foreign empire transmuted into a new theology of a mobile, universal God (Block 1997).
