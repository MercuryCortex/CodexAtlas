# Ludlul bēl nēmeqi (The Poem of the Righteous Sufferer)

## What it is
A four-tablet Akkadian poem — its title means "I will praise the Lord of Wisdom," i.e. Marduk — in which a pious nobleman, Šubši-mešrê-Šakkan, is struck without cause by disease, social ruin, and the silence of his god, descends to the brink of death, and is then restored through a series of dreams and the mercy of Marduk. Often called "the Babylonian Job," it is the classic Mesopotamian meditation on undeserved suffering and the inscrutability of the divine will.

## Primary text

### Tablet II
*Trans. Foster (2005)*
> I called to my god, but he did not show his face; I prayed to my goddess, but she did not raise her head... Yet I thought only of prayer and supplication: supplication was my concern, sacrifice my rule.

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** Kassite-period Babylon, Marduk ascendant; the poem exalts Marduk as the god who alone wounds and heals.
- **Genre.** Wisdom literature, the companion of the dialogue-form Babylonian Theodicy.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Cuneiform tablets across the first millennium; a scribal classic.

## Themes and motifs
- Undeserved suffering — the righteous man afflicted despite his piety.
- The hidden god — divine purposes beyond human grasp ("what seems good to oneself is an offense to the god").
- Restoration — healing and reintegration through divine mercy.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-1-032-babylonian-theodicy]] — *shared-milieu*: the two great Babylonian theodicy poems; the Theodicy debates in dialogue what Ludlul narrates in monologue.
- → [[phase-2-035-job]] — *parallel-motif*: the biblical Book of Job treats the same problem — a blameless man's undeserved suffering and his confrontation with an inscrutable God.
- → [[phase-2-036-ecclesiastes]] — *parallel-motif*: the shared Near Eastern wisdom-pessimism about the limits of human understanding.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **The Babylonian Job — seven centuries before Job.** Ludlul bēl nēmeqi poses the problem of the Book of Job in advance: a scrupulously pious man is stripped of health, wealth, and standing for no fault he can name, abandoned even by his god, and reduced to crying out against a justice he cannot fathom — before being restored. The parallels to Job (and to Psalms of lament) are close enough that scholars debate direct influence versus a shared Near Eastern wisdom tradition; either way, Ludlul proves that the theodicy question at the heart of Job was an old and central Mesopotamian concern long before Israel framed its own answer (Lambert 1960 pp.21–62; Annus & Lenzi 2010; Foster 2005 pp.392–409).
