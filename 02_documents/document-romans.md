# Epistle to the Romans

The **Epistle to the Romans** is the longest and most systematic of Paul's letters, and arguably the most influential single document in the history of Christian theology. Written by **[[paul-of-tarsus|Paul]]** from Corinth (c. 55–58 CE) to a church he had not founded, it lays out his gospel in sustained argument: the universal reach of sin (Jew and gentile alike), **justification by faith** apart from the works of the Law, the new life in the Spirit, the place of Israel in God's purpose (chs. 9–11), and the ethics of the transformed community.

## The cross-tradition wire

Romans is densely woven from the **Hebrew Scriptures**: Paul grounds justification in **Abraham**, who "believed God and it was reckoned to him as righteousness" (Genesis 15, in **[[document-genesis|Genesis]]**); he builds his theology of sin and salvation on the **Adam–Christ** typology ("as in Adam all die…", Romans 5); and he frames the whole by Habakkuk's "the righteous shall live by faith" (1:17). Strikingly, his opening indictment of gentile **idolatry and its moral consequences** (Romans 1:18–32) closely follows the argument of the **[[phase-3-012-wisdom-of-solomon|Wisdom of Solomon]]** (chs. 13–15) — a direct line from Hellenistic-Jewish apologetic into the foundational Christian statement of universal sin. He also draws on **[[phase-3-001-second-third-isaiah|Isaiah]]** for the remnant and the gentiles' inclusion.

## Reach forward

No text has moved Western Christianity more. Romans 5 gave **Augustine** his doctrine of original sin and grace; Romans 1:17 ("the righteous shall live by faith") ignited **Luther's Reformation**; Romans 9–11 shaped Calvinist predestination; and Karl Barth's commentary on it detonated 20th-century theology. It is the engine-room of the Christian doctrine of salvation.
