# The Book of Gates

## What it is
One of the great New Kingdom "netherworld books" carved in the royal tombs of the Valley of the Kings. It maps the sun-god's perilous voyage through the twelve hours/gates of the night, each guarded by fire-breathing serpents, toward rebirth at dawn. Along the way it stages the nightly defeat of the chaos-serpent Apophis and a tribunal of the dead before Osiris, where the blessed enter eternal life and the damned face annihilation. Its fifth hour even depicts the four "races" of humankind (Egyptians, Asiatics, Nubians, Libyans) as equally creatures of the sun-god.

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** Royal solar-Osirian theology: the king joins Ra's nightly journey and Osiris's judgment.
- **Genre.** A "netherworld book," successor to the Amduat, combining cosmic geography and moral judgment.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Tomb walls and Seti I's sarcophagus; a priestly composition for royal use.

## Themes and motifs
- [[chaoskampf]] — Ra's nightly battle against the chaos-serpent Apophis to secure the sunrise.
- The judgment of the dead — weighing before Osiris; bliss or destruction.
- Solar rebirth — death as a passage to renewal each dawn.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-1-036-amduat]] — *redaction-of*: the Book of Gates develops and systematizes the earlier Amduat's night-journey.
- → [[phase-1-010-book-of-the-dead]] / [[phase-1-009-coffin-texts]] — *shared-milieu*: the funerary corpus of judgment, transformation, and safe passage.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **The weighing of the heart and the judgment of the dead.** The Book of Gates dramatizes the Egyptian conviction — already in the Coffin Texts and Book of the Dead — that every soul faces a post-mortem judgment, the heart weighed against the feather of Ma'at, with eternal life or annihilation as the stakes. This individual moral reckoning after death, depicted here as a courtroom before Osiris, is among the oldest and most fully elaborated afterlife-judgment schemes in the world, and a frequent comparand for the development of judgment, heaven, and hell in later Jewish, Christian, and Islamic eschatology (Hornung 1999 pp.55–77).
