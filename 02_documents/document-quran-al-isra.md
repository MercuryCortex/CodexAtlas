# Sūrat al-Isrāʾ (Quran 17 — The Night Journey)

## What it is
A Meccan sura that opens with one of Islam's most consequential verses: God carried his servant by night from the Sacred Mosque (Mecca) to "the Farthest Mosque" (al-Masjid al-Aqṣā), traditionally identified with Jerusalem. From this single verse grew the whole tradition of the Isrāʾ and Miʿrāj — Muhammad's night journey and ascent through the heavens — which fixed Jerusalem as Islam's third-holiest city. The sura also addresses the Children of Israel and lays out a sequence of moral commandments echoing the Decalogue.

## Primary text

### Quran 17:1
*Trans. Abdel Haleem (2004)*
> Glory to Him who made His servant travel by night from the sacred place of worship to the furthest place of worship, whose surroundings We have blessed, to show him some of Our signs.

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** Meccan Islam; the Night Journey links the new revelation to Jerusalem, the older Abrahamic holy city.
- **Tradition.** The Miʿrāj (ascent) elaborated from this verse became a vast devotional and visionary literature and the cause of the Dome of the Rock.
- **Discovery / transmission.** The opening verse is among the most theologically generative in the Quran.

## Themes and motifs
- The Night Journey and ascent — heaven opened to the Prophet.
- Jerusalem's sanctity — "the Farthest Mosque" and its blessed surroundings.
- Moral law — the commandments of 17:22–39 (no other god, honor parents, do not kill, do not steal…).

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **One verse made Jerusalem an Islamic holy city.** Quran 17:1 — God carrying his servant "to the furthest place of worship" (al-masjid al-aqṣā) — is the scriptural root of Islam's claim to Jerusalem. Elaborated into the Isrāʾ-Miʿrāj (the Prophet's ascent through the seven heavens, meeting the earlier prophets, receiving the five daily prayers), it made the Temple Mount the site of the Dome of the Rock (691 CE) and al-Aqṣā Mosque, and bound Islam's sacred geography to the same hill holy to Jews and Christians. A single Meccan verse thus added the third claimant to the most contested sacred space on earth — and the heavenly-ascent motif itself belongs to the shared stock of Jewish merkabah and Christian apocalyptic vision (Reynolds 2010 pp.187–199; Nasr 2015 pp.693–700).
