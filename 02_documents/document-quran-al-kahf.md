# Sūrat al-Kahf (Quran 18 — The Cave)

## What it is
A Meccan sura unique for braiding together three independent legend-cycles, each of wide late-antique circulation: (1) the **Companions of the Cave** — youths who sleep for centuries to escape a persecuting king, the Islamic form of the Christian Seven Sleepers of Ephesus; (2) **Moses and al-Khiḍr** — Moses's humbling apprenticeship to a mysterious servant of God whose baffling acts conceal divine wisdom; and (3) **Dhū al-Qarnayn**, "the Two-Horned," who travels to the ends of the earth and builds an iron wall to seal Gog and Magog away until the end of time — a figure Islamic tradition identifies with Alexander the Great.

## Primary text

### Quran 18:65–66 (Moses and al-Khiḍr)
*Trans. Abdel Haleem (2004)*
> Among Our servants they found one to whom We had granted Our mercy and whom We had given knowledge of Our own. Moses said to him, "May I follow you so that you can teach me some of the right guidance you have been taught?"

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** Meccan Islam absorbing and re-voicing the legend-stock of late-antique Christianity and Hellenism.
- **Transmission.** Each of the three stories has a documented pre-Islamic source — the Ephesus Sleepers (Syriac/Greek hagiography), the Alexander wall (the Syriac Alexander Legend), and the immortal-guide motif.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Fully canonical; a Friday devotional favorite.

## Themes and motifs
- Hidden divine wisdom — al-Khiḍr's incomprehensible-then-justified acts.
- Faith preserved through time — the sleepers' miraculous survival.
- The bounded apocalypse — Gog and Magog walled until the appointed end.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **Three late-antique legends fossilized in one sura — the Seven Sleepers, the immortal guide, and Alexander.** Al-Kahf is a dense node of cross-cultural transmission. (1) The Companions of the Cave are the Christian **Seven Sleepers of Ephesus**, a hagiographic legend that circulated across the Mediterranean in Syriac and Greek before entering the Quran. (2) **Dhū al-Qarnayn's** journey to the rising and setting of the sun and his iron wall against Gog and Magog track the **Syriac Alexander Legend** (Neṣḥānā, early 7th c.) so closely that scholars treat it as a direct source, and Islamic tradition equated the "Two-Horned" with [[alexander-the-great]]. (3) **al-Khiḍr**, the deathless guide, joins a stream of immortal-wisdom figures reaching back to Gilgamesh's Utnapishtim and forward into Sufi mysticism. A single Meccan sura thus preserves three of late antiquity's great traveling legends (Van Bladel 2008; Reynolds 2010 pp.167–185; Nasr 2015 pp.737–755).
