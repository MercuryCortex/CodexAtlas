# Boethius — The Consolation of Philosophy

## What it is
Written in a prison cell in 524 CE by a condemned Roman senator awaiting execution, the Consolation is the great valedictory work of the ancient world. In alternating prose and verse, the despairing Boethius is visited by Lady Philosophy, who leads him from grief over his ruin to a Neoplatonic vision of the highest Good: fortune is fickle by nature and her gifts were never his; true happiness lies only in God, the supreme Good; evil is ultimately impotent; and divine foreknowledge, seen from eternity's standpoint, does not abolish human freedom. Strikingly, this last Roman philosopher — a Christian — consoles himself in his death-cell with pure philosophy, naming Christ not once.

## Primary text

### Book II (Fortune speaks)
*Trans. Relihan (2001)*
> "I turn the wheel that spins. I delight to see the high come down and the low ascend. Mount up, if you wish, but on this condition: that you not think it an injustice to come down when the rules of my game require it."

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Ostrogothic Italy under Theodoric; Boethius, the last of the Roman philosopher-statesmen, falls and is killed.
- **Intellectual milieu.** A Christian steeped in Neoplatonism who, facing death, turns to reason rather than revelation.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Copied, translated, and glossed throughout the Middle Ages more than almost any other book.

## Themes and motifs
- Fortune's wheel — the indifference of worldly goods.
- The highest Good — happiness found only in God, the Neoplatonic One.
- Providence and free will — eternity's vantage reconciling foreknowledge and freedom.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **The book that carried the ancient world into the medieval one.** The Consolation of Philosophy was, after the Bible, the most-read and most-translated secular book of the European Middle Ages — Englished by King Alfred, by Chaucer, and by Elizabeth I — and it gave the medieval imagination its master-image of **Fortune and her wheel**. Through Boethius's separate logical works (his Latin Aristotle and his translation of Porphyry's Isagoge), the ancient logical curriculum and the **problem of universals** passed to the scholastics. That a Christian senator, facing execution, framed the deepest consolation in purely Neoplatonic terms — God as the One Good, evil as privation, eternity reconciling providence and freedom — made philosophical theism, not just revelation, the shared inheritance of the Christian West (Marenbon 2003; Chadwick 1981; Relihan 2001).
