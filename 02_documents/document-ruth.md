# Book of Ruth

## What it is
A short, exquisitely-crafted novella: in a time of famine, the Judahite widow Naomi returns from Moab with her loyal Moabite daughter-in-law Ruth, who gleans in the field of Boaz, claims his protection as kinsman-redeemer, and bears a son — Obed, father of Jesse, father of David. A story of loyal love (ḥesed) across ethnic lines, it quietly makes a Moabite woman the ancestress of Israel's greatest king.

## Primary text

### Ruth 1:16
*Trans. NRSV*
> Where you go, I will go; where you lodge, I will lodge; your people shall be my people, and your God my God.

## Context
- **Political / religious milieu.** Most critics read Ruth as a post-exilic narrative composed against the grain of the Ezra–Nehemiah campaign to dissolve marriages with foreign women: it celebrates exactly such a marriage and roots the Davidic line in it.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous canonical transmission; one of the Five Megillot read at festivals.

## Themes and motifs
- Ḥesed (loyal love) — Ruth's covenant-loyalty to Naomi as the human mirror of YHWH's.
- The kinsman-redeemer — Boaz as goʾel, a type later read messianically.
- [[messianism]] — the genealogy to David, and through David to the Messiah.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[david-king]] — *key-figure*: Ruth is David's great-grandmother (4:17), the genealogical payload of the whole book.
- → [[phase-3-017-gospel-of-matthew]] — *direct-influence*: Matthew 1:5 names Ruth in Jesus's genealogy, one of four women highlighted in the Messianic line.
- → [[phase-2-019-deuteronomistic-history]] — *shared-milieu*: the Judges-era setting the book presupposes.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **A Moabite woman in the bloodline of David and Jesus.** Deuteronomy 23:3 bars Moabites from the assembly "even to the tenth generation"; the Book of Ruth answers by making a Moabite the great-grandmother of David — and Matthew's Gospel underscores the scandal by listing Ruth among the four women in Jesus's genealogy (Matt 1:5). A quiet post-exilic novella thus became the canon's strongest internal argument for the gentile's place in the people of God (Campbell 1975, AYB 7 pp.27–36).
