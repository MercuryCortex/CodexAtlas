# Sūrat al-Kāfirūn (Quran 109 — The Disbelievers)

## What it is
A short, decisive Meccan sura, revealed (by the traditional account) when the Quraysh offered Muhammad a compromise: he would worship their gods for a year, they his for a year, and so the dispute would dissolve. The answer is a flat refusal of any blending of worship — "I do not worship what you worship… you have your religion and I have mine." It is at once the Qur'an's sharpest statement of monotheist purity and, in its final line, a recognition that belief cannot be coerced and the two communities will go their separate ways.

## Primary text

### Quran 109:1–6
*Trans. Abdel Haleem (2004)*
> Say, "Disbelievers: I do not worship what you worship, you do not worship what I worship, I will never worship what you worship, you will never worship what I worship: you have your religion and I have mine."

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** Meccan; revealed amid pressure to merge the new monotheism back into Mecca's pluralist cult.
- **Tradition.** Recited before sleep as a nightly disavowal of idolatry; called a quarter of the Qur'an in one hadith.
- **Discovery / transmission.** The closing line (109:6) is repeatedly cited in modern discussions of Islam and religious coexistence.

## Themes and motifs
- Purity of worship — no alternating, no shared, no blended cult.
- Allegiance undivided — the refusal of compromise in the one thing that cannot be split.
- Coexistence in distinction — "to you your religion, to me mine."

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **"To you your religion, to me mine" — exclusivity and coexistence in one breath.** al-Kāfirūn captures the central tension of biblical monotheism in six verses. On one side, it is uncompromising in exactly the way of the First Commandment (Exodus 20:3), Elijah's challenge at Carmel ("how long will you go limping between two opinions?", 1 Kings 18:21), and Joshua's "choose this day whom you will serve" (Joshua 24:15): the one God permits no rival and no power-sharing in worship, and the proposed Meccan rota of alternating cults is rejected outright. On the other side, its closing line — *lakum dīnukum wa-liya dīn*, "you have your religion and I have mine" — refuses to *force* the matter, drawing a boundary rather than waging a conquest, and so is read alongside "there is no compulsion in religion" (2:256) as a Qur'anic charter for living beside those who believe differently. The sura thus holds together the two halves of the monotheist inheritance that later history so often pulled apart: an exclusive truth-claim and a renunciation of coerced belief (McAuliffe 2006 pp.181–195; Lings 1983 pp.52–53; Nasr 2015 pp.1567–1571).
