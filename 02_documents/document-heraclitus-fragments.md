# Heraclitus — On Nature (the Fragments)

## What it is
The surviving fragments — about 130 gnomic, riddling sentences — of the lost book of Heraclitus of Ephesus, "the Obscure." From them emerges a vision of the cosmos as an ever-living fire in measured flux, held together by a hidden rational order he calls the **Logos**: "Listening not to me but to the Logos, it is wise to agree that all things are one." Change is constant ("everything flows"; "you cannot step twice into the same river"), and reality is the tension of opposites in harmony ("the road up and the road down are one and the same"). His coining of *logos* as the world's rational principle is among the most consequential moves in the history of thought.

## Primary text

### Fragments (Diels-Kranz)
*Trans. Kahn (1979)*
> The Logos holds always, but humans always prove unable to understand it. (DK 1)
> Listening not to me but to the Logos, it is wise to agree that all things are one. (DK 50)
> Upon those who step into the same rivers, different and again different waters flow. (DK 12)

## Context
- **Religious / intellectual milieu.** Ionian natural philosophy; Heraclitus replaces the gods' caprice with an impersonal cosmic order.
- **Style.** Deliberately oracular — fitting, he says, the Logos that "loves to hide."
- **Discovery / transmission.** Only fragments, embedded in later authors.

## Themes and motifs
- The Logos — the hidden rational order common to all.
- Flux — perpetual change as the nature of things.
- Unity of opposites — strife and harmony as one.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-3-020-gospel-of-john]] — *direct-influence* (via the Stoa and Philo): the Johannine "Logos" descends from the philosophical Logos Heraclitus founded.
- → [[phase-3-013-philo-of-alexandria]] — *direct-influence*: Philo fuses the Greek Logos with the creative Word of Genesis.
- → [[phase-2-009-hesiod-theogony-works-and-days]] — *polemic-against*: Heraclitus mocks the poets' anthropomorphic gods in favor of an impersonal cosmic order.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **The word "Logos" runs from Heraclitus to "In the beginning was the Word."** Heraclitus is the first to use *logos* for the rational principle that orders the cosmos and is "common to all." The Stoics built their entire physics and theology on this cosmic Logos (the divine fiery reason immanent in nature); Philo of Alexandria, a Hellenized Jew, identified that Logos with the creative Word of God in Genesis; and the Gospel of John opens by proclaiming that this Logos "was God" and "became flesh" in Jesus (John 1:1, 14). The single most important bridge-concept between Greek philosophy and Christian theology — the Logos that lets a Galilean preacher be described in the language of Greek metaphysics — begins in the riddling fragments of an Ephesian aristocrat around 500 BCE (Kahn 1979 pp.21–23, 97–104; KRS 1983 pp.181–212).
