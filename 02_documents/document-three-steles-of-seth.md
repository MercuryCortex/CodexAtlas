# The Three Steles of Seth

## What it is
A Sethian liturgical text — three doxological hymns ("steles") ascribed to the antediluvian Seth — designed to carry the community up through the ranks of the divine world and back. The first stele praises the Self-Generated (Autogenes), the second the female principle Barbelo, the third the supreme Pre-existent One; together they script a collective mystical ascent to vision of the ultimate and a controlled return. It belongs to the "Platonizing Sethian" texts, the most philosophically sophisticated stratum of Gnostic literature.

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** Third-century Sethian Gnosticism in close conversation with contemporary Neoplatonism.
- **Function.** A communal ascent-liturgy, not a narrative — praise as the vehicle of mystical elevation.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Nag Hammadi Codex VII.

## Themes and motifs
- The threefold ascent — Autogenes, Barbelo, the Pre-existent One.
- Praise as ascent — doxology that lifts the worshipper through the divine ranks.
- Return — the controlled descent back from vision.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **Where Gnosticism and Neoplatonism met — and Plotinus fought back.** The Three Steles of Seth belongs to the small group of "Platonizing Sethian" treatises (with Zostrianos, Allogenes, and Marsanes) that were read and debated in the Rome circle of the great Neoplatonist Plotinus around 260 CE. Porphyry records that "apocalypses of Zoroaster and Zostrianos and... Allogenes" circulated among Plotinus's students, and that Plotinus wrote a treatise — the *Against the Gnostics* (Ennead II.9) — and assigned his pupils to refute them. The Steles thus document a real historical encounter between Gnostic mysticism and classical philosophy, and the moment Neoplatonism defined itself partly against the Gnostic ascent (Turner 2001; Layton 1987 pp.149–158; Meyer 2007 pp.523–536).
