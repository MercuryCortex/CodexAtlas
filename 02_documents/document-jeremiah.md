# Book of Jeremiah

## What it is
The longest book of the Hebrew Bible by word-count, and the fullest prophetic record of a single career. It combines (a) poetic oracles of judgment and hope, (b) third-person prose narratives about the prophet (the "Baruch biography"), and (c) Deuteronomistic prose sermons. Jeremiah's ministry runs from the thirteenth year of King Josiah (~627 BCE) through the Babylonian destruction of Jerusalem (586) and into the subsequent exile of a remnant to Egypt — roughly forty years spanning the collapse of the kingdom of Judah. The book uniquely preserves the prophet's interior anguish in a cycle of first-person laments (the "Confessions of Jeremiah," 11–20), and uniquely names its own scribe, Baruch ben Neriah, who writes the oracles on a scroll that King Jehoiakim cuts up and burns (ch. 36) — after which Jeremiah simply dictates it again, longer.

## Primary text — the New Covenant

### Jeremiah 31:31–34
*Trans. NRSV*
> The days are surely coming, says the LORD, when I will make a new covenant with the house of Israel and the house of Judah. It will not be like the covenant that I made with their ancestors when I took them by the hand to bring them out of the land of Egypt — a covenant that they broke... But this is the covenant that I will make with the house of Israel after those days, says the LORD: I will put my law within them, and I will write it on their hearts; and I will be their God, and they shall be my people... for they shall all know me, from the least of them to the greatest, says the LORD; for I will forgive their iniquity, and remember their sin no more.

*This is the only place in the Hebrew Bible where the phrase "new covenant" (bᵉrît ḥădāšâ) appears. Its interiorization of the law ("written on the heart," not on stone tablets) became the charter text of the entire Christian self-understanding as the people of the "New Testament."*

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Judah in its death-throes between two empires. Josiah's reform (-622), triggered by the "book of the law" found in the Temple (widely identified with a core of Deuteronomy), briefly centralized and purified the cult; Josiah's death at Megiddo (-609) ended it. Egypt then Babylon dominated; Nebuchadnezzar II deported Judah's elite in -597 and razed Jerusalem and the Temple in -586. Jeremiah counseled submission to Babylon as YHWH's instrument — and was branded a traitor, beaten, imprisoned, and thrown into a cistern for it.
- **Religious milieu.** Jeremiah is the prophetic voice of the Deuteronomistic movement: covenant, exclusive worship of YHWH, the Temple as no guarantee of safety (the "Temple Sermon," ch. 7, attacks the popular theology that Jerusalem was inviolable). He polemicizes against the worship of the Queen of Heaven (44:17–19), the cult of Baal, and false prophets of peace (Hananiah, ch. 28).
- **Who was behind it.** The prophet, his scribe Baruch, and Deuteronomistic tradents who shaped the prose. The book grew over a century-plus into two divergent editions.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous canonical transmission; two ancient Hebrew editions (long MT and short LXX) both attested at Qumran.

## Themes and motifs
- New covenant (31:31–34) — the law written on the heart, forgiveness of sin, the universal knowledge of God. The single most theologically generative passage in the book.
- [[messianism]] — the "righteous Branch" raised up for David (23:5–6; 33:14–16), "The LORD is our righteousness."
- [[divine-kingship]] — YHWH as the true sovereign whose word topples and plants nations (1:10); earthly kings (Jehoiakim, Zedekiah) are judged against the covenant.
- The suffering prophet — the "Confessions" (e.g. 20:7–18) make Jeremiah the prototype of the prophet who is crushed by the word he must speak; a template later read onto the Servant of Isaiah and onto Jesus.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **Jeremiah 31:31–34 is the origin of the word "Testament."** When early Christians called their scriptures the "New Testament," they were quoting Jeremiah: the Greek *diathēkē* ("covenant / testament") in "new covenant" (Jer 31:31 LXX) is the term the church applied first to the eucharistic cup ("this cup is the new covenant in my blood," 1 Cor 11:25) and then to its entire second canon. Hebrews 8:8–12 quotes Jeremiah 31 in full — the longest single Old Testament quotation anywhere in the New Testament — to argue that the first covenant is "obsolete." The interior, heart-written covenant of an obscure 6th-century-BCE Judahite prophet thus supplied Christianity both its name for itself and its core supersessionist argument (Lundbom 2004, AYB 21B pp.465–474; Holladay 1989, Hermeneia 2 pp.197–199).
- **The two-edition problem.** Jeremiah is the textbook proof that "the original text of the Bible" is a moving target: two substantially different Hebrew editions of a biblical book circulated simultaneously in the Second Temple period, and the Qumran scrolls preserve both. This single book reframes how scholars think about the fluidity of scripture before standardization (Tov 1976; Tov 2012, *Textual Criticism of the Hebrew Bible*, 3rd ed., pp.286–294).
