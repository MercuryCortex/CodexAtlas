# Mahāvaṃsa

## What it is
The **Mahāvaṃsa** ("Great Chronicle") is a Pali epic-historical poem composed at the Mahāvihāra monastery of Anurādhapura, traditionally by the monk **Mahānāma** in the early sixth century CE. In about thirty-seven chapters of verse it narrates the history of Sri Lanka from the (legendary) visits of the Buddha to the island, through the arrival of the first Sinhala king Vijaya, the conversion of the island to Buddhism under **Mahinda** (son of the emperor [[ashoka]]), the planting of the sacred Bodhi-tree cutting brought by Saṅghamittā, and the deeds of the warrior-king Duṭṭhagāmaṇī. It is a continuation and elaboration of the older, plainer **Dīpavaṃsa** (4th c. CE) and of a now-lost Sinhala commentarial chronicle kept at the Mahāvihāra. A medieval continuation, the **Cūḷavaṃsa**, carries the narrative down to the eighteenth century. The Mahāvaṃsa is at once a religious work (the history of the Dhamma on the island), a dynastic chronicle, and a charter for the relationship between the Sinhala monarchy and the Buddhist *saṅgha*.

## Primary text — selected passages

### The Buddha's resolve concerning Laṅkā (Mahāvaṃsa I)
*Trans. Geiger, W. (1912). The Mahāvaṃsa. Pali Text Society.*
> The Conqueror... seeing that Laṅkā would be a place where his doctrine should (thereafter) shine in glory, and (seeing) that from Laṅkā... it would spread abroad, came hither.

*The chronicle opens by making Sri Lanka the divinely-appointed home of the pure Dhamma. The Buddha is said to visit the island three times, subduing its non-human inhabitants and consecrating the sites of future monasteries. This frames the entire history that follows: Laṅkā is the *dhammadīpa*, the "island of the Dhamma," chosen by the Buddha himself.*

### Mahinda brings the Dhamma (Mahāvaṃsa XIV)
*Trans. Geiger 1912.*
> The thera Mahinda, the light of Laṅkā... having alighted on the fair Missaka mountain, preached the doctrine to the king and to the people.

*The conversion of King Devānaṃpiyatissa by Mahinda — Aśoka's missionary son — is the hinge of the chronicle: the moment the imperial Mauryan Buddhism of [[ashoka]] is transplanted to Lanka and becomes the state religion. The synchronism between Aśoka and the Sinhala king is the chronological backbone that lets historians anchor the chronicle to absolute dates.*

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Anurādhapura-period Sri Lanka, with the Mahāvihāra as the dominant (and self-consciously orthodox) monastic establishment competing with the Abhayagiri and Jetavana monasteries. The chronicle is in part a partisan Mahāvihāra document.
- **Religious milieu.** Theravāda Pali Buddhism, with the chronicle asserting the island's role as the preserver of the pure canonical tradition — including the claim that the Pali Tipiṭaka was first committed to writing in Sri Lanka (1st c. BCE).
- **Who was behind it.** The verse Mahāvaṃsa is ascribed to Mahānāma, said to be an uncle of King Dhātusena; he reworked older Sinhala chronicle material into elegant Pali kāvya.
- **Transmission.** Preserved at the Mahāvihāra and continued as the Cūḷavaṃsa; critically edited and translated by Wilhelm Geiger for the Pali Text Society.

## Themes and motifs
- [[divine-kingship]] — kingship and the *saṅgha* are presented as mutually constituting: the king protects and purifies the order, the order legitimates the king.
- religion and national identity — the Mahāvaṃsa fuses ethnic (Sinhala), territorial (the island), and religious (Buddhist) identity into a single narrative — a fusion with enormous modern political consequences.
- **Relic and tree** — the chronicle is structured around the arrival and enshrinement of relics (the Buddha's relics, the Bodhi-tree cutting), each founding a sacred site and legitimating a king.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-3-030-asokan-edicts]] — *historical-synchronism*: the Mahāvaṃsa's account of Aśoka and his son Mahinda dovetails with the Aśokan inscriptions; the "Mahāvaṃsa synchronism" (Aśoka's contemporaneity with named Hellenistic kings) is a key peg for ancient Indian chronology. Ref: von Hinüber 1996.
- → [[jataka-tales]] — *shared-tradition*: both belong to the Pali narrative-historical literature of the Theravāda; the chronicle deploys *jātaka*-style moral framing for its kings.
- → [[event-buddha-parinirvana-c-400-bce]] — *reckons-from*: the chronicle dates its events from the Buddha's parinirvāṇa, and its dead-reckoning is one source for the traditional Theravāda Buddha-era chronology.

## Disputes
- **Historical reliability.** The early chapters (Vijaya, the Buddha's visits) are legend; the later dynastic sections become progressively more reliable. Scholars debate how far the Duṭṭhagāmaṇī narrative reflects 2nd-c.-BCE events versus 6th-c.-CE concerns.
- **Dating of composition.** Sixth century CE for the verse Mahāvaṃsa is standard (von Hinüber), but its sources reach back centuries earlier.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges

- **A 1,500-year-old chronicle still load-bearing in a modern ethnic conflict**: The Mahāvaṃsa's framing of Sri Lanka as the *dhammadīpa* (island of the Dhamma) chosen by the Buddha, and its heroic portrayal of King Duṭṭhagāmaṇī's war against the Tamil king Eḷāra — softened by monks who reassure the king that the slain "were but beasts" in unbelief — has been read in the twentieth century as a charter of Sinhala-Buddhist nationalism and invoked in the rhetoric of the Sri Lankan civil war. Few ancient religious chronicles have so directly shaped a living political conflict (Kemper 1991, *The Presence of the Past*, Cornell UP; → religion and national identity).

- **The chronicle that fixes ancient Indian chronology**: Because the Mahāvaṃsa synchronises the Sinhala conversion with the reign of [[ashoka]], and because Aśoka's own edicts name datable Hellenistic kings, the Pali chronicle tradition provides one of the few absolute pegs by which the otherwise floating chronology of ancient India can be anchored. A Buddhist monastic history written in Sri Lanka became, for nineteenth- and twentieth-century Western historians, a primary instrument for dating the Mauryan empire (von Hinüber 1996, *A Handbook of Pāli Literature*, de Gruyter; → [[phase-3-030-asokan-edicts]]).
