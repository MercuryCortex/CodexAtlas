# Zhou Li (Rites of Zhou)

## What it is
The **Rites of Zhou** — one of the Three Ritual Classics of the Confucian canon — is an elaborate, idealized description of the **administrative organization of the Zhou state**. It sets out a complete government of some 360 offices grouped under six great ministries named for Heaven, Earth, and the four seasons, specifying each official's title, rank, staff, and duties in exhaustive detail. Although the text presents itself as a record of the institutions of the early Western Zhou (and was traditionally credited to the Duke of Zhou), scholars regard it as a **utopian-systematic compilation of the late Warring States to early Han** — a blueprint for ideal government rather than a historical record.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Composed in the centuries of Warring-States consolidation and early empire, when thinkers projected a perfectly-ordered state onto the revered Zhou past.
- **Religious milieu.** Ritual (*li*) is, in the Confucian view, the cosmic-social order itself; the *Zhou Li* fuses administration and ritual into a single sacral system of government.
- **Who was behind it.** Anonymous compilers in the Confucian ritual tradition.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Canonized among the classics; commented on continuously and repeatedly used as a charter for reform.

## Themes and motifs
- [[divine-kingship]] — the *Zhou Li* models the ruler as the pivot of a cosmically-ordered bureaucracy, sovereignty expressed as perfect administrative-ritual order.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[tradition-confucianism]] — *canonical-member*: one of the Three Ritual Classics that anchor the Confucian vision of ordered society.

## Disputes
- **Date and authorship.** The Duke-of-Zhou ascription is rejected; the text is a Warring-States/Han systematization, though it may preserve genuine older institutional memory (Loewe & Shaughnessy 1999).

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **The reform-charter of the Chinese state.** Because it offered a complete, authoritative model of ideal government, the *Zhou Li* was repeatedly invoked to justify sweeping reform — Wang Mang's Xin-dynasty revolution and Wang Anshi's 11th-century New Policies both grounded themselves in it — making a ritual classic into one of the most politically consequential texts in Chinese history (Loewe & Shaughnessy 1999; Nylan 2001).
- **The template of the cosmic city.** The *Zhou Li*'s idealized capital — a walled square oriented to the cardinal directions with the ruler at the centre — became the canonical model for Chinese (and, by transmission, Korean and Japanese) imperial city-planning, a sacral geometry of sovereignty.
