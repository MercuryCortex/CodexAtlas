# Vilayetname-i Hacı Bektaş Veli

## What it is

The *Vilayetname* ("Book of Sainthood" or "Book of Provinces" — *vilayet* carries both senses in Ottoman Turkish, punning on the saint's spiritual sovereignty and his geographic domain) is the primary hagiographic text of the [[tradition-alevi-bektashi|Alevi-Bektashi tradition]]. It records the miracles, travels, spiritual encounters, and supernatural deeds attributed to [[haci-bektas-veli|Hacı Bektaş Veli]]. The text is not by Hacı Bektaş — it was composed approximately one to two generations after his death (traditional dates 1271), in the milieu of the Hacıbektaş lodge in Cappadocia. The Gölpınarlı critical edition (1958) is the standard scholarly reference; earlier manuscript traditions show variation suggesting the text was actively expanded and modified through the 15th–16th centuries.

## Context and historical significance

Irène Mélikoff's central argument in *Hadji Bektach: un mythe et ses avatars* is that the *Vilayetname* should be read as a document of **15th-century Anatolian heterodox religion** rather than as a reliable biography of a 13th-century saint. The text was produced precisely during the period when the Bektashi order was being institutionalized — under and after Balım Sultan (d. 1516) — and when the tradition needed a foundational narrative that could compete with other Sufi orders' hagiographic literatures. The hagiographic genre (*manaqib* in Arabic, *menakibname* in Ottoman Turkish) was a standard mechanism by which Sufi orders legitimated their lineages; the *Vilayetname* is the Bektashi contribution to this tradition.

Its theological significance is undiminished by the hagiographic character: the text is the tradition's self-definition document, encoding what the Bektashi milieu in 15th-century Anatolia believed, valued, and claimed as its spiritual genealogy.

## Themes and motifs

**The lion and the deer:** In the narrative's opening episodes, Hacı Bektaş Veli is accompanied by a lion and a deer who serve as his animal familiars and submit to his authority. This animal-familiar motif has no parallel in mainstream Sufi hagiography; it echoes pre-Islamic Anatolian nature-spirit traditions and shamanic-adjacent practices documented elsewhere in Central Asian and Anatolian popular religion. The lion in particular carries layers: it is also the symbol of [[ali-ibn-abi-talib]] (Asad Allah, "Lion of God"), so the image simultaneously signals pre-Islamic animal-spirit veneration and Shia-inflected devotion to Ali — the syncretism is visible at the level of a single image.

**Kadıncık Ana:** The female saint Kadıncık Ana ("the Matron Mother") appears as a close companion, host, and spiritual interlocutor of Hacı Bektaş throughout the narrative. Her role is extraordinary: she receives the saint at her home, serves as his primary Anatolian host, and participates in the spiritual drama of the text as an autonomous agent. Female saints of this prominence and autonomy are essentially absent from mainstream Sufi hagiographic literature. Mélikoff reads Kadıncık Ana as evidence of the pre-Islamic Anatolian substrate — possibly a goddess-veneration trace repackaged in Islamic hagiographic form.

**Sufi genealogy and the Khorasan connection:** The narrative constructs a spiritual lineage running from Hacı Bektaş through Ahmad Yesevi (the major Turkic Sufi master of the 12th century) and into the Alid genealogy — claiming Hacı Bektaş as a descendant of the Imam lineage. This genealogical claim functions to simultaneously root the tradition in Central Asian Sufi legitimacy and in Shia imamate authority. Whether historically accurate (almost certainly not, in the direct genealogical sense), the claim is theologically functional: it encodes the two parent traditions ([[tradition-sufism]] and [[tradition-shia-islam]]) as the tradition's founding DNA.

**Quranic and theological content:** The text is interspersed with Quranic citations and theological commentary, but the framework is fundamentally hagiographic rather than doctrinal. The miracles attributed to Hacı Bektaş — flying over mountains, transforming into a dove, commanding wild animals — follow a standard Sufi miracle-narrative (*karamat*) pattern, but with the pre-Islamic animal-familiar and female-saint elements layered in. The theological treatise *Makalat* (attributed separately to Hacı Bektaş) provides the doctrinal complement; the *Vilayetname* is the narrative complement.

## The syncretism in the text

The *Vilayetname* is perhaps the most compact single document demonstrating the Alevi-Bektashi synthesis visible in one place:

- **Quran citations + Ali veneration** — the Islamic and Shia-Islamic layer
- **Sufi miracle-narrative (*karamat*) + pir-disciple lineage** — the Sufi organizational layer
- **Shamanic animal familiars + female saint** — the pre-Islamic Anatolian substrate layer
- **Khorasan Sufi genealogy (Ahmad Yesevi lineage)** — the Central Asian transmission claim
- **Alid genealogy (descent from the Imams)** — the Shia legitimacy claim

All five are present and interwoven in the same text. This makes it an exceptional document for cross-tradition investigation: it is not a document that *argues* for synthesis, but one that *performs* synthesis as natural — which suggests the synthesis was already experientially integrated in the community that produced it.
