# Ḥikmat al-Ishrāq (The Philosophy of Illumination)

## What it is
Suhrawardī's principal philosophical work, composed in Arabic in 1186 at Aleppo. The text founds the *Ishrāqī* (Illuminationist) tradition — a metaphysics in which the ultimate reality is *Nūr al-Anwār* (the Light of Lights), and existence is structured as graded degrees of light and shadow, with all beings essentially *lights* of greater or lesser intensity perceiving and being perceived in chains of illumination. The system explicitly displaces the standard Peripatetic (Avicennan) categories of essence and existence with light-language: instead of analyzing being in terms of *wujūd* and *māhiyya*, Suhrawardī analyzes it in terms of *nūr* and *ghasaq* (light and darkness). Knowledge is *presence* (*ḥuḍūr*) — direct, non-conceptual awareness — modeled on the way light illuminates itself; not representational knowledge through *intentional forms*, which Suhrawardī attacks as confused. The work is preceded by a critique of Peripatetic logic and metaphysics (Part I, the *Mantiq*) and culminates in mystical psychology and eschatology. Most striking: Suhrawardī claims his philosophy is *not new* but the recovery of an ancient wisdom shared by the Greek (Plato, Pythagoras) and Persian (Zoroaster, Kayūmarth, Jāmāsp) sages, lost and now restored.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Ayyubid Syria — Saladin (Ṣalāḥ al-Dīn) had recently expelled the Crusaders from Jerusalem (1187) and consolidated the region. Suhrawardī was patronized by Saladin's son al-Malik al-Ẓāhir, governor of Aleppo. He was executed in 1191 on the order of Saladin himself (transmitted through the Aleppo governor), at the urging of local ulama who accused him of heresy. The cause of execution is variously reported: claims to direct revelation, denial of the seal of prophecy, or simply too-influential a presence at a Sunni-orthodox court trying to manage its public theology. Suhrawardī's epithet *al-Maqtūl* (the executed one) has become his standard title.
- **Religious milieu.** Late 12th-c. Eastern Islamic world: post-Ghazālī Sunni-Sufi synthesis was the orthodox horizon; Avicennan falsafa was the philosophical horizon, partially under suspicion. Suhrawardī occupies a position one step further toward the heterodox edge than Ghazālī — taking philosophy more seriously than Ghazālī did and synthesizing it with explicit Zoroastrian and pre-Islamic-Greek motifs. He claimed to have revived an esoteric perennial wisdom transmitted through both Greek and Persian sage-prophets.
- **Who was behind it.** Suhrawardī himself: from Suhraward in northwestern Iran; studied at Marāgheh under Majd al-Dīn al-Jīlī (also teacher of Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī, who became Suhrawardī's effective philosophical adversary). Traveled widely in Iran, Anatolia, and Syria before settling at Aleppo. Wrote four major systematic works plus many shorter visionary recitals in Persian (the *qiṣṣa-yi ghurbat al-gharbiyya* "Tale of Western Exile" being the most famous), influenced by Avicenna's own visionary recitals (*Salāmān wa Absāl*, *Ḥayy ibn Yaqẓān*).
- **Discovery / transmission.** Suhrawardī's school flourished in Iranian, Anatolian, and Indian Sufi-philosophical circles through the medieval and early-modern periods; in the West, his discovery was largely the work of Henry Corbin (1903–1978), who edited the corpus and presented Suhrawardī as the central figure of "*spiritual Iran*" — a controversial framing that has shaped subsequent Western reception.

## Themes and motifs
- [[logos-cosmic-reason]] — the cosmos is structured by the chain of intelligences / lights, an Avicennan inheritance recoded in light-metaphysics.
- [[gnosis-as-salvation]] — knowledge is *presence* (*ḥuḍūr*), not representation; liberation is direct awareness of one's own light.
- [[soul-as-spark]] — human souls are *lights* of intermediate degree; they descend from and may return to the supernal light-orders.
- [[hidden-god]] — the *Light of Lights* is in itself overwhelmingly bright, blinding to lower lights; the structure of revelation is graded mediation.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-3-002-plato-dialogues]] — *direct-quote*: Suhrawardī cites Plato by name, especially the Sun analogy of *Republic* VI and the Cave; reads Plato as the Greek prophet of illuminationism.
- → [[phase-2-002-gathas-of-zarathustra]] / Zoroastrianism — *direct-influence*: Suhrawardī explicitly identifies his "light of lights" with Zoroastrian *Ohrmazd*, the supernal light-orders with the Amesha Spentas, and uses Pahlavi-Iranian sage-prophet names (Kayūmarth, Jāmāsp) as authorities. This is the most explicit medieval-Islamic revival of Zoroastrian metaphysical vocabulary inside Sunni Islamic frame.
- → [[phase-4-019-plotinus-enneads]] — *direct-influence* (via *Theology of Aristotle*): the emanative structure is broadly Plotinian.
- → Avicenna (Ibn Sīnā) — *redaction-of* / *polemic-against*: Suhrawardī systematically modifies and critiques Avicennan Peripatetic logic and metaphysics; the *Ḥikmat al-Ishrāq* opens with a logical critique.
- → [[phase-5-011-rasail-ikhwan-al-safa]] — *direct-influence*: shared Neoplatonized Islamic cosmology.
- → Mullā Ṣadrā (Ṣadr al-Dīn Shīrāzī, ~1640) — *direct-influence*: Suhrawardī is one of Mullā Ṣadrā's two principal sources (the other being Ibn ʿArabī); the Safavid Isfahan school of Iranian philosophy is unintelligible without Suhrawardī.

## Disputes
- **Was Suhrawardī Shi'a?** Inconclusive; he wrote within a Sunni-Ayyubid context but his Iranian inheritance includes proto-Shi'i sympathies. Corbin read Suhrawardī as a deep Shi'a / proto-Imāmī esoterist; this is contested.
- **The "Ancient Wisdom" claim.** Whether Suhrawardī's claim of recovering a perennial Greek-Persian sage-tradition is historically grounded (Walbridge 2000 argues it is) or pious fiction (an older scholarly position) remains a live debate.
- **Cause of execution.** Heresy charges (claim to direct revelation; *waḥdat al-wujūd*-style ontological claims) vs. political (too close to a son of Saladin whose father wanted to display Sunni orthodoxy). Probably both.
