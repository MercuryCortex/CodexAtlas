# Meqabyan I–III (Ethiopian Maccabees)

## What it is
The **Meqabyan** are three Ge'ez books in the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Old Testament canonical scripture, conventionally called "Ethiopian Maccabees" in English. They are **unique to the Ethiopian canon** — neither the Jewish biblical canon, the LXX/Catholic-deuterocanonical, the Protestant canon, nor any other Eastern Orthodox tradition contains them. Despite the name they are *not* translations of the Jewish/LXX Maccabean books (1–2 Maccabees recount the Maccabean revolt under Antiochus IV; 3 Maccabees is a Ptolemaic-era persecution narrative; 4 Maccabees is a Hellenistic-philosophical reflection on martyrdom). The Ge'ez Meqabyan share *only the rubric* "Maccabees" with the LXX corpus; their narrative content is entirely distinct.

The three books contain a long narrative around three figures named Abya, Seela, and Fentos — Ethiopian-pseudonymous legendary characters described as witnessing under foreign-pagan persecution; the framing is theological-pastoral, presenting the figures as exemplars of fidelity under pressure. The texts incorporate substantial moral-theological and apocalyptic material and reflect the broader medieval-Ethiopian theological-pastoral concerns of fidelity and martyrdom.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Medieval Ethiopia, probably from the late-Aksumite to the high Solomonic period. Some scholars see an earlier (Aksumite, 6th–9th c.) substratum overlaid with later (Solomonic, 13th–15th c.) elaboration; the dating is open.
- **Religious milieu.** Ethiopian Christianity in its broader-canon-retaining mode. The Meqabyan operate as scriptural witness to martyrological-pastoral themes; they are read alongside Jewish-canonical and uniquely-Ethiopian-canonical texts as scripture.
- **Who was behind it.** Anonymous Ethiopian medieval composition. The "Maccabean" rubric is *post hoc*: the figures Abya, Seela, and Fentos are not Maccabees in any historical or Hellenistic-Jewish sense; the rubric likely reflects an editorial decision to give canonical-textual position to an indigenous Ethiopian martyrological narrative complex within the Maccabean-shaped "honored witnesses of antiquity" slot of the broader OT canon.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous Ethiopian-monastic-biblical-codex transmission. Critically under-studied in Western scholarship; Cowley 2007 and Ullendorff 1968 are the standard treatments.

## Themes and motifs
- [[martyrdom-theology]] — the central pastoral-theological concern: fidelity under persecution.
- [[ethiopic-canon-broad]] — exemplary case of the uniqueness of the Ethiopian Orthodox biblical canon: a fully-canonical text found nowhere else.
- [[pseudonymity-as-strategy]] — the "Maccabean" rubric is pseudonymously deployed to position the narrative within the inherited OT-canonical framework.

## Connections (provisional)
- → Jewish 1–4 Maccabees (LXX) — *parallel-form* / *unrelated*: only the name is shared; the content is distinct. The relationship is best understood as *nominal* rather than substantive.
- → [[phase-4-082-ethiopic-biblical-canon]] — *constitutive-component*: among the most distinctive uniquely-Ethiopian-canonical texts.
- → other uniquely-Ethiopian-canonical texts — *shared-position*: structurally parallel to the canonical status of [[phase-4-081-mashafa-henok-geez-1-enoch]] and Jubilees in the Ethiopian canon.

## Disputes
- **Date of composition.** Variously placed 6th–14th c. CE; no consensus. The Aksumite substratum hypothesis is unproven; the medieval-Solomonic high-canon-period elaboration is widely accepted.
- **Relationship to LXX Maccabees.** Modern scholars uniformly hold that the Ge'ez Meqabyan are *not* translations of any of the LXX Maccabean books. The relationship is nominal-rubrical.
- **Authorship and language layer.** Whether the text reflects translation from an Arabic or Coptic Vorlage or is an original Ge'ez composition. Most scholarship favors original Ge'ez composition; some see Arabic-Coptic substrata.
- **Why the "Maccabees" name.** Some scholars (e.g., Curtin) hypothesize that medieval Ethiopian theology positioned the Meqabyan narratives in the canonical slot formerly held by Jewish-Maccabean material (which the Ethiopic OT also contains in shorter form). The exact reasoning is unrecovered.
