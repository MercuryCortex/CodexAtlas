# Heart Sutra

## What it is
The shortest and most-recited text in Mahāyāna Buddhism. In its standard 260-character Chinese form, it presents Avalokiteśvara, "coursing in the deep prajñāpāramitā," declaring to Śāriputra that the five aggregates (skandhas) are empty (śūnya), and developing a sequence of negations: no eye, no ear, no nose, no tongue, no body, no mind; no form, no sound, no smell, no taste, no touch, no thought-object; no ignorance and no extinction of ignorance, no aging-and-death and no extinction of aging-and-death; no suffering, no origination, no cessation, no path; no wisdom, no attainment, and no non-attainment. It culminates in the dhāraṇī "gate gate pāragate pārasaṃgate bodhi svāhā" ("gone, gone, gone beyond, gone altogether beyond, awakening, hail"). The text compresses the entire Prajñāpāramitā corpus's central insight — emptiness (śūnyatā) — into a recitable formula.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** The Tang dynasty under Taizong (r. 626–649) — a high-water mark of Buddhist patronage in China and of Sino-Indian intellectual exchange via the Silk Road. Xuanzang's 17-year journey to India and back (629–645) and his subsequent translation bureau at Chang'an were imperially sponsored.
- **Religious milieu.** Chinese Buddhism was reaching its classical phase: the Sui-Tang formation of the Tiantai, Huayan, Chan, and Pure Land schools. Xuanzang founded the Faxiang (Yogācāra) school. The Heart Sūtra in his recension became the single most chanted text of East Asian Buddhism — beyond any individual school.
- **Who was behind it.** This is where the live scholarly dispute begins. *Traditional view*: a Sanskrit sūtra of Indian Mahāyāna origin, translated into Chinese by Kumārajīva (~400) and then by Xuanzang (~649). *Nattier 1992 view (now majority)*: the *Chinese* text is the original — assembled by an unknown Chinese editor (perhaps Xuanzang himself, perhaps an earlier hand) from passages of the Chinese Pañcaviṃśatisāhasrikā (Kumārajīva's *Da Pin Bore Jing*) plus a closing mantra; the "Sanskrit Heart Sūtra" is a *back-translation* into Sanskrit, with telltale grammatical and terminological evidence of Chinese-source origin. This is Buddhism's most consequential apocryphal-text debate of the late 20th century.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous transmission in East Asian Buddhism; never lost. Tibetan tradition received the long recension (with framing narrative) via 8th–9th c. translations and developed a major commentary tradition (eight extant Indian commentaries on the long recension preserved in Tibetan, plus many Tibetan commentaries — Lopez 1988 surveys them).

## Themes and motifs
- [[emptiness-sunyata]] — *the* canonical compressed statement: "form is emptiness, emptiness is form" (色即是空 空即是色).
- [[non-duality]] — the negation-chain collapses the standard Buddhist categories (skandhas, āyatanas, Four Noble Truths) themselves, showing they too are empty of self-existence.
- [[wisdom-personified]] — Prajñāpāramitā as the "mother of all Buddhas" (background, not foregrounded in the Heart Sūtra specifically but the framing tradition); the text functions as a mantra-condensation of the goddess.

## Connections (provisional)
- → larger Prajñāpāramitā sūtras (Aṣṭasāhasrikā, Pañcaviṃśatisāhasrikā) — *redaction-of* / *direct-quote*: per Nattier, the Chinese Heart Sūtra reuses sections of the Chinese Pañcaviṃśatisāhasrikā verbatim plus the standard *gate gate* mantra ending.
- → [[phase-5-004-platform-sutra-huineng]] — *direct-influence*: Chan / Zen lineage canonically chants the Heart Sūtra; Huineng's "sudden awakening" pedagogy is structurally a Heart-Sūtra-style negation chain applied to self.
- → Nāgārjuna's *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* (~200 CE) — *direct-influence*: Madhyamaka's negation method is the philosophical apparatus the Heart Sūtra ritualizes.

## Disputes
- **Origin.** Indian Sanskrit original (older view, Conze) vs. Chinese composition back-translated to Sanskrit (Nattier 1992, now majority). The latter view is partly resisted by some Indian and Tibetan scholars but is now the working scholarly consensus in Western Buddhist studies (Lopez, Tanahashi, Harada).
- **Phase placement.** Standard form is post-Xuanzang (after 649 CE); a Sanskrit short-text predecessor, if it existed, would push the earliest layer back into Phase 4. Seeded here under Phase 5 because the standard text and the global liturgical influence are Tang-onward.
