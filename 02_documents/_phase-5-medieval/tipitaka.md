# Tipiṭaka (the Pali Canon)

## What it is
The **Tipiṭaka** ("Three Baskets") is the complete scriptural canon of **Theravāda Buddhism** and the most fully preserved canon of early Buddhism in any language. It is organized in three divisions: the **Vinaya Piṭaka**, the rules and case-law of the monastic order; the **Sutta Piṭaka**, the discourses attributed to the Buddha and his disciples, gathered in five collections (*nikāyas*); and the **Abhidhamma Piṭaka**, a later systematic-philosophical analysis of mind and phenomena. Together these form a vast library — many times the length of the Bible — that was carried for centuries in the memory of specialist reciters before being written down.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** The teaching originates in the city-states and kingdoms of the 5th-century-BCE Gangetic plain; its canonization is bound up with Mauryan patronage ([[phase-3-030-asokan-edicts]]) and its preservation with the Sinhalese monarchy.
- **Religious milieu.** The canon took shape through the early Buddhist **councils** (traditionally at Rājagaha, Vesālī, and Pāṭaliputra) and the reciter (*bhāṇaka*) lineages who specialized in memorizing particular collections.
- **Who was behind it.** The monastic community (*saṅgha*), collectively; no single author.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Oral for some four centuries, then written at the Aluvihāra (~29 BCE) as famine and war threatened the reciter lineages; thereafter copied across Theravāda Asia.

## Themes and motifs
- [[anatman-no-self]] — the analysis of experience into impermanent, not-self processes is the canon's philosophical core, systematized in the Abhidhamma.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-2-029-dhammapada]] / [[mahaparinibbana-sutta]] / [[jataka-tales]] / [[phase-2-016-early-buddhist-suttas]] — *constituent*: these are texts *within* the Tipiṭaka (the Dhammapada and Jātaka in the Khuddaka Nikāya; the Mahāparinibbāna in the Dīgha).
- → [[phase-3-031-asthasahasrika-prajnaparamita]] — *divergence*: the Mahāyāna sūtras define themselves against, and beyond, the canon the Tipiṭaka represents.
- → [[phase-4-076-visuddhimagga]] — *commentary-on*: Buddhaghosa's 5th-century synthesis organizes and interprets the canon.
- → [[phase-4-077-abhidharmakosa]] — *parallel*: Vasubandhu's treatise represents the (largely lost) Sarvāstivāda canonical-Abhidharma tradition that paralleled the Pali.

## Disputes
- **Authenticity and age.** How much of the Sutta Piṭaka transmits the historical Buddha's own words versus later monastic formulation is a central, unresolved question; the Abhidhamma is agreed to be later.
- **The councils.** The historicity and decisions of the early councils, and the date of the Aluvihāra writing-down, are debated within a broad consensus.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **The base text of half of Buddhism — and the control on the rest.** The Tipiṭaka is the scriptural root of the entire Theravāda world and the comparative baseline against which the Mahāyāna and Vajrayāna developments are measured; the deity- and figure-layers of South and Southeast Asian Buddhism in this vault hang from it (Norman 1983; Gethin 1998).
- **The largest surviving oral-to-written transmission in religious history.** That a library of this scale was held in memory for four centuries and then fixed in writing makes the Tipiṭaka a uniquely documented case of how scripture crystallizes out of oral tradition — the very process the Documentary Hypothesis reconstructs, here attested (von Hinüber 1996).
