# Samguk Sagi (History of the Three Kingdoms)

## What it is
The **oldest surviving history of Korea**, compiled by the Goryeo scholar-official **Kim Busik** and presented to the throne in 1145. Modelled on the classical Chinese histories of Sima Qian, its fifty volumes combine annals of the three ancient kingdoms — **Goguryeo, Baekje, and Silla** — with chronological tables, topical treatises, and a series of biographies, producing a sober, Confucian-rationalist narrative of Korea from its legendary beginnings to the Silla unification. It is the bedrock documentary source for the Three Kingdoms period.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Mid-Goryeo Korea; Kim Busik, a powerful minister, wrote to give the dynasty a properly Confucian national history on the Chinese model.
- **Religious milieu.** Its Confucian rationalism deliberately downplays the myth and miracle that the Buddhist [[samguk-yusa|Samguk Yusa]] would later restore — the two works together define the Korean historiographical inheritance.
- **Who was behind it.** A royal commission under Kim Busik.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Transmitted from Goryeo through Joseon printings; the oldest surviving edition is of 1394/1512.

## Themes and motifs
- [[divine-kingship]] — even in its rationalist mode, the *Sagi* records the heaven-favoured founders of the three kingdoms, the sacral legitimation of Korean royal lines.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[samguk-yusa]] — *complement*: Iryeon's later collection supplies the myth, religion, and folk material Kim Busik's official history left out; the two are read together.

## Disputes
- **Confucian selectivity.** Kim Busik's rationalist, Silla-centric, Chinese-modelled framework shaped (and limited) what he recorded; modern historians read him critically against archaeology and the *Yusa*.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **The documentary floor of Korean history.** As the oldest and most systematic record of the Three Kingdoms, the *Samguk Sagi* is the source against which every later reconstruction of early Korea — its kings, institutions, and its reception of Buddhism and Confucianism from China — must be measured (Shultz & Kang 2012; Seth 2010). Its very Confucian-rationalist omissions are what called the [[samguk-yusa|Samguk Yusa]] into being, a paired tension between official and religious memory that the Atlas tracks across many traditions.
