# Da Qing Huidian

## What it is
The **Da Qing Huidian** ("Collected Statutes of the Great Qing") is the administrative-statutory compendium of the Qing dynasty, modelled directly on its Ming predecessor the **[[da-ming-huidian]]**. Issued in five successive imperial editions between 1690 (Kangxi) and 1899 (Guangxu), it codifies the structure and procedures of the Qing government office by office. From the Qianlong edition (1764) on, the relatively stable *huidian* (statutes) were paired with vast, frequently-updated *zeli/shili* ("precedents") that recorded the actual administrative decisions case by case — so the work functions both as a constitutional outline and as a living archive of bureaucratic practice. Because the Qing was a Manchu conquest empire ruling Inner Asia as well as China proper, the Da Qing Huidian also codifies institutions that have no Ming equivalent — above all the **Lifan Yuan** (the court that managed Mongolia, Tibet, and their Tibetan-Buddhist religious hierarchies).

## Primary text — the document's architecture
- **By ministry and office**, as in the Ming model: the Six Ministries and their bureaus, each with its statutes and precedents.
- **The Lifan Yuan statutes.** A distinctive Qing addition: the regulations governing the empire's relations with Mongol nobles and the Tibetan-Buddhist clergy — including the legal framework for recognising reincarnate lamas and managing the Dalai and Panchen establishments.
- **State ritual.** As under the Ming, the Ministry-of-Rites sections fix the imperial sacrificial cult and the rank-graded regulation of ritual structures.

*The pairing of stable "statutes" with ever-growing "precedents" makes the Da Qing Huidian a remarkably honest record of the gap between an administrative ideal and its endless real-world adjustment.*

## Context
- **Political milieu.** The Qing dual structure — a Confucian bureaucratic state for China proper, plus distinct Manchu and Inner-Asian institutions — all gathered into one statutory framework.
- **Religious milieu.** Besides the Confucian state cult inherited from the Ming, the Qing statutes uniquely regulate the state's management of Tibetan Buddhism as an instrument of frontier governance.
- **Who was behind it.** Successive imperially-appointed editorial boards across two centuries.
- **Transmission.** Five woodblock editions (1690–1899); a core primary source for Qing institutional history.

## Themes and motifs
- [[divine-kingship]] — the Qing emperor as both Confucian Son of Heaven and (toward Inner Asia) a Buddhist *cakravartin*/bodhisattva-patron; the statutes encode this multivalent sacred kingship.
- [[theme-divine-kingship-and-temple-as-royal-pact]] — rank-graded regulation of ritual structures continues the Ming model.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[da-ming-huidian]] — *modelled-on*: the Qing Huidian deliberately follows the Ming Huidian's form, making the pair a natural comparison for continuity and change across the Ming–Qing transition. Ref: Wilkinson 2018.
- → [[tradition-tibetan-buddhism]] — *state-regulation-of-religion*: through the Lifan Yuan statutes, the Da Qing Huidian is the legal-administrative framework by which the Manchu state managed Tibetan Buddhism and the great incarnate-lama lineages. Ref: Elliott 2001.
- → [[phase-2-033-shujing-book-of-documents]] — *classical-charter*: the documentary-bureaucratic ideal descends ultimately from the Book of Documents.

## Disputes
- **Statute vs. precedent.** Scholars debate how to read the stable huidian against the voluminous zeli/shili — which better reflects "real" Qing governance.
- **Sinicization vs. Manchu distinctiveness.** The Inner-Asian institutions codified in the statutes are central to the long debate over how "Chinese" the Qing state really was (the "New Qing History").

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges

- **A Confucian statute-book that legally administers Tibetan Buddhism**: The Lifan Yuan sections of the Da Qing Huidian are a striking case of one religious-political order codifying its management of another religion: a Confucian-bureaucratic compendium that lays down the legal procedures by which the Manchu emperor — posing toward Inner Asia as a Buddhist *cakravartin* and patron of the Dalai Lama — recognised reincarnate lamas and governed the Tibetan-Buddhist establishments of Tibet and Mongolia. The same emperor is Son of Heaven in Beijing and Buddhist protector on the frontier, and a single statute-book holds both roles together (Elliott 2001, *The Manchu Way*, Stanford UP; → [[tradition-tibetan-buddhism]]).
