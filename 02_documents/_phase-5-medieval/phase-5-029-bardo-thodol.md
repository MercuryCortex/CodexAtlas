# Bardo Thödöl (Tibetan Book of the Dead)

## What it is
A Tibetan Vajrayāna Buddhist guidebook for the dying and the recently deceased — recited in the presence of the dying person and over the corpse for up to 49 days post-death, to guide consciousness through the *bardo* (intermediate state) between death and rebirth, with the aim of recognizing the true nature of mind and so achieving liberation rather than another rebirth. The text describes a graded sequence of bardos: the *chikai bardo* (moment of death and immediate post-death — when the *clear light* of awareness, identical with the dharma-body, dawns and is to be recognized); the *chönyi bardo* (luminous bardo of dharmatā — over six days the Peaceful Deities of the five Buddha families appear, embodying the awakened nature of the five aggregates; if these are not recognized, the Wrathful Deities appear over the next seven days, embodying the awakened nature of the five afflictions); the *sidpai bardo* (bardo of becoming — leading to rebirth in one of the six realms unless liberation is attained). The pedagogical framing assumes a Tantric initiate familiar with the deity-yoga visualizations described; the practical use as a deathbed text serves both initiates and ordinary practitioners. The *Bardo Thödöl* proper is a single text within a larger Karma Lingpa cycle of related materials.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** 14th-c. Tibet — fragmented, regional. The Yuan (Mongol) protectorate's hold over Tibet was weakening; the Phagmodru hegemony was rising. Karma Lingpa's terma discovery is set in the Bumthang region (now eastern Bhutan, then Tibetan religious-cultural orbit).
- **Religious milieu.** Tibetan Buddhism's Nyingma school traces itself back to Padmasambhava's 8th-c. founding mission (the imperial period); the *terma* (treasure-text) tradition — texts allegedly hidden by Padmasambhava and discovered centuries later by predestined revealers (*tertöns*) — is the operative Nyingma mode of scriptural production from ~1000 CE onward. Karma Lingpa (~1326–1386) is a major tertön; the *Kar-gling Zhi-khro* cycle is his most famous revelation.
- **Who was behind it.** Karma Lingpa, 14th-c. Tibetan tertön from southern Tibet / Bumthang; received the *Kar-gling Zhi-khro* cycle in his 15th year (~1340) at Mount Gampodar. Traditional account: Padmasambhava composed and hid the text in the 8th c.; Karma Lingpa, predestined since that earlier hiding, recovered and disseminated it. Modern philological view: substantial portions of the *Bardo Thödöl* derive from older Indian Vajrayāna materials translated into Tibetan in the imperial and post-imperial periods (the maṇḍala of Peaceful and Wrathful Deities is attested in earlier Tibetan tantra-cycles); these were synthesized in the 14th c. into the cycle Karma Lingpa "discovered." The terma framing is itself a recognized Tibetan literary-religious convention — the question of whether the text is *literally* 8th-c. is somewhat different from the question of whether the text genuinely represents an old tradition (which it does in part).
- **Discovery / transmission.** Tibetan blockprint and manuscript tradition. The text reached Western audiences via the Sikkim-American scholar Walter Evans-Wentz's 1927 Oxford UP edition (with Kazi Dawa-Samdup's translation and Theosophical-flavored apparatus). C.G. Jung's 1935 "Psychological Commentary" included in the 1935 second edition framed the text in archetypal-psychological terms — vastly influential in Western reception. The Fremantle-Trungpa 1975, Thurman 1994, and Dorje-Coleman 2005 translations have progressively brought scholarly accuracy to bear. Donald Lopez 2011 has written a critical history of the Western reception myth.

## Themes and motifs
- [[afterlife-judgment]] — but radicalized: the "judgment" is not by an external judge but is the soul's own karmically-conditioned cognition. The Peaceful and Wrathful Deities are projections of one's own mind.
- [[non-duality]] — the operative recognition: the appearances of the bardo are *not other than one's own mind*; recognizing this is liberation. The deities are explicitly identified as one's own awakened nature manifesting.
- [[soul-immortality]] — strongly qualified Buddhist version: there is no permanent self (*anātman*), but the *mindstream* (Tibetan *rgyud*) continues; the bardo is the *mindstream* between death and rebirth.
- [[psychopomp]] — the human lama or reader functions as psychopomp, reading the text into the dead person's ear to guide consciousness.
- [[mystery-initiation]] — preparation through Tantric initiation and deity-yoga during life is the precondition for effective bardo-recognition; the text is operative only for those who have received the proper transmissions.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-1-010-book-of-the-dead]] — *parallel-motif*: Evans-Wentz's 1927 title deliberately echoed the Egyptian *Book of the Dead*; the two texts share the genre of *funerary instruction-manual* but with very different cosmologies. The juxtaposition is now somewhat misleading; the Tibetan text is doctrinally Buddhist Vajrayāna, not Egyptian funerary religion.
- → Indian Vajrayāna tantric cycles (Guhyasamāja, Cakrasaṃvara) — *direct-influence*: the Peaceful and Wrathful maṇḍalas derive from Indian Buddhist tantric prototypes.
- → indigenous Tibetan Bön funerary practice — *parallel-motif* / *direct-influence?*: Bön was the pre-Buddhist Tibetan religion with elaborate funerary practices; substantial integration into Tibetan Buddhist death-rites is widely acknowledged.
- → Jung's archetypal psychology — *direct-influence* (in modern Western reception): Jung's 1935 commentary framed the Peaceful and Wrathful Deities as projections of the collective unconscious, an interpretation that powerfully shaped 20th-c. New Age reception. Whether this is *interpretively* compatible with traditional Vajrayāna self-understanding (deities as one's own mind) or *substantively* different (Vajrayāna's "mind" is not Jung's "unconscious") is much debated.

## Disputes
- **Authorship.** Padmasambhava in 8th c. (traditional Nyingma view) vs. Karma Lingpa in 14th c. (academic philological view, with acknowledgment of substantial older materials within). The terma genre makes this question more interpretively complex than it appears.
- **Translation quality and Western reception.** Evans-Wentz's 1927 framing is now widely regarded as substantially distorting (Theosophical apparatus, free translation, missing material). Lopez 2011 surveys the reception history and its distortions in detail.
- **Use of the text.** Whether the text was widely used liturgically in Tibet — or was an elite Nyingma text known mostly through reputation — has been debated. Modern Tibetan Buddhist practice does use the text liturgically.
