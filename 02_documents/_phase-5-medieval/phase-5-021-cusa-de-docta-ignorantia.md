# De Docta Ignorantia (Nicholas of Cusa)

## What it is
**De Docta Ignorantia** ("On Learned Ignorance"), written in 1440 by the cardinal-philosopher **Nicholas of Cusa**, is a three-book treatise arguing that the highest human knowledge is a *learned* ignorance: because God is infinite, and there is no proportion between the finite and the infinite, the mind can never adequately grasp the divine — but it can know, precisely, *that* it cannot, and this educated unknowing is the truest wisdom. From this Cusa develops his signature doctrine of the **coincidentia oppositorum** (the coincidence of opposites): in the infinite, the distinctions that structure finite thought — maximum and minimum, straight and curved, centre and circumference — collapse into identity. The second book applies this to the cosmos (the universe has no fixed centre and no circumference, anticipating later cosmology); the third centres it on Christ as the *maximum contractum*, the infinite present in the finite.

## Significance
De Docta Ignorantia is a pivotal bridge from medieval mysticism to Renaissance and modern thought: its negative theology continues the apophatic tradition of [[apophatic-mysticism]] and [[phase-5-028-meister-eckhart-sermons]], while its infinite, centreless cosmos and coincidence of opposites fed directly into Renaissance Neoplatonism and, through Giordano Bruno, into early-modern cosmology. The standard editions/translations are Hopkins's and Bond's.
