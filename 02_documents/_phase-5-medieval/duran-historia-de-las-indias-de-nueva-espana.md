# Historia de las Indias de Nueva España (Durán)

## What it is
The great three-part chronicle of Aztec history and religion compiled (1576–1581) by the Dominican friar **Diego Durán**, who had grown up in Texcoco and spoke Nahuatl. With Sahagún's [[phase-8-004-florentine-codex-sahagun|Florentine Codex]], it is one of the two indispensable colonial sources for **Mexica religion**: its *Book of the Gods and Rites* and *Ancient Calendar* describe the deities ([[huitzilopochtli]], [[tezcatlipoca]], [[tlaloc]]), the monthly festivals, the priesthood, and the sacrificial cult in unmatched detail, while the *Historia* proper narrates the Mexica from their legendary migration to the Spanish conquest. Durán worked from a now-lost Nahuatl source (the "**Crónica X**") that also lies behind other native-tradition chronicles, plus living informants and painted books.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Late-16th-century New Spain, two generations after the conquest, as the friars raced to record (and to extirpate) a religion still within living memory.
- **Religious milieu.** Durán writes as an evangelist troubled by surviving "idolatry," yet fascinated; his sympathy and his Nahuatl fluency make him an unusually rich witness, even as his Dominican lens shapes the telling.
- **Who was behind it.** Durán himself, with Nahua informants whose testimony he transmits.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Suppressed for centuries; the manuscript surfaced and was printed only in the 19th century, then made canonical for scholarship by Heyden's 20th-century translations.

## Themes and motifs
- [[divine-kingship]] — Durán's *Historia* presents the Mexica *tlatoani* and the cult of [[huitzilopochtli]] as fused political-religious sovereignty, the engine of imperial expansion.

## Disputes
- **The "Crónica X."** Durán's relationship to the hypothesized lost Nahuatl source (shared with Tezozómoc and the *Codex Ramírez*) is a classic problem of Mexica historiography.
- **Bias and reliability (tier note).** As an evangelizing friar, Durán both preserves and distorts: his data on ritual are invaluable, but his framing is polemical, and his speculation that the Mexica descended from the Lost Tribes of Israel (and that Quetzalcoatl might be the apostle St Thomas) is an early-modern diffusionist fantasy, not evidence — a T1 *document* requiring source-critical reading.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **Half of what we know about Aztec religion.** Together with Sahagún, Durán is the documentary foundation for the entire reconstructed Mexica pantheon and ritual calendar in this vault — the colonial transmission-channel through which a destroyed religion survives as scholarship (Heyden 1971, 1994; Clendinnen 1991). By the Atlas's model the Mexica religion is the *home* and the Dominican authorship a transmitting *wire*.
- **The friar who imagined the Atlantic syncretism.** Durán's Lost-Tribes and St-Thomas–Quetzalcoatl speculations are an early instance of the cross-tradition diffusionism the Atlas tracks critically — documenting the *idea* of connection while flagging it as unsupported (Clendinnen 1991).
