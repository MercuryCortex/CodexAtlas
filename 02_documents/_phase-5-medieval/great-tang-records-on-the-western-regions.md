# Great Tang Records on the Western Regions (Da Tang Xiyu Ji)

## What it is
The **geographical and ethnographic record of the monk Xuanzang's pilgrimage to India**, compiled in 646 CE at the command of the Tang emperor. After seventeen years (629–645) travelling overland across Central Asia to India and back — much of it in defiance of an imperial travel ban — Xuanzang set down an account of **some 110 countries he visited and 28 more he heard of**, describing their terrain, climate, customs, languages, and above all their Buddhist monasteries, relics, and sacred legends. It is by far the **richest single source for the historical geography of 7th-century India and Central Asia**, and the indispensable guide that modern archaeologists used to rediscover the great Buddhist sites.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** The early Tang empire under Taizong, expanding westward along the Silk Road; Xuanzang, who had left without permission, returned a celebrity and was set to work translating the 657 Sanskrit texts he carried home.
- **Religious milieu.** A high point of the Buddhist exchange between India and China: Xuanzang studied for years at **Nālandā**, the great monastic university, mastering Yogācāra philosophy, and his translations reshaped Chinese Buddhism.
- **Who was behind it.** Xuanzang himself, with the literary editor Bianji; his disciple Huili wrote the complementary *Biography*.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Preserved in the Chinese canon; translated by Beal (1884) and Li Rongxi (1996); mined by Alexander Cunningham and the Archaeological Survey of India to locate lost sites.

## Themes and motifs
- [[bodhisattva-vow]] — Xuanzang's arduous quest to obtain the authentic Dharma for China is itself framed as a bodhisattva's compassionate labour.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-3-030-asokan-edicts]] — *direct-evidence*: Xuanzang records standing Aśokan pillars, stupas, and inscriptions, a key witness to their state a millennium after Aśoka.
- → [[siddhartha-gautama-buddha]] — *documentary*: the record catalogues the sites of the Buddha's life (Lumbinī, Bodh Gayā, Sārnāth, Kusinārā) and the legends attached to them.
- → Faxian's earlier *Record of Buddhist Kingdoms* (c. 414) — *genre-predecessor*: the Chinese Buddhist pilgrimage-chronicle tradition Xuanzang perfects.

## Disputes
- **Eyewitness vs. hearsay.** The record mixes places Xuanzang saw with regions reported to him; scholars (Deeg) work to distinguish the strata and to gauge the influence of literary convention.
- **Relation to the *Biography*.** How far Huili's *Biography of the Tripiṭaka Master* embellishes the *Records* is debated.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **The text that let the modern world rediscover Buddhist India.** Because Xuanzang recorded distances and directions between sites, 19th-century archaeologists — Cunningham above all — used the *Great Tang Records* as a field map to locate **Nālandā, Bodh Gayā, Sārnāth, Kapilavastu, and the Aśokan pillars**, recovering the physical landscape of early Buddhism that India itself had largely forgotten (Beal 1884; Li 1996). Few documents have so directly resurrected a lost religious world.
- **From pilgrim to myth.** The historical journey was mythologized nine centuries later into the great Ming novel **Journey to the West** (*Xiyouji*), in which "Tripiṭaka" travels with the Monkey King — one of the most popular narratives in all of East Asia, a vivid case of a documentary record becoming cross-cultural legend (Wriggins 2004).
