# Tablet of the Sun (Palenque)

## What it is
The hieroglyphic tablet of the **Temple of the Sun**, the third temple of Palenque's Cross Group, carved for K'inich Kan Bahlam II and dedicated in 692 CE. Its central icon is a **war-shield** emblazoned with the face of the **Jaguar God of the underworld** (the sun in its nighttime, war-bearing aspect), crossed by two spears and supported on a throne above two bound captives — the **warfare and solar** member of the three-temple program, associated with the Triad god **GIII**. Together with its two companion tablets it completes Kan Bahlam's coordinated statement of cosmic and dynastic legitimacy: creation, sustenance, and war.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** The militarized world of Late Classic Maya city-states, in which royal legitimacy was inseparable from success in war and captive-taking.
- **Religious milieu.** The sun's daily death into the underworld as a jaguar and its rebirth model the king's martial and regenerative power.
- **Who was behind it.** Kan Bahlam II's court.
- **Discovery / transmission.** One of the most-reproduced Maya monuments; central to the iconographic decipherment of Maya art.

## Themes and motifs
- [[divine-kingship]] — the war-shield throne presents the king's sovereignty as solar-martial power, legitimated by captive sacrifice.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[palenque-tablet-of-the-cross]] / [[palenque-tablet-of-the-foliated-cross]] — *set*: the war/sun member of the Triad program.
- → [[kinich-ahau]] — *thematic*: the Maya sun god, whose nocturnal jaguar form the shield depicts.

## Disputes
- **GIII's identity.** Whether GIII is best understood as the sun, the Jaguar God of the underworld, or a war-deity composite is debated within Maya theology.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **A three-part divine charter, in stone.** Read together, the Cross Group tablets distribute the cosmos across three temples — the world-tree (creation), the maize-tree (sustenance), and the war-sun (martial power) — a Maya triadic theology mapped onto sacred architecture, one of the most complete pre-Columbian religious programs to survive, and an independent instance of the three-function sovereignty patterning the Atlas tracks across cultures (Schele & Miller 1986; Schele & Mathews 1998).
