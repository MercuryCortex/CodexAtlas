# Samguk Yusa (Memorabilia of the Three Kingdoms)

## What it is
A collection of histories, legends, and Buddhist tales of ancient Korea compiled around 1281 by the Seon (Zen) Buddhist monk **Iryeon**. It is the essential complement to the older, official [[samguk-sagi|Samguk Sagi]]: deliberately gathering the foundation myths, miracle stories, temple traditions, monk-biographies, and folk material that the sober official history had passed over. Its most consequential single contribution is the **Dangun foundation myth** — the oldest surviving account of the origin of the Korean people — and it preserves the largest body of *hyangga*, the earliest Korean vernacular poetry.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Late-Goryeo Korea under Mongol overlordship — a moment when recording a distinctively Korean national origin had real cultural weight.
- **Religious milieu.** A Buddhist monk's history: Iryeon foregrounds Korea's Buddhist heritage and its sacred geography, weaving *kami*-like local cults, Buddhist devotion, and dynastic legend together.
- **Who was behind it.** Iryeon himself, a leading Seon master.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Transmitted in Goryeo and early-Joseon printings; the oldest complete edition dates to 1512.

## Themes and motifs
- [[divine-kingship]] — the founding kings of the Korean realms descend from or are favoured by Heaven; [[tangun]], child of the heaven-prince [[hwanung]] and a bear transformed into a woman, is the archetype of Korean sacral kingship.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[samguk-sagi]] — *complement*: the official Confucian history that the *Yusa* supplements with myth, religion, and folk tradition.

## Disputes
- **Historicity vs. legend.** The *Yusa* mixes documentary record with miracle and myth; modern historians mine it critically, prizing it precisely for the material the *Sagi* excluded.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **The origin myth of a nation.** The *Samguk Yusa* is the oldest source for the **Dangun myth** — [[hwanung]] descending from heaven to a sandalwood tree, the bear who becomes a woman, and their son [[tangun]] founding Gojoseon in 2333 BCE — which became the charter of Korean ethnic identity, still commemorated in the national foundation holiday (Gaecheonjeol). Few single texts carry a people's self-origin so directly (Grayson 2002).
- **The vault of early Korean poetry.** By preserving fourteen *hyangga*, Iryeon's collection is the principal surviving witness to the earliest Korean-language verse — a vernacular tradition that would otherwise be almost wholly lost (Lee 2003).
