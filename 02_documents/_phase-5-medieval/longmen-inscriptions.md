# Longmen Inscriptions

## What it is
The **Longmen Grottoes** near Luoyang hold tens of thousands of carved niches and images, accompanied by some **2,800 dedicatory inscriptions** recording the donors who commissioned them, from the Northern Wei capital's relocation to Luoyang (~493 CE) through the Tang. These *zaoxiang ji* ("image-making records") name patrons — emperors, monks, palace women, lay societies — and state their merit-dedications: that the image be made for the health of a parent, the rebirth of the dead, or the prosperity of the dynasty. A selection of the early Northern Wei inscriptions, the **"Longmen Twenty"** (*Longmen ershipin*), became the canonical models of the vigorous "Wei stele" (*Weibei*) calligraphic style. The grandest single monument, the Tang **Fengxiansi Vairocana** (~675), was sponsored with support from Empress Wu Zetian and depicts the cosmic Buddha [[vairocana]].

## Significance
The Longmen inscriptions are an unmatched quantitative record of who actually paid for Chinese Buddhist art and why — a sociology of medieval devotion carved beside the images themselves. They document the imperial-Buddhist alliance at its height and, in the Longmen Twenty, gave Chinese calligraphy one of its foundational canons. The site's colossal Vairocana ties the donative record to the cosmic-Buddha theology shared with the Avataṃsaka and [[phase-4-101-lotus-sutra]] traditions.
