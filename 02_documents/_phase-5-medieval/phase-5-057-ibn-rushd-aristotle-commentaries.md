# Ibn Rushd's Aristotle Commentaries

## What it is
**[[ibn-rushd|Ibn Rushd (Averroes)]]** produced the most technically rigorous and historically consequential series of Aristotle commentaries in any language. Written in Arabic in al-Andalus (primarily Córdoba and Marrakesh) between approximately 1169 and 1195 CE at the request of the Almohad prince Abū Yaʿqūb Yūsuf, the commentaries exist in three distinct forms across most of the Aristotelian corpus. Five of the **Long Commentaries** — the most exacting form — were translated into Latin in the 1220s–1240s and became the standard European Aristotelian commentary tradition for more than a century: the primary intellectual apparatus through which medieval European universities engaged Aristotle.

## The three-tier commentary system
Averroes composed three levels of commentary on most Aristotelian texts, each serving a different purpose:

**1. Short Commentaries (*Talkhīṣ* — "summaries" / "epitomes")** — Paraphrastic condensations of Aristotle's arguments without direct lemmatic quotation; designed as introductions for students or as independent philosophical essays on Aristotelian questions. These circulated widely in Arabic and Hebrew but were less decisive for the Latin reception.

**2. Middle Commentaries (*Tafsīr* — "paraphrases")** — More extended paraphrase with Averroes's own analytical additions; intermediate between epitome and full commentary. The Middle Commentaries on the *Prior Analytics* and *Nicomachean Ethics* were translated into Latin by Herman the German (~1240s) and circulated in the medieval Latin curriculum alongside the Long Commentaries.

**3. Long / Great Commentaries (*Sharḥ Kabīr* — "great exposition")** — Lemmatized, verse-by-verse exegesis: Averroes quotes each passage of Aristotle's text, then provides an extended technical commentary. The most demanding form, requiring the reader to be working through Aristotle's actual text simultaneously. **Five Long Commentaries were translated into Latin and became canonical**: the *Posterior Analytics*, *Physics*, *De Caelo* (On the Heavens), *De Anima* (On the Soul), and *Metaphysics*.

## The five Latin Long Commentaries and their translators
All five were made available in Latin through the [[event-toledo-translations-1085-1150|Toledo translation enterprise]] and its extension into the Hohenstaufen court in Sicily:

- **Michael Scot (~1175–1235)** — the primary translator: rendered the Long Commentaries on the *Physics*, *De Caelo*, *De Anima*, and *Metaphysics* into Latin, working first at Toledo and then at the court of Frederick II in Sicily. The Scot translations are the ones Aquinas, Albert the Great, Bonaventure, and Siger of Brabant primarily used.
- **Herman the German (Hermannus Alemannus, ~1200–1272)** — translated the Middle Commentary on the *Nicomachean Ethics* and the *Rhetoric* and *Poetics* commentaries; extended the Averroist corpus beyond the Michael Scot core.

By the 1240s, the Latin university student reading Aristotle read Aristotle with Averroes's Long Commentary on the facing page (or interleaved in the manuscript). This is literally the format of the medieval Latin printed editions: the *Opera Aristotelica* with Averroes commentary printed page-by-page.

## The intellect controversy: the most consequential doctrine
The Long Commentary on *De Anima* is the single most controversial and historically consequential of the five. In it, Averroes argues for the **unicity of the material intellect** (*intellectus materialis*): the rational capacity for thought is not individually instantiated in each human soul, but is a single cosmic intellect shared among all humans — individuated only through each person's particular "imagined phantasms" (specific sensory-derived images). This reading of Aristotle's notoriously obscure *De Anima* III.4-5 is technically sophisticated but implies:

1. Personal immortality in the standard sense is philosophically problematic — there is no *individual* rational soul that persists after death.
2. The intellect by which we know universals is eternal and separate from the perishable individual human.

This doctrine, when the Latin translations arrived at Paris in the 1250s-1270s, produced the **Latin Averroist** movement: Siger of Brabant, Boethius of Dacia, and John of Jandun developed the unicity reading further, provoking Aquinas's direct philosophical refutation — ***De Unitate Intellectus Contra Averroistas* (1270)** — and Bishop Étienne Tempier's **Condemnation of 1277** (219 prohibited propositions, many Averroist, the largest single condemnation of philosophical theses in medieval European history). See [[ibn-rushd]] for the full Latin Averroism section.

## Aquinas and Averroes: the Commentator and his critic
[[thomas-aquinas|Thomas Aquinas]] refers to [[ibn-rushd|Averroes]] as **"the Commentator"** (*Commentator*) without naming him — the unmodified noun in Latin scholastic usage means Averroes exclusively, as "the Philosopher" means Aristotle. Aquinas cites Averroes approximately **503 times** in the *Summa Theologiae* and *Summa contra Gentiles* combined — more citations than any other non-Christian philosopher. The engagement is both dependent and critical:

- Aquinas uses Averroes's commentary framework to understand what Aristotle means, passage by passage — the intellectual dependency is real and pervasive.
- Aquinas explicitly refutes Averroes on the unicity of intellect (*De Unitate Intellectus*) — a point where Averroes's reading, if accepted, would have theologically unacceptable implications for Christian personal immortality doctrine.
- Aquinas partially corrects Averroes via [[ibn-sina|Avicenna]]: on the essence/existence distinction and the Necessary Being argument, he takes Avicenna's position over Averroes's more strictly Aristotelian one.

The relationship is: you cannot understand Aquinas's metaphysics and psychology without understanding Averroes and Avicenna. Scholasticism is, among other things, the product of a sustained three-way philosophical argument between a Dominican friar and two Islamic philosophers from Córdoba.

## The counter-intuitive reception pattern
The Averroes documentary profile presents one of the vault's sharpest counter-intuitive cross-tradition findings: **Averroes was far more influential in Christian Europe than in the Islamic world.** His philosophy was condemned and his books burned in Córdoba in 1195 — the same decade Michael Scot was beginning the Latin translations that would make him the indispensable authority at the University of Paris. The Islamic world largely moved past Averroes; the Latin Christian world adopted him as the essential Aristotelian commentary tradition. The transmission ran in the direction opposite to the cultural gradient the "clash of civilizations" framework would predict.

His commentaries also survive substantially in Hebrew (through the Jewish-Provençal translation tradition: Moses ibn Tibbon, Samuel ben Judah of Marseille, Kalonymus ben Kalonymus) — cases where the Arabic original is lost but the Hebrew translation survives, including the Commentary on Plato's *Republic*. The Jewish philosophical tradition is a third preservation pathway alongside Arabic and Latin.
