# Masnavi-i Maʿnavi (The Spiritual Couplets)

## What it is
The vast didactic mystical epic in Persian *masnavi* meter (rhyming couplets) — six books, ~25,000 couplets — that Rūmī composed (dictated to his disciple Ḥusām al-Dīn) over the last fifteen years of his life. The work is loosely organized: stories nested within stories, parables that branch into commentary that branches into further parables, Qur'anic exegesis woven through, hadith citations as anchors. The famous opening of Book One — "Listen to the reed how it tells a tale, / complaining of separations" — uses the reed-flute (cut from its reed-bed and now crying for its origin) as the master image of the work: the human soul separated from its divine source, the reed's cry as the cry of every separated soul, the music of separation as the only path home. The work is at once instructional (it teaches Sufi metaphysics, ethics, and contemplative practice) and itself a literary realization of those teachings (the *meaning* of the *Masnavi* must be experienced through the reading; cognitive paraphrase always falls short). Persian readers have called it the "Qur'an in Persian"; its devotional and cultural place in the Persianate world is roughly comparable to the *Divine Comedy*'s place in the Italian-Catholic world.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Anatolia under the Seljuk Sultanate of Rum and the catastrophic Mongol invasions. The Mongols sacked Baghdad in 1258 (the symbolic end of the Abbasid Caliphate), the year Rūmī began the *Masnavi*. Konya, where Rūmī lived, became a Mongol tributary; Sufi institutions provided social and spiritual stability while political authority disintegrated.
- **Religious milieu.** Sunni-Sufi consolidation: the Sunni-Sufi synthesis crystallized by Ghazālī (~1100) had matured into the institutional Sufi orders (*ṭuruq*); Rūmī's circle would coalesce into the Mevlevi order founded by his son Sulṭān Walad after his death. Ibn ʿArabī's *Fuṣūṣ al-Ḥikam* had been composed in Damascus 1229 by an author who knew Rūmī's stepson Ṣadr al-Dīn al-Qūnawī — the high Persianate Sufi metaphysical world was tightly networked.
- **Who was behind it.** Rūmī himself: born Balkh (modern Afghanistan) 1207; his family fled the Mongol approach and migrated west, eventually settling Konya ~1228 where his father Bahāʾ al-Dīn Walad was a Sufi master. Rūmī succeeded his father as community teacher and jurist. The pivotal event of his life was the 1244 encounter with the wandering dervish Shams-i Tabrīzī, an electric mystical friendship that transformed Rūmī from learned cleric into ecstatic mystic and poet. After Shams's disappearance / death ~1248, Rūmī's poetic output (the *Dīvān-i Shams-i Tabrīzī*, ~40,000 lines of lyric ghazals) is dedicated to Shams. The *Masnavi* belongs to Rūmī's later, more didactic period, dictated to Ḥusām al-Dīn over ~15 years.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous Mevlevi transmission; the order's *samāʿ* (whirling) ceremony preserves a ritual context for poetic-mystical practice. The Mevlevi was a major Ottoman-period order. In the West, Sir William Jones (~1790) introduced Rūmī to European audiences; Nicholson's 1925–1940 edition established the scholarly base; Coleman Barks's 1990s loose English paraphrases (working from prior translations rather than Persian) made Rūmī a US bestseller, with mixed scholarly reaction.

## Themes and motifs
- [[non-duality]] — Rūmī's ontology is in the line of Ibn ʿArabian *waḥdat al-wujūd*: all that exists is the manifestation of the one Real (*al-Ḥaqq*). The reed's separation is the apparent multiplicity of creatures; the reed's music is the trace of unity through that apparent separation.
- [[soul-as-spark]] — the reed-bed / reed image: the soul is severed from the divine origin and yearns to return. The *Masnavi* develops this image relentlessly.
- [[gnosis-as-salvation]] — direct knowing (*maʿrifa*) is the heart of religion; conceptual learning is the foothills, not the summit.
- [[mystery-initiation]] — the *Masnavi* is itself an initiatory text; its proper reading is a form of practice; the master-disciple relationship (Rūmī-Shams; Rūmī-Ḥusām al-Dīn) is the operative model.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-4-034-quran]] — *commentary-on*: the *Masnavi* is partly a Persian poetic *tafsīr* — Rūmī's exegetical treatment of Qur'anic narratives is a major dimension.
- → [[phase-5-015-al-ghazali-ihya]] — *direct-influence*: Ghazālī's Sunni-Sufi ethical-spiritual framework is the doctrinal substrate.
- → [[phase-5-021-ibn-arabi-fusus-al-hikam]] — *shared-milieu* / *parallel-motif*: Rūmī's and Ibn ʿArabī's circles overlapped (Ṣadr al-Dīn al-Qūnawī was the bridge); Rūmī expresses similar metaphysical content as Ibn ʿArabī through poetic-narrative rather than systematic-theological means.
- → Sanāʾī, *Ḥadīqat al-Ḥaqīqa* and ʿAṭṭār, *Manṭiq al-Ṭayr* — *direct-influence*: the earlier Persian Sufi didactic-poetic tradition Rūmī inherits and surpasses.
- → [[phase-5-002-heart-sutra]] / Buddhist non-dualism — *parallel-motif* (no causal influence): the structural parallel between Sufi *fanāʾ* (annihilation in the divine) and Buddhist *anātman* / *śūnyatā* has been widely noted in comparative literature, though there is no traceable transmission.

## Disputes
- **Coleman Barks reception.** Barks's English paraphrases are widely read but academically controversial — they remove the Islamic-Qur'anic substrate and translate Rūmī as a generic spiritual master. Lewis 2000 is the major academic critique; Mojaddedi 2004ff is the standard accurate-verse-translation alternative.
- **Shams's fate.** Was Shams-i Tabrīzī murdered (perhaps by Rūmī's jealous disciples or by Rūmī's son ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn — the latter being a Mevlevi tradition?) or did he simply depart? Lewis 2000 surveys the evidence — no certainty.
- **Order of composition of Book Two.** Book Two of the *Masnavi* contains a passage indicating a two-year pause between Book One and Book Two; the textual history of the *Masnavi*'s composition is partly recovered.
