# Da Ming Huidian

## What it is
The **Da Ming Huidian** ("Collected Statutes of the Great Ming") is the great administrative-statutory compendium of the Ming dynasty: a systematic, office-by-office codification of the structure, duties, procedures, and ritual obligations of the entire imperial government. First completed in 1503 under the Hongzhi emperor and then comprehensively revised into the 228-*juan* Wanli edition of 1587, it organises the state by the Six Ministries (Personnel, Revenue, Rites, War, Justice, Works) and below them by office, recording for each its regulations and precedents. Unlike a penal code (the *Da Ming Lü*), the *huidian* is an **organizational** law — it describes how the apparatus of government is supposed to be constituted and to operate, including the elaborate machinery of imperial state ritual administered by the Ministry of Rites.

## Primary text — the document's architecture
The Huidian is not a scripture with quotable verses but a structured compendium. Its organising logic is worth setting out:
- **By ministry and office.** Each of the Six Ministries is treated in turn, then its subordinate bureaus, with the relevant statutes and precedents gathered under each.
- **The ritual core.** A large portion falls under the Ministry of Rites, codifying the *jili* (auspicious rites) of the state cult — the sacrifices to Heaven and Earth, to the imperial ancestors, to the gods of soil and grain — together with sumptuary and architectural regulations governing how altars, temples, and tombs of each rank were to be built.
- **Precedent over principle.** Entries accumulate imperial decisions, so the Huidian functions as the state's institutional memory: the authoritative record of "how it is done."

*Because the Ministry-of-Rites sections fix the size, form, and furnishing of ritual structures by rank, the Huidian is a primary source for the regulation of sacred architecture in Ming China — which is why it surfaces alongside building-standard texts like the [[ying-zao-fa-shi]].*

## Context
- **Political milieu.** The mature Ming bureaucratic state, governed through a vast civil service and a documentary culture of statutes and precedents.
- **Religious milieu.** The Confucian imperial state cult, in which the emperor's correct performance of ritual sacrifice was constitutive of cosmic and political order; the Huidian is the rulebook of that cult.
- **Who was behind it.** Imperially-commissioned editorial boards of senior officials; the 1587 revision was directed by the grand secretary Shen Shixing.
- **Transmission.** Woodblock-printed; the 1587 Wanli edition is the standard reference text for Ming institutions.

## Themes and motifs
- [[divine-kingship]] — the Huidian encodes the emperor's role as the unique ritual mediator between Heaven and the human order; administrative law and cosmic ritual are continuous.
- [[theme-divine-kingship-and-temple-as-royal-pact]] — by fixing the construction standards for altars and temples by rank, the statutes make sacred architecture an instrument of the political-cosmic order.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[da-qing-huidian]] — *direct-model*: the Qing built its own Collected Statutes squarely on the Ming precedent; the two huidian are the paired institutional charters of China's last two dynasties. Ref: Wilkinson 2018.
- → [[phase-2-033-shujing-book-of-documents]] — *classical-charter*: the Huidian's vision of government-by-documented-precedent descends from the bureaucratic ideal first articulated in the Book of Documents.
- → [[ying-zao-fa-shi]] — *complementary-regulation*: where the Yingzao Fashi standardises how to build, the Huidian's ritual sections standardise what may be built and for whom.

## Disputes
- **Prescription vs. practice.** As with all administrative codes, how far the Huidian's regulations reflected actual governance (versus an idealised template) is a standing question for institutional historians.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges

- **The rulebook of the Confucian state cult — administrative law as cosmic ritual**: The Da Ming Huidian shows, more clearly than almost any other document, that in imperial China there was no separation between "church" and "state": the same compendium that regulates tax bureaus and military supply also fixes, in minute detail, how the Son of Heaven sacrifices to Heaven and to his ancestors, and how the altars and temples of each rank must be built. State ritual is administered as a branch of government. For comparative religion, the Huidian is a uniquely explicit case of a polity codifying its cult as ordinary public administration (Taylor 1998, "Official Religion in the Ming," *Cambridge History of China* Vol. 8; → [[divine-kingship]]).
