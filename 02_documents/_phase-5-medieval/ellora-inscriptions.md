# Ellora Inscriptions

## What it is
The rock-cut complex at **Ellora** is unique in carrying, side by side, the monuments of three religions — **Buddhist** caves (the earliest phase, ~6th–7th c.), then the **Hindu** Rāṣṭrakūṭa caves culminating in the colossal monolithic **Kailāsa temple** (Cave 16, ~8th c.), then **Jain** caves (~9th–10th c.). Its **inscriptions** — dedicatory records, donor notices, and the Rāṣṭrakūṭa copper-plate grants that mention the Kailāsa — document this extraordinary sequence of patronage. The most celebrated is the tradition (recorded in later grants) that the Kailāsa was excavated under the Rāṣṭrakūṭa king **Kṛṣṇa I**. The Buddhist caves' inscriptions belong to the same world of merit-donation as those at [[ajanta-inscriptions]]; the Hindu Kailāsa is dedicated to [[shiva]].

## Significance
Ellora is the supreme physical demonstration of [[theme-hindu-buddhist-architectural-continuity]]: the same rock-cutting craft tradition, passing from Buddhist to Hindu to Jain hands over four centuries, leaves a single cliff that is a cross-section of medieval Indian religion. Its inscriptions are the documentary thread through that sequence — a case where the home Buddhist phase is wired, in stone, to the Hindu and Jain traditions that succeeded it on the same site.
