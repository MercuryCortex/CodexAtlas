# Lintel 3, Temple I (Tikal)

## What it is
A carved wooden **lintel** from a doorway of **Temple I at Tikal**, the towering funerary pyramid of King **Jasaw Chan K'awiil I**. The carving shows the king enthroned beneath a gigantic protector deity, a monument of dynastic triumph: it commemorates Jasaw's pivotal **defeat of Calakmul in 695 CE**, the victory that broke a century of Tikal's subordination to its great rival and restored the city to dominance. Though Temple I is Jasaw's tomb, the lintels were carved a generation later, under his son **Yik'in Chan K'awiil**, as part of completing and glorifying the father's monument.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** The "superpower" rivalry of the Late Classic lowlands between Tikal and the Kaan (Snake) dynasty of Calakmul; Jasaw's victory is a hinge of Maya political history.
- **Religious milieu.** Royal monuments fuse military commemoration with the sacralization of the king under his patron gods.
- **Who was behind it.** The Tikal royal workshop under Yik'in Chan K'awiil.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Removed and dispersed to European museums in the 19th century; central to the modern reconstruction of Tikal's dynasty.

## Themes and motifs
- [[divine-kingship]] — the enthroned king beneath his protector-deity is the canonical Maya image of sacral, war-victorious sovereignty.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[stela-16-tikal]] — *same-reign*: another monument of Jasaw Chan K'awiil I commemorating his rule and ritual.
- → [[jasaw-chan-kawiil-i]] — *commemorates*: the lintel's subject and Temple I's occupant.

## Disputes
- **Reading the captions.** Specific glyphic readings of the lintel's secondary texts are refined in ongoing epigraphy.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **The monument of a political turning point.** Lintel 3 fixes in carved wood the single event — the 695 defeat of Calakmul — that reversed the balance of power in the Classic Maya world, a rare case where a precise dated indigenous monument records the hinge of an entire civilization's political history (Martin & Grube 2008).
