# Yingzao Fashi

## What it is
The **Yingzao Fashi** ("Treatise on Architectural Methods" or "State Building Standards") is the most complete and systematic building manual to survive from pre-modern China. Compiled by the official **Li Jie** for the Directorate of Buildings and Construction, submitted to the throne in 1100 and promulgated in 1103, it was designed to standardise the design, materials, labour, and cost of state construction projects. Its enduring importance lies in its **modular system**: the *cai-fen* (材分) method, which sets eight graded standard sizes of the basic timber unit (*cai*) and derives every other dimension of a building — bracket-sets (*dougong*), beams, columns, roof — proportionally from it. The Yingzao Fashi thereby reduces the whole art of timber-frame architecture to a rational, scalable grammar.

## Primary text — the modular principle
The treatise is technical rather than literary; its conceptual heart is the *cai-fen* module:
- **The *cai* unit.** A single cross-sectional timber size, defined in eight grades, is chosen according to the building's rank and scale.
- **Proportional derivation.** Every member — column height, bracket projection, beam depth, roof curve — is then expressed as a multiple or fraction of that unit, so that a building of any size is generated from one governing module.
- **Estimates and drawings.** The manual pairs these standards with detailed labour-and-materials estimates and technical illustrations, so that cost and quality could be controlled from the centre.

*This modular logic is the codified grammar behind the distinctive look of East Asian monumental architecture — the bracketed eaves of temples and palaces across China, Korea, and Japan are, in effect, applications of a proportional system that the Yingzao Fashi was the first to write down completely.*

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Northern Song state administration, with its concern to rationalise and audit the cost of imperial building works.
- **Cultural milieu.** A high point of Chinese technical and bureaucratic literature; the Yingzao Fashi sits alongside Song works on agronomy, pharmacology, and metallurgy as part of a broad codifying impulse.
- **Who was behind it.** Li Jie, drawing on master-craftsmen's oral tradition and earlier standard-compilations, and translating craft knowledge into administrable text.
- **Rediscovery.** Largely forgotten, the text was recovered in the early twentieth century and decoded by **Liang Sicheng** and the Society for Research in Chinese Architecture (Yingzao Xueshe) through years of fieldwork on surviving buildings.

## Themes and motifs
- [[theme-architectural-technique-enables-theology]] — by standardising the structural system, the manual made it possible to build the large bracketed timber halls that house the icons and cults of Chinese religion at scale.
- [[theme-religious-architectural-longue-duree]] — the cai-fen grammar gives East Asian sacred architecture a continuity stretching across a millennium and three countries.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[da-ming-huidian]] / [[da-qing-huidian]] — *complementary-regulation*: where the Huidian statutes fix what structures each rank may build, the Yingzao Fashi fixes how to build them; together they are the regulatory frame of Chinese monumental construction. Ref: Steinhardt 2019.
- → (the Qing *Gongcheng zuofa zeli*, 1734) — *successor-standard*: the Qing issued its own building-standards code, the natural successor to the Yingzao Fashi, which Liang Sicheng studied as a pair. Ref: Liang 1984.

## Disputes
- **Reading the modules.** The precise interpretation of some of the Yingzao Fashi's terms and proportions was the central problem of Liang Sicheng's work and remains debated in technical detail.
- **Prescription vs. surviving practice.** How closely surviving Song and later buildings follow the manual's standards is an active field of architectural-historical study.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges

- **The Rosetta Stone of East Asian architecture — and the text that founded a discipline**: The Yingzao Fashi's *cai-fen* module is the written key to the structural grammar of every bracketed timber temple and palace in China, Korea, and Japan; once Liang Sicheng decoded it in the 1930s–40s by matching its terms to standing buildings, it became the foundation of modern Chinese architectural history. A twelfth-century government building code, nearly lost, turned out to be the single document that makes a thousand years of East Asian sacred architecture legible — a building standard that became a discipline's founding text (Liang Sicheng 1984, *A Pictorial History of Chinese Architecture*, MIT Press; Glahn 1975, *T'oung Pao* 61; → [[theme-religious-architectural-longue-duree]]).
