# Rājarāja I Thanjavur Inscriptions

## What it is
The walls of the great **Rājarājeśvaram** (now **Bṛhadīśvara**) temple at Thanjavur, completed around 1010 CE by the Chola emperor **Rājarāja I**, carry one of the most remarkable epigraphic archives of the pre-modern world: long Tamil inscriptions recording, in minute administrative detail, the endowments, personnel, and economy of the temple. They list the gifts of gold, land, livestock, and cash made by the king, his sister, his queens, and his officers; they name the hundreds of temple servants — dancers (*devadāsīs*), musicians, accountants, watchmen — settled around the temple with their allotments; and they record the standardised weights, interest rates, and revenue arrangements that funded the cult of [[shiva]] as Lord of Thanjavur. A Sanskrit *praśasti* (eulogy) frames this with the king's dynastic glory.

## Significance
These inscriptions are a primary source of the first order for the economy, society, and statecraft of the Chola empire at its height — they show the royal temple not merely as a shrine but as a great redistributive institution ([[temple-economy]]) binding king, god, and society. The temple itself, the tallest of its age, is a monument of Chola [[divine-kingship]]: the emperor's power expressed as devotion to Śiva. The inscriptions were edited in the *South Indian Inscriptions* series and remain central to South Asian historiography.
