# Manṭiq al-Ṭayr ("The Conference of the Birds")

## What it is

**ʿAṭṭār's narrative mathnawi** (~4,500 rhyming couplets in Persian) is the supreme allegorical poem of the Sufi tradition. Its plot is deceptively simple: the birds of the world convene and decide to seek their true king, the Simorgh (a legendary Persian bird who lives behind the mountains of Qāf at the edge of the world). The **hoopoe** (*hudhud*) — the bird of Solomon in the Quran — is elected guide. Together they must cross Seven Valleys before they can reach the Simorgh:

1. **Ṭalab** — Seeking (the will to begin)
2. **ʿIshq** — Love (consuming passion that burns away self-interest)
3. **Maʿrifat** — Gnosis / mystical knowledge
4. **Istighnāʾ** — Detachment / needlessness (poverty of desire)
5. **Tawḥīd** — Divine Unity (the mystic's dissolution into the One)
6. **Ḥayrat** — Bewilderment / stupor (the mind's collapse before the divine)
7. **Faqr wa Fanāʾ** — Poverty and Annihilation (total extinction of the self in God)

Most birds turn back at each valley, defeated by their particular attachments. Only **thirty** (*sī*) birds survive the crossing. They arrive and discover the Simorgh — and the pun that encodes the entire philosophical thesis is unleashed: *sī murgh* in Persian means **"thirty birds."** They are the Simorgh. The king they sought is what they themselves are. The journey that seemed to lead outward was a journey inward. **The seeker discovers that the sought is the seeker.**

## Context

- **Political milieu.** Khorasan under the late Seljuq empire (~1177 CE); Nishapur was the pre-eminent intellectual and cultural center of the eastern Islamic world. ʿAṭṭār ran a pharmacy (*ʿaṭṭārī*, whence his pen-name "the pharmacist") and may have been the most widely-traveled Sufi intellectual of his generation; a tradition holds he met the young Rūmī's family as they passed through Nishapur as refugees.
- **Religious milieu.** Khorasani Sufism was the dominant intellectual-spiritual force in northeast Persia, building on a lineage from Bāyazīd Basṭāmī, Ḥallāj, and Sanāʾī. The Sufi orders (*ṭarīqas*) were institutionalizing; poetry was the primary medium of Sufi theological instruction.
- **Who was behind it.** ʿAṭṭār himself; the *Manṭiq al-Ṭayr* was his signature mature work, preceded by the *Tadhkirat al-Awliyāʾ* (Biographies of Sufi Saints) and followed by the *Muṣībatnāma*, *Ilāhīnāma*, and *Asrārnāma*.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous Persian manuscript tradition. The work was central to Persian literary education from the 13th century through the Safavid period. Numerous magnificent illustrated manuscripts survive from Timurid Herat (~1485) and Safavid Tabriz. Modern Western reception was largely shaped by Edward FitzGerald's incomplete 1889 English paraphrase (which omitted much of the theological content) until Davis and Darbandi's 1984 complete Penguin translation.

## Themes and motifs

- **[[ascent-of-the-soul]]** — The Seven Valleys are the most fully elaborated soul-ascent structure in Persian Sufi poetry; each valley corresponds to a stage of spiritual development that directly maps onto the Neoplatonic/Sufi *sulūk* (wayfaring) itinerary.
- **[[fanaa-annihilation]]** — *Fanāʾ* (annihilation of the self in God) is the terminus of the seventh valley, and the moment of the *sī murgh* / Simorgh revelation. The self must be extinguished before the divine can be known — the Sufi reformulation of Plotinus's *henosis*.
- **[[tawḥīd]]** — The fifth valley's divine-unity stage is the theological heart of the poem: all apparent multiplicity is resolved into the One; the Sufi *tawḥīd* is not merely the Islamic creedal statement but an ontological claim that multiplicity is appearance.
- **[[perennial-philosophy]]** — The poem's final revelation — that the many are the One, that the seeker IS the sought — is one of world literature's most elegant formulations of the perennialist equation: what every mystic tradition reaches (Vedantic *tat tvam asi*, Plotinus's *epistrophē*, Eckhart's *Gottheit*, the Gnostic *pneuma* recognizing its origin) is stated here in the pun of the thirty birds.

## Connections (provisional)

- → [[phase-5-025-rumi-masnavi]] — *direct-influence*: Rūmī explicitly invokes Attar as teacher; the *Masnavi* inherits the mathnawi form, the Seven-Valley-like structure of spiritual stages, and the central problem of the soul's return to divine origin. The *Nay-nāmeh* opening (the reed cut from its reed-bed) is Rūmī's reformulation of the same *fanāʾ* theology.
- → [[phase-5-021-ibn-arabi-fusus-al-hikam]] — *parallel-milieu*: Ibn ʿArabī and Attar are near-contemporaries; the *Fuṣūṣ*'s systematic *waḥdat al-wujūd* (unity of being) is the philosophical prose exposition of the same truth the *Manṭiq al-Ṭayr* states as poetic parable.
- → [[phase-5-053-hafez-divan]] — *tradition-precursor*: Hafez absorbs the Sufi mystical registers developed by Attar; the *ghazal* form's wine/beloved ambiguity builds on the *fanāʾ* theology Attar established in the mathnawi tradition.
- → **Dante's *Purgatorio*** — *structural-parallel* (MASSIVE WIN): The Seven Valleys of the *Manṭiq al-Ṭayr* map with striking precision onto Dante's seven terraces of the *Purgatorio* (also seven stages of moral-spiritual ascent, also leading to the dissolution of self in God, written within a century). The thesis of Persian-Islamic influence on Dante via Andalusian transmission (Ibn ʿArabī's *Futūḥāt al-Makkiyya* was partially available in Latin) was argued by Asín Palacios (*Islam and the Divine Comedy*, 1919). Modern scholarship has not reached consensus, but the structural homology is precise enough to be a major investigation node.
- → **Plotinus's *Enneads*** — *parallel-philosophical* (MASSIVE WIN): The *sī murgh* / Simorgh pun is the most elegant literary statement of the Neoplatonic *epistrophē* (the soul's return to the One, which is the soul itself). Plotinus VI.9 teaches that the soul, in contemplating the One, discovers it is not merely approaching the One but *is* the One — the same revelation the thirty birds receive. No documented transmission link; the convergence is independently achieved.
- → **Meister Eckhart's sermons** — *parallel-theological*: Eckhart's "the eye through which I see God is the same eye through which God sees me" (Sermon 57) is the Christian mystical equivalent of the thirty-birds pun. The soul that seeks God discovers it is already what it seeks. Again, no transmission; the convergence is the evidence of a universal mystical grammar.
- → **Upanishadic *tat tvam asi*** — *parallel-theological*: "That art thou" (*Chandogya Upanishad* 6.8.7) is the Vedantic version of the same identity-revelation. Attar, Plotinus, Eckhart, and the Upanishads reach the same formulation from independent cultural starting-points.

## Disputes

- **Dante and Attar: transmission or structural convergence?** Asín Palacios's 1919 argument for Persian-Islamic influence on Dante has been contested and partially rehabilitated; the debate remains open. The seven-stage structure appears in multiple independent traditions (Hermetic, Neoplatonic, Islamic, Christian), which weakens the case for unique transmission while strengthening the case that the seven-stage ascent is a universal contemplative grammar.
- **The hoopoe's gender.** Translators and commentators differ on whether the hoopoe should be read as a guide-figure (male, in the tradition of Khiḍr) or as a feminine figure of the *nafs* (soul). The Persian allows both; Attar likely intended the ambiguity.
