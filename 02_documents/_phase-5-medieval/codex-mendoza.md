# Codex Mendoza

## What it is
An early-colonial **Aztec pictorial manuscript** (c. 1541–42), painted by Mexica *tlacuilo* in the indigenous tradition and annotated in Spanish, that documents the Mexica empire for European eyes in three parts: (1) a **history of conquests**, listing the rulers of Tenochtitlan from the city's founding in 1325 to the eve of the Spanish conquest and the towns each subjugated; (2) a **tribute roll**, itemizing the goods — textiles, feathers, cacao, warriors' costumes — that each conquered province paid to the imperial capital; and (3) an **ethnographic account of the life cycle**, from the naming and rearing of children through marriage, occupations, the priesthood and warfare, to old age. It is the premier source for the **political and economic organization** of the Aztec empire.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Made under the first viceroy, Antonio de Mendoza, as Spain inventoried the empire it had seized; the indigenous painters worked within colonial supervision.
- **Religious milieu.** Less a religious text than an administrative-ethnographic one, but its history section centres the cult-city of [[huitzilopochtli]], and its life-cycle section records the priesthood and ritual education.
- **Who was behind it.** Native scribe-painters and a Spanish interpreter; the imperial commission is traditional but not documented.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Intended for Charles V, captured at sea, and lodged for centuries in the Bodleian — itself a small parable of the Atlantic circulation of conquered knowledge.

## Themes and motifs
- [[divine-kingship]] — the conquest-history pages bind the Mexica *tlatoque*, their patron god, and imperial expansion into a single sacred-political record.

## Disputes
- **The commission.** The attribution to Viceroy Mendoza and the intended recipient (Charles V) rest on later tradition rather than firm documentation.
- **Pictorial vs. gloss.** The relationship between the indigenous painting and the sometimes-imperfect Spanish glosses is a continuing object of study (Berdan & Anawalt 1992).

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **The empire's account book.** The tribute roll is the single richest quantitative source for the Aztec imperial economy, letting historians map a tributary state in its own categories — a case where a colonial document preserves the *indigenous* administrative imagination, not just the conquerors' (Berdan & Anawalt 1992; Smith 2012).
- **Indigenous writing under colonial frame.** Painted by Mexica scribes in their own pictographic system yet glossed for a European king, the Codex Mendoza is a vivid artefact of the membership-vs-wire model: a Mexica *home* document carried across the ocean on a Spanish *wire*.
