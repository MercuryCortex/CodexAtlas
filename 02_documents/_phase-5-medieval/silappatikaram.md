# Silappatikāram (The Tale of the Anklet)

## What it is
The earliest of the great epics of Tamil literature — the **Tale of the Anklet** — composed in classical Tamil and ascribed to **Iḷaṅkō Aṭikaḷ**, by tradition a Chera prince who renounced the world as a Jain monk. It tells the tragedy of **Kaṇṇaki** and her husband **Kōvalaṉ**: a merchant of the Chola port of Puhār who abandons his virtuous wife for the courtesan Mādhavi, ruins himself, and returns to Kaṇṇaki; the couple travel to **Madurai**, the Pandya capital, to begin again by selling one of Kaṇṇaki's jewelled **anklets**; Kōvalaṉ is falsely accused of having stolen the queen's anklet and is executed without trial; and Kaṇṇaki, proving his innocence by breaking open her anklet (hers held rubies, the queen's pearls), curses the unjust city and burns Madurai to the ground, after which she is taken up and worshipped as a **goddess**. It is at once a literary masterpiece, a treatise on the three Tamil kingdoms, and the charter-myth of the Pattini goddess-cult.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** The early Tamil country of the "three crowned kings" — Chera, Chola, and Pandya — whose realms the epic deliberately spans.
- **Religious milieu.** Strikingly plural: a Jain author tells a story saturated with the worship of Hindu deities and folk goddesses, with Buddhist and Ājīvika presences in the background — a portrait of the religiously mixed early Tamil world.
- **Who was behind it.** Iḷaṅkō Aṭikaḷ in tradition; the historical author and exact date are debated.
- **Discovery / transmission.** A continuously revered Tamil classic; recovered for modern scholarship in the 19th–20th-century Tamil renaissance.

## Themes and motifs
- [[divine-feminine]] — the power of the chaste wife (*karpu*): Kaṇṇaki's virtue becomes a destructive and then a divine force; she is apotheosized as the goddess Pattini/Kaṇṇaki.
- [[divine-kingship]] — the epic turns on royal *justice*: the Pandya king dies of shame at having killed an innocent man, dramatizing the sacral duty of the king to dharma.

## Connections (provisional)
- → the Pattini cult of Sri Lanka — *direct-influence*: Kaṇṇaki becomes the Sinhalese Buddhist goddess **Pattini**, a major cross-tradition transmission (Obeyesekere 1984).
- → [[meenakshi]] / [[shiva]] — *shared-sphere*: the epic's Madurai is the city of the Mīnākṣī–Sundareśvara temple; the Tamil goddess-and-Śiva religious world frames the tale.

## Disputes
- **Date and authorship.** Estimates for the *Cilappatikāram* range across the 2nd–6th centuries; the historicity of Iḷaṅkō Aṭikaḷ and his Chera-royal Jain identity is traditional, not documented.
- **Religious classification.** A Jain-authored work centered on a Hindu/folk goddess-cult resists single-tradition labeling — its *home* is the plural Tamil religious world, with its Jain authorship and Buddhist (Pattini) reception best read as cross-tradition wires.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **One goddess across three religions.** Kaṇṇaki, the apotheosized chaste wife of a Jain-authored Tamil epic, became the Hindu/folk goddess of Tamil and Kerala temples *and* the Buddhist goddess **Pattini** of Sri Lanka — a single cult crossing Jain authorship, Hindu practice, and Buddhist devotion, one of South Asia's clearest documented goddess-transmissions (Obeyesekere 1984).
- **The literary charter of a people.** As the foundational Tamil epic spanning the three Tamil kingdoms, the *Cilappatikāram* became central to modern Tamil cultural identity — a national epic that is also a religious document (Parthasarathy 1993; Zvelebil 1973).
