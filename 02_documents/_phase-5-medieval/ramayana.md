# Rāmāyaṇa

The **Rāmāyaṇa** ("the Journey of Rāma") is, with the Mahābhārata, one of the two great Sanskrit epics (*itihāsa*) — and by tradition the **first of all poems**, revered as the **Ādikāvya** ("first poem") and ascribed to the sage **Vālmīki**, the **Ādikavi** ("first poet"). In ~**24,000 verses across seven books (kāṇḍas)** it tells the life of **Rāma**, prince of **Ayodhyā** and an avatar of **[[vishnu|Viṣṇu]]**, and has shaped the moral imagination of South and Southeast Asia for two millennia.

## The story

On the eve of his coronation, **[[rama|Rāma]]** is exiled to the forest for fourteen years through a court intrigue, and goes willingly out of duty, accompanied by his devoted wife **Sītā** and brother **Lakṣmaṇa**. There the demon-king **Rāvaṇa** of **Laṅkā** abducts Sītā. Rāma allies with the monkey (vānara) kingdom — above all the boundlessly devoted **[[hanuman|Hanumān]]**, who leaps to Laṅkā to find Sītā, and the monkey-king Sugrīva — and an army builds a bridge to the island, storms it, and in a great war Rāma slays Rāvaṇa and recovers Sītā. He returns to Ayodhyā to a model reign, the **Rāma-rājya**, the byword for ideal kingship.

## The central concern: dharma embodied

Where the **[[phase-3-095-mahabharata|Mahābhārata]]** dwells on dharma's *ambiguities*, the Rāmāyaṇa presents dharma **idealized**: Rāma is the **maryādā puruṣottama**, the perfect man who upholds righteousness whatever the cost; Sītā is the ideal of fidelity; Hanumān is the model of selfless devotion (*bhakti*); the bonds of brother, wife, and king are drawn as they ought to be. (The epic is not without its hard edges — Sītā's fire-ordeal and later banishment have been read and re-read for centuries.) It is, with the Gītā, a foundation of the Viṣṇu-avatar theology and of Rāma- and Hanumān-devotion.

## The cross-tradition wire (the MASSIVE-WINS)

The Rāmāyaṇa is one of the most **trans-culturally mobile** texts in world literature. Within India it is endlessly retold — Tulsīdās's Hindi **[[phase-6-019-tulsidas-ramcharitmanas|Rāmcaritmānas]]**, the Tamil Kamba Rāmāyaṇam. Beyond India it became the **shared epic of Greater India**: the Thai **Rāmakien** (the national epic of Thailand), the Khmer **Reamker**, the Javanese/Indonesian **Kakawin Rāmāyaṇa**, and Lao, Burmese, and Malay versions — carved across the temple walls of **Prambanan** (Java) and **Angkor Wat** (Cambodia). As a heroic verse-epic of exile, abduction, and the war to recover the queen, it invites the same comparison as its sister-epic with **Homer's Iliad and Odyssey**. The Rāmāyaṇa is, with the Mahābhārata, the bedrock of Hindu narrative and ethics — and the single most successful cultural export of premodern India.
