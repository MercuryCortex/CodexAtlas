# Devi Mahatmya

## What it is
A 700-verse Sanskrit hymn embedded as chapters 81–93 of the *Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* but functioning independently as the foundational text of Goddess theology in Hinduism. It tells three episodes of the Devi's combat with demons: (1) Mahā-Yoga-Māyā/Viṣṇu-Māyā wakes Viṣṇu to slay Madhu and Kaiṭabha; (2) Durgā, embodied from the combined *tejas* (radiant power) of all the male gods, kills the buffalo-demon Mahiṣa whom Brahmā's boon had rendered invulnerable to males; (3) Durgā and her ferocious emanation Kālī defeat Śumbha, Niśumbha, and their generals Caṇḍa-Muṇḍa and Raktabīja. Between battles, the text places three theological hymns (the *Brahmā-stuti*, *Śakrādi-stuti*, *Nārāyaṇī-stuti*) that proclaim the Goddess as the singular ultimate reality — *prakṛti*, *māyā*, and the Brahman of the Upaniṣads in feminine form. It is recited in its entirety during Navarātrī and forms the liturgical core of contemporary Durgā worship.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Gupta and immediate post-Gupta India (~400–600 CE); regional kingdoms rising as Gupta hegemony fragments; royal patronage of temple religion expanding.
- **Religious milieu.** The Vedic pantheon (Indra, Agni, Varuṇa) has been thoroughly displaced by the Purāṇic high gods (Viṣṇu, Śiva, Brahmā). The Goddess, marginal in the Vedas, is *not* simply elevated from inside the Vedic tradition — Coburn and Kinsley both argue that *Devī-Māhātmya* absorbs and Sanskritizes pre-existing tribal and village Goddess cults, giving them theological vocabulary borrowed from Vedānta and a martial-mythological frame patterned on the *Mahābhārata*'s combat narratives.
- **Who was behind it.** Anonymous Brāhmaṇical redactors working in a milieu where the boundary between śruti and smṛti was eroding and where new Goddess cults required scriptural authorization. The fact that the text is *embedded* in a Purāṇa but functions liturgically as quasi-śruti is characteristic of how the post-Gupta period created new "scripture" by Purāṇic insertion.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous Indian recitation tradition since at least the 9th century; oldest dated manuscript Nepal 998 CE (Asiatic Society of Bengal collection). Vasudeva Sharaṇa Agrawala's 1963 critical edition remains a key philological reference.

## Themes and motifs
- [[divine-feminine]] — the text is the foundational Sanskrit statement of the Goddess as ultimate reality; *Nārāyaṇī-stuti* identifies her with *prakṛti*, *māyā*, *vidyā*, *avidyā*, and Brahman.
- [[chaoskampf]] — three combat-of-the-Goddess narratives function structurally like Marduk/Tiamat and Indra/Vṛtra: order requires the slaying of demonic chaos, but here the slayer is female.
- [[creation-by-word]] — the Goddess as *Vāc* (speech, mantric power); the recitation of the text is itself the operative theological act.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-2-017-mahabharata-ramayana-oral-layers]] — *shared-milieu* / *direct-influence*: the Mahiṣa-Asura combat is patterned on the epic battle-narratives' set-pieces; the Devī occupies the structural slot Kṛṣṇa-as-Viṣṇu occupies in the *Bhagavadgītā*.
- → [[phase-5-006-vijnana-bhairava-tantra]] / [[phase-5-014-abhinavagupta-tantraloka]] — *direct-influence*: provides the theological warrant for later Tantric Goddess-centered cosmologies; Tantric Śaktism continuously cites it.
- → [[phase-1-008-enuma-elish]] — *parallel-motif*: structural Tiamat/Marduk inversion — the goddess here is not the slain chaos-monster but the slayer.
- → [[phase-2-001-rig-veda-family-books]] — *polemic-against* / *shared-milieu*: the Vedic male pantheon is depicted as collectively insufficient — they pool their *tejas* and need the Goddess to defeat demons whose boons make them invulnerable to all males. The narrative argues the Goddess is *prior* to the Vedic pantheon.

## Disputes
- **Dating.** Hazra (1963) argued 400–500 CE; Coburn (1984) argues 550–600 CE; the range -400 to -600 is consensus, with the late end favored.
- **Pre-Vedic Goddess substrate.** Whether the Devī here is a Sanskritized form of indigenous (Indus Valley? Dravidian? tribal?) Goddess cult, or a wholly Brāhmaṇical innovation, is a long-running debate (Bronkhorst 2007; Pintchman 1994).
- **Phase boundary.** Composition spans the Phase-4/Phase-5 border; seeded under Phase 5 because reception, manuscript tradition, and liturgical use are entirely medieval.
