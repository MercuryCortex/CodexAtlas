# Magna Carta (1215)

## What it is
**Magna Carta** ("the Great Charter") is the charter of liberties that the rebellious barons of England forced upon **King John** at Runnymede in June **1215**. Drafted with the deep involvement of **Archbishop Stephen Langton**, it sought to bind the king under law: its sixty-odd clauses restrain arbitrary taxation, protect the rights of the church and the barons, and — in the celebrated clause 39 — promise that "no free man shall be seized or imprisoned... except by the lawful judgment of his equals or by the law of the land." Tellingly, its **very first clause guarantees that "the English Church shall be free"** — the document is a product of Latin Christendom, in which the liberties of the church and the limits of kingship are argued in the same breath. Pope Innocent III annulled the 1215 version within weeks, but it was reissued under Henry III (1216, 1217, and the definitive 1225 text) and passed into English statute law.

## Significance
Magna Carta is the foundational document of the Western tradition that the ruler is *under* the law, not above it — a direct counter-statement to unqualified [[divine-kingship]]. Though a practical thirteenth-century settlement, its clause 39 became, over centuries, the seed of due process, habeas corpus, and constitutional and human-rights thought far beyond England. Its earliest clauses also make it a key text for the medieval relationship between royal power and the liberty of the church. The standard modern studies are Holt's and Carpenter's.
