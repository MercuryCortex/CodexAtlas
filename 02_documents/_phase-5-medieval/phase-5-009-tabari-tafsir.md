# Jāmiʿ al-Bayān (al-Ṭabarī's Tafsīr)

## What it is
The foundational, comprehensive Sunni commentary on the Qur'an by the polymath historian and jurist Muḥammad ibn Jarīr al-Ṭabarī. The work covers every verse of the Qur'an in order, presenting for each: (1) variant readings (*qirāʾāt*); (2) lexicographical and grammatical analysis drawing on pre-Islamic and early Islamic poetry; (3) all known interpretive traditions from the Prophet, Companions, and Successors, each report supplied with its transmission chain (*isnād*); (4) where reports diverge, Ṭabarī assesses them and indicates his preferred reading. The method is *tafsīr bi-l-maʾthūr* — exegesis through transmitted report — as distinct from *tafsīr bi-l-raʾy* — exegesis through personal reasoning, which Ṭabarī treats with caution. The work is the single most important scholarly endowment of medieval Sunni exegesis: every later *mufassir* (Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Suyūṭī) cites it as foundational.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Late Abbasid period; the Caliphate's political power was already fragmenting, but Baghdad remained the intellectual capital. Ṭabarī arrived in Baghdad ~870 from his native Ṭabaristan (south of the Caspian); studied across the Islamic world; settled in Baghdad to write.
- **Religious milieu.** Sunni traditionalism had won the *Miḥna* controversy by 850; hadith canonization was advancing; Imāmī Shi'ism was crystallizing; Mu'tazilite rationalism was retreating from court but remained intellectually formidable. Ṭabarī's tafsīr is a major monument of the *ahl al-ḥadīth* synthesis: scripturalist, isnād-disciplined, hostile to allegorical "free" exegesis.
- **Who was behind it.** Al-Ṭabarī himself: independently wealthy from family land in Ṭabaristan, never married, lived a scholarly life of legendary productivity (his *History*, *Taʾrīkh al-Rusul wa-l-Mulūk*, is the other monument). He founded a short-lived legal school (the Jarīrī, extinct by ~1000) of his own; held aloof from court politics; died Baghdad 923. The story of his harassment by Hanbali traditionalist mobs in his last years is well attested.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous Islamic transmission; printed in Cairo 1903 and 1954 (Shākir edition); the standard reference of Sunni Qur'an scholarship. Wansbrough (1977) used Ṭabarī's tafsīr as principal evidence in his radical-revisionist *Quranic Studies* arguing the canonical Qur'an text crystallized later than tradition claims (~800 CE rather than ~650). This thesis is not now mainstream but framed late-20th-c. critical Qur'anic scholarship.

## Themes and motifs
- [[creation-by-word]] — Ṭabarī presents the Sunni orthodox view: Qur'an's *kun fa-yakūn* ("Be, and it is") is real efficacious divine speech, against Mu'tazilite reductions.
- [[afterlife-judgment]] — extensive exegesis of the Qur'an's eschatological passages: paradise, hell, the Hour, intercession.
- [[divine-kingship]] — God's absolute sovereignty (*tawḥīd al-rubūbiyyah*) is consistently affirmed; God's actions are not subject to creaturely standards of justice (against Mu'tazila).

## Disputes
- **Wansbrough thesis.** John Wansbrough's *Quranic Studies* argued the Qur'an's canonization was a late-8th/early-9th-c. process, not a mid-7th c. one; Ṭabarī's tafsīr is read as evidence for this late crystallization. Not mainstream but historically important.
- **Ṭabarī's Shi'a leanings.** Some classical sources accuse Ṭabarī of Shi'a sympathies because he wrote a treatise on the merits of ʿAlī. Most scholars treat him as a moderate Sunni traditionalist.
