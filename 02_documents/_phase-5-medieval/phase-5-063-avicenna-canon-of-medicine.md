The *Qānūn fī al-Ṭibb* (Canon of Medicine, c. 1012–1025 CE) by [[avicenna]] (Ibn Sina, 980–1037 CE) is the most influential medical textbook in history — the culminating synthesis of three medical traditions (Greek/Galenic, Indian/Ayurvedic, Persian) into a unified, systematic encyclopedia that defined medical education in the Islamic world and Europe for over 600 years.

## The Five Books

**Book I — General Principles**: definition of medicine; the elements and humors; the body's faculties; temperament; anatomy; causes of health and disease; preservation of health through diet, sleep, exercise, emotional management. Contains the quarantine argument (the first rigorous contagion theory in any language) and the systematic environmental medicine framework.

**Book II — Materia Medica**: 800 medicinal substances (plants, minerals, animals) organized alphabetically — the largest pharmacopoeia produced before the early modern period. Each entry: properties, taste, potency, therapeutic indications, dosage, contraindications. The foundation of subsequent Islamic and European pharmacy.

**Book III — Head-to-Toe Pathology**: diseases of every organ system organized anatomically — the same logical organization as modern clinical medicine texts. Brain, eye, ear, throat, heart, lungs, stomach, liver, kidneys, reproductive organs — systematic pathology of each.

**Book IV — Systemic Conditions**: fevers, venoms, surgery, cosmetics, contagious diseases. The surgery section includes wound management, tumor removal, and the first detailed description of bone-setting techniques.

**Book V — Pharmaceutical Compounds**: combination medicines; pharmaceutical preparation methods; antidotes and theriac. The founding systematic formulary.

## The European Canon

**Gerard of Cremona** translated the Canon into Latin at Toledo, c. 1150 CE — one of the most consequential acts of translation in European intellectual history. The Latin Canon became required curriculum at:
- Bologna (founded 1088)
- Montpellier (founded 1220)
- Paris, Oxford, Cambridge

The Canon was **printed 16 times between 1469 and 1500** — making it one of the most frequently printed texts of the early print era, alongside the Bible. Vesalius (1543), Paracelsus (who burned the Canon publicly at Basel in 1527, which testifies to its authority as much as his rejection), and Harvey (1628) studied under it before breaking from it.

## Original Contributions

The Canon is not merely encyclopedic — it contains original clinical observations:
- First clinical distinction between **mediastinitis and pleuritis**
- First description of **anchylostoma** (hookworm) as a causative agent
- The **psychosomatic love-sickness diagnosis** (pulse test while speaking the beloved's name)
- Systematic **drug trial methodology**: pure drug before compound; animal testing before human; replicated results required
- **Quarantine theory**: infected persons and soil/water can transmit disease; isolation is required
