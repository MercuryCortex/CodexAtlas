# The Mirror of Simple Souls (*Le Mirouer des simples ames*)

## What it is
A 14th-century vernacular Old French (Picard dialect) mystical-allegorical dialogue by [[marguerite-porete]], composed ~1290–1300, in 139 chapters, structured as a courtly-love-style debate among personified figures (the **Soul**, **Love** [*Amour*], **Reason** [*Raison*], **Holy Church** [*Sainte Église*], **Spirit of God**, and others) tracing the soul's progress through **seven stages of grace** culminating in the soul's *annihilation* (*néant*) and its *becoming-God-by-condition-of-love* (*l'ame qui est Dieu par condition d'amour*). **The work for which Marguerite was burned at Paris, 1 June 1310** — the first execution of the medieval Inquisition for the contents of a vernacular-language book.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Composed in Hainaut (county of the Holy Roman Empire, but in French linguistic and cultural orbit) during the 1290s. First condemned by Bishop Guy II of Cambrai (~1306) and publicly burned in front of Marguerite at Valenciennes; Marguerite refused to surrender her book and continued circulating it. Re-arrested by the Dominican Inquisitor of France Guillaume de Paris (1308) — the same inquisitor who simultaneously prosecuted the [[event-trial-of-templars-1307-1314|Templars]]. Burned 1 June 1310 at the Place de Grève, Paris.
- **Religious milieu.** The work emerges from the **13th-14th-c. Beguine vernacular mystical tradition** of the Low Countries — Hadewijch of Antwerp (~1240s), Mechthild of Magdeburg (*Flowing Light of the Godhead*, ~1250–1280), Beatrice of Nazareth, Marguerite herself. Beguines were laywomen living in communal religious-but-not-vowed houses, doing mystical-theological work in *vernacular* — the practice was institutionally suspect for that reason alone.
- **Who was behind it.** Marguerite herself; the book was apparently *examined and approved* by three senior theologians (the Cistercian Godfrey of Fontaines, the Franciscan John of Quaregnon, the secular master John of Quincampoix) before her condemnation — a detail Marguerite emphasizes in her own preface. The Inquisition rejected this prior approval.
- **Transmission.** After her burning, the book was supposed to be destroyed. **It survived** through anonymous circulation in monastic libraries — Carthusian houses especially — and through Latin, Middle English, and Italian translations. The anonymous *Mirror* was widely respected in 14th-15th-c. mystical-monastic literature and was occasionally attributed to Ruusbroec, Eckhart, or other named mystics. **Romana Guarnieri's 1946 archival work identified the anonymous *Mirror* with Marguerite's named (and condemned) treatise**, definitively published in her 1965 *Il movimento del Libero Spirito*. The 1986 CCCM critical edition is the standard.

## Themes and motifs
- [[apophatic-mysticism]] — the *Mirror* is one of the high points of medieval Western apophatic-mystical writing. The soul's progress is *through* and *beyond* affirmative theology.
- [[bridal-mysticism]] — Marguerite operates within and radicalizes the Bernardine soul-as-bride tradition.
- [[soul-as-bride]] — the *Ame* is feminine-grammatical-Active throughout; Love is masculine.
- [[via-negativa]] — explicit Dionysian negation, vernacularized.
- [[divine-darkness]] — the seventh stage's *néant* is a darkness-beyond-light register.
- [[the-godhead-eckhart]] — the Eckhartian *Gottheit*-language has a direct vernacular-Beguine prototype here.

## Disputes
- **Heretical or orthodox-apophatic?** Modern critical scholarship (Babinsky, Hollywood, McGinn, Bauerschmidt) reads Marguerite as within bounds of apophatic mysticism; her 14th-c. judges read her as Free-Spirit antinomian. The *Mirror*'s most provocative passages — that the *annihilated soul* "no longer needs the institutional Church" — are the contested ones.
- **Did Eckhart read it?** Probably; McGinn 1998 reads the conceptual continuity as strong, even if a direct-citation smoking gun is missing.
- **Carthusian preservation paradox.** The same medieval institutional Church that burned Marguerite simultaneously preserved her book through Carthusian copies and translations. The Carthusians were not knowingly preserving Marguerite (the book was anonymous in their mss) but were preserving what they took to be a serious apophatic-mystical text — which it is.
