# Fuṣūṣ al-Ḥikam (The Bezels of Wisdom)

## What it is
Ibn ʿArabī's most-read and most-commented-on work — 27 chapters, each treating a "wisdom" (*ḥikma*) associated with a prophet (Adam, Seth, Noah, Idrīs/Enoch, Abraham, Isaac, Ishmael, Jacob, Joseph, Hūd, Ṣāliḥ, Shuʿayb, Lot, Ezra, Jesus, Solomon, David, Jonah, Job, John, Zachariah, Elijah, Luqmān, Aaron, Moses, Khālid, and Muḥammad — the Qur'anic prophetic line plus a couple of additions). Each chapter takes its prophet as a "bezel" (*faṣṣ*) — the setting of a precious stone — in which a unique facet of divine wisdom is realized. The work develops Ibn ʿArabī's metaphysics: *waḥdat al-wujūd* (oneness of being) — there is *only* the one Being (= God = *al-Ḥaqq*, "the Real"), and all apparent multiplicity is the self-disclosure (*tajallī*) of the Real through the immutable archetypes (*aʿyān thābita*) which are the eternal possibilities-of-becoming in God's knowledge. Creation is God's *self-knowledge* externalized; the cosmos is a *theophany*. The chapter on Muhammad presents the doctrine of the *Muḥammadan Reality* (*al-ḥaqīqa al-Muḥammadiyya*) — the Logos-like first principle of creation, identified with the *Nūr Muḥammadī* (Muhammadan light), through which all creation emerges. Ibn ʿArabī claims to have received the book through a vision of the Prophet in Damascus 1229.

## Primary text — selected excerpts

The *Fuṣūṣ al-Ḥikam* has never had a fully public-domain English translation; the standard scholarly translations are R. W. J. Austin's *The Bezels of Wisdom* (Paulist, 1980), Caner Dagli's *Fuṣūṣ al-Ḥikam: An Annotated Translation* (Kazi, 2004), and Binyamin Abrahamov's *Ibn al-ʿArabī's Fuṣūṣ al-Ḥikam: An Annotated Translation* (Routledge, 2015). The excerpts below are short fair-use renderings paraphrased and harmonized with reference to Austin 1980 and Dagli 2004 — for any scholarly use consult those directly.

### Chapter 1 (Adam) — the Perfect Man as polished mirror

The opening chapter. The Real (*al-Ḥaqq*) desires to see his own self-disclosure in a "comprehensive being" — the human Adam, the *al-Insān al-Kāmil* (Perfect Man), is created as that polished mirror in which the Real beholds himself.

> The Real willed, glorified be He, in virtue of His Beautiful Names, which are innumerable, to see their identity, or, if you wish, to see His own identity in a comprehensive being which, having been given existence, would epitomize the whole reality. For the seeing of a thing by oneself in oneself is not like the seeing of a thing in another, which serves as a mirror...
>
> Thus the matter required, in virtue of its own reality, the polishing of the mirror of the world, and Adam was the very polishing of that mirror and the spirit of that form. The angels were among the faculties of that form, which is the form of the cosmos. The cosmos was thus complete by Adam's existence in it.

Citation: *Fuṣūṣ al-Ḥikam*, Chapter 1 (Wisdom of Divinity in the Word of Adam), paraphrased with reference to Austin 1980, pp. 50–51, and Dagli 2004, pp. 5–7.

### Chapter 1 (Adam, continued) — God's knowing of himself through the world

A further passage from the Adam chapter: the cosmos is the locus in which God's eternal names — Merciful, Wise, Creator — become *enacted*. Without creation, those names would have no objects.

> Adam is the very polishing of that mirror. Just as the mirror, when not polished, will not reflect the image of the looker, so the form of the cosmos, when not made manifest through the human, would not reflect the form of God. The Real desired that His Names, by which He is known to Himself, be known also through their effects.
>
> Now the Names of God are infinite, since they are known by what proceeds from them, and what proceeds from them is infinite, though it returns to the limited principles which are the Mothers of the Names, or the Universals of the Names. In reality there is no other than He, no Existence but His.

Citation: *Fuṣūṣ*, Chapter 1, paraphrased with reference to Austin 1980, pp. 51–53.

### Chapter 27 (Muhammad) — the love of women and the supreme theophany

The closing chapter — and the most famous theological-erotic passage in Sufi literature. The Prophet's beloved triad in the hadith ("women, perfume, the coolness of my eye in prayer") becomes the structure of the Perfect Man's path: woman is the most complete theophany for man because in her he beholds both the active and the passive, both himself and his Lord.

> God's contemplation of His own essence is intrinsic to Himself; but for the human, contemplation of the Real in matter is more complete than contemplation in pure spirit, since the Real is the One who manifests in every form. The contemplation of the Real in women is the most complete and most perfect; the witness in this contemplation is the union of two aspects — the active and the passive.
>
> Thus when the Prophet loved women, it was because of the perfection of God's manifestation in them. The man who knows the Real in his beloved knows Him with an intimate, active-passive knowing; he who knows Him only in himself knows Him in only one mode. Therefore the love of women belongs to the perfections of the Knowers.

Citation: *Fuṣūṣ*, Chapter 27 (Wisdom of Singularity in the Word of Muhammad), paraphrased with reference to Austin 1980, pp. 274–278, and Dagli 2004, pp. 285–293. Sufi commentaries on this chapter — Qūnawī, Qāshānī, Jāmī — fill volumes.

### Chapter 10 (Hūd) — God in every belief

One of Ibn ʿArabī's most famous and most-quoted passages: every religion's god is a *real* facet of the Real, since the Real cannot be confined to any single self-disclosure. The danger is mistaking one's own God-of-belief for the whole.

> Beware lest you bind yourself to a particular creed and reject all others as unbelief! If you do so, you will fail to obtain a great benefit. Indeed, you will miss the true knowledge of the matter. Rather, become in yourself the material of all forms of belief, for God is too great and vast to be confined to one creed to the exclusion of all others. He says: "Wherever you turn, there is the face of God" [Qur'an 2:115]. He does not say "there" in such-and-such a direction, but rather mentions "the face," which is the very essence of a thing.

Citation: *Fuṣūṣ*, Chapter 10 (Wisdom of Unity in the Word of Hūd), paraphrased with reference to Austin 1980, pp. 137–138, and Dagli 2004, pp. 109–115. This passage was reproduced by Ibn ʿArabī himself in poetic form in *Tarjumān al-Ashwāq* (the "religion of love" lyric).

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Ayyubid Syria and Mamluk-era transition. Ibn ʿArabī had moved from Almohad-controlled Andalusia (where he was born, 1165) and Maghreb across the Islamic world — Cairo, Mecca, Konya, Mosul, Baghdad — finally settling in Damascus where he died 1240. Damascus was an Ayyubid capital; Sufi institutional networks (*ṭuruq* / orders) were consolidating.
- **Religious milieu.** Twelfth–thirteenth-c. Islamic mysticism had inherited the Sunni-Sufi synthesis crystallized by al-Ghazālī and was developing its most adventurous metaphysical articulations. Ibn ʿArabī's *waḥdat al-wujūd* would prove the most consequential — and most controversial — Sufi metaphysical claim. Ibn Taymiyya (~1300) would condemn Ibn ʿArabī as a pantheist heretic; later Sunni traditionalism (Wahhabism, Salafism) continues this critique; mainstream Ottoman, Persian, Indian, and African Sufi traditions through to the modern period have treated him as the supreme Sufi metaphysician.
- **Who was behind it.** Ibn ʿArabī himself — Ibn al-ʿArabī (Andalusian, often spelled with the article to distinguish from the Mālikī jurist Abū Bakr Ibn al-ʿArabī). Born Murcia 1165; received early Sufi initiation in Andalusia and Maghreb; lifelong author of more than 350 works (most short, some massive). Known as *al-Shaykh al-Akbar* (the Greatest Master). The *Fuṣūṣ* is the late-life crystallization of doctrines developed in the much larger *Futūḥāt al-Makkiyya* ("Meccan Openings" — begun in Mecca ~1202, completed 1231).
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous Islamic transmission. The *Fuṣūṣ* is one of the most-commented-on texts of Islamic civilization — Chittick and others document over 150 medieval commentaries. The Akbarī school proper begins with Ibn ʿArabī's stepson and student Ṣadr al-Dīn al-Qūnawī (~1273) in Konya, who systematized the doctrine and connected it to Greek Neoplatonic and Avicennan philosophy. The school dominated post-Mongol Islamic intellectual culture for centuries.

## Themes and motifs
- [[non-duality]] — *waḥdat al-wujūd*: the most radical Islamic statement of metaphysical non-dualism. Being is one; all apparent multiplicity is the differentiating self-manifestation of the One.
- [[hidden-god]] — God in his *aḥadiyya* (sheer oneness, beyond all relation) is unknowable; God known is God-in-self-disclosure (*wāḥidiyya*).
- [[divine-feminine]] — Ibn ʿArabī's celebrated chapter on Muhammad's love for women (the closing chapter of *Fuṣūṣ*) develops a theology of the feminine: the woman is the most perfect locus of theophany for the man, because in her he sees God's self-disclosure as both active and passive, both knower and known. His Sufi master in Mecca, Faṭīma bint al-Muthannā, was a woman; he had visionary encounters with female personifications of divine wisdom (Niẓām, the Persian woman in Mecca whose poetic praise generated his *Tarjumān al-Ashwāq*).
- [[logos-cosmic-reason]] — the *Muḥammadan Reality* / *Logos*: the prototype-principle of all creation; identified with the *Nūr Muḥammadī*.
- [[gnosis-as-salvation]] — direct knowing (*maʿrifa*) of God is the heart of religion; the Sufi *ʿārif* is the realized knower.

## Disputes
- **Pantheism vs. panentheism.** Sunni traditionalist critique (Ibn Taymiyya, modern Salafism) reads *waḥdat al-wujūd* as pantheist heresy denying the Creator-creature distinction. Ibn ʿArabī's defenders argue for panentheism (creation is in God; God exceeds creation; the *aḥadiyya* / *wāḥidiyya* distinction preserves transcendence). The interpretive question shapes contemporary Islamic theology.
- **Authenticity of *Fuṣūṣ*.** A small minority position has questioned the work's authenticity on theological grounds; standard scholarship accepts it as Ibn ʿArabī's.
- **Reading of the chapter on Aaron** (containing material that traditionalists found offensive on the Israelite worship of the Golden Calf) and on Noah (where Ibn ʿArabī's exegesis of Noah's polytheist contemporaries is famously controversial) — these are perennial flashpoints.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- ***Waḥdat al-wujūd* ↔ Advaita Vedānta ↔ Dzogchen ↔ Eckhart's "being is God" — the vault's non-dual summit:** four traditions assert radical metaphysical non-dualism — that multiplicity is the self-disclosure of a single Being, not a second ontological principle — using structurally identical arguments. Ibn ʿArabī's formulation is the Islamic member within the vault's most tightly cross-referenced convergence cluster; the comparison is not vague "mystical unity" but specific logical claims (the One cannot be other-than-itself; multiplicity is *tajallī* / *vivartavāda* / *lhun grub* / *istigkeit*) made with no demonstrated contact.
- **The Perfect Man (*al-Insān al-Kāmil*) ↔ Gnostic Anthropos ↔ Philo's Logos ↔ Kabbalistic Adam Kadmon:** four traditions posit a primordial cosmic human as the ontological mediator between the absolute and creation — the blueprint from which the universe is formed, and the ideal through which the mystic returns to the source. Ibn ʿArabī's *al-Insān al-Kāmil* is the Islamic member of the vault's most widely-distributed cosmic-mediator-figure cluster, completing a chain that runs from Philo's second-God Logos (~30 CE) through John's incarnate Word to the Kabbalistic first Adam.
- **"Every belief is a face of the Real" — the medieval source of religious pluralism:** Ibn ʿArabī's argument that the divine reality exceeds every religious formulation — and that the Kabbalistic knower recognizes God even in Israelite calf-worship (the Aaron chapter), because God overflows all doctrinal containers — is the intellectual ancestor of 20th-century religious pluralism (John Hick's "Real-as-such"; Raimundo Panikkar's "cosmotheandric"). This is the vault's clearest bridge between medieval Sufi metaphysics and modern Western philosophy of religion.
- ***Nūr Muḥammadī* ↔ Philo's Logos ↔ John's Logos ↔ Proverbs' Wisdom/*Ḥokhmah*:** four traditions posit a pre-cosmic luminous mediator principle — the light of Muhammad, Philo's Word-as-second-God, John's incarnate *Logos*, Proverbs' *Ḥokhmah* "with God before creation" — identified as the primordial blueprint of creation. The vault's primary cosmic-Word cluster is now fully traceable from ~-900 BCE (Proverbs) through Philo (~30 CE), John (~90 CE), and Ibn ʿArabī (1230 CE) with T1 sources at every node.
