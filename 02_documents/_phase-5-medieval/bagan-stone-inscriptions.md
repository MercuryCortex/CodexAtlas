# Bagan Stone Inscriptions

## What it is
The plain of **Bagan** (Pagan) in central Myanmar, dense with thousands of Buddhist temples built between the 11th and 13th centuries, preserves a great body of **stone inscriptions** recording the donations — land, labour, rice-fields, and slaves — by which kings and commoners endowed those temples and earned merit. The most famous is the **Myazedi inscription** (1113 CE), a stone bearing the same dedication in four languages — Pyu, Mon, Old Burmese, and Pali — which served as the key to deciphering the extinct **Pyu** language and is celebrated as the "Rosetta Stone of Burma." The inscriptions document the Theravāda Buddhist polity that the king **Anawrahta** founded after (by tradition) importing the Pali canon and Theravāda orthodoxy from the Mon kingdom of Thaton.

## Significance
The Bagan inscriptions are the primary source for the political and religious history of the first Burmese empire and a vivid record of the Theravāda **merit-economy**: kingship legitimated through monumental temple-donation ([[divine-kingship]]). Through the Pali-language portions they connect Burma to the same canonical Theravāda world chronicled in the Sri Lankan [[mahavamsa]] and carried by the [[tradition-theravada]]. The quadrilingual Myazedi stone is, in addition, one of the most important epigraphic keys in Southeast Asian philology.
