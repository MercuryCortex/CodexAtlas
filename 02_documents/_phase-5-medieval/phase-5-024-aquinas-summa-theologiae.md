# Summa Theologiae

## What it is
Aquinas's magnum opus and the high-water mark of Christian scholastic theology — a comprehensive systematic theology in three parts comprising ~3,000 articles. Each article follows the standard scholastic form: a question (*utrum...*), objections (arguments for the opposed view), a *sed contra* (a brief counter-citation, usually scriptural or patristic), the body of the article (*responsio*) presenting Aquinas's solution, and replies to each of the opening objections. The whole moves systematically: Prima Pars treats God in himself (existence — the famous Five Ways at ST I, q. 2, a. 3; simplicity, perfection, goodness, infinity, etc.), the Trinity, creation in general, angels, the six days, and human nature; Prima Secundae treats the fundamental structure of moral life — the last end (*beatitudo*), the passions, virtues, vices, law (natural, divine, human), grace; Secunda Secundae treats specific virtues (faith, hope, charity, the four cardinal virtues — prudence, justice, fortitude, temperance) and their opposed vices; Tertia Pars treats the Incarnation, the life of Christ, and the sacraments. The integrating principle: theology is *scientia* (a deductive science in Aristotle's sense) of God, working from revealed first principles. Aquinas's synthesis of Aristotelian philosophy and Christian revelation — assigning each its proper domain, granting reason genuine power while affirming the supremacy of revelation — defined Catholic theology for centuries.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Aquinas's career spans the reigns of Holy Roman Emperor Frederick II (1220–1250) and his Hohenstaufen successors; the Latin Kingdom of Sicily / Naples (where Aquinas's family was related to imperial nobility); and the papal court at Rome and Viterbo. The 13th-c. universities (Paris in particular) were the dominant intellectual institutions and the venue for the high scholastic synthesis.
- **Religious milieu.** The mendicant revolution — Dominicans (founded 1216) and Franciscans (1209) — had transformed Western Christianity. The new Latin translations of Aristotle directly from Greek by William of Moerbeke OP (~1260–1270) had made the full Aristotelian corpus available without Arabic-philosophical mediation. The "Latin Averroism" of Siger of Brabant and others at Paris (rationalist Aristotelianism allegedly incompatible with revealed Christianity) was a live threat — Aquinas's project of Christian-Aristotelian synthesis was partly directed against it. Bonaventure, Aquinas's Franciscan contemporary at Paris, championed a more Augustinian-Neoplatonic theology. After Aquinas's death (1274), some 219 propositions including some attributable to Aquinas were condemned at Paris (1277) by Bishop Étienne Tempier — a serious shock, gradually reversed: Aquinas was canonized in 1323, declared a Doctor of the Church in 1567, and his philosophy was made the foundation of Catholic seminary education by Pope Leo XIII's *Aeterni Patris* (1879).
- **Who was behind it.** Thomas of Aquino: of the Counts of Aquino, an Italian aristocratic family; entered the Dominican order against family resistance ~1244; studied at Paris under Albertus Magnus ~1245–1252; held the Paris Dominican chair twice (1256–1259, 1268–1272); taught in Italy in between (Naples, Orvieto, Rome — where the *Prima Pars* was composed at the *studium* of Santa Sabina). He stopped writing on 6 December 1273, after a celebrated mystical experience during Mass at Naples ("All I have written seems to me like straw"); he died on the way to the Council of Lyon, March 1274.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous Catholic transmission, with the partial setback of the 1277 Paris condemnation. The post-1879 neo-Thomist revival made the *Summa* the official theological framework of Catholic seminaries through the early 20th century; the Vatican II council (1962–1965) softened the requirement but Thomism remains a (now one-among-many) major Catholic theological position. The *Summa* is read across confessional boundaries — Anglican, Reformed, and increasingly secular philosophical engagement (Anscombe, Geach, Stump, MacIntyre) has substantially broadened the audience.

## Themes and motifs
- [[unmoved-mover]] — Aquinas's Five Ways (ST I, q. 2, a. 3) are the classical statements of Aristotelian cosmological arguments adapted to Christian theism — argument from motion, efficient causality, contingency, grades of perfection, governance / teleology — the "Quinque Viae" remain the most-discussed natural theology in Western philosophy.
- [[hidden-god]] — God is known by us in this life *quoad an sit, non quoad quid sit* (that he is, not what he is); the divine simplicity tradition reaches its sharpest medieval articulation here.
- [[creation-by-word]] — creation *ex nihilo*, defended philosophically against eternalist positions; creation is *not* a temporal event in Aristotle's sense but the metaphysical dependence of all being on God.
- [[ethical-dualism]] / virtue and vice — the *Secunda Pars* is the most systematic Christian-Aristotelian moral theology ever composed.
- [[soul-immortality]] — the rational soul subsists *per se* (against pure-form-of-the-body Averroists) and is therefore naturally immortal.
- [[afterlife-judgment]] — the *Supplementum* (compiled by disciples) presents the medieval eschatological synthesis.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-3-003-aristotle-metaphysics]] — *commentary-on* / *direct-influence*: Aquinas wrote line-by-line Latin commentaries on most of the Aristotelian corpus; the *Summa* uses Aristotle's metaphysics, physics, psychology, and ethics as its philosophical framework.
- → [[phase-4-029-augustine-city-of-god]] — *direct-influence*: Augustine is the single most-cited Christian theological authority in the *Summa*.
- → [[phase-4-030-pseudo-dionysius]] — *direct-influence*: Aquinas inherits the Dionysian framework of divine names and procession-return; the *Summa*'s entire architectonic of *exitus et reditus* (procession and return) is Dionysian.
- → [[phase-5-017-peter-lombard-sentences]] — *commentary-on*: Aquinas wrote a *Scriptum super Sententias* (1252–1256) before the *Summa*; the *Summa* implicitly displaces the *Sentences* as the standard textbook.
- → [[phase-5-019-maimonides-guide-for-the-perplexed]] — *direct-influence*: Maimonides ("Rabbi Moyses") is cited repeatedly, especially on divine attributes and creation. Aquinas's negative-theology and creation-doctrine pages are unintelligible without Maimonides.
- → Avicenna and Averroes — *direct-influence* / *polemic-against*: Aquinas absorbs much Avicennan philosophy (essence-existence distinction) and rejects Averroist conclusions (single Active Intellect; eternity of world as demonstrated).
- → [[phase-5-023-liber-de-duobus-principiis]] — *polemic-against* (mirror image): the *Summa* and the *Liber de Duobus Principiis* are composed in the same decade with the same scholastic toolkit defending opposite metaphysics — Catholic monism vs. Cathar dualism.

## Disputes
- **Condemnation of 1277.** Some of the 219 propositions condemned at Paris by Bishop Tempier appear to be Aquinas's; the historical question of how serious this condemnation was for Thomism's reception is well-studied (Hissette 1977).
- **Reception of Aristotle.** Did Aquinas Christianize Aristotle, or Aristotelianize Christianity? Both views have champions; current scholarship (Burrell, MacIntyre, Stump) tends to read Aquinas as more subtle than this dichotomy.
- **The straw remark.** Aquinas's late mystical experience and dismissal of his own work as "straw" is variously interpreted as humility, depression / health-related, or genuine mystical relativization of conceptual theology.
