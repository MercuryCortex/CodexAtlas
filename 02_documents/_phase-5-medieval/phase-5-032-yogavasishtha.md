# Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha

## What it is
A monumental narrative-philosophical Sanskrit work — ~32,000 verses in the full *Bṛhad-Yogavāsiṣṭha* recension, ~6,000 in the *Laghu* abridgement — in which the sage Vasiṣṭha instructs the young pre-coronation Rāma (the future king of the Rāmāyaṇa) in the radical non-dualist metaphysics that the world is a mental construction within the singular consciousness (*cit*). The work is structured as a long series of interlocking parable-stories (the *upākhyānas* — Story of Lila, Story of Queen Cūḍālā, Story of King Vipaścit, the Crow Story, etc.) embedded within philosophical exposition. The stories are among the most metaphysically inventive in Sanskrit literature: cosmologies-within-cosmologies, mind-created worlds, time-loops, gender-swapping mystical episodes, beings who pass between embedded universes. The work is non-sectarian — it invokes neither Vaishnava nor Shaiva sectarian markers — and is widely treated as the most philosophically radical Sanskrit Advaita narrative work.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Medieval Kashmir under the Utpala and Lohara dynasties (~900–1300 CE) and the broader pan-Indian Advaita Vedanta cultural sphere. The Kashmir intellectual ferment that produced the Pratyabhijñā philosophy ([[utpaladeva]], [[abhinavagupta]]) and the Trika Tantric synthesis is the most likely originating environment for the Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha.
- **Religious milieu.** Late-medieval Kashmir was the site of intense Hindu (Trika Tantra, Pratyabhijñā Advaita, dualist Shaiva Siddhānta, Vaishnavism), Buddhist (Mahāyāna and Vajrayāna), and emerging Islamic (post-1320 Kashmiri Sultanate) interaction. The Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha's idealist non-dualism shows points of contact with Mahāyāna *cittamātra* / Yogācāra (consciousness-only) Buddhist thought; this convergence has been noted since the 19th c. (Vidyaranya 1339-ish; modern: Chenet 1998, Dunne 2011).
- **Who was behind it.** Anonymous Kashmiri Advaita-narrative tradition; the attribution to Vālmīki is mythological-canonical, identifying the work as a parallel-Rāmāyaṇa.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous Kashmir manuscript transmission; the Laghu abridgement by Abhinanda (~10th c.) made the text accessible to the broader Sanskrit reading public; modern Western reception substantially mediated by the Theosophical Society (Atreya 1936 published at Theosophical Publishing House Adyar), by Swami Vivekananda's citations, and by Swami Venkatesananda's 1984 SUNY edition.

## Themes and motifs
- [[non-duality]] — the cardinal doctrine: only *cit* (consciousness) is real; the world is a *cit-vṛtti* (modification of consciousness), structurally similar to a dream.
- [[idealism-metaphysical]] — the Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha is perhaps the most thoroughly idealist Indian work: the world is *literally* a mental construction, and the philosophical-narrative project is to demonstrate this through proliferating stories of mind-created worlds.
- [[ground-of-being]] — *cit* / *brahman* as the singular ground of all apparent multiplicity.
- [[cosmic-cycles]] — embedded universes, world-cycles within world-cycles; the metaphysical narrative architecture is fractal.
- **Mind-created worlds (*saṅkalpa-māyā*)** — the philosophical-narrative signature: the work demonstrates over and over how a person's mind can construct an entire universe in which they are a different being living a different life, only to "wake up" (sometimes after lifetimes within the mental universe) into the framing reality, which is itself revealed as another mental construction. Among the most metaphysically inventive narrative literature in any language.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-5-005-shankara-brahma-sutra-bhasya]] — *parallel-motif*: structurally non-dualist, but the Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha pushes beyond Shankara's classical Advaita in its emphasis on *cit* (consciousness) rather than *brahman*-as-ground, and in its consciousness-idealism (the world is a *thought*, not just a *māyā*).
- → [[phase-5-006-vijnana-bhairava-tantra]] — *parallel-motif* / *shared-milieu*: Kashmir Tantric-Advaita consciousness-as-ultimate position; both texts treat consciousness as the active-ontological ground.
- → [[phase-5-002-heart-sutra]] — *parallel-motif*: the Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha's *cit*-centered idealism shares with Yogācāra Mahāyāna ("consciousness-only") the position that mind is the constitutive ontological ground; this Hindu-Buddhist convergence in medieval Kashmir is one of the most striking cross-tradition philosophical-doctrinal alignments.
- → Mahāyāna Yogācāra / Vijñānavāda Buddhist tradition (Vasubandhu's *Viṃśatikā*, Asaṅga's *Mahāyāna-saṃgraha*) — *parallel-motif* / *direct-influence* (contested-direction): structural parallels are pervasive. Chenet 1998 argues for substantial direct influence on the Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha from Yogācāra; the influence-direction question is whether Yogācāra → Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha (most likely, given chronology) or whether the convergence is independently developed within a shared Kashmiri intellectual sphere.
- → Modern Advaita popularizations — *direct-influence*: [[ramana-maharshi]] (1879–1950) taught from the Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha frequently; it remains one of the most-recommended texts in the modern Advaita-satsang lineage (Nisargadatta, Papaji, the Western neo-Advaita movement).
- → [[helena-blavatsky]]'s *Isis Unveiled* (1877) and *Secret Doctrine* (1888) — *direct-influence*: Blavatsky cites the Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha and incorporates its embedded-worlds cosmological architecture into her cosmology. The Theosophical Society's 1936 publication of Atreya's study is one moment in the 19th–20th-c. Western esotericist reception.

## Disputes
- **Date**: the spread is wide (~10th c. to ~14th c.); Chenet 1998 argues for an 11th–12th-c. core with later expansion.
- **Authorship**: anonymous-school; the Vālmīki attribution is canonical-mythological.
- **Buddhist-Yogācāra influence**: contested; the convergence is undisputed.
- **Recensional history**: the relation between the *Bṛhad* (~32,000 verses) and *Laghu* (~6,000 verses) is well-attested (Abhinanda's abridgement), but the additional recensional variation is complex.
