# The Guide for the Perplexed

## What it is
Maimonides's magnum opus of philosophical theology, written in Judeo-Arabic ~1185–1191 in Fustat (Cairo), addressed to his disciple Joseph ben Judah. The work has three parts. Part I lectures on Biblical anthropomorphic language (treating divine "hand," "foot," "face," etc. as figurative — a major technical exercise in negative theology) and concludes with a treatment of divine attributes that is radically apophatic: properly understood, every positive attribute predicated of God must be denied; only privative or relational predicates are tolerable. Part II takes up the question of the world's eternity vs. creation in time, surveying Aristotle's, the kalām's, and the Platonist positions; Maimonides concludes (carefully) for creation in time as more compatible with Scripture, while making clear that Aristotle's arguments for eternity are not strictly demonstrative. Part III treats prophecy, providence, and the reasons for the commandments (*ṭaʿamei ha-mitzvot*) — explaining Levitical sacrifice and Hebrew ritual as historically conditioned concessions to the religious mentality of the people exiting Egypt, intended to wean Israel from pagan practice. The book is famously written *esoterically*, with deliberate contradictions and chapter-arrangements intended (Maimonides himself says) to be intelligible only to the philosophically prepared.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Ayyubid Egypt under Saladin (1138–1193) and his successors. Maimonides served as physician at the Ayyubid court and as *Nagid* (head) of the Egyptian Jewish community. The Jewish community of Fustat-Cairo was the leading Jewish community of the Islamic world after Baghdad's decline.
- **Religious milieu.** Twelfth-century Andalusian-Egyptian Jewish intellectual culture had thoroughly absorbed Arabic-Islamic philosophy, especially Avicenna and al-Fārābī. Maimonides was the supreme synthesizer of Jewish tradition and *falsafa*. The *Guide*'s immediate audience was a philosophically inclined Jewish elite torn between Aristotelian rationalism and rabbinic tradition — Maimonides's task is to demonstrate that the two are reconcilable, with substantial interpretive labor (especially of Scripture).
- **Who was behind it.** Maimonides himself: born Córdoba 1138 in the twilight of Andalusian Jewish life; his family fled the Almohad persecutions ~1148; sojourned in Morocco; settled in Fustat ~1166. By 1185 he was court physician and *Nagid*. His other works: the *Commentary on the Mishnah* (Arabic, ~1158–1168, including the "Thirteen Principles of Faith" in his commentary on Sanhedrin); the *Mishneh Torah* (Hebrew, ~1170–1180), a comprehensive legal code organized topically rather than as Talmud-commentary; the *Treatise on Resurrection* (response to controversy over his having allegedly denied bodily resurrection); medical treatises in Arabic.
- **Discovery / transmission.** The *Guide* triggered the "Maimonidean Controversy" within Judaism — a multi-generation dispute, particularly intense in 13th-c. Provence and Spain (Solomon ibn Adret, Abba Mari, Nahmanides), over whether Maimonides's philosophical interpretation of Judaism was orthodox or dangerously rationalist. The work was publicly burned at Montpellier in 1232 by Christian authorities (incited by anti-Maimonidean rabbis), and at Paris in 1234 along with the Talmud. Aquinas studied it as "Rabbi Moyses." Spinoza took it as a point of departure (and rejected its allegorical exegesis as fundamentally untenable). In modern reception, Leo Strauss's *Persecution and the Art of Writing* (1952) revived the question of Maimonides's esotericism.

## Themes and motifs
- [[hidden-god]] — Maimonides's negative theology is the most uncompromising in the medieval Jewish tradition: every positive predicate predicated of God must, properly understood, be denied. God is known only through negations and through actions.
- [[creation-by-word]] — but qualified: Maimonides defends *creatio in tempore* against Aristotelian eternalism while admitting the arguments are not strictly demonstrative; the choice is partly hermeneutic.
- [[unmoved-mover]] — Maimonides treats Aristotle's prime mover as fully compatible with Hebrew Bible's God *qua* metaphysics, given proper interpretation.
- [[soul-immortality]] — intellectualist: the immortal part of the soul is the actualized intellect; salvation = intellectual perfection; the masses' belief in bodily resurrection is allowed as a politically necessary accommodation. The "*Treatise on Resurrection*" qualifies this for orthodox consumption.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-3-003-aristotle-metaphysics]] — *commentary-on* / *direct-influence*: Maimonides reads Aristotle as the source of demonstrative truth about the natural world, with the divinely revealed text reread accordingly.
- → [[phase-5-010-saadia-emunot-ve-deot]] — *direct-influence* (qualified): Maimonides knew Saʿadia thoroughly and built on his project of philosophical theology but treated him as philosophically inadequate (Saʿadia worked from Kalām, which Maimonides considered superseded by Avicennan falsafa).
- → al-Fārābī / Avicenna / Ghazālī — *direct-influence*: Maimonides cites al-Fārābī as his most reliable philosophical source; Avicennan psychology and metaphysics underlie Parts I and II; Ghazālī's ambivalence toward philosophy is part of the methodological backdrop.
- → [[phase-5-024-aquinas-summa-theologiae]] — *direct-influence*: Aquinas cites *Rabbi Moyses* repeatedly, especially on divine attributes and creation.
- → Spinoza — *direct-influence* (in negation): Spinoza begins from Maimonidean negative theology and pushes it past Maimonides into a thoroughgoing rationalist monism.

## Disputes
- **Esotericism.** Leo Strauss 1952 argued the *Guide* deliberately conceals its true (Aristotelian-rationalist) teaching from the masses by contradiction and digression, accessible only to careful readers. Many modern scholars (Pines's introduction; Davidson 2005) accept this in varying degrees; others (Kreisel, Stern) qualify or reject.
- **Resurrection.** Did Maimonides really believe in bodily resurrection? His *Treatise on Resurrection* affirms it; his *Mishneh Torah* and *Guide* point toward intellectualist immortality only. Twelfth-c. anti-Maimonideans accused him of crypto-heresy on this issue.
- **Maimonidean controversy.** The 13th-c. controversy and its 1232 burnings are a focal episode in the long history of Jewish philosophy vs. tradition.
