# German Sermons (Meister Eckhart)

## What it is
The vernacular German sermons of Meister Eckhart, preached primarily ~1310–1328 at Dominican women's convents and broader lay-religious audiences in Strasbourg and Cologne. Eckhart was simultaneously a leading academic theologian at the Dominican *studia* and at Paris (where he twice held the Dominican chair) and a vernacular preacher — and the German sermons preserve, in radically simplified and intensified form, the metaphysics of his Latin academic *Opus Tripartitum*. Recurrent themes: the absolute oneness and transcendence of the *Godhead* (German: *gotheit*) beyond even the God-Trinity; the *birth of the Son in the soul* (every soul, properly receptive, is the place where God begets the Word); the *Funke* (spark) of the soul that is uncreated and uncreatable, identical with God; the *Abgeschiedenheit* (detachment) — radical letting-go of every creaturely attachment, including attachment to one's image of God — as the operative spiritual discipline; "the eye with which I see God is the eye with which God sees me" — the eye is one. The language is paradoxical, hyperbolic, simultaneously orthodox in Christological framework and ontologically vertiginous; Eckhart speaks of "God ridding me of God" (*Got mich gotes quît machet*) as the highest prayer.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** The Holy Roman Empire under Henry VII and then Louis IV (the Bavarian); Papal court at Avignon (the Avignon papacy began 1309); intense political tension between Empire and Papacy, between Spirituals and Conventuals within the Franciscan order, between Dominican and Franciscan academic theology. Eckhart was Provincial of the Dominicans in Germany 1303–1311; held the Paris chair 1311–1313; sent to Strasbourg as preacher-in-residence ~1314 with responsibility for the spiritual care of the many Dominican women's convents and lay-religious circles (Beguines, Friends of God).
- **Religious milieu.** Late-13th / early-14th-c. Rhineland mysticism: an explosion of vernacular German religious literature among Dominicans (Eckhart, Tauler, Suso), Beguines (Marguerite Porete, Mechthild of Magdeburg), Cistercians, and Carthusians. The 1310 burning of Marguerite Porete and her *Mirror of Simple Souls* at Paris is the institutional shadow over Eckhart's vernacular preaching — much of his theological vocabulary, especially around the soul "becoming nothing" and union with God, structurally resembles Porete's, and the precedent of her execution loomed.
- **Who was behind it.** Eckhart himself: from a noble family in Thuringia; entered the Dominicans at Erfurt; Paris doctorate 1302 (the "Master" of his name); produced a substantial Latin academic corpus and a much-loved vernacular preaching career. The Cologne Archbishop Heinrich von Virneburg opened a heresy investigation against Eckhart in 1326; Eckhart appealed to Avignon; he died early 1328 before the papal verdict. Pope John XXII condemned 28 propositions from Eckhart's works in the bull *In agro dominico* (March 1329), declaring 17 heretical and 11 "evil-sounding" but not formally heretical. Eckhart's defense (his *Defense before the Cologne Inquisitors*) survives and is a major source for understanding his self-interpretation.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Eckhart's reputation was suppressed officially after 1329 but his works circulated underground — Luther later read and praised Tauler (Eckhart's Dominican junior) and the *Theologia Germanica* (anonymous, Eckhart-influenced) — and the modern recovery began with Franz Pfeiffer's 1857 German edition. Schopenhauer, Hegel, and the German Romantics rehabilitated Eckhart philosophically; D.T. Suzuki's mid-20th-c. comparison with Zen brought him into Christian-Buddhist dialogue; Heidegger drew on Eckhart's vocabulary of *Gelassenheit* (releasement). The Dominican order formally affirmed Eckhart's place in its tradition in 1992.

## Themes and motifs
- [[non-duality]] — radical: "the eye with which I see God is the eye with which God sees me" — at the depth of the soul there is no distinction between divine and created; the spark (*Funke*) is *uncreated*. This formulation got Eckhart condemned.
- [[hidden-god]] — the *Godhead* (*gotheit*) beyond God-Trinity; God *prays God to rid me of God* — the highest moment of mystical theology is even God-as-image being transcended.
- [[soul-as-spark]] — the *Funke* / *Seelengrund* (ground of the soul): an uncreated point at the soul's depth, identical with God's ground.
- [[gnosis-as-salvation]] — but with the radical move that the highest "knowing" is identical with God's self-knowing; the soul is not "seeking" God so much as being the place of God's own self-occurrence.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-4-030-pseudo-dionysius]] — *direct-influence*: Eckhart cites the Pseudo-Areopagite continuously; Dionysian apophatic theology is foundational.
- → [[phase-5-008-eriugena-periphyseon]] — *direct-influence*: Eckhart inherits Eriugena's hyper-apophatic vocabulary and the same metaphysical fault line; both faced ecclesiastical condemnation along the same lines.
- → [[phase-5-024-aquinas-summa-theologiae]] — *direct-influence*: Eckhart was a Dominican formed in Thomistic theology; he uses Thomistic vocabulary throughout. But his metaphysics radicalizes Aquinas's apophatic moments past Aquinas's restraint.
- → [[phase-5-019-maimonides-guide-for-the-perplexed]] — *direct-influence*: Eckhart cites "Rabbi Moyses" by name on divine attributes and on creation; the Maimonidean negative theology is part of Eckhart's apophatic toolkit.
- → [[phase-3-002-plato-dialogues]] — *direct-influence* (mediated): Plato via Augustine, Dionysius, and Macrobius is the deep substrate of Eckhart's metaphysics.
- → Marguerite Porete, *Mirror of Simple Souls* — *parallel-motif*: structural parallels in soul-becoming-nothing language; whether direct influence is uncertain but the same Strasbourg milieu, the same Beguine spiritual culture, the same Free-Spirit-related rhetorical structures.
- → Buddhist non-dualism (Zen / Mahāyāna) — *parallel-motif*: D.T. Suzuki, Thomas Merton, and many subsequent comparativists have noted the structural parallels between Eckhart's "letting go even of God" and Zen *mu* / Mahāyāna *śūnyatā*. No historical transmission; the parallel is purely structural.

## Disputes
- **Condemnation and orthodoxy.** Was Eckhart heretical, or were the 28 propositions of *In agro dominico* extracted from context and distorted? Eckhart's defenders argue contextual misreading; some scholars argue the condemnation reflected genuine doctrinal incompatibilities; modern Dominican judgment (1992) has substantially rehabilitated him.
- **Eckhart and the Free Spirit heresy.** The Beguine "Free Spirit" movement was condemned by the Council of Vienne (1311–1312) for teachings about the soul's possible union with God that bore vocabulary similarities to Eckhart's. Whether Eckhart's vernacular preaching was epistemically connected to Free-Spirit circles is debated.
- **Vernacular vs. Latin.** Are Eckhart's "shocking" formulations in the German sermons literally meant, or are they pedagogical hyperbole intended to shake monastic / lay-religious audiences? McGinn 2001 argues for substantial continuity with the Latin works; older readings argued for a gap.
