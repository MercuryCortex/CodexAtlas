# Fetha Nagast (Law of the Kings)

## What it is
The **Fetha Nagast** (Ge'ez: *Fəth Nägäśt*, "Law of the Kings") is the Christian-canonical-and-civil legal code of imperial Ethiopia from approximately the mid-15th century to the 20th-century constitutional modernization. The code in its Ge'ez form is an Ethiopianized translation and redaction of the Arabic-language **Coptic *Nomocanon* of al-Safi ibn al-Assal** (mid-13th c., Cairo) — a compilation in turn drawing on Justinian's *Corpus Iuris Civilis*, the Byzantine *Nomocanon* tradition, the Bible (especially Pentateuchal law and Pauline epistles), and conciliar canons. The Fetha Nagast is organized in two principal parts: (i) **ecclesiastical** — orders of clergy, ecclesiastical discipline, liturgy, monasticism, marriage and divorce, baptism, Eucharist; (ii) **civil-criminal** — slavery, contracts, inheritance, theft, murder, kingship.

In structure, scope, and authority, the Fetha Nagast is the Ethiopian analogue of Justinian's *Corpus* + the Byzantine *Nomocanon* + Western *Corpus Iuris Canonici* — a single integrated Christian legal system applied across ecclesiastical and civil domains under Christian-monarchical authority.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Solomonic Ethiopia in its consolidating phase. The Ge'ez translation is traditionally credited to **Petros Abda Sayd** in the mid-15th century — likely under [[zara-yaqob-emperor]]'s centralizing-systematizing reign (1434–1468), which fits the broader pattern of Solomonic theological-political codification. The Fetha Nagast institutionalizes Christian-imperial jurisprudence in Ge'ez, replacing or supplementing earlier customary law.
- **Religious milieu.** Late-Solomonic Ethiopia: an integrated church-state system in which the Solomonic emperor and the Coptic-appointed Abuna together govern, with Christian law operative across the political-ecclesiastical-civil spectrum. The Arabic original was produced by a Coptic Christian community living under Islamic political authority (the Mamluks); the Ethiopian translation operates in the inverse setting, a Christian polity in which the legal code articulates the integration of church and state.
- **Who was behind it.** Original Arabic compilation: **al-Safi Ibn al-Assal** (or his brother al-Mu'taman; the family was a major Coptic scholarly dynasty in 13th-c. Cairo). Ge'ez translation: traditionally **Petros Abda Sayd** (mid-15th c.); the translation is heavily Ethiopianized and includes substantial omissions, additions, and reorderings.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous Ethiopian use from c. mid-15th c. through the 20th c. Critical edition: Tzadua-Strauss 1968 (Faculty of Law, Haile Selassie I University).

## Themes and motifs
- [[christian-legal-codes]] — the foundational instance of Ethiopian integrated-Christian-legal jurisprudence.
- [[ecclesiastical-canon-law]] — extensive coverage of clerical orders, sacramental discipline, monastic rules.
- [[divine-kingship]] — section on kingship articulates the Christian-monarchical-theological position; the Solomonic emperor's authority is theologically grounded.

## Connections (provisional)
- → Justinian *Corpus Iuris Civilis* — *manuscript-transmission* (indirect): Byzantine and Coptic legal traditions transmit Roman-Byzantine law into the Ibn-al-Assal-Coptic-Ethiopian chain.
- → Byzantine *Nomocanon* — *manuscript-transmission*: the *Nomocanon* genre is the immediate organizational parent of the Fetha Nagast.
- → Ibn al-Assal *Majmuʿ al-Qawanin* (Arabic, 13th c.) — *direct-translation*: the Arabic immediate Vorlage of the Ge'ez Fetha Nagast.
- → [[event-solomonic-restoration-1270]] — *shared-milieu*: the Fetha Nagast belongs to the Solomonic-restoration legal-systematizing project, with the actual translation likely under Zar'a Ya'qob in the 1440s–50s.
- → [[zara-yaqob-emperor]] — *shared-milieu*: Zar'a Ya'qob's theological-political centralization is the institutional setting for the Ge'ez Fetha Nagast's stabilization.

## Disputes
- **Date of the Ge'ez translation.** Variously placed 1240s–1450s; the mid-15th-c. date (Zar'a Ya'qob era) is now the consensus.
- **Identity of the Ge'ez translator.** The traditional attribution to **Petros Abda Sayd** is post-hoc; the actual translator(s) may have been a Ge'ez-monastic team rather than one figure.
- **Continuing application of slavery provisions.** The Fetha Nagast's regulation (not prohibition) of slavery was operative in Ethiopian legal practice until the 1923 abolition under Tafari Makonnen and the formal 1942 prohibition under Haile Selassie. Modern Ethiopian legal-historical scholarship treats this with care.
- **Status under modern Ethiopian law.** Formally superseded by the 1931 and 1955 constitutions and the 1960 Civil Code (which drew on European-civilian models); ecclesiastical-court application of Fetha Nagast continues in some monastic contexts.
