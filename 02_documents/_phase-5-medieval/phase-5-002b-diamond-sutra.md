# Diamond Sutra (Vajracchedikā Prajñāpāramitā)

## What it is
The Diamond Sutra (*Vajracchedikā Prajñāpāramitā* — "The Perfection of Wisdom that Cuts Like a Diamond / Thunderbolt") is one of the two most-recited Prajñāpāramitā compression-texts of East Asian Buddhism (alongside the [[phase-5-002-heart-sutra|Heart Sutra]]). Where the Heart Sutra (260 characters in standard Chinese) compresses Prajñāpāramitā doctrine into a recitable formula of systematic negation, the Diamond Sutra (~6,000 characters) elaborates the doctrine through a sustained Buddha-Subhūti dialogue characterized by **paradox-formulae** ("The Tathāgata has taught that X is not X, therefore it is called X") — a rhetorical strategy that anticipates the Mādhyamika two-truths analysis without being philosophically systematized. The text's most iconic passage is the closing verse: "All conditioned phenomena / are like a dream, an illusion, a bubble, a shadow / like dew or a flash of lightning / thus should they be contemplated." This is the foundational scriptural passage for East Asian Buddhist emptiness-meditation and is the verse that catalyzed [[huineng|Huineng]]'s awakening in the [[phase-5-004-platform-sutra-huineng|Platform Sutra]] narrative. The Diamond Sutra is also of unique material-cultural importance: the **Dunhuang Diamond Sutra of 868 CE** is the world's earliest dated printed book in any language.

## Primary text — selected excerpts

The excerpts below use Edward Conze's 1958 translation (*Buddhist Wisdom: The Diamond Sutra and the Heart Sutra*, the standard 20th-c. English version, reprinted Vintage 2001). Section numbers follow the standard 32-section division.

### Section 3 — the Great Vehicle vow

> "The Lord said: 'Here, Subhūti, someone who has set out in the vehicle of a Bodhisattva should produce a thought in this manner: As many beings as there are in the universe of beings, comprehended under the term "beings" — egg-born, born from a womb, moisture-born, or miraculously born; with or without form; with perception, without perception, or with neither perception nor non-perception — all these I must lead to Nirvana, into that Realm of Nirvana which leaves nothing behind. And yet, although innumerable beings have thus been led to Nirvana, no being at all has been led to Nirvana. And why? If in a Bodhisattva the notion of a "being" should take place, he could not be called a "Bodhi-being.""

Citation: Vajracchedikā § 3, trans. Conze 1958.

This is the foundational articulation of the [[bodhisattva-vow]] in its full paradoxical form: the bodhisattva vows to liberate all beings, *and* recognizes that there are no beings to be liberated (because all beings are empty).

### Section 13 — the paradox formula

The Diamond Sutra's signature rhetorical move:

> "The Lord said: 'It is called "Perfection of Wisdom," Subhūti — that is precisely why the Tathāgata has taught it. And yet what the Tathāgata has taught as the perfection of wisdom — that very thing the Tathāgata has not taught. Therefore is it called "perfection of wisdom.""

This X-is-not-X-therefore-X paradox formula recurs throughout the work. It functions to dismantle reified concept-attachment while preserving the conventional use of the concept for soteriological purposes.

### Section 26 — the verses on appearance

> "Those who by my form did see me,
> And those who followed me by voice
> Wrong the efforts they engaged in,
> Me those people will not see.
>
> "From the Dharma one should see the Buddhas,
> For the Dharma-bodies are the guides.
> Yet the Dharma's nature should not be discerned,
> Nor can it be made known."

### Section 32 — the iconic closing verses

> "As stars, a fault of vision, as a lamp,
> A mock show, dew drops, or a bubble,
> A dream, a lightning flash, or cloud,
> So should one view what is conditioned.
>
> "Thus the Lord spoke. Enraptured, the Elder Subhūti, the monks and nuns, the pious laymen and laywomen, and the Bodhisattvas, and the whole world with its Gods, men, Asuras, and Gandharvas rejoiced in the Lord's teaching."

Citation: Vajracchedikā § 32, trans. Conze 1958.

The "**six-similes verse**" (or sometimes counted as ten or nine depending on enumeration of similes) is one of the most-quoted single passages in Mahāyāna Buddhism and the canonical scriptural meditation-prompt for emptiness-contemplation in East Asian Buddhist practice.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Composed during the late Kushan to early Gupta period of Indian Buddhism (~+300 to +500); the Prajñāpāramitā corpus had been in development for ~400 years by this point ([[phase-3-031-asthasahasrika-prajnaparamita|Aṣṭasāhasrikā]] ~-100 to +100, plus the expansion-series). Transmitted to China by Kumārajīva (401 CE) at the Tang precursor-state Later Qin court; thereafter foundational to Chinese Buddhism.
- **Religious milieu.** Mature Mahāyāna Buddhism with Prajñāpāramitā as a settled doctrinal stream; the work compresses Prajñāpāramitā into a chantable, popularizable, devotionally-readable text — accomplishing for Mahāyāna emptiness what the [[phase-2-029-dhammapada|Dhammapada]] had accomplished for earlier-Buddhist ethics: a compact, accessible, eminently recitable form.
- **Who was behind it.** Anonymous Mahāyāna redactors; the text frames itself as Buddhavacana. Modern critical scholarship treats it as a post-Asthasāhasrikā Prajñāpāramitā composition; the precise date and region are uncertain (probably northwest India / Gandhāra, like the broader Mahāyāna corpus).
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous transmission via Sanskrit, Chinese (six classical translations from Kumārajīva 401 onward, with Kumārajīva's version becoming standard for East Asia), and Tibetan. The 868 CE Dunhuang printed scroll recovered by Aurel Stein 1907 is the world's earliest dated printed book; the colophon reads "Reverently made for universal free distribution by Wang Jie on behalf of his two parents on the 13th of the 4th moon of the 9th year of Xiantong (11 May 868)."

## Themes and motifs
- [[emptiness-sunyata]] — the central theme; expressed via the X-is-not-X-therefore-X paradox formula and the six-similes verse.
- [[bodhisattva-vow]] — articulated in its paradoxical form: vow to liberate all beings, and recognition that there are no beings to be liberated.
- [[anatman-no-self]] — the Diamond Sutra repeatedly denies the existence of "a self, a being, a soul, or a person" (*ātman, sattva, jīva, pudgala*) as the four-fold negation of personal identity.
- **Non-attachment to the dharma itself** — the famous "raft simile" (§ 6): "The dharma is like a raft; even the dharma should be let go, how much more so non-dharma."
- **Merit (*puṇya*) accumulation through sutra-recitation** — the text repeatedly emphasizes the merit accumulated by even hearing or reciting four verses of the Diamond Sutra. This is the explicit doctrinal warrant for the text's massive devotional reception in East Asia and one driver of its widespread reproduction (including the 868 print).

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-5-002-heart-sutra]] — *parallel-form*: the two great compression-texts of the Prajñāpāramitā corpus; the Diamond Sutra is longer and uses paradox-formula, the Heart Sutra is shorter and uses systematic negation.
- → [[phase-3-031-asthasahasrika-prajnaparamita]] — *redaction-of*: the Diamond Sutra is structurally a compression-and-reformulation of the broader Prajñāpāramitā doctrine articulated more elaborately in the Aṣṭasāhasrikā.
- → [[nagarjuna]]'s *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* — *parallel-form*: both express Prajñāpāramitā emptiness-doctrine; the MMK is the philosophical articulation, the Diamond Sutra is the scriptural compression.
- → [[phase-5-004-platform-sutra-huineng]] — *direct-quote*: Huineng's awakening in the Platform Sutra narrative is precipitated by hearing the Diamond Sutra recited at a market; the Fifth Patriarch Hongren later expounds the Diamond Sutra to him to confirm his awakening. **The Diamond Sutra is the foundational Chan/Zen scripture in the narrative-canonical sense.**
- → [[huineng]] — *foundational text*: in the Chan/Zen lineage narrative, Huineng's enlightenment via the Diamond Sutra makes this text the canonical Chan/Zen scripture.
- → **Dunhuang Diamond Sutra 868 CE** (British Library Or.8210/P.2) — *manuscript-transmission*: the world's earliest dated printed book; a Buddhist devotional act of textual reproduction that anticipates the technology of printing by ~600 years.

## Disputes
- **Date**: 300-500 is the consensus range; precise dating depends on identifying the Diamond Sutra among the lineage of Prajñāpāramitā compression-texts. Kumārajīva's 401 CE Chinese translation is the *terminus ante quem*.
- **Region of origin**: northwest India / Gandhāra is the modern majority view, consistent with the broader Mahāyāna emergence-hypothesis.
- **Identification of Bajaur Collection fragments**: some Gāndhārī fragments from the Bajaur Collection have been identified as possibly Diamond Sutra material from the 1st-2nd c. CE; this identification is contested.
- **Relation to broader Prajñāpāramitā corpus**: is the Diamond Sutra an extract from the Pañcaviṃśatisāhasrikā or an independent composition? Modern scholarship (Conze 1957; Williams 2008) treats it as independent but parallel in its compression-of-the-corpus function.
