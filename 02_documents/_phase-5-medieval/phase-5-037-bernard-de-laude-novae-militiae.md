# De Laude Novae Militiae

## What it is
A short Latin treatise by [[bernard-of-clairvaux|Bernard of Clairvaux]], written ~1129–1136 for [[hugues-de-payens|Hugues de Payens]] — the theological manifesto of the [[tradition-knights-templar|Knights Templar]]. Thirteen chapters: chapters 1–5 develop the theological justification for the *novum* of monastic-military life, chapters 6–13 give a meditative tour of the Holy Land sites (Bethlehem, Nazareth, Mount Sion, Bethphage, the Holy Sepulchre, etc.) — turning the work into both apologetic and devotional itinerary.

The treatise's most consequential single theological move is the redefinition of killing-in-Templar-arms as **not *homicide* but *malecide*** — the killing of *malefactores* (evil-doers), reframed as morally meritorious sanctioned act rather than as homicide in need of penance. Chapter 3 contains the canonical phrasing:

> *Sane cum occidit malefactorem, non homicida, sed (ut ita dixerim) malicida, et plane Christi vindex in his qui male agunt, et defensor Christianorum reputatur.*
>
> "When [the knight] kills a malefactor, he is not a homicide but, so to speak, a *malicide* — a clear avenger of Christ against those who do evil, and defender of Christians."

This reframing is the theological pivot point of medieval Western crusading ideology.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Bernard wrote ~1129–1136, while the Templar Order was newly recognized but theologically still controversial. *De Laude* is partly defensive — there were voices in Latin Christendom uneasy about the combination of monastic vow and sanctioned violence.
- **Religious milieu.** The Augustinian just-war tradition provided the foundation; Bernard's innovation was to move from *just* to *meritorious* — from defensive permission to soteriological participation.
- **Who was behind it.** Bernardine Cistercian-Templar alliance.
- **Transmission.** Wide medieval Latin distribution; the standard modern edition is Leclercq-Rochais 1963.

## Themes and motifs
- [[crusade-theology-of-malecide]] — the central theological move.
- [[martyrdom-theology]] — Templar death in arms is reframed soteriologically as martyrdom.
- [[city-of-god-vs-earthly-city]] — the Augustinian framework Bernard inherits and operationalizes.
- [[monastic-asceticism]] — the Templar vows underlie the moral status of the Order's combat.
- **Implicit anti-secular-chivalry polemic.** Chapters 1–2 contrast the *miles saecularis* (secular knight, motivated by vanity and gain) with the *miles novus* (the Templar) — a sharp critique of contemporary Western European aristocratic warrior-culture.

## Disputes
- **Was *De Laude* effective?** Mixed. Bernard's theological reframing of military monasticism stuck institutionally (the Templar, Hospitaller, Teutonic, Calatrava, Santiago, Avis orders all derive their theological self-understanding from this stream). But the broader theological reception was uneven: some 12th–13th-c. Latin theologians remained uneasy with the *miles Christi* in literal arms.
- **How much of Bernard's later crusading preaching is anchored on *De Laude*'s theology?** Substantial; Phillips 2007 ch. 4.
