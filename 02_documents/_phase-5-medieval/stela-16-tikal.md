# Stela 16 (Tikal)

## What it is
A carved **stela** at Tikal, paired with the famous Altar 5, erected by King **Jasaw Chan K'awiil I** to mark the close of a *k'atun* (a roughly twenty-year Maya calendar period) on the date corresponding to **711 CE**. The stela portrays the king in the regalia of the period-ending ceremony; it stands in a **twin-pyramid complex**, the distinctive Tikal architectural form built specifically to celebrate these calendrical milestones. It is a key fixed point in the chronology of Jasaw's long and triumphant reign.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Tikal restored to greatness under Jasaw Chan K'awiil I after his 695 victory over Calakmul; the period-ending monuments project the renewed confidence of the dynasty.
- **Religious milieu.** The *k'atun*-ending was among the most important Maya rituals, the king's performance binding the cosmic calendar to the life of the city.
- **Who was behind it.** Jasaw Chan K'awiil I's court.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Catalogued in the University of Pennsylvania's Tikal Project; a standard reference monument for Maya calendrics.

## Themes and motifs
- [[divine-kingship]] — the king as the ritual pivot of cosmic time, his sovereignty enacted in the closing of the calendar period.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[lintel-3-temple-i]] — *same-reign*: the two monuments together frame Jasaw Chan K'awiil I's reign (his war triumph and his calendrical ritual).
- → [[jasaw-chan-kawiil-i]] — *commemorates*: the stela's royal subject.

## Disputes
- **Twin-pyramid attribution.** The exact complex and the relation of Stela 16 to Altar 5's text are matters of the detailed Tikal record (Jones & Satterthwaite 1982).

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **Sovereignty as the keeping of time.** The twin-pyramid period-ending monuments express a distinctively Maya theology in which the king's central duty is to *turn the calendar* — to perform the ritual that carries the cosmos across each great cycle of time — a sacralization of calendrical order with few exact parallels in the Old World and a centerpiece of how the Atlas reads Maya divine kingship (Martin & Grube 2008).
