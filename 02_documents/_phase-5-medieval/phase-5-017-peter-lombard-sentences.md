# Sententiae (The Four Books of Sentences)

## What it is
A four-book Latin theological textbook organizing Christian doctrine through a vast harvest of "sentences" (*sententiae*) — authoritative excerpts from Scripture, the Fathers (especially Augustine), and recent authors (Hugh of St. Victor, Anselm). Book 1 treats the Trinity and divine attributes; Book 2 creation, angels, the fall, sin, and grace; Book 3 the Incarnation, the virtues, and the Decalogue; Book 4 the sacraments and the Four Last Things (death, judgment, heaven, hell). Within each book the text is divided into *distinctiones* (sub-units) — 48, 44, 40, and 50 distinctions respectively, totaling 182. The method is the *sic et non* dialectical technique pioneered by Abelard: present authoritative texts that seem to disagree, and resolve the apparent contradiction through distinction and clarification. The work's pedagogical genius — and the reason it became the standard university theology textbook for 400 years — is that every major theological problem of the 11th-12th-c. schools is given a manageable, structured presentation with the relevant patristic material attached.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Twelfth-century France in the great age of cathedral schools. Paris, under King Louis VII (1137–1180), was emerging as the leading intellectual center of Latin Europe; Peter Lombard taught at the cathedral school of Notre-Dame and was briefly bishop of Paris before his death in 1160.
- **Religious milieu.** The intellectual revolution of the 12th-c. cathedral schools (Anselm of Laon, William of Champeaux, Hugh of St. Victor, Peter Abelard, Gilbert of Poitiers, Bernard of Clairvaux). Abelard's *Sic et Non* (~1120) had developed a method of juxtaposing authorities; Hugh of St. Victor's *De Sacramentis* (~1140) had attempted a comprehensive theological summary on a different organizing principle (the two "works" of creation and restoration). Peter Lombard distilled the methods and surpassed the competitors. Within his own lifetime, Lombard's Trinitarian formulations were attacked by Joachim of Fiore and others as inadequately distinguishing the divine essence from the Persons; the Fourth Lateran Council (1215) formally vindicated Lombard against Joachim.
- **Who was behind it.** Peter Lombard ("Magister Sententiarum"): from Novara in Lombardy (hence the name); studied at Bologna, Reims, then Paris; taught at Notre-Dame; bishop of Paris briefly (1159–1160); died 1160.
- **Discovery / transmission.** From ~1220, the *Sentences* became the mandatory textbook for advanced theology students at Paris, Oxford, and every other Western university — alongside the Bible itself. Every aspiring master of theology was required to lecture on the *Sentences* as part of his degree program; this produced the genre of *Sentences-commentaries*, of which Aquinas's *Scriptum super Libros Sententiarum* (1252–1256), Bonaventure's, Duns Scotus's, William of Ockham's, and Gabriel Biel's are the most famous. The *Sentences* was displaced as the university theology textbook only in the 16th c. (in some places later) by Aquinas's *Summa Theologiae* itself.

## Themes and motifs
- [[creation-by-word]] — Book 2 systematically presents creation doctrine; the formula *creatio ex nihilo* and the seminal-reason theology of Augustine receive textbook clarity.
- [[hidden-god]] — Book 1 treats divine attributes and Trinitarian relations apophatically; the formula "*Deus est cuius esse est suum intelligere*" enters scholastic usage.
- [[soul-immortality]] / [[afterlife-judgment]] — Book 4's treatment of the Four Last Things sets the medieval template for eschatology.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-4-028-augustine-confessions]] / [[phase-4-029-augustine-city-of-god]] / Augustine *De Trinitate* — *direct-quote* / *commentary-on*: Augustine is the most-cited authority by far; the *Sentences* is a structured Augustinian theology with patristic supplements.
- → Hugh of St. Victor, *De Sacramentis* — *redaction-of* / *parallel-motif*: the same project, in a different organizing schema; Hugh's text was a candidate textbook but Lombard's *Sentences* won the historical contest.
- → Abelard, *Sic et Non* — *direct-influence*: the dialectical method.
- → [[phase-5-024-aquinas-summa-theologiae]] — *direct-influence*: Aquinas wrote his *Scriptum super Sententias* (1252–1256) before composing the *Summa*; the *Summa*'s structure (theological encyclopedia in *quaestiones*) is in conscious modification of Lombard's *distinctiones*. The *Summa* eventually replaced the *Sentences* as the standard textbook.
- → Sentences-commentary tradition — *commentary-on* (massive downstream literature): roughly 1,400 medieval Sentences commentaries are known.

## Disputes
- **Trinitarian orthodoxy.** Joachim of Fiore (~1200) and other contemporaries attacked Lombard's Trinitarian formula as quaternitarian (treating the divine essence as a "fourth" alongside the three Persons). Lateran IV vindicated Lombard in 1215.
- **Christological position.** Lombard's "homo-assumptus" Christology (sometimes characterized as "Christological nihilianism") was condemned at the Council of Sens (1170) but the condemnation was never enforced; the position quietly disappeared from theology by 1200.
