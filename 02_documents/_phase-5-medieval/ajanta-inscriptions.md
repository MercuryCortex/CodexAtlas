# Ajanta Inscriptions

## What it is
The rock-cut Buddhist caves of **Ajaṇṭā** in Maharashtra carry a set of dedicatory and donative **inscriptions** that are the documentary backbone for dating and understanding the site's celebrated paintings. They fall in two phases: a small early (Sātavāhana-period, ~1st c. BCE–1st c. CE) group, and the great later cluster of the **Vākāṭaka period** (~460–480 CE), when ministers and devotees — most famously the minister **Varāhadeva**, donor of Cave 16 — endowed the painted *vihāras* (monastery-halls) and *caitya*-halls. The inscriptions name patrons, record their religious motives and merit-dedication, and praise the reigning Vākāṭaka house, allowing scholars such as Walter Spink to reconstruct an unusually precise chronology of the site's brief, intense final flowering. The narrative subjects of the paintings themselves illustrate the *jātaka* and *avadāna* literature ([[jataka-tales]], [[divyavadana]]).

## Significance
Because Ajanta preserves the finest cycle of early Buddhist mural painting in the world, its inscriptions are disproportionately important: they convert a wall of images into a dated historical archive of patronage, merit-making, and the Vākāṭaka court's Buddhist devotion. They are a model case of donative epigraphy fixing the chronology of a great monument of [[theme-rock-cut-architecture-as-relic-of-creation]].
