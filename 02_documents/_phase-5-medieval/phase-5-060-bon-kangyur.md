# Bön Kanjur

**Bön Kanjur** (Tibetan *Bön bKa' 'gyur*) — the canonical scripture collection of Yungdrung Bön ("Eternal Bön"), systematically compiled ~13th–15th c. CE — is the pre-Buddhist Tibetan religion's scriptural parallel to the Buddhist *Kangyur* (the translated word of the Buddha). Structurally mirroring the Buddhist canon in both format and organizational categories, the Bön Kanjur contains over 300 volumes covering cosmology, ritual, meditation, biography of the founder Tonpa Shenrab, and esoteric contemplative practice. Its most significant sections for cross-tradition comparison are the **Dzogchen** (*rDzogs chen*, "Great Completeness") texts — teachings on the primordially pure nature of mind that overlap so closely with the Vajrayāna Dzogchen corpus that priority and transmission direction remain disputed in scholarship.

## Structure

The Bön Kanjur mirrors Buddhist canonical divisions:
- **Sūtra section**: narratives and teachings attributed to Tonpa Shenrab Miwoche (the Bön "Buddha equivalent"), delivered at Mount Tisé (Kailash)
- **Tantra section**: ritual and esoteric practice texts, including the *Zhang-Zhung Nyen-Gyud* (the oral lineage transmission of Dzogchen from Zhang-Zhung)
- **Dzogchen section**: the most contested and cross-tradition-significant portion — the "pointing-out" instruction texts that transmit direct recognition of the nature of mind
- **Ritual manuals**: exorcism, ransom rituals, protection, divination — preserving the oldest layer of Tibetan religious practice

## Cross-tradition significance

### Dzogchen: the most disputed priority question in Tibetan religion
The central controversy in Bön/Vajrayāna comparative studies is whether Dzogchen — the non-dual contemplative recognition of the primordially pure mind, the highest teaching in both the Nyingma school of Tibetan Buddhism and in Yungdrung Bön — originated in Buddhism and was adopted by Bön, or originated in Bön (specifically in the Zhang-Zhung oral tradition) and was adopted into Buddhism, or whether both streams derived from a common Central Asian contemplative source. The Bön claim — that the Dzogchen lineage descends from Tonpa Shenrab and was transmitted through the Zhang-Zhung oral tradition (*Zhang-Zhung Nyen-Gyud*) before the arrival of Buddhism in Tibet — is taken seriously by scholars including Karmay and Norbu. If correct, the most sophisticated contemplative system in Tibetan Buddhism has a pre-Buddhist origin — one of the vault's most consequential open questions.

### Death navigation texts and the Bardo Thodol
The Bön tradition has its own death-navigation literature — instructions for recognizing one's own nature at the moment of death and navigating the *bardo* (intermediate state) — that parallels the [[phase-5-029-bardo-thodol|Bardo Thodol]] (Tibetan Book of the Dead) so closely that shared origin or mutual borrowing is essentially certain. The texts differ in iconographic detail (different buddha-families, different deity-names) but share the structural logic: recognize the clear light at death, recognize the arising of peaceful and wrathful visions as mind's own display, use that recognition to attain liberation rather than being driven by habit-force into rebirth.

### Pre-Buddhist Tibet as the "pre-Christian" equivalent for Tibetan religion
The vault's consistent pattern — of a prior religious stratum surviving partially within or alongside the dominant tradition — finds its Tibetan expression in Bön. Just as Egyptian solar religion survives within Coptic Christianity, and Iranian Zoroastrian cosmology persists within Persian Islamic mysticism, elements of Zhang-Zhung/Bön cosmology, deity-cults, and ritual practice persist within Tibetan Vajrayāna. The Bön Kanjur is the most complete surviving record of that pre-Buddhist stratum.
