# Jātaka Tales

## What it is
A vast collection of **547 birth-stories (jātaka)** recounting the previous lives of the being who would become the Buddha — the **Bodhisatta** — as he is reborn across the aeons as king, merchant, ascetic, deity, or animal, each life perfecting a virtue (*pāramī*) such as generosity, patience, or truthfulness. Each tale is framed: the Buddha, prompted by a present event, "remembers" a past life, narrates it, and identifies the present persons with their past-life counterparts. The verses (*gāthā*) are canonical and old; the much-loved prose stories around them form a later Pali commentary, the *Jātakaṭṭhakathā*. The most celebrated is the **Vessantara Jātaka**, the climactic tale of perfect generosity, recited as a festival across Theravāda Asia.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Transmitted across the Theravāda world — Sri Lanka and, later, Burma, Thailand, Laos, and Cambodia — as both canonical scripture and popular entertainment.
- **Religious milieu.** The Jātakas teach karma, rebirth, and the long bodhisatta path in narrative form accessible to the laity; they are a principal vehicle of merit-making and moral instruction.
- **Who was behind it.** Anonymous tradents; the canonical verses are early, the prose frame is a Sinhala commentarial compilation of about the 5th century CE.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Carried in the Pali canon and in palm-leaf manuscripts across South and Southeast Asia; depicted in stone from the 2nd–1st centuries BCE onward.

## Themes and motifs
- [[bodhisattva-vow]] — the through-line: across hundreds of lives the Bodhisatta perfects the virtues that culminate in Buddhahood.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-2-029-dhammapada]] — *shared-corpus*: both belong to the Khuddaka Nikāya of the Pali canon; both are vehicles of popular ethical teaching.
- → [[phase-3-030-asokan-edicts]] — *shared-milieu*: the Mauryan-era Buddhist culture in which the Jātaka tradition and its earliest art (Bharhut, Sāñcī) crystallized.
- → [[sanchi-great-stupa]] / [[borobudur-stupa-mandala]] — *direct-influence*: Jātaka scenes are a primary subject of early and classical Buddhist relief sculpture.

## Disputes
- **Verse vs. prose dating.** The canonical verses are old (some pre-Buddhist folk material reworked); the prose stories are demonstrably later — how much each preserves is debated.
- **Boundaries of the corpus.** The number and selection (the "547") and the relation to non-canonical jātaka collections vary in the tradition.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **The deepest documented migration of stories in world literature.** Jātaka and related Indian fable material flowed outward for two millennia: into the **Pañcatantra**, thence via the Pahlavi and Arabic **Kalīla wa-Dimna** into the *Fables* of medieval Europe; and motifs shared with **Aesop** mark the two-way traffic of the Indo-Mediterranean fable. The single most spectacular case is **Barlaam and Josaphat** — a Christianized retelling of the Buddha's own life that made "**Saint Josaphat**" (a corruption of *bodhisattva*) a saint with a feast day in the medieval Christian calendar (Lopez & McCracken 2014; Appleton 2010).
- **The picture-bible of Buddhist Asia.** Carved at Bharhut and [[sanchi-great-stupa]], painted at Ajanta, and set in stone at [[borobudur-stupa-mandala]], the Jātakas are the narrative substrate of Buddhist visual culture from India to Java (von Hinüber 1996).
