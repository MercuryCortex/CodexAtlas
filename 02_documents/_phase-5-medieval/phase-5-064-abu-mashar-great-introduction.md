# The Great Introduction to Astrology (Kitāb al-Madkhal al-kabīr)

## What it is
The **standard systematic textbook of Islamic judicial astrology**, written in Baghdad c. 848 CE by **Abū Maʿshar al-Balkhī** (Latin *Albumasar*), the most influential astrologer of the medieval world. The *Great Introduction* sets out the full apparatus of horoscopic astrology and, crucially, frames it within **Aristotelian natural philosophy**: the heavens are the efficient cause of generation and change in the sublunary world, so the art of reading them is a branch of physics. This philosophical packaging is what made the work so consequential when it crossed into Latin — it carried Aristotle as well as the zodiac.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** The Abbasid caliphate at the height of the Baghdad translation movement, when Greek, Persian (Pahlavi), and Indian astral science was being gathered into Arabic.
- **Religious milieu.** Astrology was both practised at court and contested by jurists and theologians; Abū Maʿshar writes as a confident defender of the science. He synthesizes the Greek (Ptolemy), the Persian-transmitted (Dorotheus, the historical/mundane astrology of world-cycles), and Indian elements into one system.
- **Who was behind it.** A single named master, a Khurasani who came to astrology (by the famous anecdote) only in middle age after a career in *ḥadīth*.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Translated into Latin twice in the 12th century — by **John of Seville (1133)** and **Hermann of Carinthia (1140)** — as the *Introductorium maius*, becoming a core European university text.

## Themes and motifs
- [[microcosm-macrocosm]] — the Aristotelian doctrine that the celestial spheres cause sublunary change is the theoretical heart of the book.
- [[divinatory-omen-reading]] — the systematized judicial (natal, mundane, electional, interrogational) astrology of the Islamic Golden Age.
- [[wisdom-as-cosmic-order]] — astrology defended as a rational science of nature.

## Disputes
- **Date.** The *Great Introduction* is usually dated to c. 848; Abū Maʿshar's corpus (including the *Book of Religions and Dynasties* on world-historical astrology) spans the mid-9th century.
- **How "Aristotelian" the synthesis really is.** Lemay (1962) made strong claims for Abū Maʿshar as the West's route to Aristotle's physics; later scholarship (Burnett) refines but largely sustains the transmission thesis.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **The valve that re-opened Aristotle to the Latin West.** Richard Lemay's classic study argued that 12th-century Europe first absorbed Aristotle's physics of celestial causation *through* Abū Maʿshar's astrological *Great Introduction* — astrology smuggling natural philosophy across the Mediterranean (Lemay 1962; Burnett 1997). The book is thus a double transmission: the zodiac *and* the physics that justified it.
- **The Islamic hinge of the spine.** Abū Maʿshar is the central Islamic-Golden-Age node where the Greek-and-Persian inheritance ([[phase-3-038-ptolemy-tetrabiblos]], [[phase-3-040-dorotheus-carmen-astrologicum]]) is consolidated and then handed, via Toledo, to Latin Christendom — the link between the ancient and the Renaissance arms of [[tradition-western-astrology]] (Tester 1987).
