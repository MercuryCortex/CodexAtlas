# Liber de Duobus Principiis (The Book of the Two Principles)

## What it is
The most sophisticated surviving Cathar theological treatise — a scholastic Latin defense of *absolute* dualism (two coeternal first principles, one good and one evil) against opponents arguing for mitigated dualism (Satan as a fallen creature) and against Catholic monism. The work proceeds by scholastic argumentation: it presents Catholic arguments for the unity of God, then refutes them through detailed citation of Scripture (especially Pauline epistles, the Johannine corpus, and the books of Wisdom) read as supporting two principles. Topics include: the necessity of evil's having an uncreated cause; the impossibility of an omnipotent good God's permitting genuine evil; the interpretation of Genesis 1 as describing only spiritual creation, with material creation attributed to the evil principle; free will and predestination; the eternity of evil; the nature of Christ (docetic — Christ's body was not material). The surviving manuscript breaks off before the treatise concludes. The work is striking because it deploys precisely the same scholastic logical methods being developed contemporaneously at the Latin universities — *against* the universities' theology. It is the only major systematic Cathar theological text to survive.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Northern Italian communes in the early-mid 13th century — the Cathar churches at Desenzano, Concorezzo, Bagnolo, Vicenza, Florence were urban institutions until the post-1230 inquisitorial campaigns extinguished them. Languedoc had already been devastated by the Albigensian Crusade (1209–1229); Italian Cathar communities were the inheritors and refugees.
- **Religious milieu.** Cathar theology had split into competing branches: the Concorezzan church held mitigated dualism (following Bogomil precedent — Satan a fallen son of the good God); the Albanense or "absolute" church held two coeternal principles. The *Liber de Duobus Principiis* is the Albanense systematic statement, written in part to refute the Concorezzan position. The treatise's author was almost certainly a Cathar perfectus / *bonus homo* with substantial scholastic training, perhaps a former Catholic university student who had crossed over.
- **Who was behind it.** John of Lugio (Giovanni di Lugio) is the named figure most associated with the Albanense church and is the most-proposed candidate for authorship; the treatise may have been redacted by a disciple. The author writes confidently in scholastic Latin and is fluent in scholastic argumentative method, scholastic vocabulary, and the patristic citation apparatus.
- **Discovery / transmission.** The Albigensian Crusade (1209–1229) and the institutional Inquisition (founded by Pope Gregory IX 1234, structured by Innocent IV's *Ad extirpanda* 1252) systematically destroyed Cathar literature. By ~1320, organized Catharism was effectively extinct in Western Europe; Bosnian Bogomilism survived longer. The Florentine manuscript ~1265 is the single surviving witness, discovered and edited by the Dominican historian Antoine Dondaine in 1939 — an extraordinary recovery given the totality of inquisitorial destruction.

## Themes and motifs
- [[dualism-cosmic]] — *absolute* dualism: two coeternal, uncreated first principles, one good and one evil. The Cathar argument is that the existence of evil cannot be explained from a monist starting-point; only a coeternal evil principle accounts for it.
- [[dualism-spirit-matter]] — radical: matter is the evil principle's domain; bodies are prisons; salvation requires escape from materiality.
- [[demiurge]] — the evil principle is the creator of the material world; this is the same role Yaldabaoth occupies in Sethian Gnostic literature.
- [[ethical-dualism]] — the moral binary structures every doctrine: light/darkness, spirit/matter, good God/evil God, perfecti/credentes.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-5-020-interrogatio-iohannis]] — *direct-influence* / *commentary-on*: the *Liber* presupposes the cosmological narrative of the *Interrogatio*; the *Liber* is its systematic philosophical defense — the scholastic apparatus added to the apocryphal cosmogony.
- → Bogomil mitigated dualism (Concorezzan Cathar position) — *polemic-against*: the *Liber*'s defense of *absolute* dualism is explicitly directed against the Bogomil-derived mitigated position held by the Concorezzan Cathars.
- → [[phase-4-014-cologne-mani-codex]] / [[phase-4-015-kephalaia-of-the-teacher]] — *parallel-motif* / *direct-influence?*: Manichaean two-principles cosmology is the doctrinal antecedent; whether the *Liber*'s absolute dualism descends genealogically from Manichaean teaching (via Paulician → Bogomil → Cathar channels) or is independent re-creation is the foundational debate.
- → contemporaneous scholastic theology (Aquinas, Bonaventure) — *polemic-against*: the *Liber* deploys scholastic methods *against* the scholastic Catholic conclusion of divine unity. This is mirror-image of [[phase-5-024-aquinas-summa-theologiae]] (composed in the same decade, ~1265–1274).

## Disputes
- **Manichaean genealogy.** Same debate as for the *Interrogatio* — Stoyanov 2000, Hamilton, Lambert affirm continuous transmission; Moore 2012, Pegg 2001 are skeptical, reading the medieval dualist movements as essentially new heresies whose Manichaean affiliations are inquisitorial labels.
- **Authorship.** John of Lugio is the candidate but unproven; the manuscript itself does not name the author.
- **The "constructed heresy" thesis.** Pegg 2001 and Moore 2012 argue that the elaborate dualist theology presented in inquisitorial sources and the surviving Cathar Latin texts (including the *Liber*) substantially overstates the doctrinal coherence of actual Languedocian or Lombard *credentes* — i.e., the *Liber* is a product of a thin intellectual elite, and the wider movement was much less doctrinally focused. This is now a substantial minority position in Cathar studies.
