# Interrogatio Iohannis (The Secret Book of John of the Bogomils)

## What it is
A medieval Christian dualist apocryphon: at the Last Supper, John the Evangelist leans on Jesus's breast and asks a series of cosmological questions, to which Jesus replies with a complete dualist cosmogony. Before creation, there was *only* the good Father. Satanael (originally an angelic son of the Father; explicitly *not* a coeternal evil principle in the mitigated-dualist Bogomil branch, but acquiring more independent reality in Cathar absolute-dualism) rebels, takes a third of the angels, falls; with God's reluctant permission, Satanael creates the visible cosmos and human bodies — the material world is the devil's work. The Father sends his own Son to be born in / through Mary (Bogomil docetism: Mary is angelic and Christ's body is illusory or angelic, not material). Satanael's name loses the divine *-el* suffix and becomes "Satan" upon his final defeat. The text closes with an apocalyptic restoration: at the end of days, the elect (the *boni homines* / *parfaits* / Bogomil teleioi) are gathered out of the prison of bodies, the visible cosmos is dissolved, and unity with the Father is restored. The text is one of the principal scriptural authorities of Catharism — the colophon of the Carcassonne manuscript attests that it was brought from Bulgaria to the Italian Cathars by Bishop Nazarius around 1190.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** Late 12th c. — Bulgaria, the Byzantine Empire, Northern Italian communes, the County of Toulouse and Languedoc. Bogomilism had emerged in Bulgaria in the mid-10th c. (the priest Bogomil, ~940), spread to Byzantium (where the Emperor Alexios I Komnenos staged the public trial and burning of the Bogomil leader Basil the Physician ~1099), and from the late 11th c. onward had carriers in the Balkans, Northern Italy, and Southern France. The Cathars (*Cathari*, "pure ones") of Languedoc were the western European reception of the same dualist stream. The Latin *Interrogatio* is the textual evidence that what looked like an independent Western heresy was substantially rooted in transmission from the Eastern Christian dualist tradition.
- **Religious milieu.** Western Christianity's "medieval reformation" of the 11th–12th centuries had produced both Gregorian institutional reform and a wave of evangelical-poverty popular movements (Waldensians, Humiliati, and the Cathars). Cathar dualism, however, was *not* a moderate reformist impulse but a metaphysical rejection of the Catholic creation-affirming theology root and branch. The Catholic response was the Albigensian Crusade (1209–1229) and the founding of the Inquisition (1234) — extermination as policy.
- **Who was behind it.** Anonymous Bogomil author(s) working in 12th-c. Bulgaria or Macedonia; the form of the text is a pseudepigraphic revelation-dialogue, structurally very similar to Sethian Gnostic revelation-dialogues like the *Apocryphon of John*. Whether this is independent reinvention or transmission via Paulician → Bogomil → Cathar channels is the live scholarly debate. The Latin reception is attested by Cathar Bishop Nazarius's bringing the book to Concorezzo ~1190.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Carcassonne mss recovered from the Inquisition archive; Vienna mss from secular manuscript collections. Bozóky's 1980 critical edition is the modern reference.

## Themes and motifs
- [[dualism-cosmic]] — most importantly, *mitigated dualism* in the Bogomil branch (Satanael is a fallen son of the good God, not coeternal) shading toward *absolute dualism* in some Cathar branches (two coeternal principles).
- [[dualism-spirit-matter]] — the visible material world is the devil's work; bodies are prisons; only spirit is divine.
- [[demiurge]] — Satanael as the world-creator; the Old Testament's creator-god is identified with the devil. The OT is, with qualifications, rejected as Satan's testament.
- [[fall-of-humanity]] — re-coded: humans fell into bodies created by Satanael. The Cathar / Bogomil "myth of Adam" displaces the standard Genesis interpretation.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-4-002-apocryphon-of-john]] — *parallel-motif*: structural similarity is striking — both are Johannine revelation-dialogues presenting dualist cosmogony with a fallen demiurge creating the cosmos. Whether this represents *transmission* across centuries via Manichaean → Paulician → Bogomil channels (Stoyanov, Hamilton) or *independent reinvention* from heretical readings of the NT (Moore, Sennis) is *the* foundational debate in medieval dualism studies.
- → [[phase-4-014-cologne-mani-codex]] / [[phase-4-015-kephalaia-of-the-teacher]] — *parallel-motif* / *direct-influence?*: medieval Catholic polemicists (from Euthymius Zigabenus on) called the Bogomils and Cathars "Manichaeans"; modern scholarship is split on whether this is a polemical reflex or accurate historical genealogy. Stoyanov 2000 argues for substantial Manichaean → Paulician → Bogomil transmission.
- → [[phase-5-023-liber-de-duobus-principiis]] — *direct-influence*: the *Interrogatio*'s dualist cosmology is presupposed by the *Liber* (~1240), the most sophisticated systematic Cathar theological treatise.
- → [[phase-2-005-hebrew-bible-j-source]] — *polemic-against*: the OT creator is identified as Satanael; the OT is functionally rejected as the devil's scripture.

## Disputes
- **Mitigated vs. absolute dualism.** The Italian Cathars split: the Concorezzan church held the mitigated Bogomil position (Satanael a fallen son); the Albanenses (after Bishop Albanus) held absolute dualism (two coeternal principles). The *Interrogatio* is more naturally read as mitigated; the *Liber de Duobus Principiis* (1240) is absolute.
- **Manichaean genealogy.** The most-debated transmission edge in medieval religious history. *For genuine transmission*: Hamilton 1979 (Variorum essays); Stoyanov 2000; Obolensky 1948 (*The Bogomils*); Loos 1974. *For independent emergence from heretical NT readings*: R.I. Moore (*The War on Heresy* 2012); Pegg (*A Most Holy War* 2008). The debate continues.
- **Dating.** The Latin text dates ~1190 (Vienna ms colophon); a Slavonic or Greek predecessor likely existed earlier in 12th c. or late 11th c. (Bogomil literary culture).
