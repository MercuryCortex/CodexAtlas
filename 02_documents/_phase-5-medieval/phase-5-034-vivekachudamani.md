# Vivekacūḍāmaṇi

## What it is
A pedagogical Advaita Vedānta treatise in 581 Sanskrit verses (some recensions 540–600+), traditionally attributed to [[shankara|Adi Shankara]] but widely held by modern philological scholarship to be substantially later than Shankara (~13th–14th c., though some scholars maintain a Shankara attribution). The work is one of the most-read introductions to Advaita Vedānta — structured as a dialogue between a teacher and an aspirant, it presents the entire Advaita soteriological program: the qualifications for the student (the *sādhana-catuṣṭaya* — discrimination, dispassion, the six virtues, longing for liberation), the analysis of the five sheaths (*koṣa*) and the *kūṭastha-ātman* (immovable Self) beyond them, the practice of inquiry (*ātma-vicāra*), and the realization of *brahman*-*ātman* identity. The work is among the most popular Sanskrit Vedanta texts in modern global Hindu pedagogy, in substantial part because of its accessibility and its rhetorical force; the contested authorship is, in subsequent reception, almost entirely irrelevant to its popular use.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** If the late-attribution argument is correct, the composition belongs to the post-Vijayanagara medieval Advaita revival period (~14th c.) under [[vidyaranya]] and the Sringeri matha intellectual program. If the Shankara attribution is correct, ~800 CE Kerala/pan-India.
- **Religious milieu.** Either an early-9th-c. position within the original Shankarian Advaita synthesis, or a later-medieval pedagogical-revival production within the Sringeri-matha Advaita establishment under Vidyaranya's lineage.
- **Who was behind it.** Either Shankara himself or an anonymous later Advaita master in the Sringeri-matha tradition.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Continuous Advaita transmission; manuscripts from ~1500. The absence of pre-1500 commentary tradition is one of the philological grounds for the late-attribution position (Hacker, Mayeda).

## Themes and motifs
- [[non-duality]] — the central Advaita doctrine: Brahman is the singular reality; *ātman* and *brahman* are identical; multiplicity is *māyā* (cosmic illusion-producing power).
- [[apatheia-and-virtue]] — the *sādhana-catuṣṭaya* (four qualifications): discrimination (*viveka*) between the eternal and non-eternal, dispassion (*vairāgya*) for the fruits of action, the six virtues (*śama-damādi-ṣaṭka-sampatti* — mental restraint, sense-restraint, withdrawal, endurance, faith, concentration), and longing for liberation (*mumukṣutvam*).
- [[ground-of-being]] — *brahman* as the *adhiṣṭhāna* (substratum) on which all phenomena are superimposed.
- [[ascent-of-the-soul]] — the progressive *vicāra* (inquiry) through the sheaths of the self (annamaya / prāṇamaya / manomaya / vijñānamaya / ānandamaya — the five koṣas) toward the *kūṭastha-ātman* witness-consciousness behind them.

## Connections (provisional)
- → [[phase-5-005-shankara-brahma-sutra-bhasya]] — *commentary-on* / *parallel-motif*: the Vivekacūḍāmaṇi is the popular-pedagogical counterpart to Shankara's technical *Brahmasūtra-Bhāṣya*; in attribution Shankarian, in tone introductory.
- → Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha tradition — *parallel-motif*: similar Advaita-narrative pedagogical genre.
- → Modern Hindu Advaita pedagogy — *direct-influence*: the Vivekacūḍāmaṇi is among the principal introductory texts in modern global Advaita teaching (Ramakrishna Mission, Chinmaya Mission, Arsha Vidya, Western Advaita-satsang lineages).
- → [[swami-vivekananda]]'s public Vedanta lectures (1893–1902) — *direct-influence*: Vivekananda quoted the Vivekacūḍāmaṇi frequently as foundational Advaita teaching. The *Practical Vedanta* and *Jnana Yoga* lectures use Vivekacūḍāmaṇi categories.
- → Indian-Christian and Indian-Sufi pedagogical reception — *parallel-motif*: the Vivekacūḍāmaṇi's structured spiritual-pedagogical method (qualifications → discrimination → inquiry → realization) is structurally adjacent to: (a) the medieval Christian *Cloud of Unknowing* contemplative pedagogy; (b) the Sufi *suluk* (wayfaring) treatises ([[al-ghazali]]'s *Iḥyāʾ ʿulūm al-dīn*, Ibn ʿArabī's various treatises); (c) Christian Carmelite mystical theology (Teresa of Avila's *Mansions*, John of the Cross's *Ascent of Mount Carmel*). The structural parallels are striking and have been comparatively studied (Otto 1923 *Mysticism East and West* compares Shankara and Meister Eckhart specifically; Halbfass 1988 extends).

## Disputes
- **Authorship**: contested. Hacker 1968 argues, on philological grounds (vocabulary, doctrinal phrasing, the use of the *karaṇa-śarīra* causal-body terminology not characteristic of Shankara's authenticated works), that the Vivekacūḍāmaṇi is later than Shankara. Mayeda 1979 concurs. Some traditional and a minority of academic scholarship maintain the Shankara attribution. The Mayeda-Hacker position is the dominant philological consensus; the popular-devotional and Mission tradition retains the Shankara attribution.
- **Date**: if not by Shankara, the composition is generally placed ~13th–14th c. with some arguments for 11th–12th c.
