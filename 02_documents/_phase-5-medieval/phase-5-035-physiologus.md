# Physiologus

## What it is
An anonymous early-Christian **allegorical bestiary** — a collection of roughly 48 short chapters, each of which describes the (often fabulous) nature of an animal, plant, or stone, introduced by the recurring formula "the **Physiologus** [the Naturalist] says…," and then reads that nature as a hidden figure of Christ, the Church, the believer, or the Devil. Composed in Greek in the Alexandrian milieu between the 2nd and 4th centuries, it fused Hellenistic natural-history lore with Christian allegorical exegesis, and it became, after the Bible, **one of the most widely translated and copied books of the entire Middle Ages**.

## Context
- **Political milieu.** The Greek-Christian intellectual world of Roman Egypt, heir to Alexandrian learning.
- **Religious milieu.** Alexandrian allegorical reading (Clement, Origen) applied to the natural world: every creature is a cipher for a spiritual truth.
- **Who was behind it.** An anonymous compiler; the work travelled namelessly under its tag-phrase.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Translated remarkably early into Latin, Syriac, Armenian, Coptic, Ge'ez, Arabic, and Georgian, then into virtually every medieval European vernacular — the seed of the illustrated bestiary.

## The famous allegories
- The **phoenix** that burns and rises on the third day → the resurrection of Christ.
- The **pelican** that revives its young with its own blood → Christ's self-sacrifice (the source of the eucharistic pelican of Christian art and heraldry).
- The **lion** whose cubs are born dead and licked to life on the third day → the resurrection.
- The **unicorn** captured only in a virgin's lap → the Incarnation.

## Themes and motifs
- [[divine-kingship]] — the lion, "king of beasts," is read as Christ the king; the bestiary repeatedly maps natural sovereignty onto divine kingship.

## Disputes
- **Date.** Estimates range across the 2nd–4th centuries; the work grew by recension, so no single date fits.
- **Sources.** How much derives from a single compiler versus an accreting tradition of Hellenistic animal-lore is uncertain.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **The source-book of medieval animal symbolism — across every Christian language and beyond.** Because the Physiologus was translated into Latin, Syriac, Armenian, Ge'ez, Coptic, Arabic, and the European vernaculars, it carried a single set of allegorical readings of the natural world across the entire Christian *and* Islamic Mediterranean — the pelican, phoenix, and unicorn of cathedral carving, heraldry, and manuscript art all descend from it (Curley 1979; Hassig 1995). Few texts so concretely document one symbolic system propagating across linguistic and religious boundaries.
- **The ancestor of the bestiary.** The illustrated Latin bestiaries of the 12th–13th centuries — among the most beloved books of the Middle Ages — are expansions of this Alexandrian Greek core, a clean transmission-spine from late-antique Egypt to Gothic Europe (Hassig 1995).
