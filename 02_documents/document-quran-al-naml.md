# Sūrat al-Naml (Quran 27 — The Ant)

## What it is
A Meccan sura named for the moment Solomon, marching with his armies of humans, jinn, and birds, overhears an ant warning its colony to take cover (27:18) — and smiles. The narrative centerpiece is the embassy of the hoopoe (hudhud), who returns from Sheba reporting a queen and her people who "worship the sun instead of God." Solomon summons her; her throne is fetched in an instant; she is led into a palace of polished glass she mistakes for water; and she submits, declaring her faith in "the Lord of the Worlds" (27:44). The sura also recounts Moses at the burning bush, Ṣāliḥ, and Lot.

## Primary text

### Quran 27:44
*Trans. Abdel Haleem (2004)*
> She was told, "Enter the hall," but when she saw it, she thought it was a deep pool of water, and bared her legs. Solomon explained, "It is just a hall paved with glass," and she said, "Lord, I have wronged myself: I devote myself, with Solomon, to God, the Lord of the Worlds."

### Quran 27:18
*Trans. Abdel Haleem (2004)*
> When they came to the Valley of the Ants, one ant said, "Ants! Go into your homes, in case Solomon and his hosts unwittingly crush you."

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** Meccan; Solomon models the king who holds dominion over nature and spirits yet bows entirely to God.
- **Tradition.** The queen, unnamed in the Quran, becomes **Bilqīs** in later Islamic tradition — the pagan monarch who reasons her way to monotheism.
- **Discovery / transmission.** Solomon's mastery of jinn and the wind (27:17; 21:81–82; 34:12–13) seeds the immense later literature of Solomonic magic and the "seal of Solomon."

## Themes and motifs
- Dominion under submission — Solomon commands birds, ants, and jinn, but is himself a servant.
- The reasoning convert — a queen led from sun-worship to the one God.
- Knowledge as gift — "we were given knowledge" (27:15), the speech of birds, the throne fetched by one "who had knowledge of the Scripture."

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **One terse biblical visit, elaborated into three nations' charter.** In 1 Kings 10 (and 2 Chronicles 9) an unnamed Queen of Sheba travels to Solomon, tests him with riddles, marvels at his wisdom, exchanges gold, and goes home — twelve verses, no romance, no conversion. From that seed three traditions grew very different trees. **Jewish** legend (the Targum Sheni to Esther, the Alphabet of Ben Sira) turned the visit into a contest of riddles and a brush with the demonic, the queen's hairy legs hinting at Lilith. The **Quran** (27:22–44) made it a story of *daʿwa* — the sun-worshipping queen reasons her way, throne and glass-floor and all, to submission before "the Lord of the Worlds," becoming Bilqīs. **Ethiopian** Christianity, in the *Kebra Nagast*, made her Makeda, who bears Solomon a son, Menelik I — founder of the Solomonic dynasty that Ethiopia's emperors claimed unbroken down to Haile Selassie in the twentieth century. The same dozen verses of Kings thus underwrite a Jewish demonology, an Islamic conversion narrative, and an African royal house — a textbook case of one scriptural episode radiating across the Atlas (Lassner 1993 pp.1–17, 133–161; Reynolds 2010 pp.93–101; Nasr 2015 pp.937–949).
