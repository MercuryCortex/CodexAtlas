# Epistle to the Galatians

## What it is
Paul's angriest and most concentrated letter, a passionate defense of his "gospel of grace" against rival teachers who insisted that gentile converts must be circumcised and keep the Mosaic law. Omitting his usual thanksgiving, Paul recounts his independent apostolic call, his confrontation with Peter at Antioch, and then argues that no one is justified by works of the law but through faith in Christ — and that in Christ the old divisions of ethnicity, status, and gender are abolished.

## Primary text

### Galatians 3:28
*Trans. NRSV*
> There is no longer Jew or Greek, there is no longer slave or free, there is no longer male and female; for all of you are one in Christ Jesus.

## Context
- **Religious milieu.** The decisive early-Christian struggle over the terms of gentile inclusion — law or grace.
- **Authorship.** Undisputed Pauline, and a primary source for Paul's biography (chs. 1–2).
- **Discovery / transmission.** Within the Pauline corpus.

## Themes and motifs
- Justification by faith — not by works of the law (2:16; 3:11, quoting Habakkuk).
- Freedom — "for freedom Christ has set us free" (5:1); the fruit of the Spirit vs. the works of the flesh.
- The one new people — the abolition of dividing categories (3:28).

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **Galatians is the charter of Christian freedom from the law — and the Reformation's beating heart.** Its thesis, that a person is justified "not by the works of the law but through faith in Jesus Christ" (2:16), with Habakkuk 2:4 as its proof-text, gave Martin Luther the lever for the Reformation; Luther lectured on Galatians repeatedly and called it the epistle "to which I am betrothed." Its declaration that in Christ there is "no longer Jew or Greek... slave or free... male and female" (3:28) has been read ever since as the seed of Christian universalism and of arguments against slavery and for equality (Martyn 1997, AYB 33A pp.376–383; Betz 1979, Hermeneia).
