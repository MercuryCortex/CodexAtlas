# Arthur Deikman — bimodal consciousness (action / receptive, 1971)

*The 1971 paper 'Bimodal consciousness' (*Archives of General Psychiatry* 25: 481-489) proposed a structural distinction between two cognitive modes — the **action mode** (focal-grasping, object-oriented, task-driven) and the **receptive mode** (allowing-perceiving, field-oriented, contemplative). Deikman's framework supplied the cognitive-psychology mechanism that the Jamesian-Stacean phenomenological literature lacked, and his earlier 1966 paper on **deautomatization** gave the discipline an operational concept for what happens cognitively during contemplative training.*

## What Deikman proposed

Deikman's **action mode** is the cognitive configuration that dominates ordinary task-focused waking life: focal attention, object-individuation, sequential thinking, sympathetic-nervous-system arousal, motor activation. It is what's required to find your car keys, write a paragraph, drive in traffic. The action mode parses the perceptual field into discrete grasped-objects oriented toward goals.

The **receptive mode** is the cognitive configuration that dominates contemplative + mystical reports: diffuse attention, field-perception rather than object-perception, parasympathetic dominance, motor relaxation, decreased boundaries between self and surround. Deikman's case: this is not a *disordered* version of the action mode (the psychiatric default-of-the-1960s would have read it as dissociation or depersonalization disorder) but a *different mode with its own functional integrity*. Contemplative practice trains practitioners to access the receptive mode at will and to integrate it into ordinary life.

**Deautomatization** (1966) names the mechanism: ordinary perception is highly automatized (we don't see the object, we recognize-and-categorize it via well-rehearsed cognitive shortcuts). Contemplative practices like meditation systematically deautomatize perception — the practitioner is instructed to look-as-if-for-the-first-time, to attend to bare sensory data rather than to the cognitive label that usually overwrites it. This is what produces the *fresh-perception* + *unity-of-things* qualities of Stace's extrovertive mystical type.

**The observing self** (1982 monograph): the awareness *that perceives* is distinct from the contents it perceives, including distinct from the ego-narrative-self. In contemplative training the practitioner re-identifies with the observing self rather than with the contents being observed; the practical result is what the mystical literature describes as *witness consciousness*, *sākṣī*, *the soul's apex*.

## Tradition context — mid-20th-c. psychiatry takes mysticism seriously

Deikman wrote against the backdrop of mid-20th-c. clinical psychiatry's near-universal assumption that mystical-experience reports were pathological (depersonalization, dissociation, psychotic decompensation, defense against anxiety). His 1966 paper was published in *Psychiatry* (the principal psychoanalytic-psychiatric journal of the era) and made the clinical-empirical case that mystical experiences in non-pathological subjects have a different phenomenology and a different functional outcome (integration, stabilization, ethical maturation) than the apparently-similar dissociative-pathological experiences. The 1971 paper extended the argument into a positive cognitive-psychology model. This is one of the principal first-wave moves that opened psychiatry to legitimate engagement with contemplative-traditions material — running ahead to Daniel Goleman 1977 *Varieties of Meditative Experience*, the Mind & Life Institute (1987 onward, Davidson-Lutz-Wallace), and the contemporary contemplative-neuroscience field.

## Cross-tradition resonance

Deikman's bimodal framework is cross-tradition by design: he reads Christian contemplative literature (Cloud of Unknowing, John of the Cross), Sufi sources (al-Ghazālī, Ibn ʿArabī in Nicholson's translations), Hindu Vedānta (Vivekananda, Ramana — what was available in mid-20th-c. translation), Zen (Suzuki). The action/receptive distinction tracks the contemplative-Christian *being vs. doing*, the Daoist *wu-wei* (non-acting-acting), the Zen *kenshō* (seeing into one's nature), the Advaita *sākṣī* (witness-consciousness).
