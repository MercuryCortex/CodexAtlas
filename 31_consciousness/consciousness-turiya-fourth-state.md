# Turīya — the fourth state (Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad phenomenology)

*The Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad (12 verses, c. 800-200 BCE — dating uncertain) names four states of consciousness. The first three (*jāgrat* waking, *svapna* dream, *suṣupti* deep-sleep) are familiar; the fourth, **turīya** ("the fourth"), is the seed of the Advaita Vedānta phenomenology of consciousness and the canonical Hindu candidate in the cross-tradition pure-awareness family.*

## What the Māṇḍūkya proposes

The four states have a structural progression. **Waking** (*jāgrat*) — outer-cognition active, body present, called *Vaiśvānara* (collective-self) and identified with the gross body and the syllable A of OM. **Dream** (*svapna*) — inner-cognition active, awareness operating on internal-mental content, called *Taijasa* (luminous) and identified with the subtle body and the syllable U. **Deep sleep** (*suṣupti*) — undifferentiated, a "mass of cognition" without object-distinction, peaceful, called *Prājña* (cognizant) and identified with the causal body and the syllable M. **Turīya** — not a fourth alongside the three but the *always-present background* of all three, that which is awake to waking-dreaming-sleeping; described entirely by negations ("not inwardly cognitive, not outwardly cognitive, not cognitive of both") and a few positive predicates ("peaceful, auspicious, non-dual") and identified with the OM syllable taken as a whole and with the Ātman that *is* Brahman.

Gauḍapāda's *Māṇḍūkya Kārikā* (c. 6th-7th c.) extends the analysis into a four-chapter philosophical treatise, the second chapter introducing *asparśa-yoga* (the no-contact yoga): turīya is recognized when one sees that the three states never substantially happened. Śaṅkara's 8th-c. commentary on both texts canonizes the framework as the Advaita scriptural authority on consciousness-states.

## Tradition context — Upaniṣadic phenomenology to Advaita synthesis

The four-states schema is foreshadowed in the earlier *Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad* IV.3 (Yājñavalkya's dialog with Janaka on the wisdom-fire and the sleep-states) and in *Chāndogya Upaniṣad* VIII (the search of Indra and Virocana for the self), but the Māṇḍūkya is the first to crystallize *turīya* as a named technical category. The Gauḍapāda-Śaṅkara line makes the Māṇḍūkya the *foundation Upaniṣad* of Advaita: Gauḍapāda's *Kārikā* IV ("Quenching the Firebrand") gives the canonical Advaita Vedānta non-dual reading. The Vivekacūḍāmaṇi attributed to Śaṅkara, the contemporary Neo-Vedānta of Vivekananda (1893 Chicago Parliament onwards), Ramana Maharshi's *self-enquiry* method, Nisargadatta Mahārāj's *I AM* teaching — all draw their state-phenomenology from this small Upaniṣad.

## Cross-tradition resonance

Turīya sits in the cross-tradition primordial-awareness family alongside Buddhist Dzogchen rigpa and Kagyu Mahāmudrā 'od gsal, Zen *hongaku* (original enlightenment), Sufi *fiṭra* (primordial nature), Christian apophatic *Gottheit* (Eckhart). The structural-formula similarities are striking: all are described by *negation* of conceptual predicates + identification with what is always-already aware. The Advaita-Buddhist convergence is debated since at least Gauḍapāda (whose *Kārikā* IV is famously interpretable as a Mahāyāna-Madhyamaka borrowing — see King 1995 *Early Advaita Vedānta and Buddhism*). Per CON-1, the vault carries the perennialist convergence reading (Forman, Wallace) alongside the constructivist counter (Katz, Sharf, King): the *formula* is similar across traditions, the *implied ontology* (Advaita: the One Self is real and unchanged; Buddhism: the One Self is precisely what is empty) is exactly where the traditions remain in genuine disagreement.
