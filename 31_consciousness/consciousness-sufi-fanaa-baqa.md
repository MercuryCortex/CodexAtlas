# Sufi fanāʾ / baqāʾ — extinction and subsistence (al-Junayd school 9th c.)

*The Sufi technical vocabulary **fanāʾ** (annihilation / extinction of the self in God) + **baqāʾ** (subsistence / abiding after annihilation), articulated canonically by **al-Junayd of Baghdad** (c. 830-910) and inherited as the working frame of the sober mainstream Sufi tradition through every major institutional tariqa (Qādirī, Naqshbandī, Shādhilī, Suhrawardī, Chishtī).*

## What the vocabulary names

**Fanāʾ** (Arabic *fanāʾ*: passing-away, extinction) names a contemplative-realization state in which the experiencer's ordinary egoic self drops away and only the divine presence remains. The Junaydī formulation distinguishes three stages of fanāʾ in progressive depth: (1) **fanāʾ fi'l-shaykh** — annihilation in the teacher; the murīd (disciple) so attunes to the shaykh that the disciple's separate willing-and-judging drops away and the shaykh's guidance operates through the disciple. (2) **fanāʾ fi'l-Rasūl** — annihilation in the Prophet Muḥammad; the realization that the prophetic-presence (*ḥaqīqa muḥammadiyya*) is operative in all guided states. (3) **fanāʾ fi'llāh** — annihilation in God; the state in which there is no created-self over against the divine reality. The three stages are not necessarily sequential in every practitioner; they are structural depths.

**Baqāʾ** (subsistence, abiding) is the Junaydī sober-tradition's essential complement: after fanāʾ the practitioner must *return* to baqāʾ — re-emerge into ordinary life with the recognition preserved, able to function, eat, pray, work, fulfill social obligations. The 'sober' tradition (as opposed to the *intoxicated* tradition of al-Bistāmī and al-Hallāj, who emphasized fanāʾ without strong baqāʾ counter-balance) insists that genuine realization must include the baqāʾ return. Junayd's saying: "The sufi is the one in whom God's *qadīm* (eternity) abides while his own *muḥdath* (created-temporality) is annihilated."

## Tradition context — Baghdad mainstream to institutional tariqas

The 9th-10th century Baghdad Sufi milieu produced multiple founding figures (al-Muḥāsibī, Sarī al-Saqaṭī, al-Junayd's principal teacher; Junayd himself; Ḥallāj, who studied with Junayd before breaking away into the intoxicated tradition). Junayd's *sober* synthesis became the institutional mainstream because it could be reconciled with sharīʿa-jurisprudential orthodoxy in a way that Bistāmī's "Glory be to Me!" and Ḥallāj's "I am the Truth" could not. Al-Ghazālī's 11th-c. *Iḥyāʾ ʿulūm al-dīn* (Revival of the Religious Sciences) made the Junaydī Sufi synthesis the spiritual interior of mainstream Sunnī Islam.

From the 12th c. onward the institutional tariqas formed (Qādirī from ʿAbd al-Qādir al-Jīlānī d. 1166; Suhrawardī from Abū Najīb al-Suhrawardī d. 1168 + his nephew ʿUmar al-Suhrawardī d. 1234; Shādhilī from Abū al-Ḥasan al-Shādhilī d. 1258; Chishtī's Indian transmission from Muʿīn al-Dīn al-Chishtī d. 1236; Naqshbandī from Bahāʾ al-Dīn Naqshband d. 1389). Each carries the Junaydī fanāʾ-baqāʾ vocabulary as part of its core training-language; the *maqāmāt* (stations) and *aḥwāl* (states) curricula are tradition-specific elaborations on the Junaydī base.

## Cross-tradition resonance

The Sufi fanāʾ-baqāʾ pair is methodologically distinctive — most other traditions' mystical phenomenologies emphasize the *attainment* of unitive states; the Sufi tradition treats the *return* (baqāʾ) as equally essential. Cross-tradition parallels exist for the *fanāʾ* moment: Christian *unio mystica* (Eckhart, John of the Cross), Advaita *nirvikalpa samādhi*, Buddhist *anātman* recognition, Daoist *wuwei* in deep contemplative form. Parallels for the *baqāʾ* return are scarcer but include: Mahāyāna *bodhicitta* (the bodhisattva's return-to-the-world-for-others), Christian *unitive life* (Underhill's fifth stage), the Sōtō Zen *post-satori practice*.
