# W.T. Stace — extrovertive / introvertive mystical experience (1960)

*The 1960 monograph **Mysticism and Philosophy** is the most-cited single work in 20th-century analytic philosophy of mysticism. Stace's two-type typology (extrovertive vs. introvertive) and seven core characteristics provided mysticism studies with a working vocabulary; Hood's M-Scale empirically operationalizes them.*

## What Stace proposed

Stace's central methodological move is to **separate phenomenological description from theological interpretation**. The mystic reports an experience; the mystic also reports an interpretation of that experience (Christian, Hindu, Buddhist, Sufi). Stace argues these must be analytically separated: the *experience* may be cross-traditional in structure while the *interpretation* is local. This is the seed of what becomes the perennialist common-core thesis.

The **two types**: **Extrovertive** mystical experience — perceived through the senses; the unity of all visible things experienced as a single living presence. The mystic walks in a garden and *sees* every leaf as one. **Introvertive** mystical experience — achieved by inward withdrawal from sensory content; consciousness without object, pure awareness, the One. Eckhart's *Gottheit*, Shankara's *nirguna brahman*, Buddhist *śūnyatā* as Stace reads them.

The **seven core characteristics** common to both types: (1) sense of objectivity / reality, (2) feeling of blessedness / peace, (3) feeling of the sacred / divine, (4) paradoxicality, (5) ineffability, (6) consciousness of the One / unity, (7) transcendence of space and time (introvertive) / unity of all things (extrovertive). Hood's M-Scale renders these as 32 Likert items in three factors (introvertive minus, extrovertive, religious interpretation); 40+ years of cross-cultural empirical follow-up (Hood et al. 2001-2014) finds robust factor structure across Christian, Muslim, Hindu, secular samples.

## Tradition context — analytic philosophy of mysticism

Stace operates inside Anglo-American analytic philosophy (his earlier career was in epistemology and Hegel scholarship at Princeton 1932-1955). What he brings to mysticism studies is the analytic-philosophical demand for clear distinctions: experience vs. interpretation, phenomenology vs. ontology, description vs. evaluation. His common-core thesis is offered as a *philosophical* claim (the phenomenology generalizes), not a *religious* claim (whether the One is real is left bracketed). This is exactly the framing Katz 1978 will challenge — Katz argues no experience is *pre-interpretive*, the language and tradition shape the experience all the way down.

## Cross-tradition resonance

Stace's working corpus is broad: Christian mystics (Eckhart, Suso, Tauler, Ruysbroeck, Boehme, Teresa, John of the Cross, Pseudo-Dionysius), Hindu sources (Upaniṣads, Shankara, Bhagavad-Gītā), Buddhist sources (Theravāda nikāyas, Mahāyāna sūtras as available in mid-20th-century translation), Sufi mystics (Rūmī, Ibn ʿArabī, al-Junayd in Nicholson's translations), Plotinus and Neoplatonic *henosis*. The reading is the canonical perennialist reading — and the canonical target of constructivist critique. The vault per CON-1 carries both: Stace's typology + Hood's robust empirical replication + Katz-Proudfoot's polemic against the framing's underlying assumptions.
