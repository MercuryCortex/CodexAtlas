# Possession trance vs shamanic ecstasy — the Bourguignon typology

## Identity

**Possession trance** is the altered state of consciousness in which a spirit, deity, or non-human being is experienced as *entering and occupying* the practitioner — taking over the body, voice and behaviour while the person's ordinary ego-consciousness is displaced or suspended. It is defined by contrast with its complementary pole, **shamanic ecstasy** or soul-flight, in which nothing enters the practitioner; instead the practitioner's *own* soul is felt to travel *out* of the body to the spirit world. The distinction — occupation-inward versus journey-outward — is the organising axis of the cross-cultural anthropology of religious altered states, and its canonical operational form is the typology built by **Erika Bourguignon** and her students at Ohio State University in the 1960s–70s.

Bourguignon's Cross-Cultural Study of Dissociational States (funded by the U.S. National Institute of Mental Health) coded a sample of **488 societies** drawn from George Murdock's *Ethnographic Atlas* and found that roughly **90% (437)** had some institutionalised, culturally-patterned altered state of consciousness — one of the most nearly-universal traits in the ethnographic record. She then partitioned these into:

- **Trance (T)** — an altered state *not* interpreted as possession: visions, soul journeys, magical flight, the shamanic ascent.
- **Possession trance (PT)** — an altered state interpreted as *occupation by a spirit*, the spirit acting through the entranced body.

Many societies have both; the two labels name interpretations of the altered state, not two neural events. This node is the consciousness-lens counterpart of the ritual documented at [[possession-ritual]]: the hub carries the ceremony (drumming, dance, the deity's colours and foods, community recognition), while this node carries the *state* and the scholarly debate about how to classify it.

## The typology and its rivals

**Bourguignon (1973, 1976).** Beyond the survey, Bourguignon proposed a *social-structural* correlation: possession trance clusters in more complex, stratified, agricultural societies (frequently those with rigid hierarchy or a history of slavery), whereas non-possession visionary trance predominates in simpler foraging societies. The interpretation — that possession, in which one is *acted through* rather than *acting*, models the experience of persons who are themselves subordinated — is suggestive but contested (see Disputes).

**Mircea Eliade (1951), the ecstasy pole.** In *Shamanism: Archaic Techniques of Ecstasy*, Eliade made *ecstasy as soul-flight* the defining, "archaic" religious technique: the true shaman **masters** spirits and journeys through the three-level cosmos via the world-axis; to be **possessed** — mastered by a spirit — is, for Eliade, a late and "aberrant" degeneration. Eliade's ideal type supplies the pole against which possession trance is defined (and it is the ecstasy end of the axis carried in the consciousness lens at [[consciousness-eliade-sacred-profane-modes]] and in the theme hub [[shamanism]]).

**I. M. Lewis (1971), the continuum.** *Ecstatic Religion: A Study of Shamanism and Spirit Possession* is the major dissent from Eliade's segregation. Lewis treats shamanism and possession *together* as "ecstatic religion" and argues that the master-of / mastered-by distinction dissolves in the field — Siberian shamans are themselves routinely possessed by helping spirits. His enduring contribution is the **central vs peripheral** distinction: *central* possession cults uphold public morality and tend to be male-dominated, while *peripheral* cults (the Sudanese and Ethiopian **zar**, Hausa **bori**, Somali **saar**) are amoral, therapeutic, and disproportionately involve women and the socially marginal — possession as "oblique aggressive strategy," a sanctioned idiom of protest for the powerless (the **deprivation thesis**).

**Gilbert Rouget (1980/1985).** *Music and Trance* draws the line on phenomenological grounds: **trance** (movement, noise, company, sensory over-stimulation, crisis, amnesia — the profile of possession) versus **ecstasy** (immobility, silence, solitude, sensory deprivation, recollection — the profile of the mystic and, often, the journeying shaman), with music the characteristic *trigger* of possession trance.

## Ethnographic range

The possession pole is documented, in mutually independent forms, on every inhabited continent:

- **West African & Afro-diasporic orisha/lwa.** In Haitian Vodou the lwa "*monte*" (mounts) its "*chwal*" (horse); in Bahian Candomblé the deity's medium is a *cavalo* / *elegun*; in Cuban Santería-Lucumí the orisha *montar* the *caballo*. Documented by Alfred Métraux (*Voodoo in Haiti*, 1959), Roger Bastide (*The African Religions of Brazil*, 1960) and Karen McCarthy Brown (*Mama Lola*, 1991). This cluster is bound by real historical **transmission** (the Middle Passage) — see [[afro-diasporic-syncretism]].
- **Zar / bori / saar cults** (Sudan, Ethiopia, Egypt; Hausa; Somali). The spirit that "possesses" causes illness; the cure is not exorcism but **accommodation** — the host learns to coexist with the spirit through a lifelong relationship. Janice Boddy's *Wombs and Alien Spirits* (1989) is the classic study of its gendered, peripheral logic.
- **Korean *mudang*** — the (usually female) shaman becomes the vehicle of ancestors and deities in the multi-day *gut* ceremony (Laurel Kendall, *Shamans, Housewives, and Other Restless Spirits*, 1985).
- **Tibetan oracle-mediumship** — the Nechung State Oracle, whose medium (the *kuten*) is possessed by the protector deity Pehar/Dorje Drakden and consulted by the Tibetan government.
- **Balinese trance** — the *sanghyang dedari* girl trance-dancers and the *kris* (keris) self-stabbing trance of the Rangda–Barong drama (Jane Belo, *Trance in Bali*, 1960; Bateson and Mead).
- **Mediterranean & Pentecostal** — the Delphic Pythia possessed by [[apollo]]; Plato's *Ion* on the rhapsode's *entheos* ("god-within") inspiration; and, in the modern West, Pentecostal "baptism in the Spirit," glossolalia and being "slain in the Spirit" — a functionally parallel loss-of-control-under-a-spirit, differently theologised.

## Cross-tradition significance (convergence, with one transmission line)

The honest reading is that possession trance is a **convergent** near-universal, not a diffused motif. Because institutionalised altered states appear in ~90% of surveyed societies and possession trance in about half of them — on continents with no plausible contact — the parallel is best explained as a shared feature of human psychology (a capacity for dissociation, everywhere available for cultural interpretation) rather than as inheritance from a single source. This is the same logic the vault applies to other deep universals: the pattern is real; the explanation is convergence, not a common ancestor.

The important exception is the **Atlantic** cluster. West African orisha and lwa possession carried into Vodou, Candomblé and Santería is a case of genuine historical **transmission** — an unbroken ritual lineage across the slave trade — and should not be flattened into the convergence story. The methodological point (per the vault's transmission-vs-convergence discipline) is to keep the two apart: the Yoruba→diaspora line is documented continuity; the Korea–Tibet–Bali–Sudan–Greece resemblance is structural convergence.

The axis itself — possession (inward occupation) versus shamanic ecstasy (outward flight) — is likewise a *typological* contrast, not a claim of shared origin. It is useful precisely because it lets one comparative frame hold both poles at once, which is why the node wires to both [[possession-ritual]] and [[shamanism]].

## Disputes

- **Two states or a continuum?** Eliade's sharp segregation of soul-flight from possession is rejected by Lewis (and largely by later anthropology), who shows the "master/mastered" line blurring in real cases; but a weaker, descriptive occupation-vs-journey distinction remains analytically standard. The vault carries both: the poles are a useful *contrast*, not two sealed ontologies.
- **Is "possession" a Western projection?** Critics note that the very word imports Christian demonological and exorcistic assumptions (a hostile spirit to be *expelled*) that fit the accommodative zar or the welcomed lwa poorly. The category is retained as a family-resemblance term while flagging that specific traditions are better analysed on their own terms — the same caution the vault applies to the scholarly construct "shamanism" (see [[shamanism]]).
- **Pathology or skill?** Mid-20th-century psychiatry read possession trance as dissociative pathology; Bourguignon and later cross-cultural psychology (Cardeña; the debate over "dissociative trance disorder" in DSM-IV/DSM-5) argue that culturally-normative, controlled, community-validated possession is a *learned skill*, phenomenologically distinct in outcome from pathological dissociation. The distinction between sanctioned and pathological dissociation is where the live disagreement sits.
- **The social-complexity correlation.** Bourguignon's finding that possession trance clusters in stratified societies has been challenged as partly a sampling and coding artifact of the Ethnographic Atlas; the correlation is real in her data but its causal reading (subordination modelled as being-acted-through) is interpretive.
