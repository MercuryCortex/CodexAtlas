# Sahaja — the natural / spontaneous state (Indian contemplative phenomenology)

*The Sanskrit term **sahaja** ("born together", innate, coemergent, spontaneous) is one of the few technical terms that traverses Buddhist Mahāsiddha + Tantric + Bengali Sahajiyā + Advaita Vedānta usage. Phenomenologically it names a recognition-state that is **effortless** and **continuous** rather than achieved-and-lost. The mature 20th-c. Advaita usage (Ramana Maharshi) distinguishes *kevala nirvikalpa* (effortful temporary absorption) from *sahaja nirvikalpa* (the stable always-already recognition).*

## What the term names

Sahaja first becomes a technical category in the 8th-9th century Buddhist Mahāsiddha literature (Saraha's *Dohākoṣa*, the *Caryāgīti* — the songs of the wandering tantric adepts of eastern India). Its usage there: the *coemergent wisdom* (*sahaja-jñāna*) — the recognition that appearance and emptiness, mind and its expressions, samsara and nirvāṇa, are *born together* and never substantially separable. The point is that liberation is not achieved by abolishing the world; it is the recognition that *world* and *liberation* are the same when their nature is seen.

The term diffuses through two great downstream traditions: (a) **Tibetan Kagyu Mahāmudrā** translates it as *lhan cig skyes pa* and makes *sahaja-jñāna* a working category of the highest contemplative phenomenology; (b) the **Bengali Sahajiyā** Vaiṣṇava-tantric movement (12th-17th c.) makes *sahaja* the name for an erotic-mystical phenomenology of recognition (Rādhā-Kṛṣṇa as the structural figure for coemergent appearance). The Bengali Sahajiyā becomes important via Caitanya's lineage and the *Caryāgīti* literature.

Independently, classical Yoga + Advaita Vedānta use *sahaja* as a sober technical term for the *natural/spontaneous* mode of samādhi — distinguished from effortful absorption. Ramana Maharshi (1879-1950) crystallizes the 20th-century Advaita usage: *kevala nirvikalpa samādhi* is the temporary absorption in which the world disappears and only consciousness-without-object remains; *sahaja nirvikalpa samādhi* is the stable always-already recognition that requires no entry or exit, lives in the midst of activity, and IS the goal of contemplative life (Talks ##47, 187, 391).

## Tradition context — a working term across three streams

The fact that *the same term* surfaces as a technical contemplative category in (a) Buddhist Mahāsiddha tantra → Tibetan Mahāmudrā, (b) Bengali Vaiṣṇava Sahajiyā, and (c) classical-Yoga / Ramana-Advaita is unusual and load-bearing for comparative phenomenology. The standard scholarly account: the eastern-Indian tantric milieu of the 8th-11th centuries shared a Buddhist-Hindu-tantric technical vocabulary; *sahaja* belongs to that shared vocabulary; downstream lineages preserve it with significant retained semantic structure (effortless, coemergent, natural-as-against-fabricated). The 20th-century revivals (Ramana, Nisargadatta, Tibetan diaspora teachers like Tulku Urgyen and Dilgo Khyentse) re-foreground the term and bring it into international contemplative vocabulary.

## Cross-tradition resonance

Sahaja is one of the cleaner cross-tradition cases: the *term* is shared (etymologically + historically traceable), the *phenomenological structure described* is recognizably similar across the three branches, and the cases of transmission-influence are documentable. Per CON-1 the perennialist reading takes this as evidence of cross-tradition convergence on the phenomenology; the constructivist reading notes that the shared *term* doesn't guarantee shared *experience* — Bengali Sahajiyā erotic-mysticism, Ramana's witness-consciousness, and Mahāmudrā coemergent-wisdom may share the word while picking out three structurally distinct things.
