# Mircea Eliade — sacred / profane as ontological modes (1957)

*The Chicago history-of-religions program's central claim: the distinction sacred / profane is not merely affective (as Otto held) but **ontological** — sacred-time and sacred-space are different *modes of being*, not just attitudes the religious person takes toward neutral data. Codified in **Le sacré et le profane** (1957). T2 because the data-pattern is real but the metaphysical-modal claim is contested.*

## What Eliade proposed

Eliade builds on Otto's numinous (affective response to the holy) and pushes one step further: the *holy* names not a feeling but **a mode of being**. The religious person (*homo religiosus*) inhabits a world structured by *hierophanies* — moments where the sacred breaks through into the profane and reveals an organizing pattern. From hierophany Eliade derives four structuring categories:

- **Sacred space** — not all space is equivalent; some loci (temples, mountains, hearths) are *qualitatively different*, points of cosmic orientation. The **axis mundi** (world-pillar, cosmic tree, central mountain) marks the privileged connection between cosmic levels.
- **Sacred time** — not all time is equivalent; festival-time *re-presents* the time of origins (*in illo tempore*, "in that time"), abolishing profane duration. The *eternal return* names this re-actualization of the original creative moment.
- **Cosmogony as paradigm** — every meaningful action (founding a city, planting a field, marrying) is patterned on the divine cosmogonic act, repeating it.
- **Homo religiosus** — there is a universal human type whose mode of being is structured by these categories; modern secular humanity has lost access to this mode but retains it in disguised forms.

## Tradition context — Chicago history of religions

Eliade arrived at the University of Chicago Divinity School in 1956 and built what becomes "the Chicago school" of history of religions (Charles Long, Jonathan Z. Smith, David Tracy, Wendy Doniger — though Smith and Doniger end up as critics of the Eliadean program). The journal *History of Religions* (founded 1961, Eliade editor) institutionalizes comparative-phenomenological method as a discipline. Internationally Eliade is one of the most-read religious-studies authors of the 20th century; his *Patterns in Comparative Religion* (1949) and *The Sacred and the Profane* function as introductory texts in Religious Studies programs through the 1980s.

## Cross-tradition resonance + the contested status

Eliade reads cross-traditionally with extraordinary range: Australian Aboriginal dreamtime, Vedic *yajña*, Mesopotamian *akītu*, Christian liturgical year, shamanic axis-mundi, archaic agricultural cosmogonies. The data is real; the *theoretical move* — that all these instance a universal structure of homo religiosus — is the contested layer. Jonathan Z. Smith's *To Take Place* (1987) is the canonical critique: Smith argues Eliade's axis-mundi is an Eliadean theoretical artifact projected onto the data, that the local materials don't actually support the universal-pattern claim, that comparison must be *local and contrastive* rather than essentialist-universal. The Eliade-Smith debate maps onto the Forman-Katz debate in mysticism studies: in both, the *patterned data* is real, the *universalist theoretical interpretation* is contested.
