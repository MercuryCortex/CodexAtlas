# Sukr / Ṣaḥw — Sufi intoxication and sobriety (the typology of ecstatic states)

*The paired Sufi technical terms **sukr** (Arabic سُكْر, "intoxication," "drunkenness") and **ṣaḥw** (صَحْو, "sobriety") name the two great modes of the ecstatic life — the self-losing rapture of the mystic overwhelmed by the divine presence, and the self-possessed lucidity of the mystic who has returned from it. Around this pair the classical tradition built a typology of Sufi temperaments: the **intoxicated** line of Abū Yazīd al-Bisṭāmī (d. 874) and al-Ḥallāj (d. 922), whose ecstatic utterances (**shaṭaḥāt**) burst the bounds of ordinary God-talk, against the **sober** line of al-Junayd of Baghdad (d. 910), for whom the return to lucid, law-abiding selfhood is the completed station.*

## Identity

Sukr and ṣaḥw are a matched pair in the Sufi vocabulary of states. **Sukr** is the condition of one so filled by the awareness of God that the ordinary self-monitoring ego is submerged: reason is overwhelmed, self-consciousness drops away, and speech, if it comes at all, comes as rapture rather than report. **Ṣaḥw** is its complement — the "coming to" after the wine has worn off: the mystic restored to a self that can pray, judge, teach, and obey the Law, but now with the recognition preserved. The metaphor is deliberately that of the tavern: the divine presence is a *wine* (*mey*, *khamr*), poured by a *cup-bearer* (*sāqī*), that leaves the drinker *drunk* (*sukr*) and then, in time, *sober* (*ṣaḥw*).

The pair is not merely descriptive; it became the axis of a **typology of Sufi types**. The eleventh-century Persian manual *Kashf al-Maḥjūb* of al-Hujwīrī sets out the intoxicated party (the *Ṭayfūriyya*, after Bisṭāmī's patronymic Ṭayfūr) and the sober party (the *Junaydiyya*) as distinct schools, and debates which condition ranks higher. That debate — **is the rapture of losing the self the summit, or is the recovered sobriety that can hold the recognition and still function in the world the truly higher station?** — is one of the defining internal questions of Sufi psychology.

In the Codex Atlas this node is the **intoxicated pole** of that typology and the states-epistemology that frames it; its sober complement is [[consciousness-sufi-fanaa-baqa]] (the Junaydī fanāʾ/baqāʾ node), and its cross-tradition surface is the sacred-drunkenness theme [[divine-intoxication]].

## The typology in the sources

**Bisṭāmī and the intoxicated utterance.** The paradigm of sukr is Abū Yazīd al-Bisṭāmī (Bāyazīd, d. 874), a Persian mystic of Bisṭām in Khorasan remembered almost entirely through his *shaṭaḥāt* — ecstatic, "theopathic" sayings uttered from the depth of intoxication. The most famous is *"Subḥānī, mā aʿẓama shaʾnī!"* — "Glory be to Me! How great is My majesty!" — in which the ordinary liturgical formula of praising God is spoken, scandalously, in the first person, as if God speaks through the emptied mystic. Bisṭāmī's ascension narrative (a Sufi *miʿrāj* modelled on the Prophet's) is the classic text in which the intoxicated self is progressively "unsaid" (Sells 1994). Because Bisṭāmī left no writings, the sayings survive through al-Sarrāj's *Kitāb al-Lumaʿ* and ʿAṭṭār's hagiographic *Tadhkirat al-Awliyāʾ*.

**Ḥallāj and the limit case.** The sharpest instance of sukr colliding with the Law is al-Ḥusayn ibn Manṣūr al-Ḥallāj (d. 922), whose utterance *"anā al-ḥaqq"* — "I am the Real/the Truth" ([[al-haqq]] being one of the ninety-nine divine Names) — was read by his judges as a claim to divinity, the capital sin of *shirk*, and he was tortured, crucified, and beheaded in Baghdad. His defenders (Rūmī and ʿAṭṭār among them) argued that the "I" that said it was not Ḥallāj's ego but the divine reality speaking through a vessel emptied by fanāʾ — the intoxicated shaṭḥ as report, not blasphemy (Ernst 1985; Massignon 1982).

**Junayd and the sober return.** Against this stands al-Junayd of Baghdad (d. 910), the architect of *sober* Sufism, for whom fanāʾ (annihilation) must be completed by *baqāʾ* (subsistence) — the return to a lucid, functioning, Law-observant self. Al-Sarrāj preserves Junayd's own sober *commentary* on Bisṭāmī's ecstatic sayings, an attempt to interpret the shaṭaḥāt within orthodoxy rather than repudiate them: the sober master reads the drunken master. Al-Ghazālī's eleventh-century *Iḥyāʾ ʿulūm al-dīn* consolidated this guarded, sobriety-first synthesis and made Sufism defensible to the jurists, precisely by de-emphasising the dramatic shaṭḥ.

**The lyric surface.** In later Persian poetry the whole complex becomes a settled symbolic language: the *mey* (wine), *sāqī* (cup-bearer), and *mey-khāna* (tavern) of Rūmī's *Masnavī* and Ḥāfiẓ's *Dīvān* are the lyric articulation of sukr — divine love as an intoxicant, in a religion where literal wine is forbidden. This node treats the *state and its typology*; that wine-imagery, and its cross-tradition cousins (Dionysian wine, *sobria ebrietas*), belong to the theme hub [[divine-intoxication]].

## Ḥāl vs. maqām — the epistemology of states

Underneath the typology lies a technical distinction that is one of the pillars of Sufi epistemology, set out canonically in al-Qushayrī's *Risāla*: the difference between a **ḥāl** (state) and a **maqām** (station). A *maqām* is a rung the wayfarer **earns** by sustained effort and then **holds** — repentance, patience, trust, contentment; it is stable and cumulative. A *ḥāl* is a condition that **descends** as a sheer gift of God, unbidden, and **passes** — it cannot be summoned or kept. Sukr, on this analysis, is a *ḥāl*: an intoxication that comes and goes, not a possession the mystic controls.

This is why the sukr/ṣaḥw debate is not trivial. If the highest thing is a transient rapture that seizes you, then the intoxicated saint who is *taken* is nearest God. If instead the highest thing is a stable *maqām* one inhabits and lives from, then the sober saint who *returns* — who can carry the recognition back into prayer, teaching, and ordinary obligation — has gone further, because a passing ecstasy is, precisely, passing. The Junaydī tradition took the second view; the Bāyazīdī tradition prized the first. The Codex maps the ladder of stations and states themselves at [[moral-sufi-spiritual-stations-maqamat]]; this node is the states-typology limb of that same distinction.

## Cross-tradition significance (convergence vs. transmission)

The honest verdict is mixed, and the mix is the point.

**The wine-of-love metaphor is largely convergent.** The image of intoxication for ecstatic self-loss is so natural a figure that it arises independently wherever mystics try to describe the unitive state — Dionysian *mania*, Philonic and Bernardine *sobria ebrietas* ("sober inebriation"), Hindu *bhāva*, and Sufi *sukr* need not be derived from one another. The comparative caution insisted on at [[divine-intoxication]] applies here: a secure *linguistic* cognate (e.g. Vedic Soma ~ Iranian Haoma) is a different order of claim from a *structural* parallel (Sufi wine ~ Dionysian wine), and the latter does not inherit the former's evidential force. The Sufi *sukr* belongs to the convergent, metaphoric pole, not to any documented ritual transmission.

**The state itself is the disputed comparandum.** At the consciousness lens, sukr is read as a transient, introvertive rapture within a cross-tradition class that includes Plotinian *ekstasis*, Christian *raptus*, and Hindu *savikalpa*/*nirvikalpa* episodes — and the sukr (transient *ḥāl*) vs. ṣaḥw (stabilised condition) axis is the Sufi form of the very distinction, transient-episode vs. permanent-realisation, that runs through the comparative literature ([[consciousness-stace-mystical-experience-typology]]). But whether these are one experience under many descriptions, or many experiences sharing a metaphor, is exactly the unresolved perennialism-vs-constructivism question ([[consciousness-katz-constructivism-vs-perennialism]]): the constructivist reply is that "intoxication in God" is not a raw datum but is *shaped* by the Qurʾānic and tariqa-specific vocabulary of sukr, ḥāl, and shaṭḥ, so that the Sufi's rapture is not interchangeable with a maenad's or a bhakta's. The parallel is real at the level of the figure of speech; its identity at the level of experience is precisely what is contested.

**A recurring institutional shape.** More securely comparative is the *social* pattern: the intoxicated utterance that exceeds the tolerance of institutional orthodoxy and is punished, then later rehabilitated. Ḥallāj's execution for "anā al-ḥaqq" and Meister Eckhart's condemnation for formulas that sounded like *hulūl* (indwelling) are structurally the same event in two traditions — the grammar of extreme unitive speech overflowing the boundaries the guardians of the Law can allow. This is convergence of *institutional dynamics*, not of doctrine or lineage, and should be named as such.

## Disputes

- **Is the typology itself a construction?** Jawid Mojaddedi (2003) argues that the tidy "drunken Bisṭāmī vs. sober Junayd" opposition is a *later doxographic construct* — traceable to Hujwīrī's eleventh-century *Kashf al-Maḥjūb* — read back onto ninth-century figures who did not describe themselves that way. On this view the typology is a genuine feature of the *tradition's self-image* but a poor guide to the historical Bisṭāmī and Junayd. This is the principal modern dissent and is honoured here: the node presents sukr/ṣaḥw as a classical *typology*, not as a neutral report of two men.
- **Which is higher — intoxication or sobriety?** The tradition never fully settled it. The Bāyazīdī line prizes the rapture in which the self is wholly taken; the Junaydī line holds the *return* to sobriety (ṣaḥw) — the ability to hold the recognition and still function under the Law — to be the completed, higher condition. Many later Sufis (Ibn ʿArabī's school, Rūmī) treat the two as a cycle rather than a ranking: intoxication and sobriety succeed one another, and the perfected saint is "sober in his drunkenness."
- **Shaṭḥ: true report or antinomian excess?** Whether an utterance like "anā al-ḥaqq" is a truthful report from fanāʾ (the self so annihilated that only God speaks) or a dangerous overstepping is the live legal-theological question that killed Ḥallāj and shadowed the whole intoxicated tradition. Ibn ʿArabī's *waḥdat al-wujūd* ([[wahdat-al-wujud]]) later supplied the frame on which the shaṭḥ could be read as a true ontological perception rather than heresy — a rehabilitation, not a consensus.
- **Overlap with the fanāʾ nodes.** This node is deliberately the *states-typology and intoxicated pole*; the doctrinal machinery of extinction/subsistence is carried by [[consciousness-sufi-fanaa-baqa]] (sober pole) and the theme [[fanaa-annihilation]]. The three are kept distinct to avoid duplication — see selfFlags.
