# Mystical union (unio mystica) — the cross-tradition unitive-state convergence

*The comparative HUB above the tradition-specific union-states: Sufi fanāʾ/baqāʾ, Christian unio mystica and hesychast Tabor-light, Advaita nirvikalpa samādhi and Upaniṣadic turīya, Dzogchen rigpa and Kagyü mahāmudrā, Jewish devekut, Neoplatonic henōsis. This is the node where the claim "the same experience, many names" is both stated and contested — the perennialist (Huxley · Stace · Forman) vs constructivist (Katz · Proudfoot · Gimello) debate, which the vault treats as live and unsettled, not decided.*

## Identity

"Mystical union" (Latin *unio mystica*) names the comparative-studies slot for the culminating experience reported across contemplative traditions in which the ordinary boundary between the experiencer and ultimate reality is felt to dissolve, thin, or be recognised as never having been real. Every literate contemplative tradition has a technical vocabulary for something in this region, and the striking family resemblance of those vocabularies — extinction, cleaving, recognition, identity, marriage, light, the flight of the alone to the Alone — is the empirical fact this node organises.

It is a **phenomenon-convergence hub**, deliberately distinct from [[consciousness-stace-mystical-experience-typology]] (the scholarly two-type/seven-characteristic grid) and from [[consciousness-katz-constructivism-vs-perennialism]] (the constructivist critique): those are positions ABOUT the phenomenon; this node is the phenomenon and the argument over it. It sits under the [[mysticism]] umbrella and above the individual state-nodes, wiring them into one comparative frame while refusing to prejudge whether that frame tracks a real shared experience or merely a habit of comparison.

## The family of union-states

The members the Atlas carries as instances of the slot:

- **Islam — fanāʾ / baqāʾ.** Extinction of the self in God (al-Junayd, 9th c.) balanced by subsistence/return. See [[consciousness-sufi-fanaa-baqa]] and the [[fanaa-annihilation]] theme.
- **Christianity (Latin/affective) — unio mystica.** Nuptial and love-union: Bernard of Clairvaux's *Sermones super Cantica*, Teresa of Ávila's "spiritual marriage" (*Interior Castle*, Seventh Mansions), John of the Cross. See [[bridal-mysticism]], [[love-mysticism]]. (No dedicated state-node yet — an ingest gap noted below.)
- **Christianity (Greek/hesychast) — theōsis and the Tabor-light.** Deification as participation in the divine *energies* (Palamas), the uncreated light. See [[consciousness-hesychast-tabor-light]] and [[doctrine-theosis-divinization-orthodox]].
- **Vedānta / Yoga — nirvikalpa samādhi and turīya.** Absorption without subject-object distinction; the "fourth" state of pure awareness (Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad). See [[consciousness-savikalpa-nirvikalpa-samadhi]], [[consciousness-turiya-fourth-state]].
- **Tibetan Buddhism — rigpa and mahāmudrā.** Recognition of empty-luminous primordial awareness. See [[consciousness-dzogchen-rigpa-recognition]], [[consciousness-mahamudra-luminosity]].
- **Pan-Indic — sahaja.** The spontaneous, ever-present "natural state." See [[consciousness-sahaja-natural-state]].
- **Judaism — devekut.** "Cleaving" to God; on Scholem's reading a *communion* that deliberately stops short of fusion (contested by Idel). No dedicated node yet.
- **Greek philosophy — henōsis.** Plotinus's union with the One, "the flight of the alone to the Alone" (*Enneads* VI.9). See [[henosis]]; the historical hinge of the whole Mediterranean wing.

The list already exhibits the problem it raises: these are not obviously variants of ONE thing. They divide at least three ways — **theistic love-union** preserving an I-Thou (unio mystica, devekut, theōsis), **monistic identity** where there are not two to unite (turīya, nirvikalpa samādhi), and **no-self/emptiness** recognition where neither a self nor a God is posited (rigpa, mahāmudrā, Buddhist śūnyatā). Whether these are three descriptions of one state or three different states is the crux.

## Cross-tradition significance (transmission vs convergence)

The honest picture requires splitting the family, exactly as the Atlas splits secure linguistic cognates from structural parallels elsewhere.

**A documented transmission spine runs through the Mediterranean/Near-Eastern wing.** Plotinus's *henōsis* was inherited by Christian apophatic mysticism through Pseudo-Dionysius (5th–6th c.), Eriugena, and Meister Eckhart; and it reached Islamic metaphysics through the Arabic *Theology of Aristotle*, a paraphrase of *Enneads* IV–VI that circulated under Aristotle's name. So the resemblance among Plotinian henōsis, Dionysian union, Eckhart's *Gottheit*, and Ibn ʿArabī's *waḥdat al-wujūd* is not pure coincidence — there is a **real genealogical descent** of vocabulary and metaphysics. Michael Sells (*Mystical Languages of Unsaying*, 1994) reads this wing as a single, historically connected apophatic tradition sharing a performative "language of unsaying."

**The Indic and Tibetan states are a separate lineage.** Turīya, nirvikalpa samādhi, rigpa, and mahāmudrā arose in South and Central Asian contexts with no documented textual dependence on the Mediterranean tradition. Claims of ancient India–Greece contact channeling one into the other are speculative and not established (T3). Here the parallel is genuinely **typological / convergent** — the same slot filled independently — and is therefore the stronger, if riskier, evidence for a perennialist reading, since it cannot be explained away as borrowing.

Crucially, **neither finding settles the perennialist question by itself.** Documented transmission proves shared *texts and words*, not a shared private *experience* (a tradition can inherit henōsis-language and mean something new by it). And independent convergence of vocabulary does not prove convergence of experience either, since a comparativist can always find family resemblance among descriptions that the experiencers themselves would refuse to identify.

## Disputes

**Perennialist vs constructivist — the axial dispute.** The perennialist wing (Aldous Huxley's *Perennial Philosophy*, 1945, at the popular end; W.T. Stace 1960 and Robert Forman's Pure Consciousness Event, 1990, at the analytic end) holds that a common phenomenological core underlies the tradition-specific interpretations — most defensibly in the *contentless* introvertive state, which Forman argues has no content for prior concepts to have constructed. The constructivist wing (Steven Katz 1978; Wayne Proudfoot 1985; Robert Gimello 1983 from Buddhist studies) holds that there are no pure unmediated experiences: the Christian's *unio mystica*, the Advaitin's *nirvikalpa*, and the Buddhist's *śūnyatā* are shaped all the way down by their respective conceptual and ritual frameworks, so they are constitutively different experiences, not one experience variously dressed. On this reading the convergence is an artefact of the comparative method. The Atlas sides with neither (discipline rule CON-1): the debate is thirty-plus years old and unresolved.

**Dissent from within comparison — Zaehner's tripartite typology.** R.C. Zaehner (*Mysticism Sacred and Profane*, 1957) is a distinct kind of objector: himself a comparativist, but a Catholic one who argued AGAINST Huxley that there are at least three irreducibly different mysticisms — pan-en-hen-ic (nature-mysticism), monistic (the soul's isolation, Sāṃkhya/Advaita), and theistic (loving union with a personal God) — and that flattening them into one "perennial philosophy" is a category error that privileges monism and misreads the theists. This is not the constructivist point; it is the claim that even at the level of phenomenology, "union" is not one thing.

**The traditions themselves often refuse the identity reading.** Much of the theistic wing polices exactly the fusion that perennialism attributes to it. Gregory Palamas distinguished the divine *energies* (partaken) from the divine *essence* (never partaken), so that theōsis is participation, not identity. Gershom Scholem argued that Jewish *devekut* is *communion* — cleaving to, not merging with — and deliberately avoids *unio mystica* (Moshe Idel, 1988, contests this, finding unitive strands in Kabbalah: the line is disputed even within Judaism). Meister Eckhart was condemned (bull *In agro dominico*, 1329) and al-Ḥallāj executed (922) precisely because formulas that sounded like identity-with-God exceeded their traditions' tolerance. That the mystics and their theologians fought over whether the union is fusion or communion is itself evidence against the easy assumption that they were all reporting the same state.

**A meta-level critique — the category is constructed.** Grace Jantzen (*Power, Gender and Christian Mysticism*, 1995) and others argue that "mysticism" as a cross-tradition, experience-centred, privatised category is itself a modern (largely post-Jamesian, liberal-Protestant) construction that abstracts these states out of their doctrinal, institutional, and gendered contexts. On this view the convergence hub risks reifying a comparativist's artefact. The Atlas keeps the hub while flagging the charge: the family resemblance is real data, but "unitive experience" is a lens with a history, not a neutral given.

The honest residue, mirroring the vault's treatment of contested cosmogonic parallels: the **family resemblance is genuine and, in the Mediterranean wing, partly genealogical; whether it reflects one experience, several experiences, or an artefact of the comparative gaze is not established** — and the traditions' own insistence on fine distinctions (energies not essence, communion not union, recognition not merger) is a standing reason for caution.
