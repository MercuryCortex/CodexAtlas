# Steven Katz — constructivism vs. perennialism (1978)

*The 1978 essay 'Language, epistemology, and mysticism' (in **Mysticism and Philosophical Analysis**, Katz ed.) launched the constructivist / contextualist program in mysticism studies. The single most influential critique of the Jamesian-Ottoan-Stacean perennialist lineage. For 30+ years the Katz–Forman debate has been the axial dispute in the field.*

## What Katz proposed

Katz's central thesis is **epistemic**: there are no pure, unmediated experiences. Every experience is shaped by the concepts, beliefs, expectations, language, and tradition the experiencer brings. Applied to mysticism: the Christian mystic does not have a context-free unitive experience that she then describes as "union with Christ"; rather her experience is *constitutively* shaped by the Christian tradition's vocabulary, training, prior beliefs, ritual context, scriptural grammar. A Buddhist meditator's *śūnyatā* experience is not the same as a Christian mystic's *unio mystica* with surface differences — the two experiences are *constitutively different*, because the conceptual frameworks that constitute them are different.

Three consequences follow: (1) **Stace's common-core thesis fails** — the patterns Stace finds across traditions are projections of the comparative method, not features of the underlying experiences; (2) **mysticism is plural, not one** — there are Christian mysticisms, Hindu mysticisms, Buddhist mysticisms, etc., each tradition-specific; (3) **the study of mysticism must be local and historical**, not universal and phenomenological — study how this particular tradition's training and vocabulary produce this particular experience.

## Tradition context — analytic philosophy meets religious studies

Katz works in the same analytic-philosophy idiom as Stace (the dispute is internal to that tradition), but draws on three streams Stace did not engage: (a) Kantian epistemology — the structuring activity of the knowing subject; (b) Wittgensteinian philosophy of language — meaning is use, and use is tradition-embedded; (c) the sociology of knowledge — Berger, Luckmann, the social construction of reality. The 1978 anthology gathers Katz's allies (Bruce Garside, Peter Moore, H.P. Owen, R.M. Gimello, Ninian Smart) and creates a constructivist *school*. Through the 1980s-90s constructivism becomes the academic-religious-studies majority view; Forman's 1990 *Problem of Pure Consciousness* anthology and the 1999 *Mysticism, Mind, Consciousness* are the perennialist counter-response.

## Cross-tradition resonance — by negation

Where the perennialist tradition reads across traditions looking for shared structure, Katz reads across traditions looking for *constitutive difference*. His critical-method examples: Jewish *devekut* (clinging-to-God; preserves the I-Thou structure) vs. Buddhist *anātman* (no-self; abolishes the I-Thou structure) — these cannot be variants of one experience because their target ontologies are mutually exclusive. Christian *unio mystica* (love-union with personal Christ) vs. Advaita *nirvikalpa samādhi* (recognition that ātman is brahman, no two there to unite) — same argument. The point is *not* that all traditions are right; it is that traditions are *not saying the same thing* and the perennialist conflation hides genuine theological disagreement. Per CON-1, the vault carries Katz's contextualism alongside the Stace-Forman perennialist program; the [mysticism] anchor routes to both readings.
