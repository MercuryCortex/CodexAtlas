# Glossolalia — speaking in tongues as non-ordinary vocalization

*Glossolalia (Greek *glōssa*, "tongue" + *lalia*, "speech") is fluent, non-lexical vocalization uttered in a state of felt divine passivity and understood by the speaker as prayer or praise "in the Spirit." It is the defining charismatic phenomenon of Pentecostal Christianity and the classic modern test-case for the empirical study of religiously-framed altered states. This is the **consciousness lens** on the phenomenon; the ritual/liturgical treatment lives at [[ritual-glossolalia-speaking-tongues]].*

## Identity

What the consciousness literature studies under "glossolalia" is a describable non-ordinary vocal-motor state: a speaker produces rapid, prosodically-shaped, syllabically-organised speech that carries no stable lexicon or grammar, while reporting that the utterance is given rather than composed — the Lukan formula "as the Spirit gave them utterance" (Acts 2:4). Two features make it the paradigm case for this lens. First, its **phenomenology of passivity**: practitioners describe volition receding while articulate sound continues, the same "passivity" mark William James placed among the signatures of religious experience. Second, its **empirical tractability**: unlike a private mystical union, glossolalia is audible, recordable, and reproducible on demand in many practitioners, so it has been analysed linguistically, behaviourally, and (since 2006) with functional neuroimaging in a way that most non-ordinary states cannot be.

A distinction fixed at the outset governs the whole topic: **glossolalia** (free vocalization corresponding to no natural language) versus **xenoglossy** (speaking a real, existing human language the speaker never learned). The Pentecost narrative in Acts 2 reports xenoglossy — Parthians, Medes, and Elamites each hear their own tongue — whereas the Corinthian practice Paul regulates in 1 Corinthians 14, and effectively all documented modern "tongues," are glossolalia. Controlled study has never verified a case of genuine xenoglossy; what is recorded is structured non-language (Samarin 1972).

## In the Pentecostal-charismatic tradition

The scriptural spine is twofold. **Acts 2** supplies the origin scene: at Pentecost the gathered disciples "began to speak with other tongues," heard by a polyglot Jerusalem crowd, some of whom instead mock them as drunk ("full of new wine," Acts 2:13) — the intoxication-charge Peter rebuts and the intra-textual link to the [[divine-intoxication]] register. **1 Corinthians 12-14** supplies the regulation: Paul ranks intelligible prophecy above uninterpreted tongues, requires an interpreter, caps the number of speakers, and insists on order (1 Cor 14:27-40) — so the foundational proof-text of the practice is in fact Paul *restraining* it (developed at [[ritual-glossolalia-speaking-tongues]]).

Classical Pentecostal historiography traces the modern movement to a precise origin scene, though scholars such as Allan Anderson stress its origins were in fact polycentric and near-simultaneous across several continents. On 1 January 1901 at **Charles Parham's** Bethel Bible School in Topeka, Kansas, the student Agnes Ozman spoke in tongues, and Parham codified the distinctively Pentecostal **initial-evidence doctrine**: glossolalia is the necessary "initial physical evidence" that a believer has received the baptism of the Holy Spirit. Parham's pupil **William Seymour** carried the teaching to Los Angeles, where the **Azusa Street Revival** (1906-1909; [[event-azusa-street-revival-1906]]) became the launch-point of global Pentecostalism (~600 million adherents). The mid-1960s charismatic renewal then spread tongues-practice into Catholic, Anglican, and mainline Protestant churches — mostly *without* Parham's initial-evidence requirement, a fault-line that still separates classical Pentecostals from broader charismatics.

## As a state of consciousness

Three research findings define the empirical picture.

**Linguistics (Samarin 1972).** In the largest corpus study, William Samarin showed that glossolalia is not language: it draws its phonemes overwhelmingly from the speaker's own native inventory, lacks any stable sound-to-meaning mapping, and shows no consistent syntax — yet it is not random babble either. It is *phonologically structured human vocalization believed to be language*, organised into breath-groups and sentence-like intonation contours. Crucially, Samarin found many fluent glossolalists were **not** in trance or dissociation; tongues could be initiated and stopped at will and learned by imitation, which he read as socialised verbal behaviour rather than an involuntary eruption.

**Cross-cultural trance thesis (Goodman 1972).** Felicitas Goodman recorded glossolalia across US Pentecostal congregations and Yucatec Maya groups and argued the opposite emphasis: the shared prosodic template — an onset-to-plateau-to-decay arc, rhythmic pulses, a characteristic accent pattern — is the vocal *artifact* of a cross-culturally uniform neurophysiological hyperarousal (trance) state driven by the worship setting (rhythm, expectation, group contagion). On Goodman's account the utterance is the sound of the altered state, largely independent of the speaker's language.

**Neuroimaging (Newberg et al. 2006).** A preliminary SPECT study of five practitioners found decreased regional blood flow in the **prefrontal (executive-control) cortex** during glossolalia relative to gospel singing — a physiological correlate of the reported loss of intentional control. Per the vault's CON-2 rule, this is a neural signature of the *passivity*, not evidence of a supernatural cause; the sample is tiny and the result explicitly preliminary.

The **Goodman-vs-Samarin split — trance-driven artifact vs learnable verbal behaviour — is the live dispute** of this field, and Malony & Lovekin's (1985) behavioural-science synthesis broadly vindicates Samarin on one point that matters clinically: glossolalists are not, as a group, psychologically disturbed, correcting the older hysteria/pathology framing of Cutten (1927).

## Cross-tradition significance (convergence, not transmission)

Ecstatic non-lexical vocalization recurs widely: shamanic "spirit languages" delivered in trance and interpreted on return ([[shamanism]]); vocal phenomena in Afro-diasporic and *zar* possession; the possessed prophetic speech of the Delphic Pythia and Dionysiac *mania*. The [[possession-ritual]] hub gathers this altered-state complex, and Pentecostal Spirit-baptism sits squarely inside its experiential grammar — loss of intentional control, community validation, message-delivery — differing in its Trinitarian theology (the indwelling Holy Spirit, the Third Person, not an external or ancestral spirit).

The honest framing is **convergence, not transmission**. Goodman's cross-cultural data support a *typological* claim — a common neurophysiology of hyperarousal trance can generate structurally similar vocal output in unrelated traditions — and this is the sense in which glossolalia "parallels" shamanic or possession vocalization. It is emphatically **not** a genealogical claim: Pentecostal tongues did not descend from shamanic practice; it grew from a first-century Christian text-tradition (Acts 2; 1 Cor 14) reactivated in 1901-1906. The Dionysian resonance the [[divine-intoxication]] hub carries is likewise convergence — reinforced, tellingly, by the Acts 2 intoxication-charge itself, where observers reach independently for "drunk" to describe the *entheos* state.

## Disputes

1. **Trance vs learned behaviour** — Goodman (glossolalia is the artifact of an altered state) vs Samarin (it is socialised, will-controlled verbal behaviour that often involves no trance). Both are T1; the field has not resolved it, and the truth is likely that glossolalia can occur *either* way in different practitioners and settings.
2. **Xenoglossy** — the claim of speaking real unlearned languages (Acts 2's Pentecost) is unverified under controlled observation; the modern phenomenon is glossolalia. Apologetic literature that treats the two as one phenomenon overreads the evidence.
3. **Initial-evidence doctrine** — Parham's teaching that tongues is the *necessary* sign of Spirit-baptism is a specifically classical-Pentecostal position rejected by most charismatics and by cessationist Protestants; it is a theological claim, not a finding of the consciousness literature.
4. **Neuroscience over-extrapolation** (CON-2) — the Newberg frontal-hypoactivity result is a correlate of felt passivity in n=5, not a demonstration that tongues is "just" a brain-state or, conversely, that it is divinely caused.
