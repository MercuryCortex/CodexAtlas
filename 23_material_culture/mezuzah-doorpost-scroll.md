# Mezuzah (Doorpost Scroll — Physical Object)

## Identity

The **mezuzah** (Hebrew: מְזוּזָה, "doorpost"; plural *mezuzot*) is the small parchment slip — and its case — affixed to the right doorpost of every doorway in a Jewish home, synagogue, or other Jewish-occupied space. This node treats the **physical object**: the parchment + ink + case + nail-mounting. The cross-tradition iconographic / theological symbol-treatment lives at [[mezuzah]] in `09_symbols/`; this node documents the relic-class artifact and its archaeology.

The slip is inscribed with two Pentateuchal passages — Deuteronomy 6:4-9 (the Shema) and Deuteronomy 11:13-21 (V'ahavta) — both of which contain the explicit commandment:

> "And you shall write them on the doorposts of your house and on your gates." — **Deuteronomy 6:9 + 11:20**

This direct Pentateuchal instruction — write the Shema on the doorpost — is the most literal scriptural mandate for a physical religious-object practice in the entire Hebrew Bible.

## Form + manufacture

A kosher mezuzah consists of:

**The klaf (parchment slip)** — a single small rectangle of kosher animal hide (calf typically), ~7-10 cm tall and ~10-15 cm wide. The two Pentateuchal passages are written on one side in 22 lines (the standard rabbinic ruling: 22 lines, one for each letter of the Hebrew alphabet) in classical Hebrew square script (*ketav Ashurit*) with iron-gall ink. On the reverse, in the upper-third margin, the divine name *Shaddai* (שַׁדַּי) is written, often expanded mystically into the acronym *Shomer Daltot Yisrael* ("Guardian of the Doorways of Israel"). Some Sephardi + Kabbalistic mezuzot also include angelic names + Kabbalistic name-permutations in the margins (a practice Maimonides explicitly forbade as veering toward amulet-magic, but which has persisted in many Eastern + Hasidic communities).

**The bayit (case)** — a tubular container of wood, ceramic, glass, silver, plastic, or any solid material, into which the klaf is rolled (left-to-right rolling so that *Shaddai* is reachable through a small window in the case, or visible facing outward through a transparent case) and inserted. The case is open-ended or has a small window such that the letter *Shin* (ש) on the back of the klaf can be seen by the affixer. The case length must match the klaf; standard ranges are ~8-15 cm depending on the writing-density of the klaf.

**The mounting** — the case is fixed to the *right* doorpost as one enters (left-to-right horizontal Hebrew reading-order is foundational here), in the **upper third** of the doorpost (between the ⅔ height-mark and a hand's-breadth from the top lintel). Ashkenazi practice mounts the case **diagonally**, top tilted toward the inside of the room — a 13th-c. compromise between an earlier rabbinic dispute (Rashi: vertical; Rabbeinu Tam: horizontal); the diagonal acknowledges both positions. Sephardi practice mounts strictly **vertical**. The mezuzah is fixed with nails (traditional), screws, or — in modern adhesive-mount practice — sometimes adhesive strips, though halakhic authorities prefer mechanical fasteners as more permanent.

**Which doorways require a mezuzah** — every doorway opening into a regularly-inhabited room of ~2.5 × 2.5 m or larger. Exemptions: bathrooms, storage rooms not used for habitation, ritual baths (mikvaot), and (per most authorities) garages used solely for cars. A modern Jewish home thus has 5-15 mezuzot, one per qualifying doorway. Synagogues + Jewish institutions follow the same rule; the State of Israel mandates mezuzot on all public government buildings (Knesset, Supreme Court, Prime Minister's residence, Israeli embassies, IDF installations).

## The Qumran finds (Tier 1)

Like the [[tefillin-phylacteries]], the mezuzah is documented archaeologically in the Dead Sea Scrolls corpus (Cohn 2008 — Tier 1). At least 8 mezuzot-fragments are recovered from Qumran (4QMez A-G + 8QMez) + Wadi Murabba'at, dating ~150 BCE to ~135 CE. The Qumran specimens establish:

1. **The physical form is Second-Temple-period crystallised.** The cubic-case + small parchment-slip + Deuteronomy-passages combination is not a rabbinic innovation; it is already standard in the Hasmonean / early Roman period.
2. **The passages are stable.** Almost all Qumran mezuzot contain Deuteronomy 6:4-9 + Deuteronomy 11:13-21, the same two passages as the modern klaf. A small number also include the Decalogue (Deuteronomy 5), reflecting the same pre-rabbinic broader-text tradition documented in the Qumran tefillin (and the Nash Papyrus from Hellenistic Egypt, c. 150 BCE, which contains the Decalogue + Shema together and is possibly a mezuzah- or tefillin-equivalent specimen).
3. **Continuity to the present.** The Qumran klaf-text and a klaf written by a Jerusalem sofer in 2026 differ by ~0-2 letters out of ~340 — extraordinary scribal continuity for 2,100 years.

## The Maimonides amulet-vs-commandment dispute

Mezuzot have always operated at the intersection of **commandment-fulfilment** (the Pentateuchal mandate) and **apotropaic protection** (the divine name as guardian at the threshold). Maimonides, *Mishneh Torah*, Hilchot Mezuzah 5:4, mounts a famously sharp polemic against the amulet-conception:

> "There is a widespread foolish custom of writing the names of angels, holy names, verses, and various shapes inside the mezuzah. All these people forfeit their share in the world to come. For these fools not only nullify the commandment, but they make the great commandment — which is the unity of God's name, His love, and His worship — into a charm for their own benefit, as if (God forbid) the mezuzah were something that benefits the vanities of this world."

Maimonides argues: the mezuzah is *only* a commandment-reminder; treating it as a magical guardian is theological perversion. Despite his condemnation, the popular amulet-conception has persisted continuously — the *Shaddai* name written on the back is itself an early-rabbinic-era apotropaic gesture, and the Kabbalistic and Hasidic + Mizrahi traditions enthusiastically incorporate further apotropaic name-content. The contemporary Israeli practice of touching the mezuzah on entry/exit and kissing the touching hand sits ambiguously between the two — devotional remembrance and threshold-blessing.

The contemporary Israeli practice extends the amulet-mode further: it is widespread folk practice to have a sofer "check" the mezuzot when illness or bad luck strikes the household — the implication being that an invalid mezuzah is failing as protection. Maimonides would have considered this misguided; the practice continues.

## Cross-tradition pattern: threshold-inscription

The mezuzah is the most halakhically-developed example of a near-universal pattern: **scripture or holy image placed at the door as guardian / blessing / identity-marker**. The full cross-tradition table is given at the symbol-node [[mezuzah]]; here it suffices to note the Islamic *basmala-over-the-door* practice as the closest functional parallel — both are scripture at the threshold, both function dually as commandment-fulfilment (Quran + Hadith encourage Quranic verses at home entries; Torah commands the Shema on the doorpost) and as apotropaic protection. The Sephardi + Mizrahi communities of the Islamic world historically had both — the mezuzah on the doorpost *and* an Arabic Basmala or Ayat al-Kursi text or hamsa-hand on the wall nearby. The continuous practice represents one of the longest-running shared material-religious convergences in the Mediterranean + Near East.

## Holocaust + post-Holocaust significance

After the Nazi deportations, returning survivors often found their homes occupied by others, the mezuzot removed, the small bare nail-mark on the doorpost the only trace. Survivor accounts (Grossman ed. 2011) document several distinct responses:

- Some refused to replace mezuzot in the former homes — the rupture of community made the threshold-promise unreplaceable.
- Others replaced them immediately as the first reclaiming act — Jewish life resumes at the threshold.
- Some Israeli + American synagogue + cultural-center installations preserve a bare nail-mark on a memorial doorpost as a witness.

The mezuzah thus carries a layer of post-Holocaust meaning visible in 21st-c. practice: to affix one is to perform Jewish continuity in spaces where Jewish life was destroyed.
