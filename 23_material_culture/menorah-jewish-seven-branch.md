# Menorah (Seven-Branched Jewish Lampstand)

## Identity

The **menorah** (Hebrew מְנוֹרָה, "lampstand") is the seven-branched gold lampstand of the Tabernacle + Solomon's Temple + Second Temple, the canonical Jewish cultic implement and — after the destruction of the Temple in 70 CE — the supreme Jewish identity-symbol throughout the diaspora. The Pentateuchal commands (Exodus 25:31-40 + 37:17-24) prescribe the menorah's exact construction: hammered from a single talent of pure gold, with a central shaft and six branches (three on each side), each branch terminating in a cup shaped like an almond-blossom (*kaftor v'ferach*, "knop and flower"), with seven oil-lamps mounted at the top. The implement is to be tended perpetually in the sanctuary, the priests trimming the wicks each morning (*hatavah*) and lighting them each evening (*hadlakah*).

The menorah is to be distinguished from the **Hanukkah hanukkiah** (the nine-branched festival lampstand): only the Temple menorah has seven branches, and rabbinic law (b. Menachot 28b) forbids the construction of exact seven-branched replicas out of reverence for the Temple form. The hanukkiah is the festival-permitted analog.

## History + the Arch of Titus

The Pentateuchal menorah-regulations (Priestly source, codified ~6th-5th c. BCE per critical scholarship; traditionally Mosaic) prescribe the Tabernacle implement, which is carried into Solomon's First Temple (10th c. BCE) and recreated for the Second Temple after the return from Babylonian exile (520 BCE). The Second Temple menorah survives — though stolen periodically by foreign powers (Antiochus IV ca. 169 BCE, the Hasmonean recovery, Pompey 63 BCE without removal, eventually Titus 70 CE) — until the Roman destruction.

The single most-iconic depiction of the menorah is the **Arch of Titus relief** in Rome (81 CE), commemorating Titus's triumphal procession after the destruction of Jerusalem and the Second Temple in 70 CE. The relief shows Roman legionaries carrying the actual Temple menorah (along with the Showbread Table and silver trumpets) as captured spoils. The menorah on the relief is the primary surviving iconographic record of what the Second Temple menorah looked like — though scholars dispute whether the octagonal base depicted is original or a Roman-period stylization (Hachlili 2001 argues the base may show Roman embellishment of an originally tripod-footed implement).

The actual physical menorah carried in the procession was eventually placed in the Temple of Peace in Rome and disappears from the historical record after the 5th-c. Vandal sack of Rome. Whether the menorah was carried to Carthage by the Vandals, returned to Constantinople by Belisarius, or melted down at some point remains disputed; the implement itself is lost.

## Iconography + the tree-of-life pattern

The menorah's almond-blossom motif (*kaftor v'ferach*, knops + flowers, six per branch) and the seven-fold structure place it iconographically within the broader Near Eastern **stylized-tree-of-life tradition** (Yarden 1971). Mesopotamian + Egyptian + Levantine art consistently produces seven-branched or seven-fold stylized trees in cultic-symbolic contexts; the menorah belongs to this family. Yarden argues the menorah is the **iconographic descendant of the Burning Bush** itself — both are "theophanic plants" revealing divine presence through fire-without-consumption; the menorah is the burning bush domesticated into perpetual cultic form.

Hellenistic-Jewish allegorical readings (Philo of Alexandria *De Vita Mosis* II.102-105; Josephus *Antiquities* III.146) identify the seven branches with the **seven classical planets** (Sun, Moon, Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn) — Greco-Jewish cosmological identification of cultic implements with Hellenistic astronomical-symbolic systems. Whether this is original Israelite iconography or Hellenistic-period overlay is contested.

The post-70-CE diaspora develops the menorah as the **central Jewish identity-symbol**: it appears on synagogue mosaics (Beth Alpha, Tiberias, Hammat Tiberias), late-antique gold-glass funerary objects, medieval Haggadot, and — finally — the State Emblem of Israel (adopted 1949), flanked by olive branches in citation of the Zechariah 4 vision. The menorah's transformation from Temple-implement to memorial-symbol to civic-emblem is the longest single iconographic trajectory in Jewish material culture.
