# Visconti-Sforza Tarocchi — the Earliest Surviving Tarot Cards

## What the object is

Roughly fifteen hand-painted packs and fragments, made in and around **Milan in the 1440s and 1450s** for the Visconti and Sforza ducal houses, are collectively called the **Visconti-Sforza tarocchi**. They are the earliest tarot cards that survive. Three principal sets are named after their holders: the **Pierpont Morgan-Bergamo** deck (c. 1451, the most complete at 74 of 78 cards), the **Cary-Yale / Visconti di Modrone**, and the **Brera-Brambilla**.

They are luxury objects: tempera on pasteboard, laid over gold and silver leaf, punched and tooled, hand-painted at large format — roughly 17–19 cm tall, twice the size of a modern card and impossible to shuffle comfortably. They were made to be handled at a ducal table, and they carry Visconti and Sforza heraldry throughout: the radiant sun, the ducal crown, the dove with the motto *a bon droyt*, the interlaced initials of Francesco Sforza and Bianca Maria Visconti.

They were made for a **card game**.

## What is actually on the cards

The trump sequence is Petrarchan and Christian, not Egyptian and not Kabbalistic:

- **Petrarchan triumphs.** Petrarch's *Trionfi* — Love conquered by Chastity, Chastity by Death, Death by Fame, Fame by Time, Time by Eternity — was the most reproduced allegorical sequence in 15th-century Italian visual culture. The trump order is built on the same logic: each card beats the one below it, and the sequence climbs from the Mountebank through the estates of the world, through Death, to Judgement and the World.
- **Cardinal and theological virtues.** Fortitude, Justice, Temperance appear as cards; the Cary-Yale pack adds Faith, Hope and Charity, which no later standard pack keeps.
- **The estates and their reversal.** Emperor, Empress, Pope, Popess — the temporal and spiritual powers, overturned in sequence by the Wheel of Fortune, the Hanged Man (an Italian *pittura infamante*, the standard painted punishment for traitors and debtors), Death, and the Tower.
- **Heraldry.** Ducal devices are worked into the imagery as ownership marks, exactly as they are on a commissioned book of hours.

Two 16th-century Italian essays on the meaning of the pack survive and have been edited and translated (Caldwell, Depaulis & Ponzi 2010). They read the trumps as a **hierarchy of virtues, estates and vices** — a statement about moral and social order, argued over at the table. They contain no esoteric doctrine, and they are the closest thing we have to the original users explaining themselves.

## The documentary chain around the object

The cards do not stand alone; they sit inside an unusually complete paper record, which is why the tarot's origin is one of the few in this vault that is not seriously contested.

- **1370s** — playing cards reach Europe from the **Mamluk sultanate** (Egypt and Syria) via Mediterranean trade. A near-complete Mamluk pack survives in the Topkapı Sarayı, Istanbul. Its four suits — cups, coins, swords, polo-sticks — become the Italian *coppe, denari, spade, bastoni*; the polo-stick becomes a baton because polo was unknown in Europe. First European notices cluster in 1371–1377 (a Catalan reference to *naip*; a Florentine prohibition; the Dominican Johannes von Rheinfelden's Latin treatise on card play, 1377).
- **c. 1412–1425** — **Marziano da Tortona**, secretary and astrologer to Duke Filippo Maria Visconti, designs a pack with a fifth suit of **sixteen deified classical heroes** outranking the ordinary suits, painted by Michelino da Besozzo. His treatise *Tractatus de deificatione sexdecim heroum* survives; the cards do not. This is the earliest documented pack with a permanent trump suit — the structural innovation tarot is built on.
- **1442** — the earliest secure documentary reference to ***carte da trionfi***, in the account registers of the Este court at **Ferrara**. Dummett's judgment: the trumps were probably invented about two decades earlier, in the 1420s.
- **1440s–1450s** — the Visconti-Sforza packs themselves.
- **c. 1500 onward** — the pattern standardises for cheap woodblock printing and travels; the French printers of Lyon and Marseille fix what becomes the **Tarot de Marseille** (Jean Noblet c. 1650; Nicolas Conver 1760). See [[divination-tarot-marseille-modern-occult]].
- **The game never stops.** Tarocchi and its descendants — French *tarot*, Austrian and German *Tarock*, Hungarian *tarokk* — are still played competitively today. The pack has a continuous 580-year career as a game object, and a 245-year career as a divinatory one.

## Why this object matters to the vault

It is the **physical refutation** of the tarot's origin myth. In 1781 [[antoine-court-de-gebelin]] declared the pack a surviving Egyptian priestly book (see [[event-court-de-gebelin-egyptian-tarot-claim-1781]]). The oldest cards in existence were, at that moment, sitting in Italian aristocratic collections, covered in Visconti heraldry, illustrating Petrarch.

There is no esoteric layer under the Visconti-Sforza imagery to recover, and — importantly — **nothing about that diminishes the modern practice**. What it does is fix the direction of transmission. The occult tarot did not descend from Egypt through these cards. These cards were a Milanese card game onto which, three centuries later, a French philologist projected an Egypt that never existed. See [[tarot-divination-system]].

## Disputes

- **Whether the Devil and the Tower were ever painted** for the Morgan-Bergamo pack, or were painted and lost. Genuinely open. The Three of Swords and Knight of Coins certainly existed and are simply missing.
- **The painter.** **Bonifacio Bembo** is the traditional and majority attribution for the Morgan-Bergamo deck; a second hand in some cards is variously assigned to the Zavattari workshop or to Antonio Cicognara. Not settled.
- **Where the trumps were invented.** Milan, Ferrara and Bologna all have claims; the surviving cards are Milanese but the earliest paper record is Ferrarese. Dummett leaves it open between the northern Italian courts.
- **How much allegorical depth the trumps carry.** Place (2005, 2012) reads Renaissance Neoplatonic and Christian-mystical structure into the sequence — a legitimate reading, since 15th-c. Milan was a Neoplatonic milieu, and a far weaker claim than Court de Gébelin's. Dummett (1980) and Caldwell, Depaulis & Ponzi (2010) hold to the Petrarchan-moral reading the Renaissance sources themselves give. The dispute is about depth of allegory within a Christian-humanist frame — not about Egypt, and not about divination.
