# Sajjāda (Islamic Prayer Rug)

## Identity

The **sajjāda** (Arabic سَجَّادَة, "place of prostration") is the dedicated portable textile used by Muslims as a ritually-pure prayer-surface during the five daily prayers (*ṣalāh*). The rug demarcates a personal prayer-space — typically a rectangle approximately 70 cm × 110 cm, sized for one worshipper to stand, bow, and prostrate without leaving the rug. It is the canonical paired implement of individual and traveling Islamic devotional practice, complementing the [[tasbih-islamic-prayer-beads|tasbīḥ]] which provides the counting-iterational infrastructure for the supplementary *dhikr*.

The prayer rug is iconographically defined by its woven **mihrab-niche representation**: a stylized arched-niche design (echoing the architectural [[mihrab-mosque-qibla-niche|miḥrāb]] of mosque qibla-walls) is woven into virtually every prayer-rug produced from the 15th c. onward. The worshipper orients the niche-tip (the pointed arch end) toward Mecca; prostration places the forehead on the niche-floor of the woven mihrab. The rug is therefore the **portable miḥrāb** — a private fragment of mosque architecture carried wherever the worshipper goes.

## History + regional traditions

The hadith corpus describes the Prophet praying on a variety of flat surfaces — directly on the ground, on a date-palm-leaf mat (*khumra*), on a sheepskin (appropriately tanned). The dedicated prayer-rug as a distinct textile form emerges with the development of Islamic high-textile-craft traditions in the 8th-9th c., though the earliest surviving prayer-rugs date from the 15th-16th c. (organic textile decay precludes earlier physical survival).

By 1500 the regional Islamic prayer-rug traditions are mature, each immediately identifiable to textile-historians by knot-density, color-palette, motif vocabulary, and mihrab-niche style:

- **Anatolian** (Ladik, Ghiordes, Konya, Bergama, Mucur) — long-pile, characteristic Turkish prayer-niche styles, often with floral fillers
- **Persian** (Tabriz, Kashan, Qom, Isfahan) — finer knot-density, more elaborate mihrab-borders, often featuring the hanging-lamp-of-the-mihrab motif from Qurʾān 24:35 ("the Light Verse")
- **Caucasian** (Daghestan, Shirvan, Kazak) — geometric tribal styles, often with abstract-stylized prayer-niches
- **Central Asian** (Turkmen, Uzbek, Tajik) — distinctive *salor* + *tekke* tribal gül motifs
- **Indian Mughal** — elaborate floral-arabesque borders, naturalistic-floral fillers under court patronage
- **North African** (Berber, Moroccan) — characteristic long-pile Berber knotting + abstract geometric mihrabs
- **West African (Hausa, Fulani)** + **Indonesian** + **Malaysian** — leather, woven palm-fiber, batik-cloth regional variants

## Function + iconographic logic

The prayer rug's central function is **demarcating ritually-pure prayer-space**. Salāh requires the worshipper to perform prostration (sujūd) with seven body-parts touching the ground (forehead + nose + both hands + both knees + the inner toes of both feet); the surface for prostration must be ritually clean (*ṭāhir*) and ideally elevated above potential impurities. The prayer-rug provides this in portable form: unrolled, it becomes a guaranteed-clean prayer-space; rolled, it travels with the worshipper.

The **mihrab-niche iconography** materializes the prayer-direction (qibla) information textile-locally. The woven mihrab's pointed-arch end indicates the direction of prayer; the worshipper aligns the rug so the niche-tip points toward Mecca; the entire physical-geographic-directional logic of Islamic prayer is encoded in the textile. The hanging-lamp motif (Persian + Mughal traditions especially) cites the Qurʾanic Light Verse (24:35) describing Allah as "the Light of the heavens and the earth ... His light is like a niche within which is a lamp...". The lamp-and-niche iconography is therefore a compact theological symbol of divine illumination within the worshipper's prostration-space.

## Cross-tradition: portable ritually-pure surfaces

The Islamic sajjāda sits within a small cross-tradition family of dedicated portable ritually-pure ground-surfaces used for sacred action:

- **Islamic sajjāda** — woven prayer-rug for salāh
- **Vedic-Hindu *darbha*** — kuśa grass mat for sacrificial officiants + ritual implements
- **Buddhist *zafu* + *zabuton*** — Zen meditation cushion + supporting mat
- **Christian altar-cloth + *corporal*** — linen cloth on which the Eucharistic elements rest
- **Jewish *tallit* spread on the ground** — partial parallel for individual prayer-space demarcation

The cross-tradition pattern: ritual action requires demarcated-pure ground; the textile-implement materializes the demarcation. The convergent invention reflects the universal cognitive logic of ritual-space-marking; no direct contact-transmission required.
