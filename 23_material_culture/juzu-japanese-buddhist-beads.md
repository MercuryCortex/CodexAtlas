# Juzu / Nenju (Japanese Buddhist Prayer Beads)

## Identity

The **juzu** (Japanese 数珠, "counting beads"; also *nenju* 念珠, "thought beads"; honorific *ojuzu* お数珠) is the Japanese Buddhist prayer-bead implement, a closed loop of 108 main beads with a marker head-bead (the *oyadama* 親玉, "parent bead") and a single tassel hanging from the head-bead representing the practitioner's lineage transmission. It is the canonical hand-implement of every major Japanese Buddhist school: Tendai, Shingon, Jōdo Shū, Jōdo Shinshū, Sōtō Zen, Rinzai Zen, and Nichiren. The juzu is so ubiquitous in Japanese religious life that owning one — and the family ojuzu inherited from a grandparent — is normative even for nominally-secular Japanese, who carry it at funerals and ancestral memorial services regardless of personal religious commitment.

The implement is held in the hand during recitation of the school-specific formula: the Tendai *Tendai-bukkyō*, the Shingon *mantra*, the Pure Land *nenbutsu* (*Namu Amida Butsu*), the Zen counted-breath *zazen*, the Nichiren *daimoku* (*Namu Myōhō Renge Kyō*). The 108 main beads are worked one at a time, with the *oyadama* marking the start-and-end of each cycle.

## History + sect-specific variants

The juzu enters Japan with the formal transmission of Buddhism from Baekje Korea in 552 CE (Nihon Shoki record), as part of the broader Indic → Chinese → Korean → Japanese Mahāyāna transmission chain. The 108-bead form is documented as standard in early Heian Shingon + Tendai esoteric ritual following Kūkai (空海, 774-835) and Saichō (最澄, 767-822). Sect-specific variants crystallize by the Kamakura period:

- **Tendai juzu** — standard 108 main beads + 4 quadrant-markers + 2 small tasseled threads representing the 108-bead-into-2-of-54 reading
- **Shingon juzu** — most elaborate: 108 main + 4 markers (Four Heavenly Kings) + 2 *oyadama* + 20 counters + 10 *kanjizai* representing Avalokiteśvara
- **Jōdo Shū juzu** (Pure Land) — 108 main + dual-loop counter system + single large tassel; designed for *nenbutsu*-count
- **Jōdo Shinshū juzu** (True Pure Land) — simplest single-loop form (often 27 beads); per Shinran's teaching that count-aspiration is itself a form of self-power and that pure faith does not require enumeration
- **Sōtō + Rinzai Zen juzu** — 108 or 18 or 21 beads; carried but used minimally in active practice; iconographic marker of monastic status
- **Nichiren juzu** — most distinctive: 108 main beads in a single loop but with a secondary smaller loop of counter-beads + tasseled threads in specific configuration; instantly identifiable as Nichiren-school

The sect-specific variants are documented in Tanabe 1989 — the standard scholarly catalogue.

## Cross-tradition: the Buddhist transmission chain (MASSIVE-WIN)

The Japanese juzu sits at the eastern terminus of one of the cleanest religious-implement transmission chains in world religion: **Indic japa-mala → Chinese fózhū (佛珠) → Korean yeomju (염주) → Japanese juzu / nenju (念珠)**.

The 108-bead standard, the head-bead (*sumeru* in Indic → *oyadama* in Japanese), the material-symbolism (bodhi-seed = Buddha, sandalwood = general practice, crystal = peaceful practice), the held-in-right-hand worked-with-thumb-and-middle-finger technique, and the close-the-loop-at-head-bead-without-crossing-over rule — all preserve unbroken across two thousand years and three intervening cultures. The Japanese sect-specific variants are all elaborations on, not departures from, the inherited Indic structural form.

This is the second-most-elaborated descendant of the Indic japa-mala, after the Tibetan trengwa. The two together demonstrate the structural durability of the bead-counter form across radically different Buddhist sub-traditions (esoteric Vajrayāna in Tibet; Mahāyāna sutra-based traditions in Japan).

The parallel Indo-Buddhist → Islamic-Sufi → Christian-rosary chain (see [[mala-prayer-beads-indic]] + [[tasbih-islamic-prayer-beads]]) is the westward divergence of the same Indic ancestor.
