# Icon (Orthodox Sacred Image)

## Identity

The **icon** (Greek εἰκών *eikōn*, "image"; Russian икона *ikona*) is the canonical sacred image of the Eastern Orthodox + Oriental Orthodox Christian traditions — a flat painted board depicting Christ, the Theotokos (Mary), saints, angels, biblical scenes, or feast-day commemorations, painted according to strict iconographic-canonical rules that distinguish it categorically from naturalistic Western religious art. The icon is theologically a **window onto the prototype**: veneration directed at the icon refers to the holy person depicted, not to the wood-and-paint object. This veneration (*proskynesis*) is distinguished from worship (*latreia*) reserved to God alone — the distinction canonized at Nicaea II (787 CE) that resolves the Iconoclastic controversy.

The standard iconographic types: **Christ Pantokrator** (Christ Almighty, half-figure with right hand blessing + left holding the Gospel), **Theotokos Hodegetria** ("She who shows the Way" — Mary indicating Christ), **Theotokos Eleousa** (Mary embracing the Child), **Deēsis** (Christ enthroned flanked by Mary + John the Baptist), the **Hospitality of Abraham / Holy Trinity** (Rublev's three-angels icon), **the Twelve Feasts**, and saintly portraits.

## History + canonization through Iconoclasm

Early Christian sacred images are attested from the 2nd-3rd c. (Dura-Europos house-church 240s, Roman catacomb paintings) but the icon as a distinct theological category crystallizes only through the Iconoclastic controversy of 726-843:

- **726-787 First Iconoclasm** — Emperor Leo III the Isaurian orders the destruction of icons (likely influenced by Islamic aniconism in the wake of the Arab conquests); his son Constantine V intensifies the policy
- **787 Council of Nicaea II** — Empress Irene convenes the council that restores icon-veneration; legislates the *proskynesis*-vs-*latreia* distinction
- **815-843 Second Iconoclasm** — Emperor Leo V renews iconoclastic policy
- **843 Triumph of Orthodoxy** — Empress Theodora permanently restores icon-veneration; the event is commemorated annually on the First Sunday of Lent across Orthodoxy

The theological argument is articulated by **John of Damascus** (*Three Treatises on the Divine Images*, ~726-730) and **Theodore the Studite** (*Antirrhetics*, ~815). Key argument: the Incarnation itself authorizes depiction. God-made-flesh has assumed visible form; refusing to depict Christ implies a hidden Docetism. The Old Testament prohibition (Exod 20:4) is conditional on the invisibility of God, which has been overcome by the Incarnation.

## Theological grammar

The Nicaea II formulation establishes the controlling distinctions:

- **Proskynesis** (προσκύνησις, "veneration / honoring") — the relative reverence directed through the icon to the holy person depicted; structurally identical to honoring a relative or emperor's portrait
- **Latreia** (λατρεία, "worship / adoration") — the absolute worship due to God alone; categorically distinct from proskynesis

The icon is theologically a **point of contact**: when one venerates the icon of Christ, the veneration passes through the icon to Christ Himself. The icon is not magical, not auto-efficacious; its function depends on the prototype it represents. But the icon is also not arbitrary — the iconographic canon (typology, color, posture, halo, inscription) is binding: only canonical iconography succeeds in making the icon a true window.

The Orthodox iconographic canon imposes **reverse perspective** (the icon expands outward toward the viewer rather than receding into illusionistic depth), **gold-leaf background** (divine uncreated light, not naturalistic atmosphere), **flat planar treatment** (the icon is not a window onto a physical space but a window onto the heavenly realm), and **strict typological adherence** (an icon of Christ depicts Him only as the canonical traditions permit; innovations are heretical).

## Cross-tradition: image-theology divergences

The Orthodox icon tradition develops in **conscious tension** with three other major image-theology positions in Eurasia:

- **Islamic aniconism** — the broader image-prohibition of Sunni + Shi'a Islam (especially of God and the Prophets) is the direct contemporary context of the Iconoclastic controversy. Pelikan 1990 documents Islamic influence as a real factor in the Iconoclast emperors' position
- **Western Catholic image-tradition** — develops naturalistically after the Carolingian period (Charlemagne's *Libri Carolini* takes a Western-iconodule but Greek-suspicious position); diverges sharply after 1054 East-West schism + 13th-c. Italian-Gothic shift toward emotionally-naturalistic depiction (Belting 1994). The Western [crucifix-catholic] tradition is the iconographic divergence
- **Reformation iconoclasm** — 16th-c. Calvinist + Zwinglian + Anabaptist destruction of sacred images repeats the Byzantine Iconoclast position with patristic-citation continuity; the Reformed traditions retain Iconoclast image-theology to the present
- **Hindu mūrti / Buddhist iconographic traditions** — pace Islamic + Reformed aniconism, both East Asian + South Asian religious traditions develop elaborate sacred-image programs; the cross-cultural comparison is the [theme-aniconism-iconoclasm-comparative] field

The icon is one of the principal sites where Orthodox + Catholic + Protestant + Islamic image-theologies diverge — the implement materializes the doctrinal split.
