# Kippah / Yarmulke (Jewish Head-Covering)

## Identity

The **kippah** (Hebrew כִּפָּה, "dome, cap"; Yiddish *yarmulke*, etymology disputed but conventionally derived from Aramaic *yarei mei-Elohim*, "one who fears God") is the small skullcap worn by observant Jewish males (and in egalitarian Reform + Reconstructionist + Conservative + Modern Orthodox contexts by all adult Jews) covering the crown of the head. Standard halakhic practice (Karo, *Shulchan Aruch* Orach Chaim 2:6 + 91:3) requires the head to be covered during prayer + Torah study; universal observant practice extends this to all waking hours.

The kippah is the floor-form of the Jewish prayer-attire ensemble: more universally worn than the tallit (prayer-shawl, donned only at Shacharit + Mussaf) or tefillin (donned only at weekday Shacharit). It is the most-immediate visual identifier of an observant Jewish male in public space.

## History + denominational variants

The earliest specific Talmudic references to head-covering practice appear in the Babylonian Talmud — Shabbat 156b (the story of Rav Huna's son's constant head-covering) and Kiddushin 31a (Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi's never walking four cubits bareheaded). These passages establish the theological + behavioral basis: the Shekhinah (divine presence) rests above the worshipper, and head-covering acknowledges the presence. The practice solidifies as binding custom in the geonic period (8th-9th c.) and is codified in Maimonides (*Mishneh Torah*, Hilchot Tefillah 5:5, 12th c.) and Karo (*Shulchan Aruch*, 16th c.). Universal Orthodox practice from the medieval period to the present.

Denominational variants in the modern + contemporary Jewish world signal identity:

- **Black velvet kippah** — Haredi + Yeshivish + Hasidic standard
- **Knitted (*kippah serugah*)** — Religious Zionist + Modern Orthodox standard (especially in Israel)
- **Bukharian kippah** — large embroidered kippah of Central Asian Jewish origin; popular across observance levels
- **Satin kippah** — typical for guest distribution at lifecycle events (Bar/Bat Mitzvah, wedding)
- **Suede / cloth kippah** — Reform + Conservative + non-observant Jewish occasional wear
- **Lubavitch black fedora** — Hasidic outer-hat worn over a black velvet kippah

## Theological rationale

The Talmudic + medieval rabbinic rationale for head-covering centers on **Shekhinah-awareness**: the divine presence is conceived as resting above the worshipper's head, and the head-covering acknowledges that presence by physical interposition. Rabbinic anthropology treats the crown of the head as the most-vulnerable site of divine encounter (the *fontanelle* zone where infants are born with a soft spot; the *keter* — "crown" — in later Kabbalistic Sefirotic anatomy is the highest sefirah). Covering this site is a posture of reverence + acknowledgment.

The kippah is also a **declaration of identity** in the public sphere. A Jewish man wearing a kippah in non-Jewish public space announces religious identity; the practice has accordingly become politically + culturally fraught at various historical moments (medieval European mandatory yellow-hat decrees; modern French *laïcité* debates; contemporary antisemitism-driven safety concerns about wearing identifiable Jewish attire in public spaces).
