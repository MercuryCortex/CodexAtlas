# Tefillin (Phylacteries)

## Identity

**Tefillin** (Hebrew: תְּפִילִּין, "prayer-objects"; Greek-via-Christian-sources: *phylacteries*, "guards / amulets" — Matthew 23:5) are two small cubic black leather cases, each containing parchment slips inscribed with four passages from the Pentateuch, worn during weekday morning prayer (Shacharit) by adult Jewish males in observant Judaism (and in egalitarian movements by all adult Jews of any gender). One case (*tefillin shel yad*, "of the arm") is bound to the upper inner left arm and wrapped seven times down the forearm; the other (*tefillin shel rosh*, "of the head") is centered on the forehead just above the hairline and tied at the back of the skull.

The four passages — Exodus 13:1-10, Exodus 13:11-16, Deuteronomy 6:4-9 (the Shema), and Deuteronomy 11:13-21 (V'ahavta) — share a common Pentateuchal command:

> "You shall bind them as a sign upon your hand, and they shall be as *totafot* between your eyes." — **Deuteronomy 6:8; Exodus 13:9, 13:16; Deuteronomy 11:18**

The rabbinic interpretation of this fourfold-repeated command literalises the binding: the passages themselves are written on parchment and physically bound to arm and head. The tefillin are therefore the most literal possible enactment of a Pentateuchal commandment — the text of the commandment is the object that fulfils the commandment.

## Form + manufacture

A complete pair of tefillin:

**The cases (*batim*, "houses")** — black-painted cubic boxes of a single piece of hide pulled into shape over a wooden mold while wet (the *batim* of a kosher pair are still made this way, though the technique is exacting and few scribes can produce the highest-grade *batim mehudarim*). The *shel rosh* (head) case is divided internally into four chambers, each holding one of the four passages written on a separate slip; the *shel yad* (arm) case is a single chamber, with all four passages written on a single longer slip. Each case sits on a square leather base (*ma'avarta*) through which the binding strap (*retzu'ah*) passes.

**The parchment slips (*parshiyot*)** — written by a trained scribe (*sofer STaM*, where STaM = Sifrei Torah, Tefillin, Mezuzot, the three classes of holy parchment-text he is qualified to write) in classical Hebrew square script (*ketav Ashurit*) with iron-gall ink on kosher animal skin (typically calf or deer). The script is the same as used for Torah scrolls and mezuzot, with the same scribal-law constraints: no erasures, no skipped letters, every letter must be surrounded by white space, the divine name is written with special concentration.

**The straps (*retzu'ot*)** — long thin black-painted leather thongs, sourced from the same kosher animal as the cases. The *shel rosh* strap is tied at the back of the head in a knot resembling the Hebrew letter *Dalet* (ד); the *shel yad* strap is wrapped seven times around the forearm, then around the hand in a configuration that traces the letter *Shin* (ש) on the back of the hand and ending in the letter *Yod* (י) at the base of the middle finger. The three letters spell *Sh-D-Y* — *Shaddai* (שַׁדַּי, "Almighty") — the same divine name that appears on the back of the [[mezuzah]] case. The body, when wearing tefillin, is itself inscribed with the divine name.

## The Qumran finds (Tier 1 — Yadin 1969; Cohn 2008)

The single most important archaeological discovery about tefillin is the Qumran corpus. Thirty-plus tefillin and tefillin-fragments were recovered from Qumran Cave 4 (4QPhyl A-U, ~25 specimens), Cave X (X Q Phyl 1-4, 4 nearly-complete cases), and from Wadi Murabba'at and Naḥal Ḥever (Bar Kokhba period, 132-135 CE). Dating: ~150 BCE to ~135 CE.

The Qumran specimens establish three key historical facts:

1. **The practice predates the rabbinic period.** The tefillin practice is fully formed by the Hasmonean / early Roman period (2nd c. BCE). The Letter of Aristeas §159 (c. 200 BCE) refers to a parallel practice. The rabbinic literature of 200-500 CE is not the *origin* of tefillin but its codification.

2. **The pre-rabbinic practice was more textually expansive.** Several Qumran specimens include the *Decalogue* (Exodus 20 / Deuteronomy 5) in addition to (or instead of) some of the standard four passages. This matches the report in Talmud Bavli Berakhot 12a that the Decalogue was once recited daily before the Shema until its inclusion was discontinued "because of the heretics' grievance" (*minim*, plausibly early Christians or sectarians claiming the Decalogue alone was the binding law). The Qumran tefillin are thus a snapshot of the practice before this halakhic narrowing.

3. **The case-and-strap form was already standard.** The X Q Phyl cases recovered by Yadin are recognizably the same cubic black leather form used today, with the same binding-strap apparatus. The two-millennium continuity of the physical object is documented.

## The four passages

| # | Reference | Content | Position |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Exodus 13:1-10 | Consecration of firstborn; Passover mnemonic ("as a sign upon your hand") | First chamber, shel rosh; first portion, shel yad |
| 2 | Exodus 13:11-16 | Redemption of firstborn; binding command repeated | Second chamber, shel rosh |
| 3 | Deuteronomy 6:4-9 | The Shema; "Hear, O Israel" + binding command + doorpost command | Third chamber, shel rosh |
| 4 | Deuteronomy 11:13-21 | V'ahavta; if you keep the commandments, rain in season | Fourth chamber, shel rosh |

The order matters. The standard Rashi order — Exodus 13:1-10 / Exodus 13:11-16 / Deuteronomy 6:4-9 / Deuteronomy 11:13-21 — is the order used in mainstream Ashkenazi, Sephardi, and Yemenite practice. The alternative Rabbeinu Tam order swaps the third and fourth passages (placing Deuteronomy 11 third and Deuteronomy 6 fourth). The dispute is recorded in Tosafot to Menachot 34b; some pious Jews (particularly in Hasidic and Mizrahi circles) wear both pairs sequentially during morning prayer — Rashi-tefillin first for Shacharit, then Rabbeinu Tam-tefillin briefly after, to fulfil both halakhic opinions. The Qumran finds include specimens compatible with both orderings, suggesting the dispute may itself crystallize a pre-rabbinic variation.

## Order of donning

The standard rabbinic order:

1. The *shel yad* is donned first — the box is placed on the upper inner left arm directly opposite the heart (left-handed wearers reverse), the blessing *l'haniach tefillin* ("to put on tefillin") is recited, and the strap is wound seven times down the forearm.
2. The *shel rosh* is donned second — centered just above the hairline, the back-knot resting at the occiput. The second blessing *al mitzvat tefillin* ("on the commandment of tefillin") is recited.
3. The strap of the *shel yad* is then wound around the hand and middle finger in the *Shin-Dalet-Yod = Shaddai* configuration. Three verses from Hosea 2:21-22 are recited while binding: *"And I will betroth you to me forever..."* — the tefillin-binding is theologized as a marriage-covenant gesture, the hand bound with the name of the Beloved.

Tefillin are not worn on Shabbat or major festivals — the rabbinic principle being that the day itself is the "sign" (*ot*) and an additional "sign" (tefillin) is redundant. They are worn only on weekday mornings (Sephardi practice) or weekday mornings + Tisha B'Av afternoon (Ashkenazi).

## Cross-tradition pattern: scripture-on-the-body

The tefillin sit at one end of a small cross-tradition cluster of practices that **write scripture directly onto the body**:

- **Jewish tefillin** — the four Pentateuchal passages bound physically to arm and head; the body literally carries the text of the commandment.
- **Christian Coptic + Ethiopian tattoo crosses** — many Coptic and Ethiopian Christians have small cross tattoos on the wrist, indicating Christian identity even under historical pressure; in some Coptic communities the practice extends to inscribed prayers.
- **Islamic *muska* / *taʿwīdh* amulets** — small leather or cloth pouches containing Quranic verses (commonly Ayat al-Kursi, al-Fatiha, or the *muʿawwidhatān*) worn around the neck or arm; widespread in Sufi and folk-Islamic practice across South Asia, North Africa, and West Africa.
- **Hindu + Sikh *kavach* / *taveez*** — protective metal cylinders containing inscribed mantras or scriptural verses, worn on the upper arm or neck.
- **Buddhist *gau* (Tibetan)** — small reliquary lockets worn around the neck containing dharanis (Sanskrit + Tibetan magical-scriptural formulas) or a relic.
- **Buddhist *yantra* tattoos (Thai *sak yant*)** — sacred geometric + scriptural Pali tattoos applied by ordained monks, a continuing tradition descending from medieval Khmer + Mon Buddhist amuletry.

The pattern: **the body is a site of scripture-bearing**. The scripture is not merely read aloud or kept on a shelf; it is bound to the wearer, often at a vulnerable or symbolic point (heart, forehead, upper arm). The function is variously: covenant-reminder (tefillin's Pentateuchal theology), apotropaic protection (Islamic + Hindu amulet practice), identity-marker (Coptic tattoo), or active power (Buddhist dharani-pouches). The tefillin are the most textually-maximal version: not an abbreviated invocation but the full Pentateuchal commandment-text, written to Torah-scroll scribal standards on kosher parchment, in a wooden-formed cubic case.

The Christian *phylacterion* terminology (Matthew 23:5: *πλατύνουσιν τὰ φυλακτήρια*, "they enlarge their phylacteries") shows that by the 1st c. CE Greek-speaking observers were already classifying the Jewish practice as a form of *phylakterion* (Greek for "amulet / guard"). The Greek term thus retrojects the body-bound-scripture practice into the broader Mediterranean amulet category — even as Jewish halakhic theology insists tefillin are *not* amulets but commandment-fulfilments.

## Cross-references

- **Pair object**: the [[mezuzah]] (doorpost-scroll) is the apartment-threshold version of the same Pentateuchal command; the tefillin is the body-threshold version. The Talmud Bavli Menachot 36b explicitly pairs them — *"Anyone who has tefillin on his head and tefillin on his arm and tzitzit on his garment and a mezuzah on his door, is presumed not to sin..."*. The three commandment-objects together form the **Jewish enveloping practice**: the body (tefillin + tallit) and the home (mezuzah) are both inscribed with the text of the covenant.
