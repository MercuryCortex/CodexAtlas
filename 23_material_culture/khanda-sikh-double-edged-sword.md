# Khaṇḍā (Sikh Double-Edged Sword + Khalsa Emblem)

## Identity

The **khaṇḍā** (Punjabi ਖੰਡਾ) is the double-edged broadsword that serves as the central ritual + emblematic implement of Sikhism, particularly the Khalsa (the body of initiated Sikhs instituted by Guru Gobind Singh in 1699). The khaṇḍā operates simultaneously at three levels: (a) as a **ritual instrument** in the Amrit Sanchar (the Khalsa initiation ceremony), where it stirs the sacred sugar-water (*amrit*) administered to the initiate; (b) as the **central element of the composite Khalsa emblem** — central khaṇḍā + circular *chakkar* (war-discus) + two flanking single-edged *kirpāns* (curved swords) — which is the universal Sikh visual identifier displayed on the Nishan Sahib flag flown at every gurdwara worldwide and on Khalsa-initiate pendants, regalia, and cultural insignia; and (c) as a **theological symbol** — the "Sword of God" (*khaṛag*) celebrated in the Dasam Granth as a form of divine power.

The khaṇḍā is distinguished from the **kirpān** (one of the Five Ks of Khalsa identity; a smaller single-edged ritual sword carried daily by every initiated Sikh). The two together materialize the Sikh **sant-sipāhī** ("saint-soldier") doctrine: the kirpān is the carried daily form; the khaṇḍā is the ritual + emblematic form.

## History — Vaisakhi 1699 + the institution of the Khalsa

The khaṇḍā as a weapon-form predates Sikhism (the double-edged sword is a long-standing medieval Indian weapon-type). Its **ritual canonization** comes at a specific historical moment: **Vaisakhi 1699 at Anandpur Sahib**, when Guru Gobind Singh (the tenth and final living Guru) institutes the Khalsa. The historical-traditional account: Guru Gobind Singh demands five volunteers prepared to give their lives for their faith; five step forward (the *Pañj Pyare*, "Five Beloved Ones"); the Guru takes each into a tent and emerges with a bloody sword (later disclosed as goat-blood, but the dramatic test established commitment); finally the five are administered the first Amrit Sanchar.

In this ceremony, the *amrit* (sacred sugar-water) is placed in an iron bowl; the Pañj Pyare stir the amrit with the khaṇḍā while reciting the foundational Sikh prayers (Japji Sahib + Jaap Sahib + Tav-Prasad Savaiye + Chaupai Sahib + Anand Sahib); each candidate then drinks the amrit and is sprinkled with it on eyes + hair. The candidate's name is changed (males adding *Singh*, "lion"; females adding *Kaur*, "princess") and they are bound to the Five Ks: *kesh* (uncut hair), *kanga* (comb), *kara* (steel bracelet), *kachera* (cotton undergarment), and *kirpān* (small ritual sword).

The khaṇḍā-stirred amrit is the ritual element that transforms a Sikh into a Khalsa-initiate. The implement is therefore the **single most-central ritual object in Sikh practice** — present at the foundational moment of every Khalsa-initiate's religious identity.

## The composite Khalsa khanda emblem

The composite Khalsa khanda emblem — central double-edged khaṇḍā + circular chakkar + two flanking single-edged kirpāns — crystallizes in the 18th c. as the universal Sikh visual identifier. The emblem encodes the central Khalsa theological + ethical commitments:

- **Central khaṇḍā** — the double-edged sword cutting truth from falsehood; the divine power (*Akāl Purakh*, "Timeless Being") that destroys ignorance
- **Chakkar (circular war-discus)** — the eternal one-ness of God (matching the Sikh creed Ik Onkar, "One God"); also the wheel-of-dharma in martial form
- **Two flanking kirpāns** — the doctrine of *mīrī-pīrī* — the integration of spiritual sovereignty (*pīrī*, derived from Sufi tradition) and temporal sovereignty (*mīrī*); the two swords represent the two integrated powers articulated by Guru Hargobind (sixth Guru, 1606-1644)

The emblem flies on the Nishan Sahib (triangular saffron-orange flag) at every gurdwara worldwide; appears on Khalsa-initiate pendants, regalia, and Sikh civic + cultural identifiers globally.
