# Miḥrāb (Mosque Qibla Niche)

## Identity

The **miḥrāb** (Arabic مِحْراب) is the concave niche in the qibla-wall of a mosque, indicating the precise direction of prayer toward the Kaaba in Mecca. It is the **single most-defining architectural element of mosque design**, present in every functioning mosque worldwide from the 8th c. CE to the present. The imām stands in front of the miḥrāb during congregational prayer (salāh), with the worshippers aligned in rows behind facing the same direction; the niche's concave geometry amplifies the imām's voice acoustically and provides the visual focal point for the assembled congregation.

The miḥrāb is to be distinguished from the adjacent **minbar** (the pulpit-staircase from which the Friday sermon is delivered) and the **dikkah** (the platform from which the *muezzin* repeats prayers for the congregation to hear). The three together (miḥrāb + minbar + dikkah) form the basic interior chamber-furniture of a Friday-prayer-equipped mosque; smaller daily-prayer mosques may have only the miḥrāb.

## History — the al-Walīd I innovation

The concave miḥrāb (technically the *miḥrāb mujawwaf*, "vaulted niche") is an early Umayyad architectural innovation, **not** a feature of the Prophet's original mosque-form. The Prophet's first Medina mosque (622-632 CE) used only a flat marked qibla-orientation: initially toward Jerusalem (pre-2 AH), then — following the Qurʾanic command (2:144) shifting the qibla to Mecca in 624 CE — a flat stone or line on the wall facing Mecca. The Prophet himself stood at a specific spot facing the qibla to lead prayers; this spot became the proto-miḥrāb-site after his death.

The first **concave** miḥrāb (the form universally adopted thereafter) was installed by the Umayyad caliph **al-Walīd I** in the Prophet's Mosque at Medina during his great rebuilding program of 706-710 CE (Whelan 1986). The renovation marked the Prophet's standing-spot with a niche in the qibla-wall, and the concave form rapidly diffused across the Islamic world — present in the Great Mosque of Damascus (706-715), the Mosque of Amr in Fustat, the Aqsa Mosque (rebuilt under al-Walīd), and from there to every regional Islamic architectural tradition.

By the 9th c. the miḥrāb is universal; by the 11th c. the elaborate decoration program (calligraphic Qurʾanic verses around the niche, geometric and arabesque ornament, sometimes a hanging lamp suspended within the niche following Q 24:35 "the Light Verse") becomes characteristic of regional Islamic architectural traditions — the Cordobaan, Fatimid, Seljuk, Mamluk, Persian-Safavid, Ottoman, Mughal, West African Sahel, and Indonesian Nusantara variants are all immediately recognizable to specialists by their miḥrāb-decoration styles.

## The qibla-direction system

Every miḥrāb in every mosque worldwide is oriented toward the **Kaaba** in Mecca — the **qibla**, the direction of prayer. This is **literal**, not symbolic: a mosque's miḥrāb-direction is determined by precise geographic-geodesic calculation of the great-circle direction from the mosque-site to Mecca's coordinates (21.4225° N, 39.8262° E). Medieval Islamic mathematicians + astronomers developed sophisticated methods for qibla-determination — most famously al-Bīrūnī's 11th-c. spherical-trigonometric solutions — and contemporary mosque-builders use GPS-precision qibla-finding tools. The miḥrāb is therefore the architectural materialization of the entire global Islamic prayer-orientation system: from every mosque on every continent, the miḥrāb is the visual confirmation of the universal qibla.

The qibla-shift itself (from Jerusalem to Mecca, 624 CE) is one of the foundational events in Islamic identity-formation, recorded in Qurʾān 2:142-150 — and the miḥrāb is the architectural memorial of the qibla command in every prayer-space worldwide.
