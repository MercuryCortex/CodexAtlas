The Persian-Zoroastrian tradition encompasses the religious history of Zoroastrianism in its Iranian homeland — from the proto-Zoroastrian period through the Achaemenid Empire (550–330 BCE), Parthian period, Sasanian revival (224–651 CE), and into the post-Islamic minority community.

## The Avestan Legacy

The tradition's sacred language is Old Avestan (linguistically related to Vedic Sanskrit). The Avestan script was created specifically c. 400–600 CE during the Sasanian period to preserve the phonological form of the liturgical texts. See [[alphabet-avestan]] for the full account of why this script has the largest phonemic inventory of any traditional alphabet, and [[avesta]] for the text itself.

## Sasanian Zoroastrianism

The Sasanian Empire (224–651 CE) saw Zoroastrianism function as the official state religion of Persia. The Avesta was canonized, the Avestan script was created, and the Mobed priestly class was institutionally organized. The Arab conquest (651 CE) ended Sasanian rule but the Zoroastrian community survived — in Iran as a minority, and through emigration to India where they became the Parsi community.

## Relationship to Brahmi Origins

The Achaemenid Persian empire controlled northwest India (Gandhara, Taxila) from 519–327 BCE, introducing Aramaic as the administrative language. This is the period during which the Brahmi script may have developed — Aramaic was the Achaemenid imperial lingua franca. See [[alphabet-brahmi-origin]] for how this Persian administrative presence may have catalyzed the development of Indian writing.
