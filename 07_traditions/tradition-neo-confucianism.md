# Neo-Confucianism

## Identity

Neo-Confucianism is the major intellectual and religious synthesis of Song, Ming, and Qing dynasty China (~960–1911) that revitalized classical Confucianism by absorbing the metaphysical vocabulary of Buddhism and Daoism while claiming to return to the authentic tradition of Confucius and Mencius. It became the official ideology of the Korean Joseon dynasty (1392–1897) and shaped Edo-period Japanese intellectual life. It produced the imperial examination curriculum for six centuries and was the lens through which Jesuit missionaries first encountered Chinese thought.

The defining paradox of Neo-Confucianism: it is a synthesis that defines itself as a purification. Zhu Xi and Wang Yangming claim to be restoring original Confucianism against Buddhist and Daoist contamination — while actively deploying Buddhist metaphysical frameworks. The exact same move Thomas Aquinas makes: claiming to restore Christian orthodoxy while building on Arabic-mediated Aristotle.

## Historical phases

### Northern Song foundations (~960–1127)
Five masters lay the groundwork:
- **Zhou Dunyi** (周敦頤, 1017–1073): the *Taijitu shuo* ("Explanation of the Diagram of the Supreme Ultimate") — derives Confucian ethics from cosmological first principles (Wuji → Taiji → yin-yang → Five Phases → human moral nature). Crucially, this cosmological scheme is not classical Confucian — it derives from Daoist thought and possibly Buddhist influence.
- **Zhang Zai** (張載, 1020–1077): *Ximing* ("Western Inscription") — "Heaven is my father and Earth is my mother... all people are my siblings, all things are my companions." Cosmic ethics grounded in qi-monism.
- **Cheng Yi** (程頤, 1033–1107) and **Cheng Hao** (程顥, 1032–1085): the Cheng brothers establish *li* (principle) as the metaphysical ground of both cosmos and ethics; *gewu zhizhi* (investigating things to extend knowledge).

### Zhu Xi's synthesis (~1130–1200)
[[zhu-xi]] (朱熹, 1130–1200) synthesizes the five masters into a comprehensive system — the most influential philosophical synthesis in Chinese history:

**The li-qi framework:**
- *Li* (理, Principle) — the unchanging metaphysical pattern/principle that makes each thing what it is; the source of ethical goodness embedded in the cosmos
- *Qi* (氣, Vital force / material stuff) — the dynamic material-energy that actualizes li in particular forms; variable, corruptible, the source of evil through turbidity

This is Buddhist Huayan metaphysics (*li-shi* — principle and phenomena) re-coded in Confucian vocabulary. Zhu Xi strenuously denied this; modern scholars (de Bary, Tillman) confirm it.

**The Four Books:**
Zhu Xi elevated four classical texts to canonical status above the Five Classics, composing commentaries that defined their meaning for six centuries:
1. *Daxue* (Great Learning) — the eight steps from investigation to peace
2. *Zhongyong* (Doctrine of the Mean) — cosmic resonance through virtue
3. *Lunyu* (Analects of Confucius)
4. *Mengzi* (Mencius)

From 1313, Zhu Xi's commentaries on the Four Books became the required text for the imperial examination — meaning every educated Chinese person was trained in Zhu Xi's synthesis for 600 years.

### Wang Yangming's inward turn (~1472–1529)
[[wang-yangming]] (王陽明, 1472–1529) breaks from Zhu Xi's outward investigation of things and proposes:

**Liangzhi** (良知, "innate moral knowledge"): every person already possesses complete moral knowledge; it need not be acquired through exhaustive investigation of external things, but realized through sincere moral practice. The sage is not the scholar who has accumulated knowledge, but the person who has cleared away the obstacles to their innate knowing.

**Zhixing heyi** (知行合一, "unity of knowledge and action"): knowing and acting are not separate — genuine knowledge IS action. A person who "knows" that honesty is good but acts dishonestly does not genuinely know honesty.

Wang Yangming's *liangzhi* shows massive Chan Buddhist influence — the direct intuition of innate Buddha-nature translated into Confucian terms. He denied this influence, but the structural parallel is exact. His philosophy generated a vibrant populist tradition (the Taizhou school) that democratized moral knowledge beyond elite scholarship.

### Korean and Japanese reception
**Joseon Korea** (1392–1897): Zhu Xi's Neo-Confucianism became the official state ideology, enforced more rigorously than in China itself. Yi Hwang (Toegye, 1501–1570) and Yi I (Yulgok, 1536–1584) deepened the system; Joseon produced arguably the most systematic Neo-Confucian philosophical debates outside China.

**Edo Japan** (~1600–1868): Zhu Xi's school (*Shushi-gaku*) was patronized by the Tokugawa shogunate as a political stabilizing ideology. Wang Yangming's school (*Yōmeigaku*) became the more dynamic intellectual force, influencing Meiji restoration thought.

## MASSIVE WIN: Neo-Confucianism and Thomas Aquinas — the parallel synthesis

The structural parallel between Neo-Confucianism (Song, 960–1279) and Thomistic Scholasticism (Paris, ~1250–1274) is among the most precise in the vault's cross-tradition mapping:

| Feature | Neo-Confucianism (Zhu Xi) | Thomistic Scholasticism (Aquinas) |
|---|---|---|
| **Core project** | Revitalize classical tradition against Buddhist/Daoist "contamination" | Revitalize Christian theology against Islamic/pagan "contamination" |
| **Actual method** | Absorbs Buddhist metaphysics (*li-qi* ↔ Huayan *li-shi*) | Absorbs Islamic-mediated Aristotle (via Ibn Rushd / Ibn Sina) |
| **Source claimed** | Return to Confucius and Mencius | Return to Augustine and Scripture |
| **Synthesis produced** | Confucian ethics + Buddhist cosmological metaphysics | Christian theology + Aristotelian natural philosophy |
| **Canonical consequence** | Zhu Xi's commentaries define imperial exam curriculum 1313–1905 | Aquinas becomes standard theology of Catholic Church (Leo XIII 1879) |
| **Key concept** | *Li* (Principle) — the metaphysical-ethical order | *Forma* (Form) — the metaphysical-teleological structure |
| **Opponent tradition** | Buddhist "emptiness" theology (Huayan, Chan) | Islamic "double truth" (Averroism) |
| **Inward turn** | Wang Yangming's *liangzhi* — direct intuition bypasses external investigation | Late-medieval mysticism (Eckhart, Tauler) — direct union bypasses Scholastic mediation |

Both syntheses are empirically the products of encounter with rival traditions rather than purely internal development. Both define themselves as purifications. Both produce systematic metaphysical frameworks that dominate their culture's institutions for centuries.

## MASSIVE WIN: Wang Yangming's liangzhi and Protestant Inner Light

Wang Yangming's *liangzhi* (innate moral knowledge requiring no external authority) is structurally parallel to Protestant *sola scriptura* + the Inner Light doctrine of the Quakers:

| Tradition | Concept | Against | For |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wang Yangming (1508) | *Liangzhi* — innate moral knowing | External investigation of classics by Zhu Xi scholars | Direct moral intuition available to all |
| Luther (1517) | *Sola scriptura* | Church magisterium | Bible as direct authority; conscience |
| Quakers (~1650) | Inner Light | Clergy, sacraments, liturgy | Direct divine illumination in every person |
| Rousseau (1762) | *Natural goodness* (*bonté naturelle*) | Society's corrupting institutions | Native human goodness prior to civilization |

All four are **historical movements that democratized epistemic authority** — moving it from the institution/expert toward the individual's direct access to moral/spiritual truth. The fact that Wang Yangming precedes Luther by nine years and the Quakers by 140 years demonstrates that this move is not uniquely Protestant but reflects a recurring logic: once an institution becomes sufficiently rigid (Zhu Xi exam-scholarship / Roman magisterium / Anglican establishment / Ancien Régime), a "return to the inner source" becomes the counter-move.

## Confucianism → Enlightenment Europe (the Jesuit transmission)

Neo-Confucianism is the form of Confucianism the Jesuits encountered and transmitted to Europe. The transmission chain is one of the vault's most consequential cross-tradition findings:

1. **Matteo Ricci SJ** arrives in China 1582; learns classical Chinese and Neo-Confucian texts; writes *Tianzhu shiyi* (*True Meaning of the Lord of Heaven*, 1603) — presents Christianity as compatible with Confucianism (not Buddhism/Daoism).
2. **Jesuit translations** of the Four Books flood European intellectual life: *Confucius Sinarum Philosophus* (1687) — partial translation of the Four Books into Latin, produced by Couplet and three colleagues; this is the first systematic European encounter with Chinese philosophy.
3. **Leibniz** (1646–1716): *Novissima Sinica* (1697) — presents China as a moral civilization to balance Europe's technological one; discovers the binary system / I Ching parallel (Leibniz and Bouvet correspond about it ~1701); the *Monadology*'s windowless monads resonate structurally with Neo-Confucian *li* (principle) — debated.
4. **Voltaire** (*Essai sur les Moeurs*, 1756): holds Confucianism up as proof that a rational, non-revealed moral philosophy can sustain civilization — used against the Church. "Confucius had no interest in deceiving mankind."
5. **The Chinese Civil Service → European meritocracy:** The Jesuit reports on China's examination system (merit-based bureaucracy regardless of birth) directly influenced European Enlightenment discussions of governance. Macaulay's 1854 report establishing competitive exams for the Indian Civil Service (later adopted for the British home civil service 1870) explicitly cites the Chinese model. The Chinese imperial exam system (605–1905 CE) is the longest-running meritocratic institution in world history — and is the direct ancestor of modern competitive civil service examinations globally.
