# Sanjiao (三教 — The Three Teachings)

## Identity

*Sanjiao* (三教, "three teachings/religions") is the distinctively Chinese religio-philosophical synthesis in which Confucianism, Daoism, and Buddhism are practiced, understood, and institutionally organized as a complementary triad rather than mutually exclusive systems. The phrase 三教合一 (*Sānjiao héyī* — "the three teachings are one") expresses the syncretic principle at its most extreme: the three traditions, however apparently divergent, address the same ultimate truth from different angles, for different human needs, at different registers of existence.

This is not merely tolerance — it is the sustained institutional and philosophical practice of treating apparent doctrinal rivals as convergent. It is one of the most remarkable phenomena in world religious history.

---

## Historical development

### Tang dynasty (~618–907): the classical synthesis
The Tang imperial house claimed descent from Laozi (the Li family, sharing the surname 李); this created official patronage for Daoism. But Tang China also produced the greatest Buddhist translators (Xuanzang's return from India 645 CE; his *Mahāyāna-saṃgraha* translations) and maintained the Confucian examination system as the backbone of governance. The three traditions existed simultaneously in Tang elite culture without systematic contradiction.

Emperor Xuanzong convened debates between Confucian scholars, Buddhist monks, and Daoist masters. The Chan master Huineng's Platform Sutra (recorded ~780) already shows Daoist and Confucian vocabulary absorbed into Buddhist discourse. By the late Tang, the painter Wang Wei could be simultaneously a Confucian official, a devoted Pure Land Buddhist, and a poet whose sensibility was deeply Daoist — with no perceived contradiction.

### Song dynasty (~960–1279): philosophical synthesis
The Neo-Confucian synthesis of [[tradition-neo-confucianism]] is in fact a three-way synthesis: Zhu Xi's framework absorbs Buddhist metaphysics (the *li-qi* ontology draws directly from Huayan Buddhist *li-shi* framework) while insisting on Confucian social ethics. Wang Yangming's *liangzhi* (innate moral knowledge) shows strong Chan Buddhist influence while claiming classical Confucian authority.

### Ming dynasty (~1368–1644): Three Teachings Harmony as a movement
Lin Zhao'en (1517–1598) founded the first explicit Three Teachings syncretic religion — the *Sanyi jiao* (Three-in-One Teaching) — in Fujian, with an organized institutional presence. His followers built *Sanyi* temples where Confucius, Laozi, and the Buddha were worshipped together; the tradition persists today in Fujian and Taiwan.

The Ming also produced the encyclopedic collection *Sanjiao sou shen da quan* ("Complete Encyclopedia of Gods of the Three Teachings") — a theological dictionary treating the deities of all three traditions as parts of one system.

### Qing dynasty and folk synthesis
The synthesis became normative in Chinese folk religion: village temples typically served multiple traditions; a deity like [[guan-yu]] (the deified Han general Guan Yu) was simultaneously a Confucian exemplar of loyalty (*zhong*), a Daoist deity of war and commerce, and a Buddhist bodhisattva (Sangharama — guardian of the Dharma). [[guanyin]] crossed from Indian Avalokiteśvara into Chinese female deity and was worshipped by Confucians, Daoists, and Buddhists indifferently.

## MASSIVE WIN: Sanjiao as a uniquely successful cross-tradition synthesis

The question the Sanjiao phenomenon poses globally: **why did China achieve sustained institutional syncretism while most other cultures produced heresy-suppression?**

| Culture | Multiple traditions present | Syncretic outcome |
|---|---|---|
| China | Confucianism + Daoism + Buddhism | **Sanjiao synthesis** — all three institutionally coexistent; scholar-elite practiced all three simultaneously |
| Roman Empire | 100+ mystery religions + state cult + Judaism + Christianity | Syncretism norm (*interpretatio graeca*) until Constantine; then Christianity becomes exclusive and suppresses rivals |
| Mughal India | Islam + Hinduism + Jainism + Sikhism | **Dara Shikoh** synthesis attempt (*Majma al-Bahrayn*, 1655) — but Aurangzeb's counter-Reformation reversal |
| Japan | Shinto + Buddhism | **Shinbutsu-shūgō** (神仏習合) — Buddhist-Shinto dual practice, sustained from ~600 to Meiji (1868 forced separation) |
| Medieval Europe | Christianity (Catholic) | Complete doctrinal monopoly; heresy suppression; no sustained synthesis with Islamic or Jewish traditions |
| Hellenistic Alexandria | Greek + Egyptian + Jewish + Gnostic + Persian | Rich syncretism — but ultimately suppressed by both Roman Christianization and later Islamic conquest |

**The finding:** China is unique in producing a *named, institutionalized, intellectually defended* synthesis of competing religious traditions that lasted over a millennium. The social conditions enabling this were: a Confucian state apparatus that was ethically (not theologically) grounded; a Daoist tradition that had no institutional claim to exclusive truth; and a Mahāyāna Buddhist tradition with *upāya* (skillful means) doctrine that explicitly licensed doctrinal flexibility.

The comparison with **Shinbutsu-shūgō** (Japan) is especially close — Japan imported the Sanjiao framework along with Chinese culture, producing a Buddhist-Shinto synthesis (Buddhist deities identified with kami and vice versa) that lasted over 1,200 years until the Meiji state forcibly separated the traditions in 1868. Both demonstrate that state-mandated mutual exclusivity is a political choice, not a religious inevitability.

## Doctrinal structure of the synthesis

Different synthesizers offered different explanations for how the three traditions are "one":

**The division-of-labor model (most common):**
- Confucianism governs outer life: ethics, politics, family, governance
- Daoism governs inner spiritual cultivation and longevity
- Buddhism governs the afterlife and cosmic soteriology

This is the folk model, encoded in the saying: "Confucian on the outside, Daoist on the inside, Buddhist at death." It doesn't claim doctrinal identity but complementary scope.

**The three-paths-to-one-mountain model:**
All three traditions address the same human question (how to live well and align with ultimate reality) via different vocabularies — *Tian* (Heaven) / *Dao* (Way) / *Buddha-nature* are different names for the same underlying principle. This is the perennialist position.

**The Neo-Confucian synthesis model (Zhu Xi, Wang Yangming):**
The deeper truth of all three traditions is recovered by returning to the Confucian classics — Buddhism and Daoism captured partial truths that Confucianism contains more completely. Wang Yangming's *liangzhi* absorbs Chan's direct intuition while claiming primacy of the Confucian framework.

## The Wen-Wu-Fo triad in folk temples

The most widespread popular synthesis: the Wen-Wu-Fo (文武佛) triad displayed together in village temples across China, Southeast Asia, and the Chinese diaspora:
- **Wen** (文, Civil): [[confucius]] — red or scholar-robed figure
- **Wu** (武, Martial): [[guan-yu]] — red-faced warrior deity
- **Fo** (佛, Buddha): [[guanyin]] or the Buddha — compassionate divine figure

This triad synthesizes the three registers of Chinese life: civil virtue, martial protection, and spiritual compassion. It is worshipped by Chinese communities everywhere as a single altarpiece, with no sense of contradiction.
