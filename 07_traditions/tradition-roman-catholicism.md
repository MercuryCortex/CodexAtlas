# Roman Catholicism (the Catholic Church)

## Overview
The Catholic Church is the communion of Christian churches in full communion with the **Bishop of Rome**, and the largest single religious institution in the world — roughly **1.4 billion baptised members** by its own annual statistics (*Annuarium Statisticum Ecclesiae*). It is not one church but **twenty-four churches *sui iuris***: the Latin Church, overwhelmingly the largest, together with twenty-three **Eastern Catholic Churches** (Maronite, Ukrainian Greek Catholic, Melkite, Syro-Malabar, Chaldean, Coptic Catholic and others) which keep Byzantine, Alexandrian, Antiochene, Armenian or East-Syriac liturgy, law and married clergy while recognising papal primacy. "Roman Catholic" is therefore a slightly misleading name for the whole, and a precise one for its dominant part.

Its self-understanding is one of **unbroken continuity**: the church founded by Christ, governed by the successors of the apostles, with the Bishop of Rome as successor of [[peter-apostle|Peter]]. Historically, what can be traced is the emergence of a monarchical episcopate at Rome across the second century, an early and widely acknowledged **primacy of honour**, and a **primacy of jurisdiction** asserted progressively from [[leo-the-great|Leo I]] onward and consolidated by [[gregory-the-great|Gregory the Great]] — reaching its juridical maximum only in 1870.

**Where this node sits relative to its neighbours:** [[tradition-christianity-canonical]] is the first-century stratum; [[tradition-latin-christianity]] is the broad Western/Latin cultural-linguistic tradition; [[tradition-medieval-christianity]] is the 500–1500 institutional world; **this node is the confessional communion** — the Catholic Church as a bounded body defined against [[tradition-eastern-orthodoxy|Orthodoxy]] after 1054 and against [[tradition-protestantism|Protestantism]] after 1517, and as it exists today.

## Institutional and doctrinal formation

### Ancient foundations (1st–5th c.)
The Roman church is prominent early: *1 Clement* (c. 96) writes from Rome to Corinth with evident authority, and Irenaeus (*Adversus haereses* III.3.2) appeals to the Roman succession as a test of apostolic doctrine. Under Constantine and after, the ecumenical councils fix the doctrinal frame that Catholicism shares with Orthodoxy and most of Protestantism: [[event-council-of-nicaea-325|Nicaea 325]] and [[event-council-of-constantinople-381|Constantinople 381]] against [[doctrine-arianism|Arianism]] ([[doctrine-trinity-nicene]]); [[event-council-of-ephesus-431|Ephesus 431]], which gives Mary the title *Theotokos*; [[event-council-of-chalcedon-451|Chalcedon 451]], which defines the two natures in one person ([[doctrine-christology-chalcedonian]], [[doctrine-incarnation]]). These same councils produce the first permanent divisions: the [[tradition-church-of-the-east|Church of the East]] after 431 and the Oriental Orthodox churches ([[tradition-coptic-orthodox|Coptic]], [[tradition-syriac-orthodox|Syriac]], Armenian, [[tradition-ethiopian-orthodox-tewahedo|Ethiopian]]) after 451.

[[augustine-of-hippo|Augustine]] is the decisive Western voice: [[doctrine-original-sin-augustinian|original sin]], the priority of grace, the polemic against [[doctrine-pelagianism|Pelagius]], and in [[phase-4-029-augustine-city-of-god|*City of God*]] a theology of history. Almost every later Western argument — including the Reformation — is a quarrel among Augustinians.

### The Western turn (6th–11th c.)
Latin liturgy and the Vulgate; Benedictine monasticism ([[phase-4-048-benedict-rule]]) as the West's engine of literacy, agriculture and [[medicine-monastic-european|medicine]]; the alliance with Frankish power under [[charlemagne|Charlemagne]]. The **[[doctrine-filioque|*filioque*]]** — the addition of *and the Son* to the creed — enters in Visigothic Spain (Toledo, 589), is promoted by the Carolingians, and is finally sung at Rome around 1014. It is a unilateral change to an ecumenical creed, and the East never accepted it.

### Schism (1054) and the papal monarchy (11th–13th c.)
The mutual excommunications of 1054 between Cardinal Humbert's legation and Patriarch Michael Keroularios conventionally mark the [[event-great-schism-1054|Great Schism]]. Chadwick (2003) shows this is a convenience: the estrangement was slow and linguistic before it was doctrinal, and the practical rupture was the sack of Constantinople by the [[event-fourth-crusade-1204|Fourth Crusade]] in 1204.

The **Gregorian reform** of the eleventh century is the more consequential turn. Gregory VII's *Dictatus papae* (1075) claims the pope's authority over bishops, councils and emperors; the Investiture Controversy that follows ends at the Concordat of Worms (1122) with the church substantially free of lay appointment. Out of this comes Gratian's *Decretum* (c. 1140) and canon law as a systematic science — on Berman's reading, the first modern legal system in the West. [[pope-innocent-iii|Innocent III]]'s **Fourth Lateran Council (1215)** is the high-water mark: it uses the word *transubstantiated* of the Eucharist and requires annual confession and Easter communion of every adult, binding sacramental practice to the individual conscience across Latin Christendom.

The same centuries produce the universities, [[philosophy-scholasticism|scholasticism]], and the mendicant orders — [[tradition-dominican-order|Dominicans]] (confirmed 1216) and [[tradition-franciscan-order|Franciscans]] — and, on the other side of the ledger, the machinery of coercion: the [[event-albigensian-crusade-1209-1229|Albigensian Crusade]] against the [[tradition-catharism|Cathars]], the inquisitorial procedure, and later the [[event-spanish-inquisition-1478|Spanish Inquisition]].

### Crisis and reform (14th–17th c.)
The Avignon papacy (1309–1377), then the **Western Schism** (1378–1417) with two and briefly three claimants, which conciliarism resolved at Constance (1414–18) by asserting a general council's authority over a pope — a principle the restored papacy spent the next century dismantling. The [[event-council-of-florence-1438-1439|Council of Florence]] (1439) enumerated the seven sacraments and signed a union with the Greeks that Constantinople immediately repudiated.

The **[[event-protestant-reformation-1517|Reformation]]** ([[martin-luther|Luther]], [[phase-6-005-luther-95-theses|1517]]; [[john-calvin|Calvin]]) split the Latin West permanently. The **[[event-council-of-trent-1545-1563|Council of Trent]]** (1545–63) answered it and, in doing so, created Roman Catholicism as a confession:

- **Scripture *and* Tradition** as the sources of revelation, against [[doctrine-sola-scriptura|*sola scriptura*]]; the canon fixed to include the deuterocanonical books; the Vulgate declared *authentica*.
- **Justification** by grace, received in baptism, requiring and enabling human cooperation — against justification by faith alone.
- **Seven sacraments**, effective *ex opere operato*; the Mass as a true sacrifice; [[doctrine-transubstantiation|transubstantiation]] defined in Aristotelian terms taken from [[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]] (see [[mass-eucharist-catholic]]).
- **[[doctrine-purgatory-catholic|Purgatory]]**, indulgences (with the abuses curbed), invocation of the saints, and the veneration of images upheld.
- **Discipline**: a seminary in every diocese, residency for bishops, a standard Catechism (1566), Breviary (1568) and Missal (1570), and the Index of Prohibited Books.

Trent also globalised the church. The [[tradition-jesuits|Society of Jesus]] (approved 1540) and the older orders carried Catholicism into the Americas, Africa and Asia under the Iberian [[doctrine-padroado-portuguese-crown-mission|*padroado* and *patronato*]] — an evangelisation inseparable from conquest, and internally contested at the time by Las Casas and the Salamanca theologians. The Chinese Rites controversy, in which Rome condemned Jesuit accommodation to Confucian ancestral rites (1704, 1742; reversed only in 1939), cost the mission in China and is the clearest single case of the limits Catholicism set on inculturation.

### The modern church (19th c.–present)
Confronting revolution and liberalism, the nineteenth-century papacy centralised. [[pope-pius-ix|Pius IX]] defined the [[doctrine-immaculate-conception|Immaculate Conception]] (1854), issued the *Syllabus of Errors* (1864), and convened **Vatican I** (1869–70), whose *Pastor aeternus* defined papal primacy of jurisdiction and **[[doctrine-papal-infallibility|infallibility]]** — the pope teaching *ex cathedra* on faith and morals. Leo XIII's *Aeterni Patris* (1879) made [[philosophy-thomism|Thomism]] the standard of seminary philosophy, and *Rerum novarum* (1891) opened **Catholic social teaching**, with its still-characteristic refusal of both laissez-faire capitalism and socialism. Pius XII defined the Assumption in 1950.

**[[event-second-vatican-council-1962-1965|Vatican II]]** (1962–65) is the largest reorientation since Trent: the liturgy reformed and put into the vernacular (*Sacrosanctum concilium*; Missal of 1970); the church described first as the whole *people of God* and only then as hierarchy, with episcopal collegiality restored alongside papal primacy (*Lumen gentium*); a renewed doctrine of revelation and a turn to biblical scholarship (*Dei verbum*); the repudiation of the charge of deicide against the Jewish people and a positive statement on Judaism, Islam and other religions (*Nostra aetate*); and religious liberty affirmed as a right grounded in the dignity of the person (*Dignitatis humanae*), reversing a long-held position. In 1965 Paul VI and Patriarch Athenagoras mutually lifted the anathemas of 1054.

The [[event-vatican-ii-aftermath-1965-present|aftermath]] is the church's present shape: revised codes of canon law (1983 Latin, 1990 Eastern), the *Catechism* (1992), a traditionalist rejection of the council (Lefebvre and the SSPX) alongside progressive disappointment, the **sexual-abuse crisis** and the institutional reckoning it forced from 2002 onward, and a demographic centre of gravity that has moved decisively to Latin America, Africa and Asia — the context for the election of [[pope-francis|Francis]] in 2013, the first Jesuit and first Latin American pope, and for the synodality process of 2021–24.

## What Catholicism holds

- **Authority is threefold**: Scripture, Tradition and the living **Magisterium** (the pope and the bishops in communion with him), which alone authentically interprets the other two. This is the structural difference from Protestantism, and it is what makes doctrinal *development* possible — a doctrine can be defined in 1854 or 1950 that was not defined before.
- **Seven sacraments** — baptism, confirmation, Eucharist, penance, anointing of the sick, holy orders, matrimony — as effective signs that confer the grace they signify.
- **The Eucharist** as the centre: the [[mass-eucharist-catholic|Mass]] is both sacrifice and sacrament, and [[doctrine-transubstantiation|transubstantiation]] names the change. From this follow the [[monstrance-catholic-eucharist-display|monstrance]], eucharistic adoration, Corpus Christi, and the [[chalice-paten-catholic|vessels]] and [[vestment-chasuble|vestments]] of the rite.
- **Grace and nature**: grace perfects nature rather than replacing it — hence [[moral-natural-law|natural law]] as a source of moral knowledge available to reason, and hence the Catholic confidence in philosophy, [[moral-just-war-cross-tradition|just-war reasoning]], [[moral-doctrine-of-double-effect-catholic|double effect]] and the [[moral-cardinal-theological-virtues|virtue tradition]].
- **The saints and Mary**: the communion of saints, intercession, relics ([[reliquary-medieval-christian]]), and a Mariology running from *Theotokos* (431) through perpetual virginity to the Immaculate Conception (1854) and the Assumption (1950). See [[mary-theotokos]].
- **The last things**: heaven, hell, and [[doctrine-purgatory-catholic|purgatory]]; the [[doctrine-beatific-vision|beatific vision]] as the end of the human person; the [[doctrine-resurrection-bodily-christian|resurrection of the body]].
- **Order and law**: a hierarchical, episcopal structure; the Roman Curia; a celibate male priesthood in the Latin Church (a discipline, not a dogma — married clergy are normal in the Eastern Catholic churches); and a codified canon law.
- **Liturgy and time**: the Roman Rite, the [[liturgical-year-christian|liturgical year]], [[lent-christian-fast|Lent]], [[language-latin-ecclesiastical|Latin]] as the church's language until the 1960s and still its legal one.
- **Built form**: the Romanesque and Gothic cathedral, the Baroque church, and the basilica of [[st-peters-basilica-vatican|St Peter's]] as the architecture of this theology made visible — see [[chartres-cathedral]], [[notre-dame-de-paris]], [[cologne-cathedral]], [[sagrada-familia-barcelona]].

## Where it differs from its neighbours

**Against [[tradition-eastern-orthodoxy|Eastern Orthodoxy]]**, with whom it shares seven councils, the episcopate, the sacraments and most of its theology: the [[doctrine-filioque|*filioque*]]; papal jurisdiction and [[doctrine-papal-infallibility|infallibility]], where Orthodoxy grants Rome primacy of honour only; [[doctrine-purgatory-catholic|purgatory]] and indulgences; the [[doctrine-immaculate-conception|Immaculate Conception]] as a defined dogma; and a scholastic-juridical cast of theology where the East is more apophatic and participatory ([[doctrine-theosis-divinization-orthodox|theosis]]).

**Against [[tradition-protestantism|Protestantism]]**: Tradition and Magisterium alongside Scripture, against [[doctrine-sola-scriptura|*sola scriptura*]]; justification as transformative and cooperative, against forensic imputation; seven sacraments against two; the Mass as sacrifice; a sacramental priesthood against the priesthood of all believers; and the saints, Mary and purgatory.

**Against the Oriental Orthodox and the [[tradition-church-of-the-east|Church of the East]]**: the Christological definitions of 431 and 451 — though the twentieth-century common declarations have found that much of the ancient dispute was terminological.

## MASSIVE WIN — a Roman institution with an Islamic philosophy

Two transmissions define this tradition, and neither runs from Jerusalem.

**The institution is Roman.** Catholicism inherited the empire's administrative geography, its language, its archival habit and above all its legal cast of mind. A *diocese* is a Roman province before it is a bishop's territory. The church that emerged from the Gregorian reform legislates, codifies, adjudicates, keeps records and runs courts; Berman's argument (1983) is that canon law after 1075 is the **first modern legal system in the West**, and that the secular systems were built on its model. What distinguishes Latin from Greek Christianity is less a doctrine than this juridical temperament — and it is inherited from Rome, not from the Gospels.

**The philosophy is Islamic in transmission.** The intellectual architecture of Catholic theology — the vocabulary in which Trent defined transubstantiation and Vatican I defined infallibility — is Aristotelian, and Aristotle reached the Latin West through the Abbasid translation movement and three centuries of Arabic commentary by [[al-farabi|al-Fārābī]], [[ibn-sina|Ibn Sīnā]] and [[ibn-rushd|Ibn Rushd]], with [[maimonides|Maimonides]] on the Jewish side, translated at [[event-toledo-translations-1085-1150|Toledo]] and in Sicily and argued into Christian form by [[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]]. In 1879 Leo XIII made that synthesis the required philosophy of every Catholic seminary in the world.

Setting the two side by side gives the finding this vault exists to produce: **the most self-consciously continuous institution in Western history is, structurally, a Roman legal body running a Greek philosophy that was routed to it through Baghdad, Córdoba and Toledo.** Every link is documented — bulls, codices, translators' names, citations. See [[philosophy-thomism]], [[philosophy-aristotelianism]], [[event-toledo-translations-1085-1150]].

## Disputes

- **When does "Roman Catholicism" begin?** This node dates from 1054 by convention, following the vault's dating basis. The defensible alternatives are: the first century (the tradition's own claim of apostolic continuity); the Gregorian reform of the 1070s, when the papal monarchy is constituted; or Trent, when "Roman Catholic" becomes a confessional identity opposed to Protestantism. Each answer encodes a different theory of what the church is.
- **1054 as an event.** Chadwick (2003) and most current scholarship treat the mutual excommunications as symbolic rather than causal; the schism is a process running from the Photian dispute of the 860s to 1204 and beyond.
- **Development or corruption?** Newman's theory of doctrinal development (1845) defends dogmas defined late — Immaculate Conception, Assumption, infallibility — as organic growth from the deposit of faith. Orthodox and Protestant critics read the same definitions as innovation. This is the central methodological dispute about Catholic doctrinal history and it is not resolvable by evidence alone.
- **The late-medieval church.** The older account of a decadent institution ripe for reform has been substantially revised by Duffy (1992) and others, who document a vigorous and popular late-medieval lay religion; the Reformation then looks less like a rescue and more like a rupture.
- **Vatican II — continuity or break?** Whether the council is a reform in continuity (Benedict XVI's "hermeneutic of reform") or a genuine discontinuity, particularly on religious liberty and on other religions, is the sharpest live argument inside the tradition. O'Malley (2008) documents how contested the drafting itself was.
