# Parsi Zoroastrian Diaspora

The **Parsi diaspora** is one of two surviving branches of post-Sasanian Zoroastrianism (the other being the continuous Iranian-Zoroastrian community centered on Yazd). The community fled Iran in successive waves after the **Arab conquest of Iran (-651)** + the gradual Islamicization of the Iranian plateau, settling in **Gujarat, north-west India** (traditional date 936 CE per the *Qissa-i Sanjan*). From the 18th c. the community concentrated increasingly in **Mumbai**; in the 19th-20th c. a secondary diaspora emerged in the UK, USA, Canada, East Africa, Hong Kong, and Singapore.

## Origin: the Qissa-i Sanjan tradition

The principal Parsi origin-narrative is the **Qissa-i Sanjan** ("Story of Sanjan"), a Persian-language epic poem composed in 1599 CE by **Bahman Kaikobad**. The poem narrates:

1. The flight of Persian Zoroastrians from Khorasan after the Arab conquest, taking the sacred fire with them.
2. A staged migration via Hormuz and Diu, arriving in Sanjan, Gujarat (traditional date 936 CE).
3. The "five conditions" by which the Hindu king Jadi Rana accepted the refugees (adoption of Gujarati language + Indian dress for women + cessation of arms-bearing + performance of marriages after dark + explanation of Zoroastrian theology).

Modern scholarship (Williams 2009; Stausberg 2002 vol. 2) treats the 936 CE date as broadly representative of a **9th-11th-century process of staged migration** rather than a single event.

## The Sasanian liturgical stratum preserved

The Parsi liturgical-textual stratum **preserves Pahlavi-Sasanian-period Zoroastrianism** without the substantial post-Sasanian theological developments of the Iranian-continuous tradition. The Parsi **Atash Behram + Atash Adaran network** constitutes the principal surviving liturgical-infrastructure of pre-Arab-conquest Sasanian Zoroastrianism — a snapshot of 7th-c. Sasanian religious-life preserved through population-displacement.

The **eight Atash Behrams in India** (with the ninth in Yazd) are: Iranshah (Udvada, 1742), the four Mumbai Atash Behrams (Wadia, Anjuman, Banaji, Dadyseth), the Surat Atash Behram, the Navsari Atash Behram, and the Vatcha Gandhi Agiary. The Iranshah Atash Behram at Udvada is traditionally regarded as continuous with the original Sasanian fire brought from Iran in the 8th-10th c.

## MASSIVE-WIN: religious-persistence via population migration

The Parsi diaspora is the **principal case-study of religious-persistence-under-conquest via population-migration** (vs. the Yazd-Iranian-Zoroastrian case of in-situ persistence). The 1400-year preservation of Avestan + Pahlavi liturgical-language + ritual-practice in a Hindu (later British-colonial, later Indian) host-society is **one of the cleanest documented cases of a non-host-religion minority preserving an unbroken liturgical lineage** through massive host-society religious + political transformation.

## The Bombay Parsi era (18th-20th c.)

Under British colonial rule (1858-1947), the Parsi community emerged as a **disproportionately-influential commercial + industrial + philanthropic elite** in Mumbai:

- The **Tata family** (industrial conglomerate, steel, automobiles).
- The **Wadia family** (shipbuilding, Bombay Dyeing).
- The **Petit family** (cotton mills, philanthropy).
- **Dadabhai Naoroji** (1825-1917; first Indian member of British Parliament; key figure in Indian National Congress).

The British-Parsi accommodation is a distinctive religious-political configuration documented in late-19th to early-20th c. South Asian history (Palsetia 2001; Luhrmann 1996).

## Modern demographic challenges

The contemporary Parsi community faces severe demographic decline:
- ~60,000 Parsis in India in 2011 (down from ~115,000 in 1941).
- ~25,000 Parsis in the rest of the world.
- Low fertility + late marriage + the community's traditional non-acceptance of conversion are documented drivers (Hinnells 2005; Luhrmann 1996).

The contemporary debate over whether to accept converts + children of mixed marriages is a defining tension within the modern Parsi community.
