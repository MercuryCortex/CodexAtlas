# Latin Christianity (Western Church)

## Overview
Latin Christianity is the tradition of the Western Roman Church — centered on Rome, conducted in Latin, governed by the papacy, and theologically systematized through Scholasticism. It diverged from Eastern Orthodoxy in the **Great Schism of 1054 CE**, formally split into Catholic and Protestant branches in the Reformation (1517–1648), and remains the single most numerically significant strand of global Christianity (~1.3 billion Catholics today). Its intellectual identity is inseparable from the **Aristotelian-scholastic synthesis** — and that synthesis was made possible by Islamic scholarship.

## Theological distinctives that divide it from Eastern Orthodoxy

### Filioque — the 1054 schism's theological pivot
The original Nicene Creed (325/381 CE) states the Holy Spirit "proceeds from the Father." The Western church progressively inserted *filioque* — "and from the Son" — into the Creed, so that the Spirit "proceeds from the Father **and the Son**." First attested in the Visigothic council of Toledo (589 CE); adopted by Rome in 1014 CE. The Eastern churches rejected the insertion as both (a) theologically wrong (subordinating the Spirit to the Son) and (b) procedurally illegitimate (altering a Council-defined Creed without universal consent). See [[tradition-eastern-orthodoxy]] for the Eastern position.

### Papal primacy — the Petrine succession claim
Latin Christianity asserts that the Bishop of Rome (the Pope) holds universal jurisdiction over all Christians by direct succession from Peter (Matthew 16:18 — "on this rock I will build my church"). The First Vatican Council (1870) defined papal infallibility in matters of faith and morals. Eastern Orthodoxy recognizes Rome as *primus inter pares* (first among equals) but not as universal jurisdictional monarch.

### Sacramental theology unique to the Latin tradition
- **Transubstantiation** (1215 CE, Fourth Lateran Council): the Eucharistic bread and wine *substantially become* the body and blood of Christ, retaining only the *accidents* (appearance) of bread and wine. This uses Aristotelian substance/accident metaphysics — a framework Latin theology absorbed via Arabic-Latin translation of Aristotle (see MASSIVE WIN below). Eastern Orthodoxy preserves a Eucharistic real-presence doctrine without committing to the Aristotelian philosophical framework.
- **Purgatory** (doctrine elaborated 12th c.; defined Council of Lyons II 1274 and Florence 1439): the state of purification after death for those not yet fully prepared for heaven. Jacques Le Goff's *The Birth of Purgatory* (1981) documents this as a 12th-century theological innovation — it is not present in the first millennium of Christian thought in its systematic form. Eastern Orthodoxy maintains a more ambiguous position; this doctrine is absent from Eastern theology.
- **Indulgences**: the remission of temporal punishment for sins (connected to the Purgatory doctrine), dispensed by papal authority. The systematic sale of indulgences was the immediate trigger for Martin Luther's *95 Theses* (1517) and the Reformation. The system presupposed both the Purgatory doctrine and the Petrine papal treasury of merits.
- **Seven sacraments** formally defined (Council of Florence 1439): Baptism, Confirmation, Eucharist, Penance, Anointing of the Sick, Holy Orders, Matrimony. The number and theology were debated throughout the first millennium; the systematic enumeration is medieval.

### Latin as sacred language
Latin served as the sole liturgical language of the Western Church from late antiquity until the Second Vatican Council (1962–65). The 1,500-year maintenance of Latin liturgy created a precise structural parallel to:
- Arabic in Islam (the Qur'an is in Arabic; liturgical Arabic as untranslatable sacred vehicle — [[tradition-islam]])
- Hebrew in post-Temple Judaism (Hebrew as the holy tongue of Torah and prayer — [[tradition-judaism]])
- Sanskrit in Vedic and Brahmanical Hinduism (Sanskrit as the language of the gods and of sacrifice)
- Pali in Theravada Buddhism (Pali as the language of the Buddha's teaching)

The politics of sacred language: control of the untranslated text is control of interpretation. The Vulgate Bible, Jerome's Latin translation (~405 CE), became the authoritative text from which all Western theology was derived for over a thousand years. When Erasmus published a new Greek New Testament (1516) showing the Vulgate contained mistranslations, it was understood immediately as a challenge to the entire scholastic-theological edifice built on the Vulgate's rendering.

## MASSIVE WIN: Aristotle's journey — Baghdad → Toledo → Paris → Latin theology

The most significant intellectual transmission chain in Western medieval history runs:
1. **Greek Aristotle** (384–322 BCE) — most of his corpus is lost to the Greek-reading West after the 6th century CE.
2. **Arabic translation movement, Baghdad (~830–1000 CE)**: under Abbasid patronage, Greek texts translated into Arabic; Aristotle systematically commented by **[[ibn-rushd]]** (Averroes, 1126–1198 CE, Córdoba) and others. Ibn Rushd's Aristotle commentaries become so authoritative that in the Latin West he is simply called *The Commentator*.
3. **Toledo translation movement (~1130–1250 CE)**: Arabic texts (including Ibn Rushd's Aristotle commentaries) translated into Latin at Toledo, Spain — the intellectual frontier between Islamic and Christian Europe. Jewish scholars (notably Abraham ibn Ezra and Michael Scot) served as critical intermediaries in this chain.
4. **Paris and Oxford (~1250–1280 CE)**: **[[thomas-aquinas]]** synthesizes Ibn Rushd's Aristotle with Christian theology in his *Summa Theologica* and *Summa Contra Gentiles*. The result is **Scholasticism** — the intellectual foundation of all subsequent Western Catholic theology.

The paradox: **the same Aristotle that came to the Latin West through Islamic philosophical commentary ([[tradition-islamic-philosophy]] via [[ibn-rushd]]) became the backbone of Catholic systematic theology, including transubstantiation, the proofs of God's existence, the doctrine of the soul, and natural law theory.** The tool of Islamic learning became the instrument of Christian philosophical-theological dominance. The same philosophical tradition fueled both.

Thomas Aquinas was condemned twice (1270, 1277) before being rehabilitated; his use of Averroist-Aristotelian categories was seen as dangerously close to Islamic philosophy by conservative theologians. He was canonized in 1323.

## Relations to neighbors

- **[[tradition-eastern-orthodoxy]]**: 1054 Great Schism over filioque, papal primacy, and the unleavened-bread Eucharist. The theological gap widened at the Crusades (Sack of Constantinople 1204 by Latin crusaders; this is as decisive as the 1054 schism in Eastern memory).
- **[[tradition-early-christianity]]**: Continuous claimed succession from the apostolic period through the Roman church; the Bishop of Rome's succession from Peter is the foundational legitimating claim.
- **[[tradition-medieval-christianity]]**: Latin Christianity *is* the dominant institutional form of medieval Christianity; the two categories overlap almost entirely in the Western European context (1000–1500 CE). Differentiated here for analytical precision.
- **Reformation (Protestant) traditions**: The 1517 split — triggered by indulgences, papal authority, and Biblical interpretation — produced traditions that rejected the distinctly Latin developments (purgatory, indulgences, transubstantiation, papal authority, seven sacraments) while retaining the Trinitarian and Christological core.
