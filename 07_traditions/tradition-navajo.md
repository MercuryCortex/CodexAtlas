# Diné/Navajo Religion

## Overview
The religious system of the Diné ("the People" — the self-designation of the Navajo), who occupy Diné Bikéyah, a homeland in the American Southwest defined by four sacred mountains: Sisnaajiní (Mount Blanca, Colorado) in the east, Tsoodził (Mount Taylor, New Mexico) in the south, Dook'o'oosłííd (San Francisco Peaks, Arizona) in the west, and Dibé Ntsaa (Mount Hesperus, Colorado) in the north. The Diné arrived in the Southwest as Athabaskan migrants from the north ~1000–1500 CE (linguistic evidence: Southern Athabaskan divergence from northern groups), where they encountered and absorbed religious influences from long-established Pueblo peoples (Hopi, Zuni, Anasazi predecessors). The result is a distinctive synthesis that became one of the most elaborately ceremonial religious systems in Indigenous North America.

The center of Navajo religion — the concept around which everything else organizes — is **hózhó** (often rendered "beauty" or "harmony" but more precisely: *the state of right relationship, order, balance, and beauty in which the universe and human life ought to exist and to which they must continuously be restored*). Navajo religion is not a set of beliefs about supernatural beings but a *technology of restoration* — a set of ceremonies, chants, sand paintings, and ritual protocols for returning people and communities to hózhó when they have fallen out of it.

## Historical phases
1. **Athabaskan substrate (pre-1500 CE)** — northern Athabaskan ancestral traditions; migration south.
2. **Southwest synthesis (~1500–1750)** — encounter with Pueblo peoples; absorption of Pueblo ceremonial elements; Pueblo Revolt of 1680 brings additional Pueblo refugees who live among Diné for years, accelerating religious-cultural exchange.
3. **Spanish and Mexican contact (~1598–1848)** — Spanish slave-raiding; sheep/horses transform Navajo economy; Catholic missionary presence (largely unsuccessful); the sheep that become central to Navajo livelihood (and Changing Woman symbolism) arrive via the Spanish.
4. **Long Walk / Hwéeldi (1864–1868)** — US Army under Kit Carson destroys Navajo crops and livestock; forcible removal to Bosque Redondo (Hwéeldi) in eastern New Mexico, where ~8,000 Diné are confined. ~2,000–3,000 die from disease and starvation. 1868 Treaty of Bosque Redondo returns them to a portion of their homeland. The Long Walk is the central traumatic event of Diné historical memory.
5. **Reservation era (1868–present)** — expansion of Navajo Nation reservation; discovery of oil (1921), uranium (1940s — Diné uranium miners suffer radiation-caused cancer; a largely unacknowledged chapter); Livestock Reduction (1930s, John Collier) destroys sheep herds critical to Diné economy and spiritual life; forced school assimilation. Navajo Code Talkers in WWII.
6. **Revitalization (1970s–present)** — Navajo Nation government; Diné language revitalization; ceremonial system continues through *hataałii* (medicine-singer) lineages; ongoing water rights conflicts.

## Core texts
- [[phase-8-013-navajo-blessingway]] — *Hózhǫǫjí* (Blessingway): the central ceremony; the framework ceremony to which all others are referred; a full performance encompasses hundreds of songs, prayers, and sand painting construction over two nights.
- *Hózhóójí Hane'* — the Blessingway origin narrative (Wyman 1970): how the People emerged through four worlds, received Blessingway from the Holy People, and were given Changing Woman.
- *Diné Bahane'* — the Navajo creation narrative (Zolbrod 1984 *Diné Bahane': The Navajo Creation Story* — Tier 1 translation-ethnography).

## Pantheon — the Diyin Diné'é (Holy People)
- [[changing-woman]] (*Asdzą́ą́ Nádleehé*) — the most important and beloved of the Holy People; embodies the seasons and their cycles; creates the first Navajo clans from skin rubbed from her own body; gives the Blessingway ceremony. She changes with the seasons: young woman in spring, mature woman in summer, old woman in autumn, renewed in winter — an embodiment of *hózhó* as cosmic cycle.
- **Sun Bearer** (*Jóhonaa'éí*) — the Sun, who carries the sun on his back across the sky; father of the Hero Twins by Changing Woman
- **Spider Woman** (*Na'ashjé'ii Asdzáá*) — teaches weaving; saves the Hero Twins; advisor and protector; one of the most active Holy People in ceremonial narrative
- **Monster Slayer** (*Naayéé' Neizghání*) and **Born-for-Water** (*Tó Bájish Chíní*) — the Hero Twins, sons of Changing Woman and the Sun; kill the monsters (*naayéé'*) that threaten the People
- **Coyote** (*Mąʼii*) — the trickster; not evil but disruptive; creates necessary disorder; his actions often precipitate crises that the other Holy People must resolve; the origin of death is attributed to Coyote (he threw a stone into the primordial lake and declared that if it sank, humans would die — it sank)
- **Black God** (*Haashchʼéʼooghan*) — god of fire and the night sky; the one who arranged the stars in constellations
- **Talking God** (*Haashchʼééltiʼí*) — the most important grandfather figure; officiant of the Yé'ii Bicheii ceremony

## Defining doctrines

### Hózhó — The Core Concept
*Hózhó* is the master concept of Navajo religion, and one of the most important single concepts in the study of comparative religion. Witherspoon (1977) analyzes it as a composite of:
- **Sa'ah Naagháí** — long-life, the process of going on and completing one's life
- **Bik'eh Hózhó** — beautiful conditions, the blessings of a life well-lived in balance
Together: *Sa'ah Naagháí Bik'eh Hózhó* — the full expression of the ideal state, usually translated "walking in beauty" or "beauty before me, beauty behind me, beauty above and below me."

Hózhó is not achieved once and maintained passively. It is a dynamic state that must be continuously renewed against the forces of *hóchxóó* (ugliness, disorder, disharmony — not "evil" in the Christian moral sense but *wrong relationship*). Navajo ceremony is the technology of this renewal.

### The Chantway System
Navajo ceremonies are *chantways* — extensive multi-night ritual complexes involving:
- **Songs** (hundreds per ceremony, memorized by the *hataałii*)
- **Prayers** (*tsodizin*)
- **Sand paintings** (*iikááh*) — elaborate mandala-like images made from colored sand, charcoal, pollen; the ceremonial patient sits on the painting; it is destroyed at the end of the ceremony (the power is absorbed by/into the patient)
- **Yé'ii** (Holy Person impersonation) — masked dancers embody the Holy People

Major chantways: Blessingway (*Hózhóójí*), Enemyway (*Anaa'jí* — to counter the ghost-sickness of contact with enemy dead), Nightway (*Tł'eeji* — the Yé'ii Bicheii ceremony), Mountainway, Beautyway, Shootingway. Each addresses a specific category of disturbance.

### Emergence Cosmogony
The Navajo universe was created not *ex nihilo* but by *emergence* — the People rose through successive worlds (four or five, depending on tradition), each world being destroyed by its inhabitants' disharmony, requiring emergence upward through a hollow reed to the next world. The present Glittering World (the fourth or fifth) is the world of hózhó — not naturally maintained but contingent on human ceremonial action.

### Sacred Geography — The Four Mountains
The four sacred mountains define Diné Bikéyah (Navajo homeland). Each mountain is tied to a cardinal direction, a color, a gemstone, and a set of Holy People. The homeland is sacred because it is *between* the mountains — the space where hózhó can be maintained. Forced removal from this space (the Long Walk, 1864) was experienced as cosmological catastrophe, not merely political injustice.

## Relations to neighbors
- vs. [[tradition-lakota]]: both Great Plains/Southwest Indigenous North American traditions with a central cosmic-order concept (*hózhó* ↔ *wakȟáŋ*); both non-text ceremonial systems with specialist practitioners.
- vs. [[tradition-haudenosaunee]]: both Indigenous North American traditions; structurally distinct (Northeast confederacy-longhouse vs. Southwest emergence-chantway).
- vs. [[tradition-christianity-canonical]]: extensive 19th–21st-c. missionary contact; Franciscan and Protestant missions; significant Navajo Christian community; the Native American Church (Peyote Way) integrates Christian elements into an Indigenous ceremonial frame; both practiced simultaneously by many Diné.
- vs. Pueblo traditions (Hopi, Zuni): Navajo absorbed substantial Pueblo ceremonial elements after the 1680 Pueblo Revolt; the Nightway and other ceremonies show evidence of Pueblo influence; shared emergence cosmogony structure.

## MASSIVE WIN — Hózhó as Cosmic-Order Concept: Independent Convergence Across Five Traditions

**The claim (investigative):** Multiple religious traditions, entirely independently, arrived at the insight that the sacred is fundamentally a matter of *right relationship, order, and balance* — not a personal deity above the world but a *structural principle of the cosmos* that humans must maintain, align with, or restore. Navajo *hózhó* is the clearest Indigenous North American instance of this cross-tradition pattern.

| Tradition | Concept | Translation | Key Feature |
|-----------|---------|-------------|-------------|
| Navajo | *Hózhó* | Beauty / harmony / right-relationship / the ideal state | A state that must be continuously restored; ceremony = restoration technology |
| Egyptian | *Ma'at* | Truth / justice / order / cosmic balance | Personified as a goddess; maintained by pharaoh; disruption = *isfet* (chaos) | 
| Zoroastrian | *Asha Vahishta* | Best truth / highest order / righteousness | One of the seven divine principles; sustained by human righteous action; opposite is *Druj* (lie) |
| Confucian | *Lǐ* (禮) | Ritual propriety / right relationship / cosmic-social order | The structure of human relations that mirrors Heaven's order; violation = disorder |
| Hindu (Vedic) | *Ṛta* | Cosmic order / truth / right | Pre-Vedic; cosmic principle maintained by gods and humans together; ancestor of Zoroastrian Asha |

**Structural grammar shared across all five:**
1. The cosmos has a natural condition of order/beauty/right-relationship
2. This condition is not automatic — it requires maintenance
3. Human action (ceremony / righteous living / ritual propriety) is constitutive of maintaining the order
4. Disruption of the order is not "sin" in the guilt-shame sense but *wrong relationship* requiring restoration
5. The specialist role (hataałii / pharaoh-priest / zaotar / Confucian gentleman / Brahmin) is to maintain the technology of restoration

**What the comparison reveals:**
The Navajo tradition most clearly documents what Witherspoon (1977) calls the *aesthetic* dimension of sacred order — hózhó is not just moral rightness but *beauty*, the beauty of the world when everything is in its proper relation. This dimension is understated in the Mediterranean traditions (though present: Ma'at is also depicted with a feather — lightness, beauty, perfection of calibration) and may be the most distinctive Navajo contribution to the cross-tradition pattern.

**Transmission vs. convergence:** Complete independence. The Navajo received no Egyptian, Zoroastrian, Confucian, or Vedic religious influence. This is five traditions independently discovering the same structural feature of sacred reality. Witherspoon (1977) and McNeley (1981) both document the hózhó concept as pre-contact and deep in Athabaskan-Navajo tradition. The convergence here is as dramatic as the sky-earth separation myth in its demonstration of independent discovery.

## MASSIVE WIN — Sand Painting as Ritual Technology: Sacred Art as Enacted Cosmogony

Navajo sand paintings (*iikááh* — "place where the gods come and go") are ceremonial diagrams of the cosmos made from colored sands and placed under the ceremonial patient. The patient sits *on* the painting, absorbing the healing order it embodies. The painting is then destroyed.

**Cross-tradition parallel:** This is one of the most precise parallels between Navajo and Tibetan Buddhist ritual practice:

| Feature | Navajo *iikááh* | Tibetan *kalachakra* sand mandala |
|---------|----------------|-----------------------------------|
| Medium | Colored sand, charcoal, pollen, cornmeal on the ground | Colored sand on a flat board |
| Design | Diagram of the cosmos and the Holy People relevant to the ceremony | Diagram of the palace of the deity invoked |
| Function | Ritual patient sits on it; absorbs its healing order | Initiates sit within its sacred space |
| Duration | Made and destroyed within the ceremony | Made over days; destroyed in ritual at the end |
| Destruction meaning | Power is absorbed; the impermanence is the point | Impermanence (anicca); river of destruction carries merit |
| Practitioners | *Hataałii* (medicine-singer) and assistants | Monks under a vajra master |

**Investigative note:** This is a parallel-form connection (structural resemblance; no documented transmission). Navajo and Tibetan Buddhist traditions developed their sand-mandala practices entirely independently. The convergence demonstrates that ritualized sacred-cosmological diagrams that are destroyed after use represent a coherent cross-cultural ritual logic: the sacred is invoked, enters the space, does its work, and the vessel is released.
