# Nizari Ismaili State (Hashshashin / Assassins)

## Identity
The **Nizari Ismaili State** (popularly known as the "Assassins" or *Hashshashin* in Crusader sources) was a breakaway Ismaili Shia political-religious movement founded by **Hassan-i Sabbah** at Alamut Castle in the Alborz mountains of Persia (1090). The name *Hashshashin* — from which the English word "assassin" derives — was applied by their enemies; its meaning is disputed (hashish-users? followers of Hassan? base people?). The organization itself used no such name.

The Nizari state occupied a network of mountain fortresses (40+ in Persia, plus Syrian castles including Masyaf) and pursued a policy of targeted political assassination as a tool of statecraft — eliminating specific enemy leaders rather than engaging in conventional warfare. Their assassination teams (*fedayeen*) were the most feared political weapon in the medieval Near East.

## The Templar connection: one of the most remarkable cross-religious relationships in medieval history

In Syria, the Nizari castles and the Crusader territories (including Templar holdings) were geographically interleaved. The Templars and the Syrian Nizari developed one of the most complex documented cross-religious institutional relationships in medieval history:

**Mutual tribute:** The Syrian Nizari paid tribute to the Templars — an arrangement that made the Templars effectively their protectors against other Crusader lords who might attack them.

**Intelligence exchange:** Both organizations had sophisticated information networks; there is circumstantial evidence (no formal documentation) of intelligence sharing on common enemies.

**The 1173 diplomatic incident:** According to William of Tyre, the Syrian Nizari grand master (Rashid al-Din Sinan, the "Old Man of the Mountain" of Crusader legend) sent an embassy to King Amalric I of Jerusalem in 1173 indicating the Nizari were considering converting to Christianity — which would have ended the tribute arrangements and given the Crusaders a powerful ally. The embassy was assassinated on its return journey; Amalric believed the Templars responsible. Barber (1994) calls this "one of the most significant events in the history of the Crusades" — the Templars had a financial and institutional interest in preventing Nizari conversion.

**What this relationship reveals:** The Templar-Nizari contact shows that both organizations were capable of pragmatic cross-religious cooperation that defied the crusading-versus-jihad binary of their public self-presentations. The Templars defended Muslim Nizari from Christian attack in exchange for tribute; the Nizari provided political intelligence useful to Crusader strategy. This is one of the best-documented examples of medieval cross-religious realpolitik.

## The "Old Man of the Mountain"
The **Rashid al-Din Sinan** (died 1192) — the Syrian Nizari grand master during the Crusader period — was known in Crusader sources as *il Vecchio della Montagna* (the Old Man of the Mountain). Marco Polo's account (which he places in Persia, not Syria, and which describes a "paradise garden" of drugged recruits — entirely legendary) is the source of most Western literary Assassin mythology.

Daftary (1990) documents how the European Crusader accounts of hashish-smoking, paradise-garden-experiencing fanatic killers are largely legendary or wildly distorted. The actual Nizari operated as a sophisticated political-military organization whose assassination policy was precise and instrumental, not fanatical.

## Destruction: the Mongols (1256) and Mamluks (1273)
The Persian Nizari castles were destroyed by Hulagu Khan's Mongol armies (1256) — Alamut fell on 19 December 1256. The Syrian castles fell to the Mamluk Sultan Baybars (1273). The Nizari as a state ceased to exist; the Ismaili community survived (the current Imam is the Aga Khan IV, the 49th Imam of the Nizari Ismaili Muslims, based in Geneva). The tradition continues as a living faith for approximately 15 million followers worldwide.

## The vault connection
The Hashshashin sit at a remarkable intersection:
- **Ismaili Shia theology** (parent tradition in the vault's Islamic cluster)
- **Templar institutional contact** (the most documented cross-religious institutional relationship in the Crusades)
- **Medieval political assassination as theological act** (the fedayeen's killings were understood as religiously justified — a parallel to the crusade theology of *malecide* that Bernard of Clairvaux developed for the Templars)
- **Western legendary reception** (the "Old Man of the Mountain" in Marco Polo, Dante, and subsequent European literature)
