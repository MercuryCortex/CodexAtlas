# Zoroastrianism

## Overview
Zoroastrianism (Avestan: *Mazdayasna*, "Worship of Ahura Mazda"; also the *Daēnā Vanuhi*, "Good Religion") is the religion founded by — or radically reformed by — the prophet [[zarathustra]] (Greek: Zoroaster) in eastern Iran, most plausibly ~1200–1000 BCE. Its core doctrines — a supreme uncreated god (Ahura Mazda); cosmic dualism with a hostile counter-principle (Angra Mainyu / Ahriman); ethical dualism requiring individual moral choice; individual eschatological judgment at death; bodily resurrection; and a final cosmic renovation (*frashokereti*) — represent the **first systematic theology** in which these elements appear together in world religion.

It was the state religion of three successive Iranian empires: **Achaemenid** (550–330 BCE), **Parthian** (~250 BCE–224 CE), and **Sasanian** (224–651 CE). Through the Achaemenid period's sustained encounter with the Jewish exiles in Babylon and the wider Persian diaspora, Zoroastrian theological categories — dualism, organized angelology, demonology, individual soul-judgment, cosmic final judgment, bodily resurrection, a messianic savior — almost certainly contributed to the transformation of Second Temple Judaism, and thereby to Christianity and Islam. The total global Zoroastrian community today numbers ~100,000–200,000; its theological influence is disproportionate to its size by any measure in the history of religion.

## Historical phases

### Phase 1: Gathic / Early (~1200–1000 BCE)
The horizon of Zarathustra himself: the composition of the 17 Gathas in Old Avestan. A pre-state pastoralist setting in eastern Iran (Bactria / Sogdiana / Khwarezm — the exact region is debated, Bactria most commonly argued). Theologically: a deliberate **reform** of the inherited Indo-Iranian religion, demoting the *daēvas* (warrior-deities of the old religion, cognate Vedic *devas*) to demons, exalting the *ahuras* (cognate Vedic *asuras*), and proclaiming Ahura Mazda as the one supreme lord. The foundational theological move — cosmic Truth (*aša*) vs. the Lie (*druj*) as the structuring principle of existence — is launched here.

This phase's key breakthrough: **free moral choice as cosmologically structuring**. Yasna 30's "Two Spirits" passage posits two primordial beings who *chose* between life and not-life at the cosmic foundation — making the cosmos itself the product of a moral decision. This is the philosophical seed from which all later Zoroastrian theology grows.

### Phase 2: Younger Avestan (~1000–550 BCE)
A long intermediary period in which the strict Gathic theology is modulated by the gradual **re-incorporation of older Indo-Iranian deities** — especially Mithra (yazata of covenant and light) and Anāhitā (yazata of waters) — into the Zoroastrian system as *yazatas* (beings worthy of worship), subordinate to Ahura Mazda. The Younger Avesta — the Yashts (hymns to individual yazatas), the Yasna proper surrounding the Gathic core, and the Vendidad (the "Anti-Demonic Law") — was composed in this period.

The theological product: a structured pantheon with Ahura Mazda at the apex, the seven **Amesha Spentas** ("Bountiful Immortals" — abstract divine principles personified) as his immediate court, and a larger host of yazatas below. The Amesha Spentas are: Asha Vahishta (Best Truth/Order), Vohu Manah (Good Mind), Khshathra Vairya (Desirable Dominion), Spenta Armaiti (Holy Devotion), Haurvatāt (Wholeness), and Amərətāt (Immortality). **Haoma** (cognate Vedic *soma*) is re-integrated with a full liturgy despite the Gathas' apparent hostility to the old haoma cult.

### Phase 3: Achaemenid (550–330 BCE)
The empire of Cyrus the Great (r. 559–530 BCE) through Darius III marks Zoroastrianism's maximum political reach. Darius I's Old Persian inscriptions at Behistun (~520 BCE) and Persepolis invoke Ahura Mazda explicitly as supreme god, frame the defeat of rebel-kings as the cosmic victory of *arta* (Truth) over *drauga* (Lie), and Xerxes' "Daiva inscription" explicitly prohibits *daiva*-worship. The Achaemenid imperial theology is unambiguously Zoroastrian in vocabulary, though how rigorously Zoroastrian in doctrine is debated (Boyce vol. II).

**The theological encounter with Judaism** is the fulcrum of Zoroastrian influence on Western religion. From Cyrus's conquest of Babylon in 539 BCE — the event that allowed the Jewish exiles to return — through the entire Persian period (539–332 BCE), the Jewish community lived under Persian imperial rule in sustained contact with Zoroastrian theological culture. Features that develop in post-exilic and Second Temple Judaism but are absent or marginal in pre-exilic Hebrew Bible — organized angelology (named archangels), organized demonology (named cosmic adversary), cosmic dualism, bodily resurrection, final judgment — are all present in Zoroastrianism before this period. The *Qumran War Scroll*'s cosmic dualism (Sons of Light vs. Sons of Darkness) is the single closest Jewish parallel to Zoroastrian dualism in any pre-Christian text. Ref: Boyce & Grenet 1991; Hultgård 1998; Barr 1985 *JAAR*.

Cyrus's release of the Jewish exiles (539 BCE), recorded in the Hebrew Bible (Ezra 1:1–4), and his designation in Second Isaiah as Yahweh's "anointed" (*mashiach* — Isa 45:1), is the most symbolically charged moment of Zoroastrian-Jewish contact. It is also the single most discussed moment in discussions of cross-tradition influence.

### Phase 4: Hellenistic and Parthian (330 BCE – 224 CE)
Alexander's conquest of Persia in 330 BCE is framed in Zoroastrian tradition as a catastrophe — the destruction of the Great Avesta, the killing of priests. Whether this reflects historical reality or post-Sasanian mythologization is debated. The subsequent **Seleucid** period (330–250 BCE) saw Greek-Iranian cultural syncretism in the Zoroastrian heartland. The **Arsacid Parthian** dynasty (~250 BCE – 224 CE) partially restored Zoroastrian institutional religion but left little systematic evidence of theological codification.

This period also sees the westward spread of the **Mithra cult**: the Roman mystery cult of Mithras, spread through the Roman Empire from ~1st c. CE onward, drew on Iranian materials (tauroctony / bull-slaying, the *specus* cave sanctuaries, Sol Invictus associations). The relationship between Roman Mithraism and Zoroastrian Mithra is debated — Ulansey's "celestial mechanics" thesis vs. Beck's Iranian-origins argument — but the Iranian substrate is not doubted. Ref: [[mithra-zoroastrian]]; Beck 1984.

Greek philosophers from Plato onward associated the "Magi" with ancient wisdom. The Hellenistic world produced a genre of pseudo-Zoroastrian philosophical texts that have nothing to do with the actual Gathas — part of the long history of Western philosophical misappropriation of "Zarathustra" that culminates in Nietzsche.

### Phase 5: Sasanian (224–651 CE)
The Sasanian dynasty (founded 224 CE by Ardeshir I) is Zoroastrianism's apogee of institutional power. The empire was explicitly Zoroastrian in state ideology — fire temples were state institutions, the Magi (Mowbeds) held legal authority, and religious minorities were periodically persecuted.

**The codification of the Great Avesta:** The *Sasanian Avesta* was organized into **21 nasks** (volumes), structured on the model of the three lines and 21 words of the Ahuna Vairya prayer. Only a fraction survives (the Yasna + Yashts + Vendidad + Visperad). The most complete surviving account of the lost nasks is preserved in the 9th–10th century **Denkard** (books 8–9), which describes each nask's content. Initial codification began under Shapur I (r. 241–270 CE); the systematic effort reached its peak under Shapur II (r. 309–379 CE) and continued through the later Sasanian period.

**Pahlavi theological literature:** The *Bundahishn* ("Original Creation" — Zoroastrian cosmology and cosmogony in Pahlavi), the *Denkard* ("Acts of the Religion" — 9-volume encyclopaedia), the *Zādspram* (theological selections), the *Arda Viraz Namag* (a vision journey through heaven and hell), and the *Wizirkard-i Denig* constitute the systematic theological legacy of Sasanian Zoroastrianism. These texts are the primary source for Zoroastrian mythology, cosmology, and eschatology beyond the Avesta.

**Zurvanism:** A theological current within Sasanian Zoroastrianism that posited a higher deity, *Zurvan* (infinite Time), as the common father of both Ahura Mazda and Angra Mainyu — the two spirits as twins born of Time. This resolved the logical problem of how a supremely good god could have generated a hostile spirit, at the cost of subordinating both to a higher amoral principle. Whether Zurvanism was Sasanian mainstream or a theological fringe is debated; the evidence is largely preserved in external (Christian and Manichaean) polemic. Zurvanism was suppressed by the post-Sasanian Zoroastrian restoration as theologically deviant.

**Manichaeism** (founded by Mani, ~240 CE, in the Sasanian empire) drew heavily on Zoroastrian categories — cosmic light/darkness dualism, the eschatological return of light, the concept of the cosmic savior — recombined with Christian and Buddhist elements. The Sasanian Zoroastrian establishment persecuted Manichaeism as a rival; Mani was executed under Bahram I (~276 CE). See [[phase-4-014-cologne-mani-codex]].

### Phase 6: Islamic conquest and diaspora (651 CE onward)
The Arab conquest of the Sasanian Empire was completed by ~651 CE with the death of Yazdegerd III. Zoroastrians were given *dhimmi* status (protected peoples of the book) under Islamic law — subject to the *jizya* tax but not forced conversion. The Islamization of Iran was gradual: Zoroastrian majorities persisted in parts of Persia through the 9th–10th century before conversion became the overwhelming norm. By the 11th–12th century, Zoroastrianism had contracted to a minority concentrated in Yazd and Kerman.

**The Parsi migration to India** is the tradition's great survival story. A community of Zoroastrian refugees — the tradition's founding legend is preserved in the *Qissa-i Sanjan* ("Story of Sanjan") — migrated from the region of Pars and Khorasan to the western coast of India, settling in **Gujarat** at the hospitality of the local Rajput ruler Jadav Rana. The migration occurred in waves from the **7th–10th centuries CE**; the main community settlement at Sanjan is traditionally dated ~716 CE, though scholars debate the precise chronology. These refugees became known as **Parsis** (Persians). In Gujarat and subsequently Bombay (Mumbai), the Parsi community developed extraordinary success in commerce, industry, and philanthropy: the Tata industrial dynasty, Zubin Mehta, Freddie Mercury (born Farrokh Bulsara), and Homi Bhabha (nuclear physicist) are among notable Parsis.

**Iran today:** A remnant *Zartoshti* community survives in Iran, concentrated in Yazd and Kerman, with their fire temples and priestly traditions (though under the Islamic Republic with significant legal constraints). Estimates: 20,000–60,000.

**Global modern diaspora:** From the 19th century onward, Parsi and Iranian Zoroastrian communities established themselves in Britain, North America, and Australia. The total global Zoroastrian community is estimated at **100,000–200,000** — one of the world's smallest major religious communities — while its theological influence across the Abrahamic traditions remains one of the most consequential cross-tradition transmissions in the history of religion.

## Defining doctrines

### Cosmic and ethical dualism
The foundational doctrine: the universe is the arena of a conflict between two primordial principles — **Truth** (*aša* / *arta*) and **the Lie** (*druj* / *drauga*). Two primordial Spirits, Spenta Mainyu (Bounteous) and Angra Mainyu (Hostile), chose between life and not-life at the cosmic foundation (Yasna 30). This dualism operates at every level — cosmic, social, individual. The Zoroastrian ethical summary — *humata, hūkhta, hvarshta* (good thought, good word, good deed) — is the daily practice of alignment with Truth. See [[theme-asha-druj-cosmic-opposition]] for the full cross-tradition treatment of this axis.

### Individual moral choice
Every human soul is genuinely **free to choose** — this is explicit in Yasna 30.2 ("the wiser of the two shall choose aright; not so the fool"). The cosmos was created from a free choice by two spirits; human moral life recapitulates that cosmic choice. This doctrine of genuine individual moral freedom combined with cosmic significance is among the earliest in world religion.

### Eschatology — individual and cosmic
**Individual:** At death, the soul crosses the *Chinvat Bridge* (Bridge of the Selector). The righteous cross to the Best Existence; the wicked fall to the Worst Existence.

**Cosmic:** History ends with the coming of the *Saoshyant* (future savior, born of a virgin miraculously impregnated by Zarathustra's seed preserved in a lake), the general resurrection of the dead, a final smelting — the mountains melt into a river of molten metal through which every soul passes (experienced as warm milk by the righteous, as molten metal by the wicked) — the defeat of Angra Mainyu, and the *frashokereti* (renovation / "making wonderful") of the world. This is the telos of Zoroastrian cosmic struggle: not cyclical return but linear history with a final resolution. See [[frashokereti-cosmic-renovation]], [[cinvat-bridge-judgment]], [[saoshyant]].

### Ritual purity — the Vendidad system
Zoroastrian ritual law codifies purity with exceptional detail: corpse pollution (*nasu*, a manifestation of the Druj), fire contamination, and elaborate purification rites are the core concerns. Zoroastrian disposal of the dead in "Towers of Silence" (*dakhma*) — where vultures consume corpses rather than polluting earth, water, or fire — is a direct consequence of the purity theology.

### Reverence for fire
Fire (*ātar* / *ātash*) is the visible manifestation of Ahura Mazda's presence and of Asha. The sacred fire in a fire temple (*ātaškadag*) is classified in three grades of holiness: the *Ātash Bahrām* (highest, maintained with unbroken flames through elaborate ritual procedures); the Ātash Ādarān; and the Ātash Dādgāh. Some fires in Iran are claimed to have burned continuously for over a thousand years.

## Theological innovations — what Zoroastrianism introduces to world religion

These features appear for the first time in a systematic, theologically-argued form in the Gathas or early Zoroastrian theology, and did not previously exist in comparable form in the Ancient Near East:

1. **Cosmic dualism with a named adversary** — not divine conflict (which is ancient) but a named principle of cosmic evil (Angra Mainyu) who is not a subordinate god but a metaphysical counter-principle.
2. **Universal individual moral choice** — not priestly ritual alone but every individual's thought, word, and deed as cosmologically significant.
3. **Named archangels** (Amesha Spentas) organized in a hierarchy.
4. **Named demons** (*daēvas*) organized in a counter-hierarchy under Angra Mainyu.
5. **Individual eschatological judgment** of the soul at death (not collective judgment of the nation).
6. **Bodily resurrection** at the end of history.
7. **Final cosmic renovation** (*frashokereti*) — linear time with a decisive final resolution, not cyclical recurrence.
8. **A future cosmic savior** (Saoshyant) who will bring that renovation.

All eight features appear in post-exilic and Second Temple Judaism in forms without clear pre-exilic Hebrew precedent, and the Persian period context makes Zoroastrian influence the strongest available explanation for their emergence (Boyce & Grenet 1991; Hultgård 1998; Barr 1985).

## Relations to neighboring traditions

### ↔ Vedic Hinduism
[[tradition-vedic-hinduism]]: **shared Indo-Iranian inheritance, inverted theological valuation**. The same root deities — Mitra/Mithra, Varuṇa/*ahura*-class, Indra/Indra-cognate — appear in both traditions with precisely reversed evaluations. Vedic *devas* are good; Avestan *daēvas* are demonic. Vedic Indra is a cosmic hero; in the Gathas the Indra-cognate is among the condemned *daēvas*. *Aša* = Vedic *ṛta* (cosmic order — same concept, same Proto-Indo-Iranian etymology). *Haoma* = *soma*. *Yasna* = *yajña*. *Zaotar* = *hotṛ*. This shared-yet-inverted system is the strongest evidence that Zoroastrianism is a deliberate **reform** of the inherited Indo-Iranian religion. Ref: Boyce 1975; West, *Indo-European Poetry and Myth* (2007, OUP).

### → Judaism (MASSIVE-WIN)
[[tradition-judaism]]: The **Persian period contact** (539–332 BCE) is the most consequential and contested mechanism of cross-tradition influence in comparative religion. See above (Phase 3). The post-exilic Jewish theological developments most plausibly explained by Zoroastrian influence: named archangels (Gabriel, Michael, Raphael, Uriel — first in Enochic literature, post-exile); named cosmic adversary (ha-Satan in Job is possibly Achaemenid-period; Belial/Mastema in Qumran is fully Zoroastrian-structured); cosmic dualism (1QS "Two Spirits" treatise); bodily resurrection (first clearly attested in Daniel 12:2, a text of the Maccabean period but with earlier Second Temple antecedents); final cosmic judgment. Ref: Boyce & Grenet 1991; Hultgård 1998; Barr 1985; [[theme-zoroastrian-jewish-exchange]]; [[theme-satan-angra-mainyu-transfer]]; [[theme-paradise-etymology]]; [[persian-period-injection]] (the full documented-channel analysis of this theological injection event).

### → Manichaeism
Mani (3rd c. CE, Sasanian Persia) built directly on Zoroastrian frameworks — the cosmic light/darkness dualism, eschatological liberation of light from darkness — recombined with Christian and Buddhist elements. Mani's followers regarded the Zoroastrian Magi as their primary religious antagonists. See [[phase-4-014-cologne-mani-codex]].

### ← Western philosophy (reception)
Hegel (*Lectures on the Philosophy of History*, ~1830) positioned Achaemenid Persia as "the strict beginning of world history" and Zoroastrianism's light/darkness dualism as the first abstract universal principle of Spirit. Nietzsche (*Also Sprach Zarathustra*, 1883–85) inverted the historical Zarathustra into a herald of the Übermensch and the overcoming of morality — the most famous and most systematically misleading Western appropriation of a non-Western religious figure in modern philosophy. The actual Zarathustra is Nietzsche's theological opposite in every major respect. See [[zarathustra]] for the full treatment of the Nietzsche misappropriation and the Hegel reception.
