# Alevi-Bektashi

## Overview

Alevism is a heterodox Islamic tradition centered on the veneration of [[ali-ibn-abi-talib]] and the Twelve Imams, but sharply distinct from mainstream Twelver Shia Islam in practice: Alevis do not use mosques (they have no mosque-going tradition), observe no five daily prayers in the Sunni form, and do not fast during Ramadan in the standard sense. Their defining ritual is the *cem* ceremony — a communal gathering involving sacred music (*nefes* hymns), poetry, liturgical prayer, and ritual movement (*semah* dance) — conducted with men and women seated together. The *cem* has no parallel in any branch of Sunni or mainstream Shia Islam; it is the living core of Alevi religious identity. The tradition encompasses 5–20 million people primarily in Turkey, with major communities in Albania, the Balkans, and the European diaspora.

## The Bektashi Sufi order

The organized Sufi wing of this synthesis is the Bektashi Order, formally institutionalized under Hacı Bektaş Veli's posthumous name by Balım Sultan (d. 1516). The Bektashi order held a unique structural position in the Ottoman state: it was the affiliated spiritual order of the Janissary corps, the elite Ottoman infantry. This entanglement meant the tradition ran through the heart of Ottoman military and political history for three centuries. When Mahmud II dissolved the Janissaries in the 1826 "Auspicious Incident" (*Vaka-i Hayriye*), he simultaneously suppressed the Bektashi order, closing its lodges (*tekkes*) and exiling its leaders. The order survived underground, and notably flourished in Albania, where Bektashism became one of the major religions — the World Bektashi Headquarters relocated to Tirana after Mustafa Kemal Atatürk's 1925 ban on all dervish orders in the Turkish Republic. Today the Albanian Bektashi community is one of the largest organized Bektashi institutions in the world.

## Cross-tradition synthesis — the MASSIVE-WIN node

The Alevi-Bektashi tradition is the vault's most fully documented case of a *living* cross-tradition synthesis drawing on four distinct religious streams. These are not theoretical borrowings; they are ethnographically and historically attested in the same communities:

**FROM SHIA ISLAM:** The doctrinal core — the Twelve Imams, the absolute centrality of [[husayn-ibn-ali]]'s martyrdom at [[event-karbala-680]], Ashura mourning, the esoteric (*batin*) layer of Quranic interpretation that supplements the external (*zahir*). Alevis venerate the Imams in ways structurally parallel to Twelver Shia devotion, while rejecting the Shia clerical establishment and the five-pillar practice framework.

**FROM SUFISM:** The *pir-murid* (teacher-disciple) relationship is the operational structure of Alevi community transmission. The *maqamat* (stages of the mystical path) are encoded in the "Four Gates, Forty Stations" (*dört kapı kırk makam*) doctrine — a Sufi-origin stage system that Alevism adopted and made its own. The *nefes* hymn tradition and the *semah* ritual movement are structurally related to Sufi *sama* practice.

**FROM PRE-ISLAMIC ANATOLIAN RELIGION:** The most archaeologically provocative layer. Female saints play a role in Alevi tradition with no parallel in mainstream Islam: Kadıncık Ana ("the Matron Mother") appears as Hacı Bektaş Veli's close companion and spiritual interlocutor in the founding hagiography. Nature-spirit veneration, sacred trees, sacred springs, and animal-familiar symbolism (the lion and the deer of the [[phase-5-055-vilayetname-haci-bektas]]) point to a pre-Islamic substrate. Shamanic healing, trance states, and holy lineages (*ocaklar*) transmitted through specific families trace patterns that Ocak (1980) and others connect to pre-Islamic Turkic-Anatolian shamanic practice.

**FROM HETERODOX CHRISTIAN / MANICHAEAN SURVIVALS:** The most contested and most historically interesting layer. Mélikoff's fieldwork documented remote Alevi communities in central Anatolia who venerated Ali as a figure structurally equivalent to Christ — the martyred divine mediator who returns at the end of time. The Alevi practice of including wine in the *cem* ceremony — unique in any Islamic context — echoes Eucharistic practice and has no parallel in Sunni or mainstream Shia Islam. Some scholars (Mélikoff, Ocak) see possible Manichaean-dualist echoes in remote Alevi theological formulae. The exact historical pathway is disputed and may be unrecoverable; the *documented fact* of these convergences in living communities is not.

This makes the Alevi-Bektashi tradition the single node in the vault where Shia Islamic theology, Sufi mysticism, pre-Islamic Anatolian shamanic practice, and heterodox Christian-inflected survivals coexist in a documented, living community — not as theoretical synthesis but as ethnographically observed practice.

## Historical formation

The tradition's origins are rooted in the social disruption of post-Mongol Anatolia (13th–15th centuries). The Mongol invasions destroyed the settled Islamic establishment in Central Asia, generating waves of displaced dervishes, Sufi wanderers, and heterodox communities who moved westward into Anatolia's frontier zones. These *ghazi* warrior-mystic communities on the Byzantine-Seljuk-Mongol frontiers were precisely the environment where religious boundaries were fluid. The *Babailer Revolt* of 1240 — a massive Turkmen uprising against the Anatolian Seljuk state, associated with the heterodox dervish Baba Ishaq — is the immediate historical context in which Hacı Bektaş Veli appears. Whether Hacı Bektaş participated in the revolt or was merely associated with its milieu is debated; he is not documented as a rebel leader.

The critical 16th-century development is the Kizilbas movement ("red heads," named for their distinctive red headgear) — the Anatolian Turkmen communities who were partisans of the Safavid dynasty of Iran. The Ottoman-Safavid wars (1514 onward, beginning with the Battle of Chaldiran) drove Anatolian Alevis underground: the Ottoman Sunni religious establishment (*ulema*) issued fatwas labeling Kizilbas-Alevis as heretics, and periodic massacres followed. This persecution created the *taqiyya*-adjacent practice of religious concealment that shaped Alevi identity for centuries — the tradition was transmitted within families, not in public institutions, which is why it survived Ottoman suppression and later Atatürk's 1925 ban on dervish orders.

## Modern Alevism

Atatürk's secular nationalism was initially embraced by Alevis as structural protection from Sunni majority pressure; Alevis disproportionately supported the Republican People's Party (*CHP*) and the secular state project. This alignment has made Alevism a political as well as religious identity in modern Turkey. The **Sivas massacre of 1993** — when a Sunni mob set fire to a hotel hosting an Alevi cultural festival (the Pir Sultan Abdal Festival), killing 35 intellectuals and artists — is the defining modern trauma of the community. Alevis in Turkey continue to seek official recognition: they have no legal standing as a religious minority, their *cemevi* gathering-halls receive no state funding equivalent to mosques, and the Diyanet (state religious affairs directorate) represents only Sunni Islam. The modern Alevi movement is both a religious revival and a civil-rights political movement. In Albania, the Bektashi World Center operates openly and the order is one of four officially recognized religious communities.
