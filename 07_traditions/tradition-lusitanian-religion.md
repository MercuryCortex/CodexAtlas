# Lusitanian Religion (Pre-Roman Iberian)

## Identity
Lusitanian religion is the indigenous polytheism of the western Iberian Peninsula — the theological system of the Lusitanians and related Gallaecian peoples whose territory corresponds broadly to modern Portugal and Galicia. Attested almost entirely through Roman-period votive inscriptions (1st–4th c. CE), Lusitanian religion represents a pre-Roman tradition that continued to function — and was recorded — only after Roman conquest, creating a paradox: the documentation of this indigenous faith is entirely in Latin and Greek, using Roman epigraphic forms.

## Language and identity
The **Lusitanian language** — preserved in ~6 stone inscriptions and numerous deity-name attestations in Latin votive texts — is the subject of scholarly debate: is it Celtic (a P-Celtic dialect close to Gaulish and Brythonic), a distinct para-Celtic Indo-European branch, or an archaic IE language separate from Celtic entirely? Prósper (2002) argues for a Celtic affiliation; Koch (2009) reviews the evidence cautiously. The deity names show clear PIE roots regardless of their precise classification.

## The deities
Over 100 indigenous deity names are attested in the Lusitanian-Gallaecian corpus (Encarnação 1975). Most are highly localized — regional or valley-specific deities of the kind common in ancient Celtic religion. The most widely attested include:

- **[[endovelicus]]** — the most important: oracle, healing, underworld; 80+ inscriptions; major sanctuary at São Miguel da Mota
- **[[nabia]]** — water/river/fertility goddess; 30+ inscriptions in Gallaecia and northern Lusitania; sometimes appears with Roman *Vittoria* in compound dedications
- **Bandua** — protective/martial deity; the most widely attested (50+ inscriptions across Gallaecia); epithet-formations (Bandua Veigebreaego, Bandua Roudeaeco, etc.) suggest a deity-type applied to many local protective forces
- **Reue** — thunder-related deity; PIE \*reu- ("to thunder") linguistic root posited by Prósper; if correct, a cognate of Vedic Parjanya and Norse Þórr in the Iberian attestation
- **Trebaruna** — sovereignty/land/battle; etymology from \*trebā ("settlement/homestead") + \*runa ("mystery/knowledge"); possible sovereignty-goddess function

## Roman interpretatio
Roman colonizers engaged in *interpretatio Romana* with Lusitanian deities: Endovélico is identified with Silvanus and Dis Pater in later inscriptions; Nabia occasionally appears with Roman Vittoria. This interpretatio is partial — most Lusitanian deities retained their indigenous names alongside or instead of Roman equivalents, suggesting the Roman religious framework was applied selectively and that local cults maintained strong independent identity.

## The continuity problem
The tradition ends documentarily around the 4th century CE with Christianization. However, several scholars (Bermejo Barrera 1986) have traced Lusitanian cult-site continuities into Christian saints' feast days and sacred-spring cults in modern rural Portugal and Galicia. The cult center of Endovélico at São Miguel da Mota became a church dedicated to the Archangel Michael — the single most common Christian-over-pagan-oracle-deity substitution in Western Europe (cf. Mont-Saint-Michel, San Michele al Gargano, Skellig Michael).

## Connection to Celtic tradition
Lusitanian religion belongs to the broader western Celtic or para-Celtic IE religious family — the same family as Gaulish, Brythonic, and Gaelic tradition. The PIE comparanda for Lusitanian deity-names (Reue/thunder, Nabia/river, Bandua/protection) connect westernmost Iberia to the same reconstructed PIE substrate as Irish, Welsh, and Continental Celtic religion. The vault's [[boann]] (Irish river goddess) and [[zemyna]] (Baltic earth goddess) are cognate in function with Nabia; Reue is a cognate-type of [[velinas]] and the entire PIE thunderer tradition.
