# Protestantism

## Overview
The third major branch of historical Christianity alongside Catholicism and Eastern Orthodoxy, originating in Martin Luther's 1517 critique of indulgences and the resulting 1517–1530 rupture from Roman Catholicism. Protestantism is theologically defined by the *five solas* — *sola scriptura* (Scripture alone as final authority), *sola fide* (justification by faith alone), *sola gratia* (salvation by grace alone), *solus Christus* (Christ alone as mediator), *soli Deo gloria* (to God alone be glory) — and ecclesiologically by the priesthood of all believers and the rejection of papal supremacy. The tradition divides into three major confessional branches established in the 16th century — Lutheran, Reformed (Calvinist), and Anglican — and the Radical Reformation (Anabaptists, Mennonites, later Baptists and Quakers), and has continued to fragment and recombine, producing the global Protestant landscape of ~900 million adherents today (Lutheran, Reformed, Anglican, Methodist, Baptist, Pentecostal, evangelical-nondenominational, etc.).

## Historical phases
1. **Foundation** (1517–1530) — Luther's 1517 *95 Theses* through the 1530 Augsburg Confession.
2. **Confessional differentiation** (1530–1577) — Lutheran-Reformed split at Marburg 1529; Calvin's *Institutes* (1559) and Reformed consolidation; Anglican settlement under Elizabeth (1559); Lutheran *Formula of Concord* (1577).
3. **Confessional Orthodoxy and the Thirty Years' War** (1577–1648) — confessional hardening; the Thirty Years' War devastates Central Europe; the Peace of Westphalia (1648) institutionalizes confessional plurality.
4. **Pietism and Wesleyan revivals** (1675–1800) — Spener's *Pia Desideria* (1675); Halle Pietism; the Moravians; Wesleyan revival in 18th-c. England; the Great Awakening in colonial America.
5. **19th-century liberal Protestantism and missionary expansion** — Schleiermacher's *On Religion* (1799); German liberal theology; global Protestant missionary movement; American denominationalism.
6. **20th-century fragmentation and renewal** — Karl Barth's neo-orthodoxy; Pentecostalism (1906+, now the fastest-growing form of Christianity); evangelical movement; ecumenical movement; Global South Protestant expansion.

## Core texts
- The Lutheran corpus: [[phase-6-005-luther-95-theses]], [[phase-6-007-luther-bondage-of-will]], Augsburg Confession (1530), Apology of the Augsburg Confession (1531), Smalcald Articles (1537), Formula of Concord (1577), Luther's two Catechisms (1529).
- The Reformed corpus: [[phase-6-010-calvin-institutes]], Heidelberg Catechism (1563), Belgic Confession (1561), Canons of Dort (1619), Westminster Confession and Catechisms (1646–1647).
- The Anglican corpus: Book of Common Prayer (1549 Cranmer; revised 1552, 1559, 1662), Thirty-Nine Articles (1571).
- The Radical-Reformation corpus: Schleitheim Confession (1527), Mennonite Confession of Faith.

## Defining doctrines
- *Sola scriptura* — Scripture alone is the final authority for doctrine.
- *Sola fide* — justification by faith alone.
- *Sola gratia* — salvation by grace alone (not by works).
- *Solus Christus* — Christ alone as mediator.
- *Soli Deo gloria* — to God alone the glory.
- Priesthood of all believers.
- Reduction of the seven Catholic sacraments to two (Baptism and Lord's Supper) in most Protestant traditions.
- Rejection of papal supremacy.
- Vernacular Scripture, liturgy, and theology.

## Branches and sub-traditions
- **Lutheranism** — Germany, Scandinavia.
- **Reformed (Calvinism)** — Switzerland, Netherlands, Scotland, France (Huguenots), Hungary; later: New England Puritanism, Presbyterianism.
- **Anglicanism** — England, the global Anglican Communion.
- **Radical Reformation / Anabaptism** — Switzerland, Netherlands, southern Germany; later: Mennonites, Hutterites, Amish.
- **Methodism** (18th c., John Wesley) — Anglican offshoot, then global.
- **Baptists** (17th c. English Separatist origin) — global.
- **Pentecostalism** (1906+, Azusa Street) — global, now the fastest-growing Christian movement.

## Relations to neighbors
- vs. [[tradition-christianity-canonical]] (Catholic): the defining rupture; Trent (1545–1563) formalized the Catholic counter-position; ecumenical reconciliation 1999 (Joint Declaration on Justification) on key doctrinal points.
- vs. Eastern Orthodoxy: theologically more distant; less direct historical conflict.
- vs. [[tradition-renaissance-hermeticism]]: complex — many Protestant intellectuals (Reuchlin, Dee, Fludd, Boehme, Andreae) engaged Hermetic-Kabbalist material; magisterial Protestant orthodoxy generally resisted overt esotericism.
