The druids were the priestly-scholarly-judicial class of ancient Celtic society, attested by Classical authors (Caesar, Strabo, Diodorus Siculus) and preserved in Irish and Welsh medieval literature. They combined the functions of priest, judge, scholar, astronomer, and poet in a single hereditary-and-trained role.

## Primary Sources and Problems

The most extensive ancient description is Julius Caesar's *Gallic War* (VI.13–14), written from the perspective of a military enemy of Gaul. Caesar describes 20 years of training; the deliberate use of memory rather than writing (to prevent the teachings from becoming widely known); and a hierarchy of druidic authority with a chief druid.

The Celtic world did not produce indigenous written records of druidic practice — the filid wrote down mythology after Christianization, but filtered through a later religious framework. What we know of actual druidic practice is reconstructed from hostile Roman sources and from comparative evidence.

## Tree-Lore and the Ogham Alphabet

The druidic relationship to trees was central. The sacred grove (*nemeton* in Gaulish, *nemed* in Irish) was the primary sacred space. Tree knowledge (*bile* = sacred tree) was simultaneously ecological, spiritual, and symbolic knowledge.

The [[alphabet-ogham]] script names each letter after a tree — this is the literary crystallization of the druidic tree-lore system. The bríatharogam system attaching cosmological kennings to each tree-letter may preserve druidic conceptual categories in literary form.

## Oral Transmission and Written Restriction

Caesar explicitly states that the druids refused to commit their teachings to writing "lest they should become public property." This is the classical antecedent of the hypothesis that Ogham was designed as a restricted script — accessible to the trained few, not the Roman-literate public.
