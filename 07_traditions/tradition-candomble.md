# Candomblé

Afro-Brazilian religious tradition developed primarily in Bahia (Salvador), Brazil, by enslaved Africans of Yoruba, Fon, and Bantu origin from the early 19th century onward. The Nagô (Yoruba-derived) nation is the most theologically influential, preserving Yoruba orisha theology, liturgical language (Yoruba as ceremonial language), and Ifá-derived cosmology in a Brazilian context. Candomblé is distinguished from Cuban Lucumí/Santería by its more explicit preservation of African identity and its resistance to Catholic syncretism (though Oxalá/Obatalá-as-Jesus identification remains prominent in some houses). Iemanjá celebrations on New Year's Eve at Rio de Janeiro beaches draw millions of participants. UNESCO recognized the Candomblé tradition-complex as cultural heritage.

## Historical phases
1. **Middle Passage and early formation (~1700–1830)** — Yoruba, Fon, and Bantu enslaved peoples brought to Bahia; maintenance of ritual knowledge in African-language secret societies under conditions of chattel slavery.
2. **Bahian consolidation (~1830–1900)** — the founding *terreiros* (ceremonial houses) established; the Casa Branca do Engenho Velho (c. 1830) and Ilê Axé Opô Afonjá (c. 1910) become defining institutional nodes.
3. **Emergence and legitimation (~1900–1960)** — ethnographic documentation by Edison Carneiro and others; Roger Bastide's comparative sociology of the African diaspora religions; African identity increasingly asserted against Catholic syncretism.
4. **Modern (~1960–present)** — UNESCO recognition; millions of practitioners in Brazil; spread globally through Brazilian diaspora; tension between Nagô-purity movements (desyncreting from Catholicism) and syncretic houses.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **Orisha-saint syncretism ↔ Roman deity-Catholic saint continuity (the vault's clearest case of religious survival through forced overlay)**: Enslaved Yoruba Africans preserved their orisha theology by mapping orishas onto Catholic saints with similar iconographic or functional attributes — Oxalá (Obatalá) = Jesus Christ/Our Lord of the Good End; Iemanjá (Yemoja) = Our Lady of the Immaculate Conception; Ogum ([[ogun]]) = St. George; Xangô ([[shango]]) = St. Jerome; Exu ([[eshu]]) = St. Anthony (or the Devil in hostile readings). This is structurally identical to the syncretism by which Roman deities were absorbed into Catholic saint veneration (Mercury → St. Hermes; Diana → Our Lady / various Mary apparitions; the Roman Terminus → boundary saints). Both cases: a minority religion surviving under a dominant one by mapping its figures onto the dominant religion's approved figures. The Candomblé case is distinguished by its historical conditions — not elite cultural synthesis but survival under chattel slavery.
- **Candomblé / Santería / Vodou cluster (African diaspora religion as cross-Atlantic transmission)**: Candomblé, [[tradition-santeria-lucumi]], and [[tradition-vodou-haitian]] are three independent reconstructions of West African religion (primarily Yoruba and Fon) in three different colonial contexts (Brazil/Portugal; Cuba/Spain; Haiti/France). All three preserve [[olodumare]] as supreme deity, orisha/lwa as divine intermediaries, spirit possession as the primary ritual mechanism, and initiatory transmission through lineage-based houses. The differences — degree of Catholic syncretism, specific orisha-saint mapping, liturgical language — reflect the different colonial Catholic substrate. Together they are the vault's case study in how a religious tradition reconstructs itself across forced displacement and cultural genocide.
- **Iemanjá/Yemoja ↔ Isis Lactans ↔ nursing-mother-of-waters (divine-feminine transmission across the Atlantic)**: The Candomblé figure of Iemanjá — the great mother of the waters, queen of the sea, associated with fertility and the moon — is the West African [[yemoja]] (Yoruba: "Mother whose children are like fish") transplanted to the Atlantic. Her iconography in Brazilian popular devotion merges with the Madonna and positions her on the sea — receiving white offerings on New Year's Eve at the beach. This creates an Atlantic bridge between the Yoruba divine feminine, the Catholic Marian tradition, and (structurally) the Egyptian Isis-on-the-water iconography that itself persisted across the Roman-Christian transition. See [[divine-feminine]].
