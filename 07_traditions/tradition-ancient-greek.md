# Ancient Greek Civilization

## Overview

The civilization of the Greek-speaking world from the consolidation of the *polis* and the adoption of the alphabet around 800 BCE to the absorption of the last Hellenistic kingdom by Rome at Actium in 31 BCE. It is not a state and never was one: at its Classical height it was several hundred small independent communities sharing a language, a poetic corpus, a sacrificial practice and four athletic festivals, and agreeing on almost nothing else.

**What this node covers, and what its neighbours cover.** This is the civilization node — the religion as a *social form*, plus literacy, philosophy, and the transmission out of the tradition. [[tradition-greek-religion]] holds the pantheon, the divine genealogies and the Near Eastern transmission of individual gods; the gods themselves have their own nodes and are wired, not re-listed here. [[tradition-hellenistic-philosophy]] takes over the philosophical schools from 323 BCE. [[tradition-mystery-cults]] and [[tradition-orphic]] hold the initiatory strand in detail. The religion outlived the political frame that this node's `date-end` marks: public Greek cult runs on into the fourth century CE — see [[tradition-greco-roman-paganism]] for the late stratum.

## The shape of the religion

Greek religion had **no church, no creed, no scripture, no clergy as a separate class, no conversion, and no doctrine of the afterlife that most people acted on**. It had no word for "religion." Nothing in it corresponds to belief in the Abrahamic sense: the gods' existence was assumed rather than professed, and no one was ever asked to affirm a proposition. What it demanded was **correct practice** — the right offering, at the right altar, on the right day, by the right person. Impiety (*asebeia*) was a ritual and civic offence, not a failure of conviction, which is why [[socrates]] could be executed for it in 399 BCE without anyone producing a heresy.

### Cult is a function of citizenship

Sourvinou-Inwood's model — the frame most scholarship still works from — is that the *polis* was the unit of religious authority. The city owned the sanctuaries, voted the sacrificial budgets, inscribed the sacred laws on stone, and appointed the priests, usually by election or lot for a fixed term, from the same citizen body that filled the magistracies. A Greek priest was an office-holder with a portfolio, not a spiritual authority; there was no seminary and no doctrine to be trained in. Religion was not a department of civic life but its idiom: an assembly opened with sacrifice, a treaty was sworn to named gods, a colony carried fire from its mother-city's hearth ([[hestia]]).

The nested units ran household → *genos* / phratry → deme → *polis* → the Panhellenic sanctuaries. Each had its own cults, its own dead to tend, and its own calendar. Kindt's critique is worth holding alongside the model: it describes civic cult well and personal, magical and elective religion badly — the [[phase-4-106-greek-magical-papyri|magical papyri]], the curse tablets and the private initiations are not civic at all.

### Sacrifice, and what it actually was

**[[ritual-sacrifice|Animal sacrifice]] (*thysia*) was the central act**, and it was a meal. The animal was led to the altar, sprinkled with water so its head-movement could be read as assent, killed, and butchered; the thigh-bones wrapped in fat and the tail were burned for the god, and **the meat was distributed and eaten by the participants**. For most Greeks, most of the time, eating meat *was* eating sacrifice — the two were not separable, which is why Detienne and Vernant read the sacrificial meal as the mechanism that constituted the civic body. A *hecatomb* is nominally a hundred oxen; the great civic festivals fed the population.

[[hesiod]] gives the aetiology at *Theogony* 535–560: Prometheus tricks Zeus into choosing the bones-and-fat portion, so humans keep the meat. A myth that ends with the gods getting the inedible part is a striking piece of self-awareness in a tradition often described as naive.

Bloodless offerings ran alongside — cakes, incense, first-fruits, libations of wine, milk or honey — and [[votive-offering-cross-tradition|votive dedications]] filled the sanctuaries with the surviving material record: tripods, statuettes, weapons, inscribed thanks.

### Reciprocity, not grace

The logic is *charis*: I gave, so give. Prayers characteristically remind the god of past service and name what is wanted. This is a contractual relation between unequal parties and it carries no expectation of divine love, no promise of salvation, and no moral demand beyond the ritual one. Ethical obligation ran through *xenia* (guest-friendship), oath-keeping and the avoidance of *hubris* — all divinely sanctioned, none doctrinally systematised.

### Pollution, not sin

The Greek concept nearest to sin is *miasma*: pollution incurred by contact with birth, death, sexual activity or bloodshed, contagious, and removable by **[[ritual-purification-cross-tradition|purification]]** — water, fire, pig's blood, time. It is a matter of state rather than of guilt, and it can be contracted innocently: Oedipus pollutes Thebes without having intended anything. The extreme form is the [[pharmakos]], a human scapegoat expelled to carry the city's pollution out with him. Parker (1983) is the standard treatment.

### The festival calendar is the civic calendar

Each city ran its own year of named months and fixed festivals; Athens' is the best documented ([[calendar-athenian-attic]]), reconciled to the solar year by intercalation and later by the [[calendar-metonic-cycle|Metonic cycle]]. Athens held something like 120 festival days a year. The great ones — the Panathenaia with its procession up to the [[acropolis-athens-sacred-precinct|Acropolis]] and the robe for [[athena]]'s statue, the City Dionysia at which **tragedy and comedy were performed as acts of cult**, the women-only Thesmophoria for [[demeter]] — were the occasions on which the city displayed itself to itself. Drama is not a secular art form that happened to use myth; it is a competitive offering to [[dionysus]] at his festival, and Athenian tragedy's habit of putting the gods' justice on trial ([[phase-2-031-aeschylus-oresteia|the *Oresteia*]], [[phase-2-030-euripides-bacchae|the *Bacchae*]]) happened inside the rite, not against it.

### The Panhellenic sanctuaries

The only institutions that were *pan*-Hellenic were sanctuaries and games. Olympia's contests are traditionally dated from 776 BCE ([[ritual-olympic-games]]); the Pythian games at Delphi, the Isthmian and the Nemean were reorganised in the 580s–570s into the four-year *periodos* circuit — Olympic and Pythian every four years, Isthmian and Nemean every two — with a sacred truce protecting travellers. Delphi was administered by an Amphictyony of member peoples. These sanctuaries were where a fractured world could act as one — and, as the Sacred Wars show, where it fought over the right to say so.

### Oracles as a decision procedure

Consultation was **an instrument of public policy**, not a devotional practice. Cities asked [[oracle-of-delphi|Delphi]] whether to found a colony, adopt a cult, or go to war, and the *omphalos* stone there ([[omphalos]]) marked the navel of the earth. The Pythia answered on a limited number of days; Fontenrose (1978) showed that the historically attested responses are mostly plain sanctions of a proposal already framed by the enquirer — the riddling ambiguity of the legends is largely literary. Dodona answered through the oak and by lot; other techniques ran the whole range: [[cleromancy-lot-casting|lot-casting]], [[astragalomancy-knucklebone|knucklebones]], [[divination-molybdomancy-cledonomancy|molten lead and chance utterance]], bird-signs, and the reading of the sacrificial victim's liver before a battle. See [[greek-oracular-divination]].

Healing had its own procedure: the patient slept in the sanctuary of [[asclepius-greek]] and the god prescribed in a dream ([[dream-incubation-asklepieion]], [[temple-incubation]]). Inscribed cure-records at Epidauros are among the frankest religious documents from antiquity.

### Hero cult — the powerful local dead

Distinct from the gods and enormously important: the **hero** is a dead human of outsized power whose cult is tied to a physical tomb and therefore to one place. Heroes are local where gods are shared; they act in a small radius, cure, protect crops and win battles, and can be dangerous. Cities acquired them deliberately — Athens brought Theseus' supposed bones home from Skyros around 475 BCE, a political act performed as a religious one. Founders of colonies were heroised at death, and by the Hellenistic period living rulers were receiving comparable honours, a road that leads to [[apotheosis]] and to the Roman imperial cult. The old textbook rule that heroes got night-time holocausts and gods daytime *thysia* has been substantially dismantled by Ekroth (2002) on the inscriptions: in practice most hero-cult looked like a normal sacrificial meal.

### The dead, and the mysteries as the alternative

The default afterlife is the strengthless shade in Hades — [[charon]]'s ferry, the asphodel, no reward and no punishment for the ordinary dead ([[underworld-descent]]). Funerary duty mattered intensely; expectation did not. **The mystery cults are the exception, and that is their whole significance.** At Eleusis ([[ritual-eleusinian-mysteries]], [[event-eleusinian-mysteries-c1500-bce-396-ce]]) initiates of any status — women, foreigners, slaves — drank the [[substance-kykeon|*kykeon*]], passed a night in the Telesterion and saw something ([[eleusinian-mystery-revelation]]) that guaranteed a better lot after death. The secret was kept for a millennium and is still not known. [[tradition-orphic|Orphic]] and Bacchic initiates carried inscribed [[document-orphic-gold-tablets|gold tablets]] with instructions for the underworld — the plainest evidence that some Greeks did expect a personal destiny. These were **elective and supplementary**, never a rival church: an initiate went on sacrificing in the ordinary civic way the next morning.

## The literate revolution

Around 800 BCE the Greeks adapted the Phoenician consonantal script by re-purposing signs for sounds Greek did not use as **dedicated vowel letters** — the first script to write vowels with letters of their own ([[alphabet-greek-vowel-revolution]]). Its descendants include Etruscan and Latin, Coptic, Gothic, Armenian, Georgian, and — through the ninth-century Byzantine mission to the Slavs — [[alphabet-glagolitic-cyrillic|Glagolitic and Cyrillic]]. Havelock's stronger claim, that alphabetic literacy is the *cognitive precondition* of Greek abstract thought, is a contested T2 position; the transmission of the script is not in doubt. See [[language-ancient-greek]] and, for the Hellenistic common tongue in which the New Testament and the Septuagint were written, [[koine-greek]].

## The philosophical turn

From the sixth century a small literate minority began explaining the world without reference to divine agency — and, unusually, was allowed to do so in public. [[xenophanes]] observed that Ethiopians make their gods snub-nosed and Thracians theirs red-haired, and that oxen would draw gods like oxen; [[philosophy-apeiron|Anaximander]] derived the cosmos from an indefinite principle; [[philosophy-heracliteanism|Heraclitus]] named an ordering *logos*; [[philosophy-eleaticism|Parmenides]] argued that being cannot come from non-being; [[philosophy-atomism-greek|the atomists]] built a world of void and particles. [[philosophy-pythagoreanism|Pythagoreans]] made number the structure of reality and taught transmigration; [[philosophy-sophism|the sophists]] professionalised argument, and Protagoras opened his book on the gods by admitting he could not know whether they exist — the earliest surviving explicitly agnostic statement in Greek.

The tradition then produced its own critique of itself. [[plato]] barred most of [[homer]] from the ideal city because the poets tell lies about the gods, and replaced them with a Demiurge, eternal Forms and an immortal soul; [[aristotle]] built the categories — substance, essence, potency, act, the unmoved mover — that would later carry doctrines no Greek imagined. [[hippocrates]] and the Hippocratic writers argued that the "sacred disease" was no more divine than any other. None of this displaced the cult: [[socrates]] was executed in 399 BCE for impiety and corrupting the young, and the same Athens funded its festivals as before. Philosophy became a *rival* religion only much later, in [[tradition-neoplatonism|Neoplatonism]] — [[plotinus]], [[porphyry]], [[iamblichus]] (who added [[theurgy|theurgic]] ritual), [[proclus]] — which read the myths as allegories of a metaphysical hierarchy and gave the old gods a philosophical afterlife. See [[event-axial-age-800-200bce]], [[philosophy-platonism]], [[philosophy-aristotelianism]], [[philosophy-stoicism]], [[philosophy-epicureanism]], [[philosophy-cynicism]].

## Periodisation

- **Bronze Age substrate (to c. 1100 BCE).** Minoan Crete and Mycenaean Greece; Linear B already names Zeus, Poseidon, Hera, Athena, Dionysus. See [[bronze-age-substrate-of-greek-religion]], [[tradition-minoan-religion]].
- **Archaic (c. 800–480 BCE).** Alphabet, *polis*, colonization, monumental temples, Homer and Hesiod fixed, the Panhellenic sanctuaries established, heavy Near Eastern borrowing (Burkert's Orientalizing Revolution).
- **Classical (480–323 BCE).** Athenian empire and democracy; the [[parthenon-athens|Parthenon]] ([[event-parthenon-construction-447-432-bce|447–432 BCE]]); tragedy; the Peloponnesian War; Socrates, Plato, Aristotle.
- **Hellenistic (323–31 BCE).** Alexander's conquests carry Greek language, cult and civic form to Egypt, Mesopotamia, Iran and the Indus. Ruler cult, Tyche, and large-scale syncretism ([[syncretism-egyptian-greek]]); Alexandria becomes the intellectual capital ([[euclid]], later [[ptolemy-astronomer]] and [[galen-of-pergamon]]); the philosophical schools take their mature form.
- **Under Rome (31 BCE onward).** The political frame ends; the religion and the language do not. Rome absorbs the pantheon by *interpretatio* ([[tradition-roman-religion]], [[tradition-greco-roman-paganism]]), the mysteries spread empire-wide, and Greek remains the language of the eastern Mediterranean — and therefore of the New Testament and of the Church councils.

## How it ended, and how it did not

Public Greek cult was dismantled by Christian emperors over roughly a century: Theodosius I's legislation of 391–392 ([[theodosius-i]]) prohibited sacrifice and closed the temples; the Eleusinian sanctuary fell to Alaric's invasion in 396; and in 529 [[justinian-i|Justinian]] barred pagans from teaching and ended the Athenian Neoplatonic school's public life ([[event-closure-platonic-academy-529]]). Alan Cameron (2011) is the corrective here: this was a long attrition, not a single dramatic death, and much of the drama is retrospective construction.

What did not end was the material. The [[language-ancient-greek|language]], the philosophical vocabulary, the astronomical and medical corpora, and the manuscripts themselves passed into the Christian Greek East — which preserved, argued with, and eventually handed them back to the West. See [[tradition-byzantine-christianity]].

## MASSIVE WIN — the words of a religion without theology became the words of theology itself

Greek religion produced no systematic theology of its own, and its philosophers wrote against the cult rather than for it. Yet the technical vocabulary those philosophers built became the operating language of Christian doctrine — and the doctrine could not have been stated without it.

- **ousia** — Aristotle's term for substance or essence — enters the Nicene Creed of 325 in *homoousios*, "of the same *ousia*" as the Father. See [[event-council-of-nicaea-325]], [[doctrine-trinity-nicene]].
- **hypostasis** — in earlier usage a near-synonym of *ousia*, and treated as such by Nicaea's own anathema — is prised apart from it by [[basil-of-caesarea|Basil]], [[gregory-of-nazianzus]] and [[gregory-of-nyssa]] to yield the settled formula *one ousia, three hypostaseis*. See [[event-council-of-constantinople-381]].
- **physis** and **prosopon** carry [[event-council-of-chalcedon-451|Chalcedon]]'s definition in 451: two natures in one person and one *hypostasis* ([[doctrine-christology-chalcedonian]]).
- **logos** — Heraclitus' ordering principle, the Stoics' cosmic reason ([[logos-stoic]], [[logos-cosmic-reason]]), Philo's mediating Word ([[logos-philonic]]) — opens the Gospel of John ([[logos-johannine]], [[theme-logos-theology]]).
- **energeia** — Aristotle's actuality — becomes the *uncreated energies* of Palamite theology in the fourteenth century ([[gregory-palamas]], [[doctrine-theosis-divinization-orthodox]]).
- The **apophatic** method that Plato's *Parmenides* and Plotinus' One made philosophically respectable becomes, through [[pseudo-dionysius-the-areopagite|Pseudo-Dionysius]], the negative theology of the Christian East ([[doctrine-apophatic-via-negativa]], [[nous-plotinus]], [[neoplatonic-henosis]]).

**The mainstream caution matters as much as the finding.** This is transmission of *terminology and conceptual apparatus*, not Harnack's old claim that Christian dogma is Greek metaphysics in disguise. Ayres (2004) shows the Cappadocian distinction between *ousia* and *hypostasis* has no exact precedent in pagan philosophy: the terms are Greek, the use they were put to was new, and the councils repeatedly bent the vocabulary against its philosophical grain. The vault's claim is the documented one — the words, the categories and the habit of technical definition are Greek; the doctrines are not.

The arc closes twice over. In 529 a Christian emperor shut the Athenian school; in 1438–39 a Byzantine delegation to [[event-council-of-florence-1438-1439|Florence]] brought [[gemistos-plethon]], who lectured Italians on Plato and set Ficino's translation programme in motion. The philosophy the Greek East had suppressed was carried back to the Latin West by the Greek East's own churchmen, at the end.

## Relations to neighbours

- **[[tradition-greek-religion]]** — same civilization, different cut: that node holds the pantheon, the genealogies and the deity-level Near Eastern transmissions (Aphrodite from the Inanna–Ishtar–Astarte chain, the Hesiodic succession myth against the Kumarbi cycle). This node holds the practice and the institutions. Neither should re-list the other.
- **[[tradition-roman-religion]]** — Rome absorbs the Greek pantheon by systematic identification and the Greek philosophical schools by adoption; Latin literature is written with a Greek model in view throughout.
- **[[tradition-minoan-religion]]** — the pre-Greek Aegean substrate visible in the Mycenaean layer and surviving in myth (the Labyrinth, Ariadne, the bull).
- **[[tradition-judaism]]** — Hellenistic Judaism is a Greek-language phenomenon: the Septuagint, [[logos-philonic|Philo]], the Wisdom literature. The confrontation runs both ways, from the Maccabean revolt to the rabbinic borrowing of Greek argumentative form.
- **[[tradition-hermeticism]]**, **[[tradition-hellenistic-alchemy]]** — Greco-Egyptian syntheses produced in Roman Egypt in Greek.
- **[[tradition-byzantine-christianity]]**, **[[tradition-eastern-orthodoxy]]** — the successor civilization in the same language and largely the same territory; see the hinge above.
- **[[tradition-islam]]** — the Abbasid translation movement takes the Greek scientific and philosophical corpus into Arabic, whence it returns to Latin Europe at [[event-toledo-translations-1085-1150|Toledo]]; [[phase-5-048-theology-of-aristotle-arabic|the Arabic *Theology of Aristotle*]] is in fact Plotinus, and the misattribution shaped Islamic philosophy for centuries.

## Disputes

- **Is "polis religion" the right frame?** Sourvinou-Inwood's model organises the civic evidence beautifully and the personal, elective and magical evidence badly. Kindt (2012) argues it should be one lens among several. This node uses it as the organising frame and flags the limit rather than hiding it.
- **Did the Greeks believe in their gods?** Veyne's question is still live. "Belief" may be the wrong category for a religion that never asked for assent; but the trial of Socrates, Aristophanes' jokes and Euripides' interrogations all presuppose that something was at stake.
- **Olympian versus chthonic.** The neat handbook dichotomy — sky gods get daytime meals, underworld powers get night-time holocausts — does not survive the epigraphic record (Ekroth 2002). Treat it as a heuristic, not a structure.
- **How much of it is Near Eastern?** Burkert (1992) and West (1997) document a deep Archaic-period debt; how far this is borrowing versus a shared eastern Mediterranean *koine* is a matter of degree, and the strongest formulations remain contested.
- **What did the alphabet do?** That the Greeks added vowels is fact. That this caused Greek rationalism (Havelock, Ong) is a T2 claim with serious critics, not a finding.
- **When does the tradition end?** 31 BCE (political), 391 (legal), 529 (institutional) or never (the material passes into Byzantium). This node takes 31 BCE for its civilizational frame and says so.
