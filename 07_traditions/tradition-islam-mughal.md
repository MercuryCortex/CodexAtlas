# Mughal Islam

**Mughal Islam** is the distinctive form of Sunni Islam practiced and patronized by the Mughal Empire (1526–1858) in the Indian subcontinent — a synthesis of Timurid Central Asian, Persian Safavid, and Indian subcontinent religious and cultural traditions.

The Mughal tradition encompasses a remarkable internal range:
- **Akbar's Din-i-Ilahi** (~1582): Emperor [[akbar-mughal]]'s syncretic "Divine Faith" that drew from Hinduism, Zoroastrianism, Jainism, and Islam — one of the most systematic premodern experiments in royal religious syncretism. The closest historical parallel to Cao Dai in its explicit synthetic program.
- **Aurangzeb's orthodox reaction**: Emperor [[aurangzeb-emperor]] (r. 1658–1707) reversed Akbar's syncretism, enforcing strict Sunni orthodoxy, reimposing the *jizya* (poll tax on non-Muslims), and executing Guru Tegh Bahadur (1675), the ninth Sikh Guru, after he refused to convert. This execution is the founding martyrdom of the **Khalsa** identity.

**Cross-tradition significance**: The Mughal court was the world's most sophisticated Islamic patronage culture for 150 years — producing the Taj Mahal, Urdu literature and ghazal poetry, miniature painting as sacred art, and court music that synthesized Hindustani and Persian traditions. Simultaneously, its relations with Sikhism ranged from dialogue (Akbar visited the Golden Temple and gave land grants) to execution (Guru Arjan Dev, 1606; Guru Tegh Bahadur, 1675) — the full range of possibilities between syncretic accommodation and violent suppression.
