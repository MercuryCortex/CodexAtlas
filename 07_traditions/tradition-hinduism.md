# Hinduism — the Sanātana Dharma family

## Overview

**Hinduism** is not one religion in the sense that Christianity or Islam is one religion. It is a family of related but genuinely distinct Indian religious traditions — Vaiṣṇava, Śaiva, Śākta, Smārta, Vedāntic, Tantric, and hundreds of regional and caste-specific cults — that share a ritual and conceptual grammar, a body of prestige texts, and a social world, but no founder, no creed, no single scripture, no church, and no central authority competent to say who is inside. It is the third-largest religious complex in the world by adherents (roughly 1.1–1.2 billion, the overwhelming majority in India and Nepal), and by any continuous-attestation measure it is the oldest religious tradition still practised: the [[phase-1-031-rigveda|Rigveda]] has been transmitted orally in an unbroken priestly chain since roughly 1500–1200 BCE.

Because it is a family rather than a religion, the interesting question about Hinduism is not "what does it teach" but **"what holds it together, and who decided that."** That question is where this node starts.

## The name problem — "Hinduism" as an umbrella

The word is foreign in origin and late in application.

- **"Hindu"** descends from Old Persian *Hindu*, itself from Sanskrit *Sindhu* — the river Indus. It entered Greek as *Indos* and Arabic/Persian usage as a **geographic and ethnic** label for the people living beyond that river. Nothing in the term's early history is religious.
- Under the Delhi Sultanate and the Mughals the word functioned mainly as a demographic contrast term — the non-Muslim inhabitants of *al-Hind*. It grouped by exclusion, not by shared doctrine.
- **"Hinduism"** — the abstract noun, the *-ism* — is an English coinage of the **late eighteenth to early nineteenth century**, produced inside the colonial and missionary encounter and consolidated by Orientalist scholarship, census categories, and the codification of "Hindu law."

This has generated one of the sharpest live debates in the study of religion, and the vault records both sides as legitimate T1 scholarship rather than picking a winner:

**The constructionist position (T1).** Von Stietencron, Frykenberg, Richard King and (in a different register) Frits Staal argue that "Hinduism" reifies into a single religion what were, on the ground, several religions — Vaiṣṇavism, Śaivism, Śāktism — no more one faith than Judaism, Christianity and Islam are one faith because they share a region and a scripture-family. On this reading the unity is a colonial artefact with an administrative purpose.

**The counter-position (T1).** [[shankara|Śaṅkara]]'s doxographies, the *ṣaḍ-darśana* ("six viewpoints") schema, and the polemical literature of the sultanate period show Indian thinkers themselves grouping their traditions against outsiders long before any European arrived. David Lorenzen (1999) dates a self-conscious Hindu identity, defined against Islam, to roughly **1200–1500 CE**. Andrew Nicholson (2010) shows the decisive doxographic unification happening between the **twelfth and sixteenth centuries** in thinkers such as Vijñānabhikṣu, who retro-fitted the earlier schools into a single orthodox lineage. On this reading colonialism hardened and administered a unity that already existed in outline.

**The self-designations** are worth separating from the exonym. *Vaidika dharma* ("Vedic dharma") and *varṇāśrama dharma* ("the dharma of station and life-stage") are old insider terms with narrow, mostly Brahmanical reference. ***Sanātana dharma*** ("eternal / perennial dharma") is an old phrase, but its use as a name for the **whole tradition** is largely a nineteenth-century development, adopted in the reform era precisely because a name was now needed to answer the colonial question "what is your religion."

**What, if anything, actually unifies the family.** Scholarship converges on a family-resemblance answer rather than an essence:

1. **Nominal deference to the Veda.** The orthodox *āstika* / heterodox *nāstika* boundary is drawn by acceptance of Vedic authority — which places [[tradition-buddhism|Buddhism]], [[tradition-jainism|Jainism]] and [[philosophy-carvaka|Cārvāka]] outside. This is a *Brahmanical* boundary-drawing device, not a neutral description, and most practising Hindus have never read the Veda.
2. **A shared soteriological grammar** — [[doctrine-karma-action-fruit|karma]], [[doctrine-reincarnation-samsara|saṃsāra]], and [[doctrine-moksha-hindu-liberation|mokṣa]] — held in common with the śramaṇa traditions that reject the Veda, which is exactly why criterion 1 alone cannot do the work.
3. **A shared social order** — varṇa, āśrama, and the four *puruṣārthas* (dharma, artha, kāma, mokṣa).
4. **A shared narrative repertoire** — the epic-Purāṇic world of [[phase-3-095-mahabharata|Mahābhārata]], [[ramayana|Rāmāyaṇa]] and the Purāṇas, which is the actual common culture across region, caste and sect.

## Historical phases

### Phase 0 — the Indus substrate (~2600–1900 BCE): contested

The Harappan cities predate the Vedic corpus and their [[indus-valley-script|script]] is undeciphered, so every claim about religious continuity from them rests on iconography. John Marshall (1931) read seal M-304 as a "proto-Śiva" — a horned, seated, possibly ithyphallic figure surrounded by animals, hence *Paśupati*, lord of beasts. Doris Srinivasan (1975–76, and 1997) dismantled the reading on iconographic grounds, and it is **no longer the mainstream position**. Ritual bathing installations, terracotta female figurines and possible sacred-tree motifs are suggestive of continuities in purity practice and goddess veneration, but the honest statement is that **the Harappan contribution to later Hinduism is real in principle and undemonstrated in detail.** Treat any confident claim in either direction as overreach.

### Phase 1 — Vedic (~1500–500 BCE)

Indo-Aryan-speaking pastoralists composing in Old Indo-Aryan produce the **śruti** ("that which is heard") corpus: four Vedas — [[phase-2-001-rig-veda-family-books|Ṛg]], [[music-sama-veda|Sāma]], Yajur and [[phase-2-003-atharva-veda|Atharva]] — each layered into Saṃhitā (hymns), [[phase-2-006-brahmanas-aranyakas|Brāhmaṇa]] (ritual exegesis), Āraṇyaka (forest texts) and Upaniṣad.

The religion is **sacrificial, not temple-based**: the *śrauta yajña* performed at a fire altar by specialist priests, oriented to [[indra]] the warrior, [[agni]] the fire and mouth of the gods, [[varuna]] the guardian of ***ṛta*** (cosmic order — the direct cognate of Avestan *aša*), and the pressed drink [[soma]]. There are no temples, no images, and no doctrine of rebirth in the earliest stratum. The [[ritual-yajna-vedic-fire|yajña]] maintains the cosmos; the sacrifice *is* the world-machine.

The **Puruṣa Sūkta** (RV 10.90) — a late hymn in the latest book — narrates the dismemberment of a cosmic man ([[purusha]]) from whose mouth, arms, thighs and feet the four *varṇas* emerge. It is the earliest statement of the fourfold social order and the single most consequential ninety lines in Indian social history.

### Phase 2 — Upaniṣadic and śramaṇa (~800–200 BCE)

The oldest Upaniṣads — [[phase-2-012-brihadaranyaka-upanishad|Bṛhadāraṇyaka]] and [[phase-2-013-chandogya-upanishad|Chāndogya]], roughly 7th–6th century BCE (Olivelle 1998) — perform the tradition's decisive interiorization. Sacrifice is re-read as knowledge; the ritual fire becomes breath; and the identity of *ātman* (self) with ***[[brahman-impersonal|brahman]]*** (the absolute) is asserted in the great sayings — *tat tvam asi*, *ahaṃ brahmāsmi*. [[yajnavalkya|Yājñavalkya]]'s *neti neti* ("not this, not this") establishes the apophatic method that Advaita will later systematize.

**Karma, saṃsāra and mokṣa appear together here for the first time**, and they appear simultaneously in the [[tradition-buddhism|Buddhist]], [[tradition-jainism|Jain]] and [[philosophy-ajivika-determinism|Ājīvika]] movements arising in the same eastern Gangetic ferment. The śramaṇa traditions share the problem (bondage in rebirth) and the goal (release) while rejecting Vedic authority and Brahmin priesthood. Whether the complex is Vedic in origin, śramaṇa in origin, or drawn by both from a shared non-Vedic substrate is unresolved.

The [[phase-2-021-shvetashvatara-upanishad|Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad]] is the hinge: the first clearly **theistic** Upaniṣad, identifying the absolute with a personal Rudra-Śiva. Monism opens toward devotion.

### Phase 3 — epic, classical, Purāṇic (~400 BCE – 800 CE)

- **The epics.** The [[phase-3-095-mahabharata|Mahābhārata]] accretes over roughly 400 BCE – 400 CE; the [[ramayana|Rāmāyaṇa]] core is earlier and its growth continues into the early centuries CE (Brockington 1998). Their [[phase-2-017-mahabharata-ramayana-oral-layers|oral layers]] are the tradition's real popular scripture.
- **The [[phase-2-027-bhagavad-gita|Bhagavad Gītā]]** (c. 2nd century BCE – 2nd century CE), embedded in Mahābhārata book 6, performs the synthesis that defines classical Hinduism: it reconciles ritual duty, renunciatory knowledge and personal devotion as three *yogas* converging on one goal, and it makes [[krishna|Kṛṣṇa]] not an aspect of the absolute but the absolute itself.
- **Dharmaśāstra.** The [[phase-3-042-manusmriti|Mānava Dharmaśāstra]] (Manusmṛti, c. 2nd–3rd century CE per Olivelle 2005) codifies varṇa, āśrama and the duties of station. It is a normative Brahmanical text, not a description of how Indians lived, and its later colonial elevation into "Hindu law" gave it an authority it never held in practice.
- **The six darśanas.** Nyāya, Vaiśeṣika, Sāṃkhya ([[philosophy-samkhya]]), [[philosophy-yoga-darshana|Yoga]], [[philosophy-mimamsa-purva-ritual-metaphysics|Pūrva Mīmāṃsā]] and Vedānta crystallize as commentarial schools; [[phase-3-023-yoga-sutras-of-patanjali|Patañjali's Yoga Sūtras]] give the Yoga school its charter. The grouping *as six* is a later doxographic convention, not a classical self-description (Nicholson 2010).
- **The Purāṇic turn.** Sacrifice yields to **temple and image**: [[ritual-puja-hindu|pūjā]], [[darshan-deity-viewing-hindu|darśana]] (seeing and being seen by the deity), pilgrimage and festival. The [[doctrine-trimurti-hindu|Trimūrti]] schema (Brahmā–Viṣṇu–Śiva) and the [[avatar-doctrine|avatāra]] doctrine take shape in the [[phase-4-109-vishnu-purana|Viṣṇu Purāṇa]] and its successors. The old Vedic gods recede: [[indra]] becomes a minor king of a lower heaven; [[vishnu|Viṣṇu]] and [[shiva|Śiva]], marginal in the Rigveda, become supreme.
- **Tantra and the Śaiva Age.** From roughly the fifth–sixth century, initiatory Tantric systems ([[tradition-tantra]], [[ritual-tantra-initiation]]) spread across Śaiva, Śākta, Vaiṣṇava, Buddhist and Jain frames. Alexis Sanderson's "Śaiva Age" thesis (2009) argues Śaivism was the dominant religious formation of early medieval South and Southeast Asia and the donor of ritual grammar to its neighbours.

### Phase 4 — bhakti (~600–1700 CE)

Devotional religion in **vernacular languages** reorganizes Hindu practice from below. The Tamil [[nammalvar|Āḻvārs]] (Vaiṣṇava, including the woman poet [[andal|Āṇṭāḷ]]) and [[tamil-three-nayanars|Nāyaṉārs]] (Śaiva) begin the turn in the sixth to ninth centuries; it moves north over the following millennium — the Vīraśaiva/Liṅgāyat movement of [[basava|Basava]] in twelfth-century Karnataka ([[tradition-lingayat]]), the Sant poets [[kabir|Kabīr]] and his milieu ([[tradition-bhakti-sant]]), [[mirabai|Mīrābāī]], [[surdas|Sūrdās]], [[tulsidas|Tulsīdās]], and [[chaitanya-mahaprabhu|Caitanya]]'s ecstatic Bengali Vaiṣṇavism ([[event-bhakti-movement-c-700-1700]], [[kirtan-call-response-bhakti]]).

Bhakti's structural significance is social as much as theological: it puts scripture in spoken languages, admits women and low-caste poets as authoritative voices, and in the Sant stream mounts an explicit critique of caste and ritualism. John Stratton Hawley (2015) cautions that the *unified* "bhakti movement" is itself partly a modern retrospective frame — the poets are historical, the single movement is a construction.

### Phase 5 — Vedāntic systematization (8th–14th c.)

The **Prasthānatrayī** (Upaniṣads + Brahmasūtra + Gītā) becomes the shared commentarial base on which the schools differentiate:

- **Advaita** (non-dualism) — [[shankara|Śaṅkara]], c. 700–750 CE (traditional dates 788–820). Only brahman is real; plurality is *māyā*. [[philosophy-advaita-vedanta]], [[doctrine-advaita-vedanta]], [[phase-5-005-shankara-brahma-sutra-bhasya]].
- **Viśiṣṭādvaita** (qualified non-dualism) — [[ramanuja|Rāmānuja]], traditionally 1017–1137. Souls and world are the real body of a personal God. [[philosophy-vishishtadvaita-vedanta]], [[doctrine-vishishtadvaita-qualified-non-dualism]], [[phase-5-016-ramanuja-sribhasya]].
- **Dvaita** (dualism) — [[madhva]], traditionally 1238–1317. God, souls and matter are eternally and really distinct. [[philosophy-dvaita-vedanta-madhva]], [[phase-5-022-madhva-brahma-sutra-bhasya]].
- **Bhedābheda** (difference-and-non-difference) — Bhāskara and [[nimbarka|Nimbārka]]. [[philosophy-vedanta-bhaskara]].
- **Śuddhādvaita** (pure non-dualism) — [[vallabhacharya|Vallabha]]. Non-dualism with a real, non-illusory world.

In parallel, Kashmir's non-dual Śaivism ([[tradition-kashmir-shaivism]]) reaches its summit in [[abhinavagupta|Abhinavagupta]] (fl. c. 975–1025) — [[phase-5-014-abhinavagupta-tantraloka|Tantrāloka]], [[phase-5-033-shiva-sutras|Śiva Sūtras]], [[phase-5-006-vijnana-bhairava-tantra|Vijñāna Bhairava]] — while Tamil [[tradition-shaiva-siddhanta|Śaiva Siddhānta]] develops the dualist Śaiva theology, and the [[gorakhnath|Nāth]] yogins produce the haṭha-yoga corpus ([[phase-5-035-hatha-yoga-pradipika]]).

### Phase 6 — the Islamic encounter (~1000–1800 CE)

Sultanate and Mughal rule produced both **temple destruction and temple patronage**, and the historiography of that period is politically charged in present-day India; the vault records the scholarly range rather than a verdict. What is not contested is the **exchange**: Sufi and Sant idioms interpenetrate, and [[tradition-sikhism|Sikhism]] emerges from precisely that Sant milieu with Guru Nānak (1469–1539) — a distinct religion, not a Hindu sect, though its early vocabulary is shared.

The period also produces the vault's cleanest textual-transmission chain out of India: the Mughal prince **Dārā Shukoh's Persian translation of fifty Upaniṣads** (*Sirr-i Akbar*, 1657) → **Anquetil-Duperron's Latin *Oupnek'hat*** (1801–02) → **Schopenhauer**, who called it the consolation of his life and built the *Welt als Wille* around a reading of it. Through Schopenhauer it reaches Nietzsche, Wagner, Deussen and the whole German reception of Indian thought (Halbfass 1988).

### Phase 7 — colonial, reform, modern (1800 – present)

- **Orientalist translation.** Charles Wilkins' English [[phase-2-027-bhagavad-gita|Gītā]] (1785) — the first direct Sanskrit-to-English translation of a Hindu scripture — followed by William Jones and H. T. Colebrooke and the Asiatic Society. The Gītā reaches Emerson, Thoreau and American Transcendentalism, and becomes the most globally circulated Hindu text.
- **Reform.** [[ram-mohan-roy|Rammohan Roy]]'s Brahmo Sabha (1828); [[dayananda-saraswati|Dayānanda Sarasvatī]]'s Ārya Samāj ([[event-formation-of-arya-samaj-1875|1875]]) with its "back to the Vedas" programme and rejection of image worship; [[ramakrishna|Ramakrishna]] and his disciple [[swami-vivekananda|Vivekānanda]], whose address to the **World's Parliament of Religions in Chicago, 1893** established Neo-Vedānta ([[philosophy-neo-vedanta]], [[tradition-hindu-modernism]]) as Hinduism's public face abroad: Advaita as the tradition's essence, all religions as paths to one summit.
- **Twentieth-century figures.** [[sri-aurobindo|Aurobindo]] ([[phase-7-027-life-divine]]), [[ramana-maharshi|Ramaṇa Maharṣi]], [[paramahansa-yogananda|Yogānanda]] ([[phase-7-026-autobiography-of-a-yogi]]), [[nisargadatta-maharaj|Nisargadatta]].
- **Modern postural yoga.** [[yoga-asana-postural|Āsana]] practice as globally exported is substantially a twentieth-century synthesis — Krishnamacharya's Mysore lineage crossed with European physical culture — rather than a direct continuation of the [[phase-3-023-yoga-sutras-of-patanjali|Yoga Sūtras]], which describe almost no postures (Singleton 2010, T1). This is one of the most common popular misconceptions the vault corrects.
- **Anti-caste critique and exit.** [[ambedkar|B. R. Ambedkar]]'s *Annihilation of Caste* (1936) argued that caste is not a corruption of Hinduism but structurally sanctioned by its normative texts; on 14 October 1956 he and several hundred thousand followers converted to Buddhism, founding [[tradition-navayana-buddhism|Navayāna]].
- **Hindutva.** V. D. Savarkar's *Hindutva: Who Is a Hindu?* (1923) and the RSS (1925) define Hindu-ness **territorially and ethnically** — India as both holy land and fatherland — rather than devotionally, and Savarkar himself insisted the category was political, not religious. It is treated in the vault as a modern political theology distinct from the religious tradition: [[tradition-hindutva]], [[phase-7-029-hindutva]] (Jaffrelot 1996, T1).

## Core texts

**Śruti** (revealed, authorless): the four Vedas with their Brāhmaṇas, Āraṇyakas and Upaniṣads — [[phase-1-031-rigveda]], [[phase-2-001-rig-veda-family-books]], [[phase-2-003-atharva-veda]], [[music-sama-veda]], [[phase-2-006-brahmanas-aranyakas]], [[phase-2-012-brihadaranyaka-upanishad]], [[phase-2-013-chandogya-upanishad]], [[phase-2-021-shvetashvatara-upanishad]].

**Smṛti** (remembered, authored): the epics and the [[phase-2-027-bhagavad-gita|Gītā]] ([[phase-3-095-mahabharata]], [[ramayana]]), the Purāṇas ([[phase-4-109-vishnu-purana]], [[phase-5-050-bhagavata-purana]]), Dharmaśāstra ([[phase-3-042-manusmriti]]), the Sūtra literature ([[phase-3-023-yoga-sutras-of-patanjali]], [[mathematics-sulba-sutras]]), the Āgamas and Tantras ([[phase-5-006-vijnana-bhairava-tantra]], [[phase-5-033-shiva-sutras]]), and the technical śāstras ([[phase-3-024-natyashastra]], [[phase-3-036-charaka-samhita]], [[vastu-shastra]], [[shilpa-shastra]]).

The śruti/smṛti distinction is a **claim about authority**, not a description of use: most Hindus have lived their whole religious lives inside smṛti.

## Defining concepts

- ***Dharma*** — not "religion" but order-plus-duty: cosmic regularity and the obligation proper to one's station and stage ([[moral-dharma-svadharma-hindu-station-duty]]). Its Vedic ancestor is *ṛta*.
- ***Karma*** and ***saṃsāra*** — act-and-fruit, and the beginningless round of rebirth it drives ([[doctrine-karma-action-fruit]], [[doctrine-reincarnation-samsara]], [[moral-karma-as-moral-law]]).
- ***Mokṣa*** — release from that round; the fourth and overriding *puruṣārtha* ([[doctrine-moksha-hindu-liberation]]).
- ***Ātman*** and ***brahman*** — the self and the absolute; whether they are identical (Advaita), inseparable-but-distinct (Viśiṣṭādvaita) or eternally separate (Dvaita) is the axis on which Vedānta divides ([[brahman-impersonal]]).
- ***Māyā*** — the world's status as appearance, illusion, or the real creative power of God, depending on the school.
- ***Avatāra*** — divine descent into form; the Vaiṣṇava ten (Matsya, Kūrma, Varāha, Narasiṃha, Vāmana, Paraśurāma, Rāma, Kṛṣṇa, the Buddha, Kalki) is the standard list, and the inclusion of the Buddha is an absorption move ([[avatar-doctrine]]).
- ***Bhakti*** — loving devotion to a personal deity as a sufficient path ([[bhakti-devotion]]).
- ***Pūjā*** and ***darśana*** — image worship and the reciprocal gaze between devotee and deity ([[ritual-puja-hindu]], [[darshan-deity-viewing-hindu]], [[circumambulation-pradakshina-hindu]], [[mantra-japa-hindu]]).
- ***Varṇa*** and ***jāti*** — the fourfold theoretical order and the thousands of actual endogamous groups. Their relation is contested: Dumont's structural reading (*Homo Hierarchicus*, 1966) against Nicholas Dirks' argument (*Castes of Mind*, 2001) that the colonial census and ethnography hardened a far more fluid system. Both T1.
- ***Āśrama*** and the four ***puruṣārthas*** — student, householder, forest-dweller, renunciate; and dharma, artha, kāma, mokṣa as the legitimate human ends.
- ***Aum*** — the syllable as sonic absolute ([[aum-om]], [[music-aum-cosmic-sound]]).

## Cross-tradition transmissions

1. **Indo-Iranian sibling and inversion (T1, MASSIVE-WIN).** Vedic and Avestan religion are branches of one Proto-Indo-Iranian stock: *ṛta*/*aša*, *soma*/*haoma*, *yajña*/*yasna*, *hotṛ*/*zaotar*, Mitra/Miθra. The Gathic reform inverts the valuation — Vedic *devas* (good) become Avestan *daēvas* (demonic), Vedic *asuras* (ambivalent) become *ahuras* (divine). Hinduism preserves the pre-inversion state. [[asura-deva-inversion]], [[tradition-zoroastrianism]], [[tradition-proto-indo-european-religion]].
2. **The soma–haoma chain (T1).** [[theme-soma-haoma-eucharist]], [[soma]].
3. **The śramaṇa branching (T1).** [[tradition-buddhism]] and [[tradition-jainism]] arise inside the same Gangetic milieu, inherit karma/saṃsāra/mokṣa, and reject Vedic authority — the vault's clearest case of a shared problem-set with opposed metaphysics ([[anatman-no-self]] against *ātman*).
4. **Hindu deities absorbed into Buddhism (T1).** Indra becomes Śakra; Brahmā becomes an interlocutor; Gaṇeśa and the dikpālas enter the Vajrayāna maṇḍala. [[theme-buddhist-transmission-of-hindu-deities]].
5. **Southeast Asian transmission (T1).** Śaiva and Vaiṣṇava state cults, Sanskrit court culture and temple grammar carried east by trade and Brahmin ritual specialists: [[angkor-wat-temple-complex]], the [[khmer-empire|Khmer]] and [[sailendra-dynasty|Śailendra]] polities, and Balinese Hinduism as a still-living descendant. [[theme-hindu-buddhist-architectural-continuity]], [[indian-ocean-trade]].
6. **Greek contact (T1 for contact, T2 for philosophical influence).** [[event-alexander-indian-campaign-327-325-bce]], Megasthenes' *Indica*, and the Indo-Greek kingdoms give documented contact. The stronger claim — that Pyrrho's scepticism derives from Indian sources (Beckwith, *Greek Buddha*, Princeton UP 2015) — is an **academic-minority** position, not consensus; the contact is certain, the doctrinal debt is not.
7. **Upaniṣads → Europe (T1, MASSIVE-WIN).** Dārā Shukoh (1657) → Anquetil-Duperron (1801) → Schopenhauer → German Idealism; Wilkins' Gītā (1785) → Emerson and Thoreau → American Transcendentalism → the twentieth-century Western reception of "Eastern" thought (Halbfass 1988).
8. **Ahiṃsā → modern nonviolence (T1).** [[mahatma-gandhi|Gandhi]] reads ahiṃsā out of the Gītā and his Gujarati Jain milieu into a political method; it reaches the American civil-rights movement through King. Note the doctrinal core is more Jain than Vedic ([[moral-ahimsa-nonviolence]], [[tradition-jainism]]).
9. **Mathematics and medicine (T1).** The [[mathematics-sulba-sutras|Śulba Sūtras]]' altar geometry; the decimal place-value system with zero, transmitted west through Arabic mathematics; [[medicine-ayurveda|Āyurveda]] and its humoural-analogue *doṣa* theory; [[alchemy-rasayana-hindu|rasāyana]] alchemy.
10. **Neo-Vedānta → Western esotericism and the New Age (mixed tiers).** Vivekānanda's 1893 Chicago address and the [[tradition-theosophy|Theosophical Society]]'s Indian turn are the main channels by which Vedāntic vocabulary entered Western alternative spirituality. **Tier note:** the historical channel is T1; the Theosophical *doctrinal* claims about ancient Indian wisdom (Blavatsky's root-races and mahatmas) are **T4 — popular claim, rejected**, and are not repeated here as fact. See [[tradition-theosophy]] for the tiered treatment.

## Disputes

**Indo-Aryan origins.** The T1 consensus — comparative philology, archaeology, and since 2019 ancient DNA (Narasimhan et al., *Science*) — is that Indo-Aryan speakers entered the subcontinent from the Central Asian steppe in the second millennium BCE, after the Harappan decline, and that the Rigveda is the product of that arrival. The **"Indigenous Aryan" / "Out of India"** position (Talageri, Kazanas, and a wider popular literature) holds that Indo-Aryan is autochthonous and the Rigveda far older. It is **T3 — alternative-school**: it has published advocates and a substantial readership, it is entangled with [[tradition-hindutva|Hindutva]] identity politics, and it is rejected by mainstream philology, archaeology and genetics (Witzel, "Autochthonous Aryans?", *EJVS* 2001; Narasimhan et al. 2019). The vault records the claim and the rebuttal together; it does not present it as consensus.

**Rigvedic dating.** Mainstream: c. 1500–1200 BCE. Alternative astronomical datings pushing the text to the fourth or fifth millennium BCE (Tilak; Kak) are T3 and not accepted in Vedic philology.

**The Sarasvatī river.** The Rigvedic Sarasvatī is often identified with the palaeo-channel of the Ghaggar-Hakra. The identification is plausible and widely discussed; the **chronology** it is used to support (a pre-Harappan Rigveda) does not follow from it and is disputed.

**The Paśupati seal.** See Phase 0. Marshall's proto-Śiva reading is no longer mainstream (Srinivasan 1975–76, 1997).

**Did "Hinduism" exist before colonialism?** A genuine T1-versus-T1 dispute; see the name-problem section above. Both positions are held by mainstream scholars and both are recorded here.

**Caste: ancient structure or colonial hardening?** Dumont (1966) versus Dirks (2001) versus Susan Bayly's middle position. All T1. Ambedkar's argument that caste is textually sanctioned rather than incidental is a primary-source position in the same debate.

## Relations to neighbouring traditions

- **↔ [[tradition-zoroastrianism]]** — sibling Indo-Iranian branch with inverted theological valuation; see transmission 1.
- **→ [[tradition-buddhism]] / [[tradition-jainism]]** — śramaṇa movements arising within the same milieu, sharing the karma–saṃsāra–mokṣa problem while rejecting Vedic authority; later, major two-way traffic in deities and tantric ritual.
- **→ [[tradition-sikhism]]** — emerges from the Sant bhakti milieu with Guru Nānak; a distinct religion with shared devotional vocabulary, not a Hindu sect.
- **→ [[tradition-navayana-buddhism]]** — Ambedkar's 1956 anti-caste exit; the most consequential modern conversion movement out of Hinduism.
- **↔ Islam** — eight centuries of conflict, patronage, polemic and Sufi–Sant exchange; the Upaniṣadic transmission to Europe runs through a Mughal prince.
- **→ Western philosophy and esotericism** — through the Oupnek'hat chain to Schopenhauer, the Gītā to Transcendentalism, and Neo-Vedānta to the modern global spirituality market ([[tradition-theosophy]], [[philosophy-neo-vedanta]]).
