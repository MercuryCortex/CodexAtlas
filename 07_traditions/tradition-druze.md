# Druze

## Overview
A monotheistic religious tradition that emerged in early-11th-c. Fatimid Cairo around the figure of the caliph al-Ḥākim bi-Amr Allāh (r. 996–1021), whom Druze theology regards as the most recent manifestation of the divine. The founding theological work was done by Hamza ibn Ali and Bahā' al-Dīn al-Muqtanā; the *Rasāʾil al-Ḥikma* (Epistles of Wisdom, 111 epistles) is the closed scriptural canon. The community established itself in the Levantine highlands (Mount Lebanon and Hauran), where it has maintained a distinct cultural-religious identity for a millennium. The community is divided between an initiated minority (*ʿuqqāl*, the "knowers") who have access to the religious texts and a non-initiated majority (*juhhāl*, the "ignorant") who participate in cultural-communal but not esoteric life.

## Distinctive doctrines
- **Divine unity emphasized to the exclusion of Islamic prophetology**: Druze theology denies that Druze are Muslims; the *shahāda* and Islamic ritual obligations do not apply.
- **Reincarnation**: souls transmigrate among Druze bodies; the doctrine is a defining Druze tenet, contra mainstream Islam.
- **Closed community**: Druze do not accept converts (closed since 1043); intermarriage with non-Druze is doctrinally disallowed.
- **Taqiyya**: outward conformity to surrounding religious environment is permissible to protect the community.

## Historical phases
1. **Founding** (1017–1043) — Cairene origin; Hamza's epistolary teaching.
2. **Levantine consolidation** — withdrawal to the mountains; political-confessional autonomy under Ottoman and later Mandate-period rule.
3. **Modern** — political prominence in Lebanon (Jumblatt family), Israel (separate Druze military-political status), Syria.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **al-Ḥākim deification ↔ divine-ruler apotheosis pattern (Abrahamic case of the royal-divinity claim)**: Druze theology regards the Fatimid caliph al-Ḥākim bi-Amr Allāh (r. 996–1021) as a divine or theophanic manifestation — the most recent appearance of the ultimate divine reality in human form. This is structurally parallel to Roman imperial apotheosis (Julius Caesar and Augustus deified posthumously; later emperors during their lifetime), Egyptian pharaonic divine kingship (the living pharaoh as Horus; the dead king as Osiris), and Japanese Shinto imperial divinity (the emperor as descendant of Amaterasu, with formal divine status maintained until 1945). The pattern — a historical ruler elevated to divine status within a religious-political framework — is one of the vault's most cross-cultural recurring structures. The Druze case is notable because it occurs within a tradition that otherwise insists on strict divine transcendence (Islam's rejection of *shirk*/divine association). Al-Ḥākim's disappearance in 1021 — he went out for a night walk and was never found — structurally parallels other hidden/departed divine figures awaiting return.
- **Druze reincarnation ↔ Dharmic rebirth doctrine (the clearest case of transmigration entering an Abrahamic-adjacent tradition)**: The Druze doctrine of metempsychosis — souls transmigrating exclusively among Druze bodies at death, maintaining fixed total population — is the clearest example of reincarnation belief entering a tradition derived from Islam, a religion structurally committed to linear history and bodily resurrection. The Druze transmission route is debated: Neoplatonic soul-theory (via the [[tradition-ismailism]] Fatimid apparatus) is one channel; Pythagorean-Platonic soul-doctrine another. The result is a community that practices what is functionally a Dharmic rebirth cosmology within an Abrahamic institutional framework — and has sustained this synthesis for a millennium.
- **Druze / Yazidi / Mandaean cluster (closed-esoteric survival strategy)**: The Druze, Yazidis ([[tradition-yazidi]]), and Mandaeans form a cluster of small, endogamous, non-proselytizing religious communities in the Levant and Kurdistan that preserved non-mainstream doctrines by refusing conversion and intermarriage with outsiders. All three have survived over a millennium of majority-Muslim pressure. The "closed-esoteric" institutional survival strategy — no converts, strict endogamy, esoteric restriction of inner teaching — is the alternative to proselytizing expansion. The contrast with Christianity and Islam (which survive by growing) is total: these communities survive by staying closed, small, and genealogically defined. The Druze are the largest and most politically successful of the three.
