# Druze

## Overview
A monotheistic religious tradition that emerged in early-11th-c. Fatimid Cairo around the figure of the caliph al-Ḥākim bi-Amr Allāh (r. 996–1021), whom Druze theology regards as the most recent manifestation of the divine. The founding theological work was done by Hamza ibn Ali and Bahā' al-Dīn al-Muqtanā; the *Rasāʾil al-Ḥikma* (Epistles of Wisdom, 111 epistles) is the closed scriptural canon. The community established itself in the Levantine highlands (Mount Lebanon and Hauran), where it has maintained a distinct cultural-religious identity for a millennium. The community is divided between an initiated minority (*ʿuqqāl*, the "knowers") who have access to the religious texts and a non-initiated majority (*juhhāl*, the "ignorant") who participate in cultural-communal but not esoteric life.

## Distinctive doctrines
- **Divine unity emphasized to the exclusion of Islamic prophetology**: Druze theology denies that Druze are Muslims; the *shahāda* and Islamic ritual obligations do not apply.
- **Reincarnation**: souls transmigrate among Druze bodies; the doctrine is a defining Druze tenet, contra mainstream Islam.
- **Closed community**: Druze do not accept converts (closed since 1043); intermarriage with non-Druze is doctrinally disallowed.
- **Taqiyya**: outward conformity to surrounding religious environment is permissible to protect the community.

## Historical phases
1. **Founding** (1017–1043) — Cairene origin; Hamza's epistolary teaching.
2. **Levantine consolidation** — withdrawal to the mountains; political-confessional autonomy under Ottoman and later Mandate-period rule.
3. **Modern** — political prominence in Lebanon (Jumblatt family), Israel (separate Druze military-political status), Syria.
