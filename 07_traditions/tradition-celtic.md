# Celtic Religion (pre-Christian)

## Overview
The diverse religious systems of Iron Age and Roman-period Celtic-speaking peoples — Gauls, Britons, Iberian Celts (Celtiberians, Lusitanians), Galatians, Irish, and others — sharing a partly common pantheon (Lugus/Lugh attested across the Celtic-speaking world from Lyon-Lugdunum to Ireland), a druidic priestly class (Caesar *Bellum Gallicum* VI describes the Gaulish druids; Irish sources describe the *druid* as judge-poet-priest), oral transmission rather than scriptural textuality, cult-sites at sacred springs, groves, and bog-deposits, head-cult, polycephalic and zoocephalic deities, calendric organization around festivals on quarter-day cross-points (Beltane, Lughnasadh, Samhain, Imbolc — partially attested for Iron Age, fully attested in medieval Ireland), and warrior-aristocratic narrative cycles preserved in medieval Insular Celtic redactions.

## Historical phases
1. **Hallstatt and La Tène (~800–50 BCE)** — Iron Age Celtic-speaking peoples expand across continental Europe; iconographic Celtic art tradition emerges (La Tène curvilinear style); cult-sites and ritual deposits (Llyn Cerrig Bach Wales, La Tène Switzerland, Gundestrup cauldron Denmark / Thracian).
2. **Roman conquest and Romanization (~58 BCE – ~400 CE)** — Caesar conquers Gaul (58–51 BCE); Britain conquered (43 CE onward); Roman *interpretatio* identifies Gaulish gods with Roman (Lugus = Mercury; Belenos = Apollo; Taranis = Jupiter; etc.); abundant epigraphic evidence from Roman provincial Celtic territory.
3. **Insular survival (post-Roman, ~400–600 CE in Britain; into the medieval period in Ireland)** — Britain Romanized then partly de-Romanized in Anglo-Saxon period; Ireland never conquered by Rome, allowing distinctively-Celtic Christianity (~5th–6th c. onward).
4. **Christian redaction of Irish material (~700–1200)** — *Lebor Gabála Érenn* ("Book of Invasions"); the four Irish narrative cycles (Mythological, Ulster, Fenian, Historical/Kings); the Welsh Four Branches of the *Mabinogi* (~12th–13th c.). Christian monks redact pre-Christian oral narrative tradition.
5. **Modern reception (17th c. onward)** — antiquarian-romantic druidism (John Aubrey, William Stukeley); 18th-c. neo-druidic revival (Iolo Morganwg's largely-invented "ancient" druidic material); 20th-21st-c. scholarly Celtic-religion studies; modern Druidry and Celtic Reconstructionism.

## Core texts and source materials
- **Irish:**
  - *Lebor Gabála Érenn* ("Book of Invasions" / "Book of the Taking of Ireland") — Christian-era pseudo-historical synthesis ~12th c.
  - Mythological Cycle — Tuatha Dé Danann narratives
  - Ulster Cycle — *Táin Bó Cúailnge*, Cú Chulainn, Conchobar
  - Fenian Cycle — Fionn mac Cumhaill
- **Welsh:**
  - The Four Branches of the *Mabinogi* (Pwyll, Branwen, Manawydan, Math)
  - *Culhwch and Olwen*, *The Dream of Rhonabwy*, *The Dream of Macsen Wledig*
- **Continental:**
  - Caesar, *Bellum Gallicum* VI (esp. ch. 13–18 on the druids; ch. 17 names the Gaulish gods)
  - Lucan, *Pharsalia* I.444–462 (the Druidic-doctrinal passage)
  - Strabo, Diodorus Siculus, Pliny
  - Epigraphic evidence (CIL XIII for Gaul; RIB for Britain)
  - Coligny Calendar (~2nd c. CE Gaulish bronze tablet, the principal pre-Christian Celtic ritual-calendrical document)

## Pantheon
- **Lugus / Lugh** — pan-Celtic skilled / many-skilled god; place-names from Lyon (Lugdunum) to Carlisle (Luguvalium)
- **Dagda** — Irish good-god, father-figure
- **Brigit** — Irish goddess of poetry, smithcraft, healing; absorbed into Saint Brigid
- **Morrigan** — Irish war-goddess
- **Cernunnos** — Gaulish horned god (Gundestrup cauldron; Pillar of the Boatmen)
- **Belenos** — Gaulish solar / healing
- **Epona** — Gaulish horse goddess
- **Manannán mac Lir** — Irish sea god
- **Nuada** — Irish king-god
- **Taranis** — Gaulish thunder
- **Esus** — Gaulish (with disputed iconography)
- **Sucellos** — Gaulish "good striker"
- Many regional / tribal deities

## Defining doctrines (with appropriate source-critical caution)
- *Druidic* priest-judge-poet class (best-attested for Gaul via Caesar; Irish *druíd* continuing into the medieval period)
- Belief in soul-transmigration (Caesar, Lucan; controversial — Christian-era reading?)
- Sacred groves (*nemeton*); spring/water cult
- Head-cult; severed heads as ritual objects
- Otherworld / *Síde* (in Irish, the *aos sí*, fairy-mound dwellers, were the pre-Christian Tuatha Dé Danann driven underground)
- Festivals (Irish): Samhain (Nov 1, season-marker), Imbolc (Feb 1), Beltane (May 1), Lughnasadh (Aug 1)

## Source-critical caveats
- *No continuous pre-Christian Celtic religious text survives.* All sources are: (a) hostile Roman-period ethnography; (b) Insular medieval Christian redactions of older oral narrative; (c) archaeological / epigraphic. Direct reconstruction of pre-Christian Celtic theology is therefore difficult and contested.
- The *Druids* of romantic-modern imagination (especially Iolo Morganwg's 18th-c. inventions) are largely a 17th–19th-c. construction with thin ancient warrant. Hutton 2007 is the standard demolition of romantic-druidic myth and recovery of what little is actually attested.
- The "Celtic" categorial label is itself contested in modern archaeology — whether there was a unified Celtic identity in antiquity, or only loosely-related Celtic-speaking peoples, is debated (Collis 2003; James 1999).

## Relations to neighbors
- vs. [[tradition-norse]]: separate Indo-European traditions in Northwest Europe; structural parallels (warrior-poetry, tree-cosmology); contact especially in Viking-Age Britain and Ireland.
- vs. [[tradition-roman-religion]]: extensive Roman *interpretatio* identification of Gaulish gods with Roman; provincial Roman-Celtic synthesis in epigraphy and cult.
- vs. [[tradition-christianity-canonical]]: Insular Celtic Christianity (~5th–6th c. onward) absorbed substantial pre-Christian narrative material; Brigit-as-Christian-saint is the paradigm syncretic case.
- vs. [[tradition-wicca-modern-pagan]]: modern Druidry and Celtic Reconstructionism draw on Celtic material; their relation to genuine pre-Christian Celtic religion is uneven.
