# Sikhism

## Overview
Monotheistic religion founded in the Punjab by Guru Nanak (1469–1539), developed through a line of ten human Gurus (1499–1708), and continuing thereafter under the authority of the *Guru Granth Sahib* — the scripture installed as the eleventh and eternal Guru in 1708. Sikh theology centers on Ek Onkar (the One Divine Reality), accessed through *nam-simran* (remembrance of the Divine Name) and the *shabad* (the divine Word as preserved in the scripture). The community (*Panth*) is organized around the Sangat (congregation), the Langar (free communal kitchen, a radical caste-defying institution), the Gurdwara (temple), and (since 1699) the Khalsa (the disciplined martial-religious order founded by Guru Gobind Singh). With ~25 million adherents worldwide, Sikhism is the fifth-largest organized religion.

## Historical phases
1. **The Ten Gurus** (1499–1708) — Guru Nanak through Guru Gobind Singh; foundational doctrine, the compilation of the Adi Granth (1604), the political conflict with the Mughal Empire, and the closing of the canon.
2. **18th-century survival and consolidation** — the Sikh confederacies (Misls) and political-military development under Mughal collapse.
3. **Sikh Empire** (1799–1849) — Ranjit Singh's kingdom of Punjab.
4. **British colonial period** (1849–1947) — Sikh military prominence in British India, the Singh Sabha reform movement (1873+), the institutional consolidation of modern Sikhism, the Akali movement for Gurdwara reform.
5. **Post-1947 diaspora and Partition** — substantial trauma of 1947 Partition; growing global diaspora; 1980s–1990s Khalistan movement; ongoing community in Punjab and worldwide.

## Core texts
- [[phase-6-016-guru-granth-sahib]] — the central scripture and eternal Guru.
- The *Dasam Granth* (attributed to Guru Gobind Singh; status partly contested).
- The *Janamsakhi* hagiographies of Guru Nanak (~1570 onward).
- Bhai Gurdas's *Vars* (early-17th-c. exegetical-praise compositions).

## Defining doctrines
- **Ek Onkar / Akal Purakh** — the single, formless, eternal divine reality, beyond image and confessional name, who is the source of all.
- **Nam-simran** — remembrance of the Divine Name as the supreme spiritual practice for the present age.
- **Shabad (the Word)** — the divine self-communication that is the Guru's voice and is fixed in the Granth.
- **Equality** — radical theological and social equality across caste, gender, and religious origin; the langar institutionalizes this.
- **Householder spirituality** — Sikhism rejects asceticism in favor of religious life within ordinary family and work life.
- **The Three Pillars** — *Naam Japo* (meditate on the Divine Name), *Kirat Karo* (earn honest livelihood), *Vand Chhako* (share with others).
- **Miri-Piri** — temporal and spiritual sovereignty integrated; the Sikh community defends itself politically and militarily when needed (institutionalized from Guru Hargobind).
- **The Khalsa** (1699+) — the disciplined initiated community marked by the Five Ks.

## Pantheon
Sikhism is strictly monotheistic; the One is referred to under multiple non-confessional names (Ek Onkar, Vahiguru, Akal Purakh, Sat Nam, Ram, Hari, Allah — the last two used non-sectarianly within the scripture). No pantheon; the Granth incorporates references to Hindu and Muslim figures but does not deify any of them.

## Relations to neighbors
- vs. [[tradition-vedic-hinduism]] / Hindu Sant tradition: substantial heritage (Kabir, Ravidas, Namdev are in the Adi Granth); Sikhism is institutionally distinct.
- vs. [[tradition-sufism]] / [[tradition-islam]]: Sheikh Farid's Punjabi Sufi verses are in the Adi Granth; the Sikh anti-idolatry and divine-uniqueness parallel Islamic theology but are not derived from it.
- vs. [[tradition-jainism]]: shared subcontinental religious milieu; minor direct interaction.
