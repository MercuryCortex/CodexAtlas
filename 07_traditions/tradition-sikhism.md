# Sikhism

## Overview
Monotheistic religion founded in the Punjab by Guru Nanak (1469–1539), developed through a line of ten human Gurus (1499–1708), and continuing thereafter under the authority of the *Guru Granth Sahib* — the scripture installed as the eleventh and eternal Guru in 1708. Sikh theology centers on Ek Onkar (the One Divine Reality), accessed through *nam-simran* (remembrance of the Divine Name) and the *shabad* (the divine Word as preserved in the scripture). The community (*Panth*) is organized around the Sangat (congregation), the Langar (free communal kitchen, a radical caste-defying institution), the Gurdwara (temple), and (since 1699) the Khalsa (the disciplined martial-religious order founded by Guru Gobind Singh). With ~25 million adherents worldwide, Sikhism is the fifth-largest organized religion.

## Historical phases
1. **The Ten Gurus** (1499–1708) — Guru Nanak through Guru Gobind Singh; foundational doctrine, the compilation of the Adi Granth (1604), the political conflict with the Mughal Empire, and the closing of the canon.
2. **18th-century survival and consolidation** — the Sikh confederacies (Misls) and political-military development under Mughal collapse.
3. **Sikh Empire** (1799–1849) — Ranjit Singh's kingdom of Punjab.
4. **British colonial period** (1849–1947) — Sikh military prominence in British India, the Singh Sabha reform movement (1873+), the institutional consolidation of modern Sikhism, the Akali movement for Gurdwara reform.
5. **Post-1947 diaspora and Partition** — substantial trauma of 1947 Partition; growing global diaspora; 1980s–1990s Khalistan movement; ongoing community in Punjab and worldwide.

## Core texts
- [[phase-6-016-guru-granth-sahib]] — the central scripture and eternal Guru.
- The *Dasam Granth* (attributed to Guru Gobind Singh; status partly contested).
- The *Janamsakhi* hagiographies of Guru Nanak (~1570 onward).
- Bhai Gurdas's *Vars* (early-17th-c. exegetical-praise compositions).

## Defining doctrines
- **Ek Onkar / Akal Purakh** — the single, formless, eternal divine reality, beyond image and confessional name, who is the source of all.
- **Nam-simran** — remembrance of the Divine Name as the supreme spiritual practice for the present age.
- **Shabad (the Word)** — the divine self-communication that is the Guru's voice and is fixed in the Granth.
- **Equality** — radical theological and social equality across caste, gender, and religious origin; the langar institutionalizes this.
- **Householder spirituality** — Sikhism rejects asceticism in favor of religious life within ordinary family and work life.
- **The Three Pillars** — *Naam Japo* (meditate on the Divine Name), *Kirat Karo* (earn honest livelihood), *Vand Chhako* (share with others).
- **Miri-Piri** — temporal and spiritual sovereignty integrated; the Sikh community defends itself politically and militarily when needed (institutionalized from Guru Hargobind).
- **The Khalsa** (1699+) — the disciplined initiated community marked by the Five Ks.

## Pantheon
Sikhism is strictly monotheistic; the One is referred to under multiple non-confessional names (Ek Onkar, Vahiguru, Akal Purakh, Sat Nam, Ram, Hari, Allah — the last two used non-sectarianly within the scripture). No pantheon; the Granth incorporates references to Hindu and Muslim figures but does not deify any of them.

## Relations to neighbors
- vs. [[tradition-vedic-hinduism]] / Hindu Sant tradition: substantial heritage (Kabir, Ravidas, Namdev are in the Adi Granth); Sikhism is institutionally distinct.
- vs. [[tradition-sufism]] / [[tradition-islam]]: Sheikh Farid's Punjabi Sufi verses are in the Adi Granth; the Sikh anti-idolatry and divine-uniqueness parallel Islamic theology but are not derived from it.
- vs. [[tradition-jainism]]: shared subcontinental religious milieu; minor direct interaction.

## Guru Nanak's founding synthesis: the intentional boundary crossing

[[guru-nanak]] (1469–1539) was born into a Hindu family in Talwandi (present-day Nankana Sahib, Pakistan) in a village where Hindu and Muslim communities shared daily life. He received a traditional Punjabi education, encountered both Hindu and Islamic intellectual culture, and made pilgrimage to Mecca as well as major Hindu sacred sites. His founding insight, formulated after the formative mystical experience at age 30 ("There is no Hindu, there is no Muslim"), was not a synthesis of the two traditions but a transcendence of the very category of confessional boundary.

The opening formula of the [[phase-6-016-guru-granth-sahib]] — *Ik Oṅkār* (ੴ, "The One Is") — is a single grapheme that cannot be articulated in any single confessional vocabulary. It is not Allah. It is not Brahman. It gestures at an ultimate reality that all confessional names inadequately approximate.

Nanak drew from three primary streams:
- **Hindu *bhakti* devotion**: specifically the *nirguṇa* (formless, beyond attributes) strand of the Sant tradition — Kabir, Ravidas, Namdev — which rejected image worship, caste hierarchy, and Brahminic ritual authority in favor of direct devotional relationship with the formless divine.
- **Islamic Sufi *tawhīd***: the Sufi insistence on divine unity (*tawhīd*), the *fanāʾ* (self-annihilation in God), and direct personal relationship with the divine unmediated by legalistic structures. Sheikh Farid's Punjabi Sufi poetry is incorporated into the Adi Granth as canonical.
- **Rejection of caste and ritual hierarchy**: shared with both the Sant bhakti tradition and certain Sufi lineages; radicalized in Sikh practice through the *langar* institution.

## MASSIVE WIN 1: Sikhism as the clearest case of intentional syncretism

The vault documents many cases of organic syncretism — traditions that blend gradually through cultural contact (Sanjiao, shinbutsu-shūgō, Greco-Roman interpretatio). Sikhism is the vault's clearest instance of *intentional* founding syncretism: Nanak's "There is no Hindu, there is no Muslim" is a deliberate founding repudiation of existing religious boundaries as his first theological proposition. The [[phase-6-016-guru-granth-sahib]] itself embeds this intention structurally — it is the only major world scripture that incorporates texts from multiple other religious traditions as part of its canonical devotional core:

| Contributor | Tradition | Hymns in Adi Granth |
|---|---|---|
| [[kabir]] | Sant bhakti (Hindu milieu, possibly Sufi influence) | 541 hymns — the largest non-Sikh contribution |
| Sheikh Farid | Punjabi Sufi Islam | 134 *śloks* |
| Ravidas | Dalit Sant tradition | 41 hymns |
| Namdev | Vaiṣṇava Sant tradition (Maharashtra) | 60 hymns |
| Beni | Sant tradition | 3 hymns |
| The Sikh Gurus | Sikhism | ~5,894 hymns |

This is not citation or quotation — these are canonical devotional texts treated with the same ritual authority as the Gurus' own compositions. The scripture IS the inter-religious dialogue, fixed in permanent canonical form.

Structural comparisons:
- **Bahā'ī**: another explicitly boundary-transcending religion with a founding prophet; prophetic architecture similar (Nanak/Bahā'u'llāh; ten Gurus/Letters of the Living); both claim to supersede prior confessional boundaries. See [[tradition-baha-i]].
- **Akbar's *Dīn-i-Ilāhī*** (~1582): a Mughal court experiment in deliberate syncretic religion, roughly contemporary with the later Sikh Gurus; both tried to dissolve Hindu-Muslim boundaries from a position of political power. The structural parallels are documented; the historical relationship between Akbar's Mughal court and the Sikh Gurus is direct (Guru Arjan was executed by Jahangir in 1606).

## MASSIVE WIN 2: Langar — sustained large-scale religious egalitarianism as daily practice

The *langar* (community kitchen) attached to every Gurdwara serves free vegetarian meals to all visitors regardless of religion, caste, gender, or social status. Everyone sits on the floor together (*pangat* — "row") and eats the same food. No hierarchy of seating. No distinction of servings. Founded by Guru Nanak; institutionalized by Guru Amar Das (who required all visitors, including the Mughal Emperor Akbar, to eat in the langar before meeting him).

The Golden Temple (*Harmandir Sahib*, Amritsar) langar serves approximately 100,000 free meals per day, 365 days per year — making it one of the largest free kitchens in human history, operated entirely by volunteer service (*sevā*). The four doors of the Golden Temple face all four cardinal directions, an architectural statement: the temple is open to all people from all directions.

This is not doctrine about equality — it is daily enacted equality, sustained institutionally for 500 years. The cross-tradition structural comparison:

| Tradition | Institution | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Sikhism | *Langar* (~1500 CE onward) | Free communal meal for all; caste hierarchy dissolved by shared eating |
| Early Christianity | *Agapē* feast (1st–3rd c. CE) | Communal meal at worship; dissolves social hierarchy; Paul rebukes Corinthians for class differentiation (1 Cor 11:17–22) |
| Sufism | *Samāʿ* / *sama* gathering | Inclusive communal gathering; music as devotion accessible to all; dervish lodges fed the poor |
| Buddhism | *Dāna* meal (Vinaya tradition) | Monks receive food from all donors without social distinction; the meal is the social-leveling act |

The langar is the vault's most sustained and largest-scale institutional enactment of religious egalitarianism as physical daily practice. See [[tradition-sufism]].

## MASSIVE WIN 3: Guru Granth Sahib as living person — scripture as Guru

The declaration of the Guru Granth Sahib as the eternal, living Guru (1708) is theologically radical: the scripture IS the teacher, not a record of teaching. The Guru Granth is treated with the physical respect accorded to a living person:

- Carried on the head (never held at waist level or below)
- Given its own room (*takht*, throne) in the Gurdwara
- Put to rest at night with ceremony (*sukhasan*) and wakened in the morning (*prakash*)
- Never placed below human eye level when being read
- Fanned with a chauri (flywhisk) as one fans a living dignitary

This is the vault's most developed case of **scripture-as-person** — a category that also includes:

| Tradition | Treatment of scripture as person |
|---|---|
| Sikhism | Guru Granth Sahib IS the living Guru; physical reverence as for royalty |
| Judaism | Torah scroll carried in procession; congregation stands; kissed; dressed in mantle; given its own ark |
| Islam | Quran never placed on floor; carried carefully; ritual purity required to touch; read with ceremonial care |
| Ethiopian Orthodox | Ark of the Covenant (*Tabot*) carried in procession; congregants prostrate |

The Guru Granth Sahib extends this further than any other tradition: it holds the legal status of a living Guru. Modifications to its text are not permitted. Its "death" is impossible. It is in this sense indestructible. See [[phase-6-016-guru-granth-sahib]].

## The Khalsa and the sant-sipāhī ideal

[[guru-gobind-singh]] founded the Khalsa (*Khālsā*, "the pure") on Vaisakhi 1699 at Anandpur Sahib. The initiation ceremony (*amrit sanscar*) created a new category of Sikh: marked by the Five Ks (*pañj kakār* — keś, kaṅghā, kaṛā, kacchā, kirpān), bound by a code of conduct, and committed to both spiritual discipline and defense of the oppressed. The founding mythology is precise: Guru Gobind Singh called for volunteers willing to die for the faith; five men stepped forward (the *Pañj Pyāre*, "the Five Beloved Ones"), who were then initiated as the first Khalsa.

The *sant-sipāhī* (saint-soldier) ideal unites contemplative religious life with readiness for martial action in defense of the weak — not aggressive expansion but protective force. This is structurally parallel to the medieval Christian warrior-monk orders:

| Tradition | Institution | Saint-Soldier ideal |
|---|---|---|
| Sikhism | Khalsa (1699+) | *Sant-sipāhī*: spiritual discipline + martial readiness to defend the oppressed |
| Christianity | Knights Templar (1119+) | Monk + soldier; Cistercian spiritual discipline + military service; "soldiers of Christ" | 
| Christianity | Hospitallers / St. John (1080+) | Care for the sick + military defense of pilgrims; prayer + sword |
| Islam | *Ribāṭ* / *Murābiṭūn* | Frontier garrison monks; Sufi-military fusion in North African tradition |

The crucial distinction: the Khalsa Sant-sipāhī ideal is explicitly defensive — *Deg Tegh Fateh* ("the cauldron of charity + the sword of protection"). The militant energy is directed toward protecting those who cannot protect themselves, not toward conquest. See [[tradition-knights-templar]].
