The Vedic religion is the earliest attested Indo-Aryan religious tradition in South Asia, centered on the Vedas — a corpus of Sanskrit hymns, ritual instructions, and philosophical texts composed from c. 1500–500 BCE and preserved through an extraordinarily rigorous oral transmission tradition (sruti, "that which is heard").

## Core Features

**The Sacrifice (yajna):** The Vedic cosmos is sustained by ritual sacrifice — an elaborate exchange between humans and devas (divine beings) mediated by fire (Agni) and the Soma drink. The Agnicayana ("piling of the fire altar") documented by Frits Staal in Kerala in 1975 is a surviving 3,000-year-old Vedic ritual.

**Oral Transmission (sruti):** Vedic texts were considered too sacred to write down for approximately 1,000 years — the Brahmin priestly class preserved them through memorization using elaborate mnemonic-recitation techniques (vikrti paths, ghana-patha) that allowed verification of exact transmission. Modern acoustic analysis confirms the precision of preservation.

**Vak and the Power of Sound:** The goddess [[vac-goddess]] (Vak) is sacred speech personified; the Vedic tradition holds that precisely pronounced Sanskrit phonemes have direct cosmic effects. This is the root of the later mantra science codified in [[alphabet-devanagari-sacred]].

**Devas and Asuras:** The cosmic conflict between the shining devas (Indra, Varuna, Mitra, Agni, Soma, Vishnu, Rudra) and the asuras forms the dramatic narrative of the Vedic cosmos.

## Relationship to Later Hinduism

Vedic religion is the primary root of [[tradition-hinduism]], though later Hinduism represents a significant transformation: the centrality of the yajnic sacrifice diminished; devotional worship (puja) and temple religion emerged; the Upanishadic philosophical tradition (c. 800–400 BCE) refocused religious attention from sacrifice to inner realization.
