# Byzantine Christianity (the Church of the Eastern Roman Empire)

## Overview

Byzantine Christianity is the Chalcedonian church of the Eastern Roman Empire, from the dedication of Constantinople on 11 May 330 to the city's fall on 29 May 1453. It is a **historical formation, not a confession**: an imperial church, in Greek, run from a capital that thought of itself as the continuation of Rome. Its people called themselves *Rhōmaioi* — Romans — and used *Hellene* to mean *pagan*; "Byzantine" is a sixteenth-century coinage by a German scholar for an empire that never heard the word.

**Where this node sits relative to its neighbours.** [[tradition-eastern-orthodoxy]] is the **confession** — the doctrines (theosis, palamism, apophaticism, the theology of the icon) as a living communion of some 260 million people, of which this tradition is the historical root. [[tradition-greek-orthodoxy]] is one of its national successors. [[tradition-early-christianity]] is the pre-Constantinian stratum. **This node is the empire's church**: the councils as imperial events, the liturgy as the state's central rite, the mission that built a commonwealth, and the long divergence from the Latin West. When the empire ended, the church did not — it continued without a state, which is where the Orthodoxy node picks it up.

## The empire's church

### Symphonia — two powers, one body

The Byzantine settlement of church and state is *symphonia*, stated classically in the preamble to [[justinian-i|Justinian]]'s *Novella* 6 (535): priesthood and empire are the two greatest gifts of God, proceeding from one source, and the concord between them is the health of the world. In practice the emperor convened and funded councils, ratified their acts as imperial law, appointed and deposed patriarchs, and legislated on doctrine — while the priesthood alone could baptise, absolve, ordain and consecrate, and the emperor was a layman at the altar rail.

**"Caesaropapism" is a polemical Western label that most Byzantinists now reject** (Dagron 2003). The record does not support it: [[athanasius-of-alexandria|Athanasius]] went into exile four times rather than accept an emperor's doctrine, [[john-chrysostom|Chrysostom]] was banished for preaching against an empress, [[maximus-the-confessor|Maximus the Confessor]] had his tongue and right hand cut off for refusing the imperial [[doctrine-monothelitism|monothelite]] formula, and the iconoclast emperors lost their century-long argument to monks. The relation was a permanent negotiation in which the emperor usually held the instruments and the church usually held the outcome.

The **five-see model** — Rome, Constantinople, Alexandria, Antioch, Jerusalem, later named the pentarchy — was the East's ecclesiology. Rome had the first place of honour; it did not have jurisdiction over the other four. That is the structural disagreement with the Latin West, and it long predates 1054.

### The councils as imperial events

The seven councils the tradition recognises as ecumenical were summoned by emperors, held on imperial territory at imperial expense, and given force by imperial law:

- **[[event-council-of-nicaea-325|Nicaea I, 325]]** — [[constantine-the-great|Constantine]]; the Son *homoousios* with the Father, against [[doctrine-arianism|Arius]]. See [[doctrine-trinity-nicene]].
- **[[event-council-of-constantinople-381|Constantinople I, 381]]** — Theodosius I; the creed completed, the Spirit's divinity affirmed after [[phase-4-044-basil-on-the-holy-spirit|Basil's treatise]]. Canon 3 gives the new capital's bishop precedence after Rome.
- **[[event-council-of-ephesus-431|Ephesus, 431]]** — Theodosius II; [[cyril-of-alexandria|Cyril]] against Nestorius; Mary confirmed as *Theotokos* ([[mary-theotokos]]).
- **[[event-council-of-chalcedon-451|Chalcedon, 451]]** — Marcian and Pulcheria; two natures in one person and one *hypostasis* ([[doctrine-christology-chalcedonian]], [[doctrine-incarnation]]), with [[phase-4-050-leo-tome-to-flavian|Leo's Tome]] acclaimed. **Canon 28** grants Constantinople privileges equal to Old Rome's, *because it is New Rome* — Rome refused to ratify it, and the refusal is the seed of everything that follows.
- **[[event-council-of-constantinople-553|Constantinople II, 553]]** — Justinian; the Three Chapters condemned in an attempt to win the miaphysites back, and Origenist propositions anathematised ([[doctrine-apokatastasis-universal-restoration]], [[origen]]).
- **[[event-council-of-constantinople-680-681|Constantinople III, 680–81]]** — Constantine IV; two wills in Christ, reversing the imperial [[doctrine-monothelitism|monothelite]] policy and vindicating the dead Maximus.
- **[[event-council-of-nicaea-787|Nicaea II, 787]]** — the Empress Irene; icons restored ([[icon-orthodox-image-theology]]).

**Every definition cost the empire a church.** After 431 the [[tradition-church-of-the-east|Church of the East]] went its own way; after 451 the miaphysite churches of Egypt, Syria and Armenia ([[tradition-coptic-orthodox]], [[tradition-syriac-orthodox]], [[tradition-armenian-christianity]]) separated permanently. When the Arab armies took Syria and Egypt in the 630s and 640s, the empire lost provinces whose Christians it had spent two centuries anathematising — though the older claim that they welcomed the conquest as deliverance is now heavily qualified (Hoyland 2015).

### The liturgy is the tradition's central artefact

Where the Latin West made law its characteristic product, the Greek East made **worship**. The Divine Liturgies of St [[john-chrysostom|John Chrysostom]] and St [[basil-of-caesarea|Basil]] are still celebrated substantially unchanged; the sixth-century kontakia of Romanos the Melodist and the Akathist hymn to the Theotokos are among the great bodies of Christian poetry; the chant tradition runs unbroken to the present ([[music-byzantine-chant-ison]]). Time itself was liturgical — the [[julian-calendar]], the [[calendar-computus-easter-dating|paschal computus]], the [[liturgical-year-christian|church year]].

The setting matched the rite. [[hagia-sophia|Hagia Sophia]] (532–537), with its floating dome on [[architectural-pendentive-dome|pendentives]], gold mosaic and marble, was built to be **an argument about God made in space** — and the Rus' conversion narrative in the Primary Chronicle turns on envoys who could not tell, standing in it, whether they were in heaven or on earth. That story is a literary construction written a century later, but it records accurately what the tradition believed its own liturgy did.

### Monasticism and the interior life

Monasticism was the tradition's spiritual authority and its political counterweight. The Stoudios monastery in the capital drove the iconophile resistance; [[st-catherine-monastery-sinai|St Catherine's at Sinai]], continuously inhabited since the sixth century and outside imperial control after the Arab conquest, preserved the icons that iconoclasm destroyed elsewhere; the Great Lavra on Mount Athos (963) began the monastic republic that still governs itself. See [[attire-orthodox-monastic-schema-mantle]].

Its inward discipline is **hesychasm** — stillness, the Jesus Prayer, the vision of uncreated light ([[hesychasm-jesus-prayer]]). Contested by the Calabrian Barlaam in the fourteenth century, it was vindicated for [[gregory-palamas|Palamas]] at Constantinople in 1341, 1347 and 1351 with the distinction between God's unknowable essence and his participable energies. See [[phase-5-030-palamas-triads]], [[doctrine-theosis-divinization-orthodox]], and [[tradition-eastern-orthodoxy]] for the doctrines themselves.

### Iconoclasm — the crisis it settled against its emperors

For 117 years (726–843, in two phases) the empire fought itself over whether images of Christ and the saints could be made and venerated. Leo III began the removals; Constantine V made iconoclasm a theology and a persecution; the Council of Hieria (754) ratified it; Nicaea II (787) reversed it; a second iconoclasm from 815 was ended for good on 11 March 843, the **Triumph of Orthodoxy**, still commemorated annually. Both restorations were carried by a regent empress. The winning argument, formulated by [[john-of-damascus|John of Damascus]] — living under Umayyad rule and therefore beyond the emperor's reach — is incarnational: God took matter, so matter can bear the holy, and honour paid to the image passes to its prototype. See [[event-iconoclast-controversy-726-843]], [[theme-iconoclasm]], [[icon-orthodox-image-theology]].

### The commonwealth — Byzantium exported by translation

Byzantium's most consequential act was a linguistic one. In 863 the emperor sent Constantine-Cyril and his brother Methodius to Moravia; they **invented an alphabet for a language that had none** and translated scripture and liturgy into it, against the Frankish position that only Hebrew, Greek and Latin were fit for worship. Old Church Slavonic became a third liturgical language ([[language-old-church-slavonic]], [[alphabet-glagolitic-cyrillic]]).

This is the structural difference from Rome, and it produced different maps. Bulgaria converted in 864, Kievan Rus' in 988, Serbia and the Romanian lands in turn; Georgia was already in the orbit ([[language-old-georgian]], [[tradition-georgian-orthodox]]). Obolensky named the result the **Byzantine Commonwealth**: a family of self-governing churches sharing a rite, a canon law and a calendar, each worshipping in its own tongue, with Constantinople first among them rather than over them. After 1453 the Russian church inherited the position, and Filofei of Pskov gave it the Third Rome doctrine.

## 1054 from the eastern side

The Latin account of the schism is on [[tradition-roman-catholicism]], written from Rome's vantage. The facts are the same; the shape of the story is not.

**Constantinople did not experience 1054 as a schism.** On 16 July 1054 Cardinal Humbert laid a bull of excommunication on the altar of Hagia Sophia; Patriarch Michael Keroularios anathematised **the legates**, not the Roman church. The bull was of doubtful validity in the first place: Pope Leo IX had died on 19 April, three months earlier, and a legation's authority ordinarily died with the pope who sent it. Communion between the sees had lapsed and been restored several times before — the Acacian schism (484–519) lasted thirty-five years — and there was no reason in 1054 to think this different. Byzantine chroniclers barely mention it.

**The Greek grievances were not the mirror-image of the Latin ones.**

- **The *filioque*.** The West added *and the Son* to a creed fixed by an ecumenical council. The Eastern objection is first **procedural** — no bishop, not even Rome's, may alter conciliar text unilaterally — and then **theological**, that it compromises the Father's role as sole source within the Trinity. Photios put the case in the 860s; the point has never been conceded. See [[doctrine-filioque]].
- **Azymes.** The Latin use of unleavened bread at the Eucharist occupied more space in the eleventh-century Greek polemic than the *filioque* did. Modern readers find this trivial; the Byzantines did not, because rite was the visible form of doctrine.
- **Jurisdiction.** Against the papal claim of universal ordinary jurisdiction stood the pentarchy and the councils. **The root is Chalcedon's canon 28 (451)**, which Rome never accepted — six hundred years before Humbert's bull.
- **Southern Italy and the Balkans.** Concrete disputes over which see governed which territory, running under the theology throughout.

**1204 is where the schism became real.** The Fourth Crusade sacked Constantinople, installed a Latin emperor and a **Latin patriarch** in the city, and looted its relics into the churches of Western Europe, where many remain. What had been an argument among bishops became a popular and permanent estrangement. See [[event-fourth-crusade-1204]].

**The reunions failed for the same reason twice.** At Lyon II (1274) and at Ferrara-Florence ([[event-council-of-florence-1438-1439|1438–39]]) emperors signed union under military necessity — the second time with Ottoman armies at the gates and a Western crusade as the price. Mark of Ephesus was the only Greek bishop present who refused to sign at Florence; the union was repudiated in Constantinople and formally rejected by a synod there in 1484. The Byzantine anti-unionists preferred the sultan's turban to the cardinal's hat — a remark the historian Doukas attributes to Loukas Notaras, and probably a literary construction, but an accurate report of the mood.

**Consistency with the Latin node, deliberately.** Both accounts agree that 1054 is a convention rather than an event boundary, that the estrangement was gradual and linguistic before it was doctrinal, that 1204 is the practical rupture, and that Paul VI and Patriarch Athenagoras lifted the mutual anathemas on 7 December 1965. Where they differ is vantage: Rome's node reads the schism forward from a primacy asserted and resisted; this one reads it forward from a conciliar order the West left.

## 1453 — and what survived it

Constantinople fell on 29 May 1453 after a 53-day siege; [[constantine-xi-palaiologos|Constantine XI]] died in the fighting, Mehmed II entered Hagia Sophia and it became a mosque. The imperial church ended.

The **institution** did not. Within months Mehmed installed the anti-unionist scholar Gennadios Scholarios as patriarch with authority over the Orthodox population under the *millet* system: the church lost the empire and acquired, in exchange, civil jurisdiction over a subject people. Runciman (1968) is the standard account of the four centuries that followed — captivity and survival at once. See [[event-fall-of-constantinople-1453]].

And the **manuscripts** went west. Manuel Chrysoloras had been teaching Greek in Florence since 1397; [[gemistos-plethon]] came to Ferrara and Florence in 1438–39 with the union delegation, lectured on the differences between Plato and Aristotle, and impressed Cosimo de' Medici enough to set the Florentine translation programme in motion; Bessarion, metropolitan of Nicaea and then a Latin cardinal, made over his Greek library to Venice in 1468 as the founding collection of the Marciana. See [[tradition-renaissance-hermeticism]].

## MASSIVE WIN — the empire that suppressed Greek philosophy became its ark

Two transmissions define this tradition, and they run in opposite directions across a thousand years.

**Inward: the doctrine is stated in the vocabulary of the religion it replaced.** Every definition the Byzantine councils produced is built from Greek philosophical terms — *ousia* and *hypostasis* at [[event-council-of-nicaea-325|Nicaea]] and [[event-council-of-constantinople-381|Constantinople]], *physis* and *prosopon* at [[event-council-of-chalcedon-451|Chalcedon]], *energeia* in the Palamite settlement of 1351 — and argued with Greek logical apparatus. [[pseudo-dionysius-the-areopagite|Pseudo-Dionysius]] wrote Proclus into Christian theology under an apostolic pseudonym around 500, **within a generation of [[event-closure-platonic-academy-529|Justinian closing the Athenian school in 529]]**, and the East canonised the result. The tradition was uneasy about this to the end: *Hellene* was its normal word for *pagan*, and Kaldellis (2007) traces a refusal of Greek identity that only reverses in the empire's last two centuries — at which point [[gemistos-plethon|Plethon]] went so far as to propose a reconstructed pagan religion.

**Outward: the same tradition carried the pagan corpus back to the West.** The manuscripts of Plato, Aristotle, the tragedians, Euclid, Ptolemy and Galen survived because Christian Greek scribes copied them for a millennium in a state that had legislated the schools shut. The Latin West had recovered Aristotle through Arabic at [[event-toledo-translations-1085-1150|Toledo]]; it recovered **Plato in Greek** from Byzantines. Ficino's Latin Plato (1484) and Plotinus (1492) rest on codices and teachers that came from Constantinople.

Set the two together and the finding is exact: **the state that shut Plato's school in 529 preserved his text for nine hundred years and handed it back to Europe in 1439, through bishops who had come to argue about the *filioque*.** Every link is documented — legislation, colophons, council registers, library bequests. See [[tradition-ancient-greek]], [[tradition-neoplatonism]], [[event-closure-platonic-academy-529]], [[event-council-of-florence-1438-1439]].

## Where it differs from its neighbours

- **From [[tradition-roman-catholicism|Rome]]**, with which it shares seven councils, the episcopate and the sacraments: no [[doctrine-filioque|*filioque*]]; conciliarity and the pentarchy against universal papal jurisdiction; no [[doctrine-purgatory-catholic|purgatory]] or indulgences; leavened bread and communion in both kinds; a married parish clergy; and a theology that is apophatic and participatory where the West became scholastic and juridical.
- **From the Oriental Orthodox** ([[tradition-coptic-orthodox]], [[tradition-syriac-orthodox]], [[tradition-armenian-christianity]]) and the [[tradition-church-of-the-east|Church of the East]]: Chalcedon 451 and Ephesus 431. The twentieth-century joint declarations found much of the ancient dispute to have been terminological.
- **From [[tradition-islam]]**: the neighbour, the rival and the interlocutor for eight centuries — [[event-byzantine-sasanian-war-602-628|war]], frontier, trade, the aniconic pressure that shadowed iconoclasm, and, in [[john-of-damascus|John of Damascus]], the earliest substantial Greek Christian account of Islam, written by a man whose family served the caliph's own administration in Damascus.
- **From [[tradition-eastern-orthodoxy]]**: not a difference of content but of frame. That node is the confession as it lives now; this one is the imperial tradition it came from and the thousand years in which its doctrine, rite and canon law were fixed.

## Disputes

- **When does "Byzantine" Christianity begin?** 330 (the city), 381 (the creed and Constantinople's precedence), 451 (canon 28), 610 (Heraclius, and the shift to a Greek-speaking state — he took the Greek title *basileus* in 629), or never, since the tradition claims continuity from the apostles. This node takes 330 by convention and says so.
- **Caesaropapism.** A nineteenth-century Western category that Dagron (2003) and most current Byzantine scholarship reject as a description; the emperor's religious role was real, contested and bounded. Any node that reproduces the old label uncritically should be corrected against this one.
- **How destructive was iconoclasm?** Brubaker and Haldon (2011) argue the surviving picture of mass destruction and systematic martyrdom derives from the victorious iconophile narrative and overstates both. The controversy was real; the atrocity literature is partly polemic.
- **Did the eastern provinces welcome the Arab conquest?** The older thesis — that miaphysites preferred Muslim rule to Chalcedonian persecution — is heavily qualified in current scholarship (Hoyland 2015). Doctrinal alienation was real; a general welcome is not demonstrable.
- **Was Byzantium Greek?** Kaldellis (2007) argues the Byzantines were Romans who became Greeks only at the very end, and that reading them as "the Greek church" throughout is a Western back-projection. The vault uses "Greek East" as a convenient shorthand and flags it here as one.
- **Is 1054 the schism?** No, and the vault says so on both sides of the aisle. See the account above and the parallel section on [[tradition-roman-catholicism]].
