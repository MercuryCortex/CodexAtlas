# Won Buddhism — 圓佛敎

## Overview
Won Buddhism (원불교, *Wonbulgyo* — literally "Circle Buddhism" or "Round Buddhism") is a Korean Buddhist reform tradition founded in 1916 by Pak Chungbin, known by his religious title **[[sotaesan]]** (少太山, "Young Great Mountain"). Its central symbol is the **Irwon-sang** (일원상, 一圓相 — "the mark of the One Circle"): a simple circle representing the dharmakāya, the ground of being, and the ultimate reality toward which all religious practice aims. Won Buddhism describes itself as a modern reform of Buddhism (*bulgyo hyeoksin*), but in practice synthesizes Confucian ethics, Daoist cosmology, and Christian charitable ideals into a unified householder path.

Founded during the Japanese colonial occupation of Korea (1910–1945), Won Buddhism emerged from the same milieu as [[tradition-donghak|Donghak]] (1860) — the Korean crisis of modernity, colonial disruption, and search for indigenous religious authenticity. Where Donghak was explicitly political and millennial, Won Buddhism turned inward: "Let the world be reformed through the practice of each individual."

## Historical phases
1. **Founding and early formation (1916–1943)** — Sotaesan's enlightenment experience in 1916 after 20 years of seeking. Gathers initial disciples at Yongsan-dong, engages them in a land reclamation project as first spiritual training. *Bulgyo Jeonggeon* (1935) establishes canonical doctrine. Sotaesan dies 1943 before completing the full scripture.
2. **Consolidation under Chŏngsan (1943–1962)** — Second patriarch Song Kyu compiles and finalizes the *Wonbulgyo Gyojeon* (1943, revised 1962). Survives Korean War (1950–1953) with Iksan headquarters intact. Establishes educational institutions.
3. **Expansion era (1962–present)** — Third and fourth patriarchs oversee international expansion. First North American temple opened Sacramento 1974. Today Won Buddhism has ~500,000 adherents, primarily in Korea, with a global network of temples and social service institutions. Won University (Iksan) and Wonkwang University are affiliated.

## Core doctrines

### Irwon-sang — the One Circle
The Irwon-sang is Won Buddhism's dharma seal (법인, *beopin*): an empty circle, the visual equivalent of the Buddhist concept of *śūnyatā* (emptiness) but also of the Mahayana *dharmakāya* — the formless absolute body of the Buddha. Sotaesan identifies the Irwon as identical to: the Dharmakāya Buddha of Mahayana, the Śūnya (emptiness) of Madhyamaka, the Brahman of Vedanta, and the Tao of Daoism. The circle is simultaneously the primordial ground, the model for ethical cultivation, and the goal of practice.

> "The Il-Won is the original source of all existence in the universe, and also the mind-seal of all Buddhas and sages." — *Wonbulgyo Gyojeon*, Doctrine of the Il-Won-Sang

### Saenghwal Bulgyo — "Everyday Buddhism"
The most radical reform: Won Buddhism abolishes the monastic/lay distinction as the organizing principle. All practitioners are *gyomu* (교무, religious workers) regardless of whether they marry or observe celibacy. Practice does not happen in retreat from the world but *through* work, relationship, and social engagement. Sotaesan's critique: traditional Korean Buddhism had become monastery-centered and inaccessible to ordinary people.

### Samahak Byongsu — Triple Cultivation
The three simultaneous practices that replace the eightfold path as the primary structure:
1. **Spiritual cultivation** (*jeongsin suyang*) — seated meditation, mindfulness, mental stillness
2. **Study of facts and principles** (*sari yongu*) — understanding dharma, inquiry, scripture study
3. **Mindful choice in activity** (*jakup chwidong*) — discerning right action in all daily situations

### Eunhye — Grace / Beneficence
The four sources of *eunhye* (은혜, grace-beneficence) that sustain all existence and require reciprocal gratitude and service:
1. Heaven and Earth (*cheonji eun*) — the natural world
2. Parents (*bumo eun*) — family and ancestors
3. Brethren (*dongpo eun*) — the human community
4. Laws (*beop eun*) — legal, moral, and cosmic order

This fourfold debt-of-gratitude ethic replaces the Confucian five relationships as Won Buddhism's social ethics while absorbing its logic.

## MASSIVE wins — cross-tradition edges

### Irwon-sang ↔ Zen ensō ↔ Plotinus' One
The circle as symbol of ultimate reality appears independently in three traditions without direct historical linkage: Won Buddhism's Irwon-sang (~1916), Zen/Chan's ensō (ink circle, ~8th c. CE Japan, earlier Tang China), and Plotinus' "the One" as ineffable source beyond Being (*Enneads*, 3rd c. CE). All three use a circle-or-sphere as the only adequate figure for the absolute — not because it has properties, but because it is the simplest closed form, the limit of form before becoming formless. The structural convergence maps onto Neoplatonic → Islamic → Buddhist chains but also supports an independent cognitive-symbol hypothesis: the circle is the natural human symbol for totality. See [[tradition-neoplatonism]], [[tradition-zen]].

### Sotaesan's enlightenment (1916) ↔ William James' four marks of mystical experience
[[william-james]] (*Varieties of Religious Experience*, 1902) identifies four marks of the mystical state: (1) ineffability, (2) noetic quality (the sense of having learned something true), (3) transiency, (4) passivity (the sense of being "held by" a higher power). Sotaesan's 1916 account — recorded by his disciples — matches all four. Won Buddhism thus illustrates James' claim that mystical experience is cross-culturally invariant at the phenomenological level, even where doctrinal frameworks differ radically. This is the most precise Buddhist data-point for the James-mysticism thesis because the Won tradition preserves Sotaesan's account with relative historical clarity compared to ancient founders.

### Won Buddhism reform ↔ Protestant Reformation — structural parallel
Both movements stripped an institution they regarded as ritually overloaded and socially inaccessible:
- Luther/Calvin attacked indulgences, Latin Mass, monasticism → Won attacks monk-centered Buddhism, relic veneration, esoteric ritual
- Both emphasized direct encounter with the absolute (scripture / Irwon-sang) without clerical mediation
- Both emerged from political-economic crises (German states, Peasants' War milieu / Korean colonial occupation)
- Both produced diaspora communities that became transmission vehicles for the reformed tradition
The parallel is structural, not historical. No direct influence. It maps the sociology of reformation across traditions.

### Won Buddhism synthesis agenda ↔ Ramakrishna Mission / Perennial Philosophy
Sotaesan's claim that Buddhism, Confucianism, Daoism, and Christianity all point to the same Irwon truth is structurally identical to:
- Swami Vivekananda's neo-Vedanta (all religions are paths up the same mountain, 1893 World's Parliament of Religions — 23 years before Won's founding)
- [[perennial-philosophy]] as articulated by Aldous Huxley (1945) and Frithjof Schuon
- The Bahá'í progressive revelation model
All four emerge ~1880–1950 in response to the crisis of Western colonialism, but from different cultural starting points (Bengali Hinduism, Korean Buddhism, European intellectualism, Persian Islam). The convergence suggests colonial encounter forced each tradition into a comparative-religion self-presentation.

### Donghak → Won Buddhism — sequential Korean synthesis arc
[[tradition-donghak]] (founded 1860 by [[choe-je-u]]) and Won Buddhism (1916) form a sequential pair: both arise in Korea, both synthesize indigenous and foreign religious elements, both emerge under external pressure (Western incursion / Japanese occupation), both have founder-martyrdom or founder-death-in-crisis as origin story. Donghak's god-within-you (*innaecheon*, 人乃天) anticipates Won's every-act-as-sacred-practice. The 56-year gap suggests a relay: the Donghak movement's political suppression (1894 Peasant Revolution crushed) may have opened the space for Won's more quietist synthesis.

## Relations to neighbors
- vs. [[tradition-buddhism]]: Won is explicitly a *reform* of Korean Buddhism, not a new tradition. Its central metaphysics (dharmakāya, śūnyatā, karma, rebirth) is standard Mahayana. The innovations are sociological (householder-centered) and symbolic (Irwon replaces Buddha-image as primary focal object).
- vs. [[tradition-zen]]: Won incorporates Seon (Korean Zen) sitting meditation as *jeongsin suyang* but rejects the koan-encounter format and retreat-centered monastic structure. The Irwon is Won's equivalent of the ensō's function in Zen iconography.
- vs. [[tradition-donghak]]: same milieu, overlapping themes (synthesis, Korean modernity, founder crisis), but Donghak is explicitly political and theistic-immanent (Hanullim = God-within) while Won is apophatic-Buddhist (Irwon as beyond-personal).
- vs. [[tradition-confucianism]]: Won's *eunhye* fourfold debt structure is a deliberate reframing of Confucian reciprocity. Chŏngsan's systematization made Confucian ethics explicit in the scripture.
