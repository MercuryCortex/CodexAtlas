# Political Islamism

## Overview
A family of 20th- and 21st-century movements seeking the establishment of Islamic political-legal order (variously defined). Distinct from Islam-the-religion (with which Islamism shares textual sources but constitutes a specifically modern political mobilization). Treated here as a Phase-7 movement because its doctrinal foundations (al-Banna, Maududi, Qutb) are recognizably modern responses to colonial encounter, nationalism, and the collapse of the Ottoman Caliphate (1924), rather than continuations of classical Sunni jurisprudence in straightforward sense.

## Historical phases
1. **Founding period** (1928–1949) — Hasan al-Banna founds the Muslim Brotherhood in Ismailia, Egypt; assassinated 1949.
2. **Maududi and South-Asian Islamism** (1941 onward) — Jamaat-e-Islami in pre-Partition India / Pakistan; key vocabulary (*jahiliyyah*, *hakimiyyah*) developed.
3. **Qutbist radicalization** (1954–1966) — Qutb's prison radicalization; *Milestones* (1964); execution 1966; the militant strand established.
4. **State-level Islamism** (1979 onward) — Iranian Revolution (Shia variant); Pakistani Islamization under Zia ul-Haq (1977–1988); Saudi Wahhabi expansion globally.
5. **Jihadist period** (1980s onward) — Afghan jihad against Soviet occupation; al-Qaeda (1988); 9/11 attacks (2001); ISIS proclaimed caliphate (2014–2017).
6. **Post-Arab-Spring period** (2011 onward) — Muslim Brotherhood electoral victories and reversals in Egypt and Tunisia; ongoing reconfiguration.

## Defining doctrines
- The contemporary world (including ostensibly Muslim societies) is in a state of *jahiliyyah*.
- Islam is a comprehensive political-legal-economic system, not "merely" a religion.
- Establishment of divine sovereignty (*hakimiyyah*) is a religious obligation.
- Vanguard mobilization to transform society (Qutbist variant).
- Offensive jihad against systems of *jahiliyyah* is legitimate (Qutbist, jihadist variants — contested by mainstream Sunni jurisprudence).

## Relations to neighbors
- vs. mainstream [[tradition-islam]]: most Islamist movements claim continuity with classical Sunni Islam; mainstream classical-Sunni jurisprudence and major institutions (Al-Azhar, Mauritanian Quranist scholars, etc.) have variously contested Islamist political-theological readings.
- vs. Salafism: overlap exists, but Salafism (theological-puritan reformism) and Islamism (political mobilization) are not identical; the Madkhali Salafis are explicitly quietist.
