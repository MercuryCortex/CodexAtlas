# Catharism (Albigensian heresy)

## Identity

**Catharism** (from Greek *katharos*, "pure" — an external label; adherents called themselves *bons chrétiens*, "good Christians") was a dualist Christian movement centered in **Languedoc** (southern France) from approximately 1143 to 1321. Also called **Albigensian** (from Albi, a major center). At its height (~1200–1210), Catharism commanded the sympathy of significant Languedoc nobility and large lay communities — the most serious internal heretical challenge the medieval Latin Church ever faced.

## Theology

**Radical dualism.** Cathar theology held that reality is divided between two principles:
- A **good God** — spiritual, transcendent, creator of the invisible, unchanging world of souls and light
- An **evil principle** — sometimes a second co-eternal god (*absolute dualism*), sometimes a fallen angel (*mitigated dualism*) — who created the **material world**, including human bodies and — most radically — the God of the Old Testament himself (who commands war, demands animal sacrifice, creates biological life — all evil per the Cathar frame)

**Pneumatic anthropology.** Human souls are divine sparks trapped in evil material bodies, cycling through reincarnation until liberated by the *consolamentum* (spirit-baptism by laying on of hands) — the one valid sacrament, substituting for water baptism.

**The two orders:**
- **Perfecti** (*Parfaits* / *Parfaites*) — fully initiated; received the *consolamentum*; bound to celibacy, vegetarianism, no killing, no property, perpetual ministry. Only they could administer the *consolamentum* to others.
- **Credentes** (believers) — ordinary lay sympathizers who deferred the *consolamentum* to their deathbed (*endura*) and supported the Perfecti materially.

**Rejection of Catholic sacraments:** The Cathars denied transubstantiation (matter cannot become divine), the physical Incarnation (Christ could not have had a material body), the resurrection, marriage (reproduction traps more souls in matter), and the Church's institutional authority.

## The Bogomil ancestry

The Cathars' theological dualism was not invented independently. The **[[tradition-bogomilism|Bogomils]]** (Bulgaria, 10th c.) developed a strikingly similar theology: material creation is the work of an evil Demiurge, the soul is a divine spark imprisoned in matter, the *consolamentum* is the only valid rite. Cathar emissaries visited a Bogomil community (Drugunthia) around 1167 — direct theological contact is documented. Lambert (1998) argues Cathar theology developed from Bogomil contact, not from ancient Gnosticism directly (the older "Manichaean heresy" label is a polemical oversimplification).

## The Albigensian Crusade (1209–1229)

In 1208, the papal legate Pierre de Castelnau was assassinated; Pope Innocent III blamed Count Raymond VI of Toulouse. Innocent authorized a crusade — the **[[event-albigensian-crusade-1209-1229|Albigensian Crusade]]** — the first launched by a pope against European Christians. Led by Simon de Montfort, it was marked by massacres: at **Béziers (1209)**, Cistercian legate Arnaud Amalric reportedly said, "Kill them all; God will know his own" — estimates of 20,000 killed. The crusade broke the Cathar hierarchy. The **Inquisition** (institutionalized 1234, Gregory IX) completed the work.

The **Pamiers register** (Bishop Jacques Fournier, ~1318–1325) is the richest surviving record of medieval village-level Cathar life — the basis for Emmanuel Le Roy Ladurie's *Montaillou* (1975).

## Montségur (1244) and the "Cathar treasure"

**Montségur** — a dramatic mountain fortress in the Pyrenean foothills — was the last major Cathar stronghold. After a nine-month siege, it fell March 1244. Approximately 220 Perfecti refused to abjure and were burned at the base of the castle. Before the final surrender, a small group escaped by night, allegedly carrying the **"treasure of Montségur."** Inquisitorial records suggest money and documents. Modern esoteric tradition (beginning with Otto Rahn in 1933) claims it was the **Holy Grail**.

**This identification has no medieval evidentiary basis.** Wolfram von Eschenbach's *Parzival* (~1210) — the text whose *Munsalvæsche* is identified with Montségur — was composed in German, for a German court, with **Templar**-identified Grail guardians, not Cathar ones. No medieval source connects the poem to Montségur. The identification is entirely Rahn's invention.

## Otto Rahn and the Nazi appropriation

**Otto Rahn** (1904–1939), a German scholar, spent years in Languedoc and published *Crusade Against the Grail* (1933): Montségur = Wolfram's Munsalvæsche; the Cathar Perfecti = the *Templeise*; the Grail = a stone of light; the Catholic Church = the destroyer of the pure Grail tradition.

Rahn joined the **SS** in 1936. **Heinrich Himmler** — obsessed with Aryan-mystical prehistory and the SS as a new Templar-Grail brotherhood — funded Rahn's work through the **Ahnenerbe** (the SS ancestral heritage organization). The Cathar-Grail mythology became one strand of SS ideological mysticism. Rahn died in 1939 in the Austrian mountains — circumstances disputed.

**Goodrick-Clarke (2002)** traces the full chain: Rahn → Himmler → SS mythology → post-war neo-Nazi esoteric literature. A 13th-century German Grail romance weaponized for 20th-century racial ideology — one of the most extraordinary reception-history arcs in the vault.

## The Templar connection: geographic fact, no doctrinal evidence

The Templars held extensive property in Languedoc but **did not participate in the Albigensian Crusade**. Barber (2000) explains this as institutional pragmatism: Outremer commitments + complex landlord-tenant relationships with Cathar-sympathizing nobility. **The absence is NOT evidence of doctrinal sympathy.** The esoteric narrative — that the Templars secretly shared Cathar dualism or protected the Cathar Grail — has no medieval documentary support. It is a 19th–20th century construction beginning with Hammer-Purgstall (1818) and reaching modern form in Rahn (1933) and *Holy Blood Holy Grail* (1982).

The geographic coincidence is real: two of the most anomalous medieval institutions occupied the same territory simultaneously. The doctrinal connection is not.
