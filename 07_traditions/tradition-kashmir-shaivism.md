# Kashmir Shaivism (Trika)

## Overview
Kashmir Shaivism — more precisely the **Trika** ("three-fold") tradition — is the non-dualist Śaiva philosophical and Tantric system that flourished in the Kashmir Valley from approximately 850–1200 CE. It is the most philosophically developed non-dual consciousness framework in the Indian tradition and arguably in any pre-modern religion: its central claim is that **consciousness itself (Śiva) is the sole reality**, that the entire manifest universe is Śiva's free self-expression (*svātantrya*), and that liberation (*mokṣa*) is not acquisition of anything new but **recognition** (*pratyabhijñā*) of one's always-already-present identity as Śiva. This "recognition" move makes it structurally unique among Indian philosophical schools and generates strong cross-tradition parallels to Valentinian gnosis, Meister Eckhart's Seelenfünklein, and Dzogchen's rigpa pointing-out instruction.

## Sub-schools
Three interlocking sub-schools:
- **Spanda** ("vibration/pulsation"): founded by Vasugupta's *Śiva Sūtras* (~825 CE) and the *Spanda Kārikā*. The cosmos is Śiva's vibrating self-expression. Consciousness is fundamentally dynamic, not static.
- **Pratyabhijñā** ("recognition"): founded by Utpaladeva (~925 CE), systematized by Abhinavagupta. Liberation = recognizing that you already are Śiva. The philosophical apparatus argues against Buddhist momentariness and Śankara's Māyā doctrine.
- **Kaula/Trika ritual**: the ritual-liturgical-initiatory framework, including the transgressive Kaula practices (cremation grounds, transgression of caste-purity norms) that Sanderson 2009 showed were borrowed by Vajrayāna Buddhism.

## The Abhinavagupta synthesis
Abhinavagupta (~975–1025 CE) is the towering figure of the school. In the *Tantrāloka* (37 chapters, the most comprehensive Tantric synthesis ever written) and the *Parātrīśikā-Vivaraṇa*, he integrated the three sub-schools, synthesized Buddhist logico-epistemological arguments into a Śaiva framework, and developed the most sophisticated theory of aesthetic experience (*rasa*) in Sanskrit literature — arguing that aesthetic experience (*camatkāra*, "wonder") is structurally identical to the recognition of Śiva-consciousness.

## Cross-tradition connections
- **→ Vajrayāna Buddhism (directional, Tier-1)**: Sanderson 2009 demonstrated that the Yoginitantras (Cakrasaṃvara, Hevajra) were composed largely via direct borrowing from Śaiva Tantric sources (Jayadrathayāmala, Picumata). The 24 sacred *pīṭhas* of the Cakrasaṃvara system are lifted from the Śaiva *Tantrasadbhāva*. This is the most thoroughly documented case of inter-tradition borrowing in medieval Indian religion.
- **↔ Neoplatonism (structural parallel, Tier-2)**: Both traditions posit a single absolute consciousness from which all reality emanates. The key distinction: in Kashmir Shaivism the world is *real* (Śiva's own free expression), whereas Plotinus' matter tends toward non-being. This distinction maps onto the Eckhart/Śaṅkara comparison: Eckhart is closer to Trika than to Advaita for the same reason.
- **→ Kashmiri Sufism via Lal Ded (documented, Tier-1)**: Lalleshwari (*Lal Ded*, ~1320–1392) articulated Trika recognition doctrine in vernacular Kashmiri poetry (*vāks*). Shaikh Nuruddin Wali (*Nund Rishi*, d.1439), the founder of the Kashmiri Rishi Sufi movement, drew directly on her poetic vocabulary. This is the most documented Kashmir-internal transmission from Shaivism into Islamic mysticism.
- **↔ Pratyabhijñā ↔ Valentinian gnosis (parallel-form)**: The structural move — liberation = recognition of a pre-existing divine identity, not acquisition — mirrors Valentinian pneumatics who are "already saved by nature."
- **↔ Meister Eckhart's Seelenfünklein (parallel-form)**: Eckhart's "spark of the soul is already the Godhead" is the closest Western parallel to Pratyabhijñā. Rudolf Otto's 1926 comparison of Eckhart with Śaṅkara is standard; Trika is the tighter fit because in Eckhart's system the world is real (not Māyā).

## Decline and transmission
The tradition declined in Kashmir after the Sultanate period (~1350 CE), when Persian Islamic political and cultural influence became dominant. The texts survived primarily in Nepal and South India. The 20th-century Kashmiri scholar Swami Lakshman Joo (1907–1991) was responsible for much of the modern transmission to Western scholars.
