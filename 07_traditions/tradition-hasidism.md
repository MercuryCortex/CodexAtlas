# Hasidism

## Overview
A populist mystical-pietistic movement within Eastern European Ashkenazi Judaism, founded by Israel ben Eliezer (the Baal Shem Tov, c. 1698–1760), developed by his successor Dov Ber of Mezeritch (the Maggid, d. 1772) and the Maggid's students, and crystallized into the multi-dynasty institutional structure that became the dominant form of Eastern European Jewish religion by the early 19th century. Hasidism democratized Lurianic Kabbalah, making mystical experience and *devekut* (cleaving to God) available to ordinary Jews rather than only to a learned elite, and institutionalized the *tzaddik* (charismatic spiritual leader) as the channel of divine connection for the community. The 1939–1945 Holocaust destroyed the East European Hasidic centers but the surviving dynasties reconstituted in Israel, North America, and Western Europe.

## Historical phases
1. **Foundation by the Besht** (1740s–1760) — at Międzybóż; oral teaching; small initial circle.
2. **Expansion under the Maggid** (1760–1772) — Dov Ber of Mezeritch trains the second-generation leaders who carry Hasidism across Eastern Europe.
3. **Crystallization into dynasties** (1772–1815) — Elimelech of Lizhensk (Galician Hasidism), Schneur Zalman of Liadi (Chabad-Lubavitch), Nachman of Breslov (Breslov), the Maggidim of Chernobyl, etc. The Mitnagdic opposition under Elijah of Vilna (the Vilna Gaon, d. 1797) intensifies; multiple bans (*herem*) issued against Hasidim 1772–1781.
4. **19th-century institutional development** — dynasties consolidate; rabbinic-dynastic succession; the Hasidic *court* becomes a recognized institutional form.
5. **20th-century Holocaust and reconstitution** — destruction of the Eastern European centers; reconstitution in Israel (Bnei Brak, Jerusalem), the USA (Brooklyn — Crown Heights for Chabad, Williamsburg and Borough Park for Satmar et al.), and elsewhere.

## Core texts
- *Tzava'at ha-Rivash* (testament of the Besht).
- *Keter Shem Tov* (1794, Besht's teachings compiled).
- *Shivchei ha-Besht* (1814, hagiography).
- *Toledot Yaakov Yosef* by Jacob Joseph of Polonnoye (1780, first printed Hasidic theological work).
- *Tanya* by Schneur Zalman of Liadi (1797, Chabad foundational text).
- *Likkutei Moharan* of Nachman of Breslov.
- The various dynastic *Torah*-anthologies (Mei Hashiloach, Sfat Emet, Bnei Yissaschar, etc.).

## Defining doctrines
- **Devekut** — continuous cleaving to God in all activity, not only formal prayer or study.
- **Lurianic-Cordoveran integration** — Hasidic theology synthesizes both Kabbalistic streams in service of accessible mystical experience.
- **The Tzaddik** — the charismatic spiritual leader who functions as channel of divine connection for the community.
- **Joy and ecstatic prayer** — the Hasidic emphasis on *simcha* (joy) and ecstatic worship contrasts with the more austere Mitnagdic-Lithuanian focus on Talmudic study.
- **Hitbonenut / Hitbodedut** — Hasidic meditative practices (Chabad's intellectual *hitbonenut*; Breslov's solitary *hitbodedut*).
- **Sparks of holiness everywhere** — every mundane activity contains sparks to be redeemed (Lurianic *tikkun* democratized).

## Relations to neighbors
- vs. Mitnagdic / Lithuanian-Yeshivish Judaism: the principal Jewish opponent; the conflict 1772–1820 was substantial; the modern modus vivendi places the two streams as parallel-but-cooperating expressions of traditional Judaism.
- vs. [[tradition-kabbalah]]: direct descent; Hasidism is the principal popular-institutional vehicle of Lurianic Kabbalah from 1800 onward.
- vs. modern Jewish movements (Reform, Conservative, Modern Orthodox): Hasidism is the principal "ultra-Orthodox" / *Haredi* tradition along with the Lithuanian *Yeshivish* world.
- vs. [[tradition-judaism]]: a sub-tradition within Orthodox Judaism.

## Disputes
- **Sabbatean influence on early Hasidism.** Scholem argued for covert Sabbatean influence on the Besht and the early movement; subsequent scholarship (Piekarz, Idel) has substantially complicated the picture without denying all crosscurrents.
