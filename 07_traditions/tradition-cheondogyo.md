# Cheondogyo — Religion of the Heavenly Way

## Overview
Cheondogyo (천도교, "Religion of the Heavenly Way") is the institutional successor to [[tradition-donghak]], formally reorganized in 1905 by Son Byong-hui as the movement's third patriarch. The renaming — from "Donghak" (Eastern Learning) to "Cheondogyo" (Heavenly Way) — was a strategic rebranding after the catastrophic failure of the 1894 Peasant Revolution; it retained all of Donghak's core theology while distancing the movement from its association with armed rebellion.

The central doctrine remains *innaecheon* (人乃天, "Humans are Heaven") — the radical claim that every person directly contains and embodies the divine principle, without class, gender, or caste hierarchy. Cheondogyo played a decisive role in the March First Independence Movement of 1919 against Japanese colonial rule; Son Byong-hui was among the 33 signatories of the Korean Declaration of Independence. Today Cheondogyo has roughly one million adherents in North Korea and a smaller community in South Korea, making it the only recognized indigenous Korean religion with a substantial presence in the DPRK.

## Historical phases
1. **Donghak origin (~1860–1894)** — Choe Je-u's original revelation, persecution, and execution (1864); second patriarch Choe Si-hyeong's underground leadership; 1894 Donghak Peasant Revolution and its defeat by Japanese and Korean government forces.
2. **Reorganization (~1905)** — Son Byong-hui's return from Japanese exile and reorganization as "Cheondogyo" with more centralized institutional structure.
3. **March First Movement (1919)** — Cheondogyo as the primary organized force behind the national independence declaration; Son Byong-hui imprisoned, dies 1922.
4. **Division-era (1945–present)** — community divided by the 38th parallel; Cheondogyo retains a substantial base in North Korea (where it is officially recognized as a minority religion); smaller community in South Korea; overseas Korean diaspora.

## Defining doctrines
- **Innaecheon** (人乃天): "Each person is Heaven" — the divine is not transcendent but fully immanent in every human being. No mediation through priest, temple, or ritual is required; every person encounters the divine in themselves and in others.
- **Si천主** (侍天主): "Serving/hosting the Lord of Heaven" — the active practice of recognizing and treating the divine in every person encountered.
- **East-West synthesis**: Donghak/Cheondogyo explicitly frames itself as synthesizing Confucian ethics, Buddhist non-attachment, and Daoist naturalness — the "three teachings" of East Asia — with the anti-colonial assertion that Korea's indigenous spiritual tradition is not inferior to Western Christianity.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **Innaecheon ↔ Ātman=Brahman ↔ Orthodox theosis ↔ Vajrayana deity-yoga (East Asian radical-immanence doctrine)**: The Cheondogyo doctrine that "humans are Heaven" — every person directly is the divine, without mediation or qualification — is structurally identical to the Upanishadic *ātman=Brahman* identification (the individual self is the universal ground, Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 1.4.10), to Orthodox theosis (the human person participates in and becomes God through uncreated energies, 2 Peter 1:4 as proof-text), and to Vajrayana deity-yoga's completion stage (the practitioner's nature is recognized as always-already-Buddha). All four traditions posit radical divine immanence in the human person; all four generate institutional controversy about whether this compromises divine transcendence; all four have been accused of "making humans equal to God." The Cheondogyo case is striking because it arrives at this position from a Confucian-Buddhist East Asian framework rather than a Western mystical or Vedic framework — independent convergence at the same doctrinal apex.
- **Donghak/Cheondogyo 1894 ↔ Latin American Liberation Theology ↔ Gandhian Satyagraha (religion-as-anti-colonial-organizing structure)**: The 1894 Donghak Peasant Revolution and the 1919 March First Movement are East Asia's great anti-colonial religious uprisings, both organized around the theological claim that all people are divine and therefore all colonial hierarchy violates cosmic order. This is structurally parallel to Latin American Liberation Theology's "option for the poor" as a theological mandate for anti-colonial politics (Gustavo Gutiérrez, 1971) and Gandhi's use of Hindu *ahimsa* and *satyagraha* as the spiritual foundation of anti-colonial resistance (1919–1947, exact same era as March First). All three: use of indigenous/traditional religious framework to organize against colonial or oligarchic domination; theological claim that human dignity derives from divine nature; nonviolent (or initially nonviolent) mass mobilization.
- **Cheondogyo in North Korea ↔ religious survival under totalitarianism (the vault's live case study)**: The persistence of Cheondogyo with approximately one million adherents in the DPRK — officially recognized under the Choson Democratic Party, which is a Cheondogyo political organization — is one of the vault's most unusual ongoing cases. It is a pre-communist indigenous Korean religion that survived (in modified, state-supervised form) the totalitarian suppression of all other religious expression in North Korea. The mechanisms of survival — political accommodation, institutional complicity, genuine underground practice — parallel the survival of the Catholic Church in the Soviet bloc and Tibetan Buddhism under Chinese occupation.

## Relations to neighbors
- vs. [[tradition-donghak]]: direct institutional continuation; the same theology, renamed and reorganized.
- vs. Confucianism, Buddhism, Daoism: the tradition explicitly synthesizes all three and claims to transcend their limitations.
- vs. Korean Christianity: contemporary rival for Korean national identity; both provided anti-colonial organizing infrastructure in the 1910–1945 period, but from different traditions.
