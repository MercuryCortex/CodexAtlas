# Rabbinic Judaism

## Overview

**Rabbinic Judaism** is the form of Judaism constructed in the centuries after the Roman destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE, and the ancestor of every living Jewish denomination. Its defining move is a substitution: **Torah study, prayer, and the interpretation of law replace sacrifice**; the *rabbi* — a teacher whose authority is competence in a text, not descent — replaces the hereditary priest; the study-house and the synagogue replace the Temple. Where Second Temple Judaism was organized around a place, rabbinic Judaism is organized around a curriculum.

The legitimating doctrine for that substitution is the **Oral Torah** (*Torah she-be-ʿal peh*): the claim that alongside the written Torah, God gave [[moses]] at Sinai a second, oral revelation, transmitted in an unbroken chain to the rabbis themselves. *Pirkei Avot* 1:1 states the chain in a single sentence — Moses received Torah at Sinai and passed it to Joshua, Joshua to the elders, the elders to the prophets, the prophets to the Men of the Great Assembly — and thereby licenses rabbinic innovation as *retrieval*. Everything the rabbis create is, on this account, already given. It is the most consequential single claim in the tradition, and it is what makes [[moses]] the anchor-figure of a movement that begins thirteen centuries after him.

## The rupture and the reconstruction

[[event-destruction-of-second-temple-70]] ended the sacrificial cult, the priesthood's operative function, and the Sanhedrin. It also removed the object over which the Second Temple sects — Pharisees, Sadducees, Essenes, the Qumran community, the various apocalyptic movements — had been arguing. Sectarianism did not survive the loss of the thing being contested.

The rabbinic account of what followed centres on [[event-jamnia-yavneh-90]] and on [[yohanan-ben-zakkai]], who in the tradition is smuggled out of the besieged city in a coffin and secures Roman permission to teach at Yavneh. Modern scholarship treats this narrative with care. Shaye Cohen (1984) reads Yavneh not as a Pharisaic takeover but as a **grand coalition** whose achievement was the end of sectarianism — the institutionalization of disagreement *inside* one community rather than schism between communities. The older idea of a "Council of Jamnia" that closed the biblical canon is a scholarly construct with no support in the sources and is now generally abandoned.

The [[event-bar-kokhba-revolt-132-136]] was the second catastrophe: Judea was devastated, Jerusalem refounded as the pagan colony Aelia Capitolina, and [[rabbi-akiva]] — who according to the Talmud had hailed Bar Kokhba as messiah — was executed. The centre of gravity moved north to Galilee (Usha, Sepphoris, Tiberias) and east to Babylonia.

## Mishnah → Gemara → Talmud

- **Mishnah** (redacted c. 200 CE, ascribed to [[judah-ha-nasi]]) — six orders (*Zeraim*, seeds; *Moed*, appointed times; *Nashim*, women; *Nezikin*, damages; *Kodashim*, holy things; *Tohorot*, purities) in 63 tractates, written in [[language-mishnaic-hebrew]]. It is not a commentary on scripture and it rarely cites scripture; it is a free-standing legal-topical corpus in an idiom of its own. It legislates in detail for the Temple that no longer exists — a fact that has been read as anticipation of restoration, as an act of memory, and as an assertion that the law is independent of its circumstances.
- **Tosefta** — a parallel Tannaitic collection, roughly four times the Mishnah's length, following the same order; the relationship between the two is unsettled.
- **Halakhic midrash** (*Mekhilta*, *Sifra*, *Sifre*) — the alternative Tannaitic form, which stays anchored to the biblical verse.
- **Gemara** — the Amoraic (c. 220–500 CE) discussion of the Mishnah, produced in two centres and surviving as two Talmuds:
  - the [[phase-4-032-jerusalem-talmud]] (Palestinian; redacted c. 400 CE in Galilee, abruptly and evidently under duress), and
  - the [[phase-4-033-babylonian-talmud]] (*Bavli*; redacted c. 500–650 CE at Sura, Pumbedita and Nehardea, with a further anonymous editorial stratum — the *stam* — that David Weiss Halivni argues is the layer that gives the Bavli its argumentative character). The Bavli covers fewer tractates than the Yerushalmi but treats them far more extensively; it became **the** Talmud, and its standard pagination derives from the Romm (Vilna) printing of 1880–86.

**What the Talmud is.** Not a code. The Gemara preserves the *argument* — objections, counter-objections, unresolved questions, and named minority opinions that were rejected. The Mishnah itself defends the practice (*Eduyot* 1:5–6: minority opinions are recorded so that a later court may rely on them). A scriptural canon that transmits its own dissent, verbatim and permanently, is structurally unusual, and it is the feature that makes the corpus generative rather than closed.

**Halakhah and aggadah.** The corpus runs on two registers: *halakhah* (the legal-normative — "the way one walks") and *aggadah* (narrative, exegesis, ethics, theology, folklore, medicine, magic). They are interleaved, not segregated, and the tradition has never had a settled account of aggadah's binding force — a question that becomes explosive in the [[maimonides|Maimonidean]] controversies, where allegorical readings of aggadah are exactly what the anti-rationalists object to.

## The academies and the Geonim

Babylonian Jewry, outside Roman jurisdiction and later inside the Abbasid caliphate, became the tradition's institutional centre for some six hundred years. The academies of **Sura** (founded by Rav, c. 219 CE) and **Pumbedita** produced the Bavli, and their heads — the **Geonim** (c. 589–1038, ending with Hai Gaon) — governed a diaspora by correspondence. The ***responsum*** (*she'elot u-teshuvot*), a written legal answer to a written question, becomes the tradition's characteristic administrative instrument and remains so to the present.

Two pressures shaped the Geonic period:

1. **Karaism.** From the 8th–9th c., a scripturalist movement — associated in later polemic with Anan ben David, and crystallized by Benjamin al-Nahawandi and Daniel al-Qumisi — rejected the Oral Torah outright and insisted on the written text alone. [[saadia-gaon]]'s polemics against it forced the Rabbanite side to argue for the Oral Torah rather than assume it. The Rabbanite/Karaite split is the tradition's sharpest internal test of its own founding premise.
2. **The Islamic intellectual world.** The Geonim wrote in Judeo-Arabic, read *kalām*, and worked inside Islamic legal culture. [[saadia-gaon]]'s [[phase-5-010-saadia-emunot-ve-deot]] (933) is the first systematic Jewish philosophical theology and is built on a Muʿtazilite frame (see [[philosophy-mutazila]]).

## Rishonim, codification, and the medieval settlement

The **Rishonim** (c. 1000–1500) are the commentators and codifiers who made the Talmud usable. Rashi of Troyes (1040–1105) wrote the running gloss without which the Bavli is close to unreadable; his descendants and successors, the Tosafists, turned the same page into a dialectical apparatus of cross-tractate comparison. In the Islamic west, a different temperament produced **codes**.

- [[maimonides]]'s ***Mishneh Torah*** (c. 1170–1180) — fourteen books in clear Hebrew, covering the whole of halakhah including the Temple law that had been inoperative for a millennium, and citing **no sources at all**. That last decision provoked the critical glosses of Abraham ben David of Posquières and set the terms of the first Maimonidean controversy. See [[moral-mishneh-torah-ethical-code]].
- Jacob ben Asher's *Arbaʿah Turim* (14th c.) restricted itself to law in force, in four divisions — the structure that won.
- Joseph Karo's ***Shulḥan ʿArukh*** (Venice, 1565) follows the *Turim*'s four-part shape; Moses Isserles's Ashkenazi glosses, the *Mappah*, were printed alongside it. The composite — a Sephardi code wearing an Ashkenazi commentary — became the standard reference work of Jewish law and remains so.

Talya Fishman (2011) argues that the Talmud only became the *curriculum* of ordinary Jewish life in this medieval period, as it shifted from an orally-performed tradition to a written text that could be glossed, indexed, and codified.

## The mystical counter-current

Alongside the legal-philosophical mainstream runs an esoteric stream that repeatedly reorganizes the tradition from inside: the *Hekhalot* and *Merkavah* ascent literature of late antiquity; *Sefer Yetzirah*; the German Pietists; and then the [[phase-5-027-sefer-ha-zohar]], which appears in Castile in the 1280s presented as a 2nd-century work of Shimon bar Yochai, and which [[gershom-scholem]] assigned to [[moses-de-leon]]. In 16th-century Safed, [[isaac-luria]] recast kabbalah around *tzimtzum* (divine self-contraction), *shevirat ha-kelim* (the shattering of the vessels) and *tikkun* (repair) — a myth that made exile cosmic and repair a human task, and that spread through the diaspora with unusual speed. Its messianic charge helped carry the [[shabbatai-tzvi]] movement of 1665–66, whose collapse into apostasy left a long shadow. [[tradition-hasidism]], from [[baal-shem-tov]] in mid-18th-c. Podolia, made the Lurianic frame popular and devotional and was fiercely resisted by the *Mitnagdim* under the Vilna Gaon — a conflict that ended in mutual accommodation once both faced the Haskalah.

## Modernity

Emancipation and the Haskalah broke the assumption that halakhah was the community's enforceable civil law, and the denominational structure of modern Judaism is the response: **Reform** (Hamburg Temple 1818; Abraham Geiger), **Positive-Historical / Conservative** (Zacharias Frankel; Breslau 1854), **Neo-Orthodoxy** (Samson Raphael Hirsch's *Torah im Derekh Eretz*), **Reconstructionist** (Mordecai Kaplan), and the ultra-Orthodox and Hasidic communities that rejected the premise of the question. See [[tradition-reform-judaism]], [[tradition-conservative-judaism]], [[tradition-orthodox-judaism]]. Every one of them argues its case out of the rabbinic corpus; that shared reference is what makes them all *rabbinic* Judaism.

## What the sources overstate — the current scholarly correction

The vault's posture is investigative, so the historiographical caveat belongs in the entry and not in a footnote. Rabbinic literature narrates its own authority as immediate and total, and for most of the 20th century scholarship followed it. Three revisions have changed the picture:

- **Jacob Neusner's documentary method** — read each rabbinic document as a whole with its own program and date, rather than harmonizing attributed sayings across centuries into "what the rabbis held." Attributions in the Bavli are not reliable evidence of who said what when.
- **Catherine Hezser (1997)** — the Tannaitic and early Amoraic rabbis were a loose network of masters and disciples, not an institution. "The academy" as a formal body is largely a Babylonian and post-Talmudic retrojection.
- **Seth Schwartz (2001)** — between 135 and roughly 350 CE the rabbis were a small elite with limited hold over ordinary Jews; rabbinic Judaism's *normativity* is a late achievement, consolidated under Islamic rule and completed in the Middle Ages. Fishman (2011) makes the parallel argument for the Talmud's own curricular dominance.

None of this makes the tradition less consequential. It relocates the moment of consolidation from the sources' preferred date to a demonstrable one.

## MASSIVE wins — cross-tradition

- **The portability move.** A religion whose central act was a sacrifice performed by a hereditary caste in one building converted, within roughly a century and a half, into a religion whose central act is the reading and argued interpretation of a text — a practice that can be performed anywhere by anyone competent. This is the structural condition for two thousand years of [[diaspora-judaism]] and the reason the tradition survived the destruction of its centre. See [[theme-temple-cult-to-rabbinic-judaism-transition]] and [[theme-persecution-as-legitimation]].
- **Judaism and Christianity as co-emergent siblings.** Boyarin (2004) argues the boundary between them was *produced* — by heresiologists on both sides across the 2nd–5th centuries — rather than inherited from a clean 1st-century parting. Rabbinic Judaism and [[tradition-early-christianity]] are two post-70 reconstructions of Second Temple Judaism, defining themselves partly against each other. Later reception was violently asymmetrical: the 1240 Paris disputation was followed by the public burning of cartloads of Talmud manuscripts in 1242, and Christian censorship shaped the printed Talmud's text thereafter.
- **The Abbasid intellectual milieu.** The Geonic academies were embedded in Islamic Iraq. [[saadia-gaon]] wrote philosophy in Arabic on a Muʿtazilite frame; Libson (2003) documents concrete Islamic-legal influence on Geonic halakhic instruments; the Rabbanite–Karaite argument runs in parallel to Islamic debates between traditionist and rationalist scripturalism. See [[tradition-islam]] and [[philosophy-mutazila]].
- **Recorded disagreement as a legal technology.** Rabbinic Judaism and Islamic jurisprudence independently institutionalized the *preservation* of rejected opinions — Talmudic minority views and Islamic *ikhtilāf* with its four coexisting Sunni madhhabs. A structural parallel between two neighbouring, mutually-aware legal cultures, and the mechanism by which both stayed adaptable across a millennium.
- **The Aristotelian relay.** [[maimonides]], writing Arabic inside the rabbinic tradition, is one of the principal channels by which Arabic Aristotelianism reached Latin Christendom — see his node for the documented Aquinas and Eckhart dependencies. A tradition-internal legal authority became a source-text for a rival tradition's theology.

## Relations to neighbours

- **vs. [[tradition-second-temple-judaism]]** — parent, not predecessor-in-name-only. The rabbis inherit the scriptures, the calendar, the festivals and the priestly memory, and discard the sacrificial system and the sectarian structure.
- **vs. [[tradition-samaritanism]]** — the other surviving Israelite line, holding only the Pentateuch and rejecting Jerusalem for Gerizim. It never accepted the Oral Torah premise and so never became rabbinic.
- **vs. Karaism** — the rejected internal alternative: written Torah alone. The controversy is what forced the Oral Torah from an assumption into an argument. (No node yet in the vault; the omission is a real gap.)
- **vs. [[tradition-early-christianity]]** — sibling reconstruction; see above.
- **vs. [[tradition-islam]]** — the host civilization for the tradition's most productive centuries (Babylonia, al-Andalus, the Maghreb, Egypt), and the language and philosophical vocabulary in which [[saadia-gaon]] and [[maimonides]] wrote.

## Disputes

- **When rabbinic Judaism actually became normative.** The sources say immediately; Schwartz and Hezser say late antiquity at the earliest, with Fishman placing the Talmud's curricular dominance in the medieval period. Treat any "the rabbis ruled Jewish life from 70 CE" statement as the tradition's self-presentation, not a finding.
- **Yavneh.** Whether it was an institution, a coalition, or largely a literary construct.
- **Mishnah and Tosefta.** Which depends on which, and whether the Tosefta preserves earlier material.
- **The *stam*.** How much of the Bavli's argumentation is late anonymous editorial work (Halivni, Friedman) versus authentic Amoraic debate.
- **The historicity of the Oral Torah chain.** No critical scholar treats *Pirkei Avot* 1:1 as a historical transmission record. It is a claim of legitimacy, and it should be read as one.
