# Rabbinic Judaism

**Rabbinic Judaism** is the form of Judaism that emerged after the destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE and has been the dominant form of Jewish religious life ever since. Its defining move: the replacement of Temple sacrifice with Torah study, prayer, and the rabbinic interpretation of law (*halakha*) as the central religious act. The Rabbi (teacher-sage) replaces the Priest (Temple officiant) as the community's spiritual authority.

The foundational texts of Rabbinic Judaism — the **Mishnah** (codified c. 200 CE under Rabbi Judah ha-Nasi) and the **Babylonian Talmud** (redacted c. 500–600 CE) — constitute one of the most extensive bodies of legal-theological commentary in world religion. The Talmud is not merely a legal code but a preserved argument: the debates of generations of rabbis are recorded in full, preserving minority opinions alongside majority rulings.

**Historical significance**: The transformation of Judaism from a Temple-based sacrificial religion into a portable text-based tradition enabled Judaism's survival through 2,000 years of diaspora. The destruction of the Temple, which appeared to be Judaism's death blow, was converted by the rabbinic movement into Judaism's greatest adaptive achievement: a religion that required no land, no Temple, and no priesthood — only a book and a community of learners.

**Sub-traditions** that emerged from the rabbinic trunk include: [[tradition-judaism-sephardic]] (Iberian), Ashkenazi Judaism (Northern European), Hasidic Judaism (Eastern European mystical revival), and the major modern denominations (Orthodox, Conservative, Reform, Reconstructionist).
