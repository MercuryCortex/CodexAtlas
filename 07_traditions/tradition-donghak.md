# Donghak — Eastern Learning

## What it is
Donghak ("Eastern Learning," 동학) was founded by [[choe-je-u]] (최제우, 1824–1864) in 1860 in Joseon Korea as an explicit synthesis of Korean indigenous shamanism, Confucianism, Buddhism, and Daoism. The name "Eastern Learning" (*Dong-hak*) was a deliberate counter-naming against *Seo-hak* ("Western Learning"), the Korean term for Catholicism, which had been spreading in Korea since the late 18th century and was perceived as both a spiritual threat and a vehicle for foreign political influence. Donghak positioned Korean spiritual resources against Western encroachment — making it one of the earliest explicitly anti-colonial religious movements in East Asia.

## Core doctrines

**Innaecheon (人乃天)**: "Humans are Heaven" / "God is within humans." Every person — regardless of class, gender, or social rank — contains the divine principle. In the rigidly hierarchical Confucian Joseon society, this was a radical egalitarianism with explosive social implications. The peasant, the woman, the slave: all carried the sacred.

**Sicheonju**: "Serving the Heavenly Lord" — a personal, direct relationship with the cosmic divine (*Hanullim*, the Heavenly Lord), accessible to all without priestly intermediaries or Confucian scholarly credential.

**The Thirteen-Character Mantra** (주문, *jumun*): a meditative and ritual formula blending Korean, Chinese, and indigenous elements — "Chigiwon Hyeon Daedo Gang ryeong" — used for spiritual attunement and healing. The mantra practice echoes Buddhist *dharani* and Daoist breathing-cultivation simultaneously.

**Gaebyeok** (개벽, "Opening of Heaven"): the coming cosmic renewal — a new age in which the present corrupt social order would be fundamentally transformed. Gaebyeok is Donghak's eschatological motor: it drives the sense of urgency that made the movement simultaneously a spiritual path and a political force.

## Historical phases

**1. Foundation (1860–1864):** Choe Je-u receives his founding vision (*Cheonju*, the Heavenly Lord, speaks to him directly in 1860), establishes Donghak, and rapidly attracts followers among peasants and local scholars dissatisfied with both the corrupt Joseon yangban establishment and the foreign Catholic missionaries. The Joseon government arrests him in 1863 and executes him in 1864 on charges of "deceiving the people" with heterodox teachings — an execution that paradoxically accelerated the movement's spread. The [[executed-divine-claimant]] pattern is explicit.

**2. Underground growth (1864–1894):** Under the second patriarch Choe Si-hyeong (최시형), Donghak systematizes its doctrine, expands its network of local communities (*jeopso*) across the Korean countryside, and develops a formal organizational structure while remaining suppressed by the state.

**3. Donghak Peasant Revolution (1894):** The movement's political explosion. Hundreds of thousands of Donghak adherents and Korean peasants rise against feudal corruption, brutal tax collection, and Japanese and foreign economic penetration. The Joseon government's appeal for Qing Chinese military assistance to suppress the uprising triggered Japan to send troops under the 1885 Tientsin Convention — directly precipitating the First Sino-Japanese War (1894–95). A religious movement's uprising became the proximate cause of a geopolitical transformation that ended the Sinocentric East Asian order. See [[event-donghak-peasant-revolution-1894]].

**4. Transformation (1905–present):** Following the revolution's suppression and the death of Choe Si-hyeong (executed 1898), the movement reorganizes under the third patriarch Son Byong-hui as *Cheondogyo* (天道教, "Religion of the Heavenly Way") in 1905 — absorbing the spiritual vision while distancing from the failed revolutionary label. Cheondogyo remains a recognized Korean religion today, with adherents primarily in North Korea (~1 million) and South Korea. It played a significant role in the March First Independence Movement (1919) against Japanese colonial rule.

## MASSIVE-win connections

**Donghak ↔ Catharism — suppressed syncretic response to dominant religion:**
Both movements arose as responses to a dominant outside religion pressing into their territory (Catholicism entering Korea; Roman Catholic Church in Languedoc); both were explicitly syncretic; both built substantial popular movements among peasants and lower classes; both were violently suppressed by the established political-religious order; both left successor movements that survived suppression. The structural parallel is strong even without transmission — this is the pattern of indigenous religiosity asserting itself against imperial-religious pressure. See [[tradition-catharism]] and [[event-albigensian-crusade-1209-1229]].

**Innaecheon ↔ Advaita *tat tvam asi* ↔ al-Hallaj's *Anā al-Ḥaqq*:**
Three traditions across three centuries and three continents asserting human-divine identity — and all three generating persecution responses from established authority. [[tradition-advaita-vedanta]]'s "That art thou" (*Chandogya Upanishad* 6.8.7); [[al-hallaj]]'s "I am the Truth" (*Anā al-Ḥaqq*, executed 922 CE Baghdad); Choe Je-u's *innaecheon* (executed 1864 Joseon). The grammar of mystical immanence — "God is in every human" — is consistently read as sedition by hierarchical state religions. The [[fanaa-annihilation]] theme maps the Sufi side of this pattern.

**Donghak ↔ Pentecostalism — colonial-margin syncretic eruptions:**
Both are 19th–early-20th century syncretic movements formed at the precise intersection of colonial pressure and spiritual innovation: Donghak (1860, Joseon Korea under threat of Western/Japanese pressure) and [[tradition-pentecostalism]] (Azusa Street 1906, Black Americans in post-Reconstruction Los Angeles). Both originate in populations experiencing institutional marginalization. Both developed vernacular, ecstatic, egalitarian spirituality. Both spread rapidly through lower classes. Both are structurally "responses from the margins." See [[event-azusa-street-revival-1906]].

**Choe Je-u's founding vision (1860) ↔ Muhammad's first revelation (610 CE) ↔ Joseph Smith's first vision (1820):**
The "founding vision" archetype in new religious movements: a solitary human receives a direct divine communication that becomes the basis of a new tradition. [[muhammad-ibn-abdullah]]'s cave at Hira; [[joseph-smith]]'s Sacred Grove; Choe Je-u's 1860 mystical encounter with the Heavenly Lord. All three share: the founder's initial confusion/fear about the vision; a divine identity claim; a mandate to teach; subsequent persecution by established authorities; accusations of deception. The pattern is documented in [[tradition-mormonism]], [[tradition-islam]], and now in Donghak.

**Donghak Peasant Revolution → First Sino-Japanese War:** theology driving geopolitics:
One of the rare cases in the vault where a religious movement's uprising directly precipitated a geopolitical transformation of continental scope. The movement that began as spiritual renewal in 1860 triggered (through the cascade: revolution → government request for Qing intervention → Japanese intervention under Tientsin Convention) a war that ended the Sinocentric East Asian world order and began Japan's rise to regional hegemony. This is the causal structure of [[event-donghak-peasant-revolution-1894]] → First Sino-Japanese War.

**Donghak ↔ Tangun founding mythology:**
Donghak positioned itself in explicit continuity with Korean national-spiritual identity, which centers on [[tangun]] as the divine founder of Korea. The *innaecheon* doctrine — every human contains the divine — is the universalized form of the founding-ancestor myth: Tangun's divine origin (son of [[hwanung]]) is now democratized into every Korean soul. Cheondogyo (the successor) explicitly invokes Tangun's sacred identity.
