# Advaita Vedanta

## Overview
Advaita Vedanta ("non-dual Vedānta") is the Hindu philosophical-theological school that holds the ultimate nature of reality to be a single, undifferentiated, infinite consciousness — *Brahman* — and that the individual self (*ātman*) is identical with Brahman. Apparent multiplicity (persons, world, deities) is *māyā* — cosmic illusion or appearance that conceals the underlying non-dual reality. Formalized most powerfully by [[shankara|Adi Shankara]] (~788–820 CE), it became the most influential and internationally recognized school of Hindu philosophy, and it deeply shaped the modern Hindu self-presentation to the West (via Vivekananda, Aurobindo, and the Ramakrishna Mission).

## Historical phases
1. **Upanishadic sources** (~-800 to -200) — the identification formula *tat tvam asi* ("that thou art," Chandogya Upanishad 6.8.7) and *aham brahmāsmi* ("I am Brahman," Brihadaranyaka 1.4.10) as the foundational equations from which all later Advaita unfolds.
2. **Gaudapada** (~7th c. CE) — Shankara's philosophical grandfather; his *Māndūkya Kārikā* introduces the *ajāta-vāda* (doctrine of no-origination), arguing that *nothing ever arises* — influenced by Mādhyamaka Buddhist *śūnyatā* philosophy.
3. **Adi Shankara** (~788–820 CE) — systematic commentaries on the Brahmasūtras, ten major Upanishads, and the Bhagavad Gītā; the *vivarta-vāda* (appearance-doctrine); the two-truth distinction (*vyāvahārika / pāramārthika*: conventional reality vs. absolute reality); establishment of the four *āmnāya maṭhas* (monastic centers).
4. **Post-Shankara debate** (~900–1300 CE) — Rāmānuja's *Vishishtādvaita* ("qualified non-dualism," ~1100 CE): Brahman is qualified by individual souls and the material world as real but internal modes; refutes Shankara's *māyā* doctrine. Madhva's *Dvaita* ("dualism," ~1250 CE): Brahman and ātman are fundamentally distinct.
5. **Neo-Vedanta** (1860s onward) — Ramakrishna Paramahamsa's experiential mysticism; Swami Vivekananda's reformulation as universal religion and perennial philosophy for a colonial-era Hindu cultural revival; global spread to the West.

## Core doctrines
- **Brahman** — the single infinite self-luminous being-consciousness-bliss (*sac-cid-ānanda*). Neither personal nor impersonal in the ordinary sense; beyond all predicates, accessible only via negation (*neti, neti* — "not this, not this").
- **Ātman** — the individual self, which on investigation is found to be identical with Brahman: *ātman = Brahman*.
- **Māyā** — the power by which Brahman appears as the multiplicity of the world; neither real (it is not ultimately existent) nor unreal (it produces effects); a third ontological category.
- **Avidyā** — ignorance (*avidyā*) of the ātman-Brahman identity is the root of suffering and rebirth; [[reincarnation|saṃsāra]] continues until this ignorance is removed.
- **Moksha** — liberation: the recognition (*jñāna*) that one never was bound, that ātman was always Brahman. Advaita is a *jñāna mārga* (path of knowledge) school par excellence.
- **Two-truth doctrine** — at the conventional/empirical level (*vyāvahārika*), world, persons, and even Īśvara (personal God) are real; at the absolute level (*pāramārthika*), only Brahman exists.

## MASSIVE wins — cross-tradition edges
- **Advaita ↔ Neoplatonism / Plotinus**: Plotinus's *Enneads* (~270 CE) describe the One as the source of all being, the Intellect and Soul as emanations, and the return of the soul to the One as the goal (*henosis*). The structural homology with Brahman (One), Hiraṇyagarbha (Cosmic Intellect), and jīva (soul-returning-to-Brahman) is one of the most studied parallels in comparative philosophy. See [[henosis]].
- **Advaita ↔ Meister Eckhart / Christian mysticism**: Eckhart's *Gottheit* (Godhead, beyond the personal God) and the soul's identity with it parallels Shankara's Brahman/Ātman identity; Rudolf Otto wrote *Mysticism East and West* (1932) explicitly comparing them.
- **Advaita ↔ Gnosticism**: the material world as appearance concealing an ultimate spiritual reality; the self as a spark of the absolute; liberation through *gnōsis* / *jñāna*. See [[idealism-metaphysical]].
- **Advaita ↔ Buddhist Mādhyamaka / Yogācāra**: Gaudapada's *ajāta-vāda* is visibly influenced by Nāgārjuna's Mādhyamaka; Shankara was accused by Rāmānuja of being a "crypto-Buddhist." The *māyā* / *śūnyatā* relationship is one of the most contested and productive comparative axes in Indian philosophy. See [[emptiness-sunyata]].
- **Neo-Vedanta ↔ Perennialism / Theosophy**: Vivekananda's reformulation directly influenced the Perennialist movement (Guénon, Schuon, Aldous Huxley's *Perennial Philosophy*) and 20th-century Western esotericism.

## Relations to neighbors
- vs. [[tradition-vedic-hinduism]]: Advaita Vedanta is the systematic philosophical apex of the Vedic inheritance — it claims to disclose the deepest meaning of the Upanishads. Shankara's *Prasthānatraya* commentaries (Upanishads + Brahmasūtras + Gītā) set the gold standard for Vedantic interpretation.
- vs. [[tradition-buddhism]]: both acknowledge the conventional reality of the empirical self while holding ultimate reality to be non-personal and beyond ordinary conceptualization. Rāmānuja accused Shankara of being a disguised Buddhist; modern scholarship confirms Gaudapada's Mādhyamaka debts. Key divergence: Advaita asserts a universal ātman/Brahman identity; Buddhism denies any permanent self (*anātman*).
- vs. [[tradition-gnosticism]]: both posit the material world as appearance veiling a deeper reality; both offer liberation via knowledge; both distinguish a cosmic ignorance-principle (Demiurge / Avidyā) from the true ground of being.
- vs. [[tradition-hermeticism]]: the Hermetic principle "as above, so below" and the divine spark (*pneuma*) ascending to the One offers structural parallels to ātman-Brahman identity; both traditions influenced early modern comparative religion.
- vs. [[tradition-neoplatonism]]: see MASSIVE wins above. The Plotinian One / Intellect / Soul emanation structure is the closest Western philosophical parallel to Brahman / Hiraṇyagarbha / jīva.
