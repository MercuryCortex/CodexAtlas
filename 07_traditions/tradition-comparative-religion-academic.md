# Comparative Religion (Academic Discipline)

## Overview
The modern academic study of religion — variously called "comparative religion," "history of religions," "religious studies," "*Religionswissenschaft*" — emerged as a distinct discipline in late-19th-century Europe (Max Müller's coinage "science of religion," 1873) and developed across the 20th century into a globally institutionalized academic field. **This is a meta-tradition: the Codex Atlas vault itself is a Phase-7 artifact within this tradition,** assembling its materials according to the discipline's evolving methodological consensus on what counts as evidence, how to treat religious claims, and how to construct cross-tradition comparison.

## Historical phases
1. **Founding period** (1873–1915) — Müller's *Sacred Books of the East*; Tylor's *Primitive Culture* (1871); Robertson Smith's *Religion of the Semites* (1889); Frazer's *Golden Bough* (1890–1915); Durkheim's *Elementary Forms* (1912); Otto's *Das Heilige* (1917).
2. **Mature classical period** (1920s–1960s) — phenomenology of religion (van der Leeuw, Wach, Eliade); Jungian comparativism; Eranos Conferences (Ascona, 1933+); Scholem's Kabbalah studies; Jonas's Gnosticism; Yates's Renaissance Hermeticism.
3. **Institutionalization** (1957 onward) — Eliade at Chicago; the History of Religions program; American Academy of Religion (founded 1909, expanded mid-century); rapid US religious-studies department growth.
4. **Postmodern / critical period** (1980s onward) — Jonathan Z. Smith's methodological critique; Asad on the discipline's Protestant-secular constitution; McCutcheon, Fitzgerald, Masuzawa on the construction of "world religions"; King 2003 and Williams 1996 on "Gnosticism" as construction; ongoing decolonizing-religious-studies reflection.

## Defining features
- Cross-tradition comparison as a methodological principle.
- Religious phenomena studied as historical, cultural, social, and (variably) experiential phenomena — not adjudicated for truth-value.
- The "equal-weight document rule" (Codex Atlas vault methodology): preservation and reception are historical findings, not verdicts on textual evidentiary status.
- Continued methodological self-critique.

## Cross-phase notes
**This tradition has performed a substantial portion of the modern recovery of Phases 1–6.** Decipherment of Egyptian hieroglyphics (1822), cuneiform (1857), Ugaritic alphabet (1929–30); Nag Hammadi discovery (1945) → Robinson translation (1977) → Pagels popularization (1979); Dead Sea Scrolls discovery (1947) → Vermes, VanderKam, Fitzmyer, Schiffman scholarship; Scholem's recovery of Kabbalah (1941); Yates's recovery of Renaissance Hermeticism (1964); Jonas's recovery of Gnosticism (1958). Without this Phase-7 academic-recovery layer, the vault's Phase-1-through-Phase-6 nodes would not be possible as currently constituted. The vault is the discipline's child.

## Disputes
- **Sui generis sacred**: whether religion is a *sui generis* category (Otto, Eliade) or analyzable into other categories (Durkheim, McCutcheon).
- **"World religions" framework**: whether the Müller-derived "world religions" taxonomy is a useful classification or an arbitrary 19th-century Protestant-secular construction (Masuzawa 2005).
- **Insider/outsider methods**: phenomenological-empathetic vs. naturalistic-explanatory methodologies.
