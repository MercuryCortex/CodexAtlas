# Neoplatonism

## Overview
The dominant philosophical tradition of late antiquity, founded by Plotinus (~204/5–270) and extending through Porphyry, Iamblichus, the Athenian school (Plutarch, Syrianus, Proclus, Damascius), and the Alexandrian school (Hypatia, Hierocles, Olympiodorus), until Justinian's 529 closure of the Athenian Academy and the broader Christianization of intellectual life. Neoplatonist metaphysics articulated a hierarchical descent of being from the absolute simplicity of the One through Intellect and Soul into the material cosmos; soteriology was the ascent in reverse, by philosophical contemplation (Plotinus) or theurgic ritual (Iamblichus, Proclus).

## Historical phases
1. **Plotinian school** (245–270) — Plotinus in Rome; Porphyry editing.
2. **Syrian school** (~280–325) — Iamblichus at Apamea; theurgy added to contemplation.
3. **Athenian Neoplatonism** (~410–529) — Plutarch, Syrianus, Proclus, Damascius; systematization in axiomatic form.
4. **Alexandrian Neoplatonism** (~400–600) — Hypatia, Hierocles, Olympiodorus, Stephanus; less theurgical, more compatible with Christianity (Christian Neoplatonists emerge).
5. **Christian and Arabic afterlife** — Pseudo-Dionysius (Proclean Christian); Eriugena; *Liber de Causis* (Arabic Proclean redaction); medieval scholastic engagement.

## Defining doctrines
- The One — absolute simplicity, beyond being, ineffable.
- Hypostatic emanation: One → Nous → Soul → cosmos.
- Procession-Reversion-Remaining (Proclus' triad).
- Union (*henōsis*) with the One as the philosophical goal.
- Theurgy as the soul's ritual ascent (Iamblichus onward).

## Relations to neighbors
- vs. [[tradition-hellenistic-philosophy]]: parent tradition — Neoplatonism is the late-antique consolidation of Middle Platonism.
- vs. [[tradition-gnosticism]]: Plotinus' Ennead II.9 explicitly polemicizes.
- vs. [[tradition-hermeticism]]: parallel currents; Iamblichus' theurgy draws on Hermetic and Chaldean material.
- vs. [[tradition-christianity-canonical]]: Christian Neoplatonism via Augustine, Pseudo-Dionysius, the Cappadocians; pagan Neoplatonism explicitly anti-Christian via Porphyry's *Against the Christians* and Julian's *Against the Galileans*.

## MASSIVE WIN: Cross-Tradition Parallels

### The Neoplatonic One ↔ Dao ↔ Ein Sof ↔ Nirguna Brahman
The vault's central structural win. Plotinus's One is beyond being, beyond Intellect, beyond even the Good as predicated quality — the apophatic absolute from which all things proceed by necessary emanation. This is structurally identical to the Daodejing's Dao ("The Dao that can be named is not the eternal Dao"), the Kabbalistic *Ein Sof* ("Without End, beyond all thought and word"), and Advaita's *nirguna* Brahman (beyond all attributes; *neti neti*). All four are: ineffable, absolute, source of all being by overflow or self-expression, and the goal of mystical return. No historical contact connects Daoism to Neoplatonism; the connection to Kabbalah and Vedanta is mediated (via Arabic Neoplatonism and Upanishadic Neoplatonism respectively). The independent structural convergence is the philosophical MASSIVE WIN. See [[tradition-daoism]], [[tradition-kabbalah]].

### Proclus henads → Kabbalistic sefirot (documented transmission)
The specific historical connection that Scholem (1962) argued and the vault needs to hold. Proclus's *henads* — divine unities that mediate between the absolutely transcendent One and the lower hypostases of Being, Life, and Intellect — are the Neoplatonic predecessor to the Kabbalistic sefirot. Each henad is a "god" in a precise sense: a real aspect of the One's self-differentiation, neither identical with nor fully separate from the One. The channel: Arabic Neoplatonism (al-Kindī, al-Fārābī, ibn Gabirol's *Fons Vitae* — itself a Jewish-Neoplatonic synthesis) reached the Provençal Kabbalists through 11th-12th century Iberian Jewish philosophy. Isaac the Blind's circle in Provence (~1200) transformed the abstract Proclean henads into the mythological, personified, sexually-charged sefirot — the Judaization and emotionalization of the Proclean scheme. See [[proclus]], [[sefirot]].

### Proclus → Pseudo-Dionysius → entire Western Christian apophatic tradition
The most consequential single philosophical transmission in Western Christianity. Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite (5th-6th c.) is a Christian who rewrote Proclus — his *Celestial Hierarchy*, *Mystical Theology*, and *Divine Names* are translations of Proclus's hypostatic hierarchy and apophatic logic into a Christian theological framework. The Dionysian texts became canonical Christian mystical theology after Maximus the Confessor's commentary (~630) and Eriugena's Latin translation (~860). Medieval Europe received Neoplatonism primarily through this Dionysian filter, making Proclus — unacknowledged for centuries — the silent shaper of Thomas Aquinas, Meister Eckhart, and the entire apophatic tradition. This is a documented historical transmission of the highest order. See [[pseudo-dionysius-the-areopagite]], [[john-scotus-eriugena]].

### Proclus → Arabic *Liber de Causis* → Islamic philosophy → Latin scholasticism
The *Kitāb al-Khayr al-Maḥḍ* ("Book of Pure Goodness" / Latin: *Liber de Causis*) is an Arabic redaction of Proclus's *Elements of Theology*, probably compiled in 9th-century Baghdad. Translated into Latin in the 12th century (Toledo, ~1185) and misattributed to Aristotle, it became a major source for medieval European Aristotelianism — until Aquinas identified the actual Proclean source by comparing it to Moerbeke's new Latin translation of the *Elements*. Al-Fārābī and Ibn Sīnā's Neoplatonized Aristotelianism derives partly from this transmission. This is the specific channel by which Proclus shaped Islamic philosophy and thence Latin scholasticism without being named.

### Plotinus ↔ Upanishadic Brahman — the founding perennialist comparison
Plotinus's "flight of the alone to the Alone" (*Enneads* VI.9.11) and the Upanishadic *tat tvam asi* ("you are That") — both describe the individual soul discovering its identity with the infinite absolute. The parallel drove the modern perennialist school (Aldous Huxley's *Perennial Philosophy*, 1945; Huston Smith; Frithjof Schuon). The historical question is open: Plotinus's teacher Ammonius Saccas is reported to have been interested in Indian thought; Plotinus personally tried to accompany Gordian III's expedition to meet Persian and Indian sages (Porphyry, *Vita Plotini* 3). No direct borrowing is documented, but the structural proximity is among the closest in world philosophical history.
