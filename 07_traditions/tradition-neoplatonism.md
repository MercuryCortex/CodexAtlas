# Neoplatonism

## Overview
The dominant philosophical tradition of late antiquity, founded by Plotinus (~204/5–270) and extending through Porphyry, Iamblichus, the Athenian school (Plutarch, Syrianus, Proclus, Damascius), and the Alexandrian school (Hypatia, Hierocles, Olympiodorus), until Justinian's 529 closure of the Athenian Academy and the broader Christianization of intellectual life. Neoplatonist metaphysics articulated a hierarchical descent of being from the absolute simplicity of the One through Intellect and Soul into the material cosmos; soteriology was the ascent in reverse, by philosophical contemplation (Plotinus) or theurgic ritual (Iamblichus, Proclus).

## Historical phases
1. **Plotinian school** (245–270) — Plotinus in Rome; Porphyry editing.
2. **Syrian school** (~280–325) — Iamblichus at Apamea; theurgy added to contemplation.
3. **Athenian Neoplatonism** (~410–529) — Plutarch, Syrianus, Proclus, Damascius; systematization in axiomatic form.
4. **Alexandrian Neoplatonism** (~400–600) — Hypatia, Hierocles, Olympiodorus, Stephanus; less theurgical, more compatible with Christianity (Christian Neoplatonists emerge).
5. **Christian and Arabic afterlife** — Pseudo-Dionysius (Proclean Christian); Eriugena; *Liber de Causis* (Arabic Proclean redaction); medieval scholastic engagement.

## Defining doctrines
- The One — absolute simplicity, beyond being, ineffable.
- Hypostatic emanation: One → Nous → Soul → cosmos.
- Procession-Reversion-Remaining (Proclus' triad).
- Union (*henōsis*) with the One as the philosophical goal.
- Theurgy as the soul's ritual ascent (Iamblichus onward).

## Relations to neighbors
- vs. [[tradition-hellenistic-philosophy]]: parent tradition — Neoplatonism is the late-antique consolidation of Middle Platonism.
- vs. [[tradition-gnosticism]]: Plotinus' Ennead II.9 explicitly polemicizes.
- vs. [[tradition-hermeticism]]: parallel currents; Iamblichus' theurgy draws on Hermetic and Chaldean material.
- vs. [[tradition-christianity-canonical]]: Christian Neoplatonism via Augustine, Pseudo-Dionysius, the Cappadocians; pagan Neoplatonism explicitly anti-Christian via Porphyry's *Against the Christians* and Julian's *Against the Galileans*.
