The Irish-Celtic tradition encompasses the pre-Christian and early medieval religious, literary, and cultural practices of Ireland and related Celtic-speaking communities in the British Isles. It is documented primarily through early medieval Irish manuscripts (c. 6th–12th c. CE) that preserve material of much earlier provenance.

## The Filid — Poet-Priests

The *filid* (singular *fili*) were the learned class of medieval Ireland — simultaneously poets, historians, law-keepers, and religious practitioners. They occupied a position analogous to the Indian Brahmin priestly-scholar class: inheritors of oral tradition who underwent years of training in poetry, genealogy, mythology, and sacred knowledge.

The filid maintained the connection between Ogham writing and its deeper layers of meaning — see [[alphabet-ogham]] for the bríatharogam (kenning-ogham) system through which each Ogham letter carries its cosmological associations.

## Sacred Landscape

Irish-Celtic religion was deeply embedded in the landscape: sacred hills (Tara, Emain Macha), sacred rivers (the Boyne — see [[boann]]), sacred trees, and the *síde* (fairy mounds — the dwelling places of the Tuatha Dé Danann after they withdrew from the visible world).

## Relationship to Celtic Tradition

The Irish-Celtic tradition is the best-preserved branch of the broader [[tradition-celtic]] family. Comparative Celtic scholarship uses Irish sources (along with Welsh, Gaulish, and Continental evidence) to reconstruct pre-Christian Celtic religious practice.
