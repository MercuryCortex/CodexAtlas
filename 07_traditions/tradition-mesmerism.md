# Mesmerism (Animal Magnetism)

## Overview

Mesmerism is the healing movement founded by the Viennese physician **Franz Anton Mesmer** (1734–1815), built on his claim that a single superfine physical fluid — *animal magnetism* — pervades the universe, connects the heavenly bodies, the earth, and all animate bodies, and that disease is an obstruction of its flow which the trained magnetizer can remove by channeling the fluid and provoking a healing "crisis" (the 27 propositions of the 1779 *Mémoire*). Two things about this doctrine matter for the Atlas. First, it was **explicitly materialist**: Mesmer presented animal magnetism as Newtonian natural science, indignantly rejected supernatural readings of his cures (his 1775 verdict on the exorcist Johann Joseph Gassner was that Gassner healed by unwitting magnetism, not by God), and sought the endorsement of the academies, not the churches (Darnton 1968; Pattie 1994). Second, the doctrine was **debunked in his own lifetime** — by the 1784 royal commissions, in a landmark of blinded, sham-controlled assessment (see next section) — and yet the *practice* not only survived the death of its theory but became one of the most productive seedbeds of nineteenth-century religious and psychological innovation (Ellenberger 1970). Ellenberger's *The Discovery of the Unconscious* (1970) opens with Mesmer and calls the tradition he started "the first dynamic psychiatry."

In the Atlas this node is the missing nineteenth-century connective tissue between five hubs: upstream to [[paracelsus]] (the magnetic-medicine lineage) and [[isaac-newton]] (the fluid's actual theoretical dress); downstream to [[tradition-spiritualism]] (via Andrew Jackson Davis's trance mediumship), to [[sigmund-freud]] (via the hypnotism chain Braid → Charcot/Bernheim → the cathartic method), to [[tradition-new-age]] (via Quimby and New Thought), and laterally to [[emanuel-swedenborg]], whose visionary cosmology was fused with mesmeric trance in American reception.

## The 1784 royal commissions (the fluid debunked — a landmark of blind assessment)

In March 1784 Louis XVI appointed two commissions to examine animal magnetism — one from the Faculty of Medicine and the Academy of Sciences, chaired by **Benjamin Franklin** and including **Antoine Lavoisier**, the astronomer **Jean Sylvain Bailly** (who drafted the report), and **Joseph-Ignace Guillotin**; a second from the Royal Society of Medicine. They examined the practice of Mesmer's chief Paris disciple **Charles Deslon** (Mesmer himself refused to cooperate, and disavowed Deslon). The method, largely designed around Lavoisier's and Franklin's insistence on isolating the imagination, is why historians of medicine treat the episode as a founding moment of blind assessment: subjects were **blindfolded** and told they were being magnetized when they were not (and vice versa); at Franklin's residence in Passy a sensitive young subject was asked to find the one "magnetized" tree in the garden, and collapsed in crisis before the wrong one; sham and real procedures were systematically swapped (Rapport 1784; Kaptchuk 1998; Darnton 1968). The conclusion: the fluid does not exist; the effects are real but are the products of **imagination, imitation, and touch**. A separate secret report to the king warned of the erotic dangers of the crisis-treatment of women. The Society of Medicine's botanist **Antoine-Laurent de Jussieu** filed the lone dissenting report, holding that some agent — he suggested "animal heat" — remained unexplained.

The commissions are the reason mesmerism is documented in this vault the way Rosicrucianism's invented Brotherhood or the Golden Dawn's forged charter are documented: the **debunking is part of the record, not an embarrassment to be smoothed over**. But note precisely what was and was not refuted: the commissioners disproved the *fluid* while conceding the *effects*. That concession — cure by imagination — is the exact door through which every successor current entered: Puységur's and Braid's psychological readings of the trance, and the religious readings of Davis, Quimby, and the Spiritualists.

## Historical phases

1. **Viennese origins (1766–1778)** — the 1766 dissertation (planetary influence on the body, via Mead's Newtonian tidal medicine); the 1774 Oesterlin magnet cures and the leap to an internal fluid; the January 1775 circular announcing animal magnetism; the 1775 Gassner affair (exorcism re-explained as magnetism — Ellenberger's opening scene); the 1777 Maria Theresia von Paradis scandal (the blind pianist whose treatment failed publicly) and Mesmer's departure.
2. **Parisian vogue and crisis (1778–1785)** — the clinic with the *baquet* (the communal magnetic tub), group séances to glass-armonica music, aristocratic clientele; the subscription **Société de l'Harmonie Universelle** (1783) spreading the method through provincial lodges; the 1779 *Mémoire*; the 1784 commissions and Mesmer's eclipse (he left Paris c. 1785, dying at Meersburg in 1815).
3. **The somnambulic turn and continental diffusion (1784–1848)** — the Marquis de **Puységur**, magnetizing the peasant Victor Race at Buzancy (1784), found not a convulsive crisis but a lucid sleep-waking state with rapport and post-trance amnesia: **artificial somnambulism**, the pivot of the whole tradition from physics to psychology (Crabtree 1993). Deleuze systematized (1813); German romantic medicine made the somnambule a visionary — Justinus Kerner's *The Seeress of Prevorst* (1829) records spirit-communication in magnetic trance, proto-Spiritualism a generation early.
4. **British medical mesmerism and the birth of hypnotism (1837–1860)** — Elliotson's UCL demonstrations and forced resignation (1838), his journal *The Zoist*; Esdaile's mesmeric surgical anesthesia in Bengal; and **James Braid**, who after seeing the demonstrator Lafontaine (1841) re-derived the trance from fixed attention, discarded the fluid entirely, and renamed the phenomenon **hypnotism** (*Neurypnology*, 1843) — the rebranding that made the trance admissible to science (Winter 1998; Gauld 1992).
5. **American religious reception (1836–1875)** — Charles Poyen's New England tours (1836 onward) seeded a magnetizer culture; the watchmaker **Phineas P. Quimby** of Maine, first a stage magnetizer with the clairvoyant Lucius Burkmar, dropped the fluid for pure mind-cure in the 1850s; his patients included Warren Felt Evans, Julius Dresser, and (1862–65) Mary Patterson, later [[mary-baker-eddy]] (Fuller 1982; Albanese 2007). In parallel, **Andrew Jackson Davis** of Poughkeepsie turned magnetic trance into revelation (see next section).
6. **Dissolution (c. 1848–1900)** — the word "mesmerism" faded as its content was inherited: by Spiritualism (1848 onward), by academic hypnotism (Liébeault and Bernheim at Nancy; Charcot's February 1882 rehabilitation of hypnosis before the Académie des Sciences), and by the mind-cure movement. What had been one movement in 1784 was three by 1890.

## The therapeutic-to-occult drift (three documented exits)

The investigative payload of this node is the **drift**: a materialist therapy, refuted as physics, survived as technique and was re-theologized three separate times. Each exit is a documented transmission chain, not a typological parallel.

- **Exit 1 — mediumship (into Spiritualism).** Davis, magnetized from 1843, reported a March 1844 trance encounter with Galen and the spirit of [[emanuel-swedenborg]], then dictated *The Principles of Nature, Her Divine Revelations* (1847) in magnetic trance — Swedenborgian cosmology (the spheres, the "Summer-Land") delivered through the mesmeric state. When the Hydesville rappings began in 1848, the theology was already waiting; Davis became [[tradition-spiritualism]]'s chief systematic thinker, and the Spiritualist medium is, historically, the magnetic somnambule with the magnetizer removed (Crabtree 1993; Taves 1999; Godwin 1994). This is the Swedenborg wire: **fusion in reception** — Mesmer and Swedenborg never touched in origin.
- **Exit 2 — hypnotism (into dynamic psychiatry).** Puységur's somnambulism → Braid's hypnotism → the Nancy school (suggestion) and Charcot's Salpêtrière (hypnosis and hysteria). The young [[sigmund-freud]] studied under Charcot (1885–86), translated Bernheim, visited Nancy (1889), and practiced hypnotic catharsis (*Studies on Hysteria*, 1895, with Breuer) before replacing hypnosis with free association — Ellenberger's grand thesis is precisely that psychoanalysis and [[tradition-jungian-depth-psychology|Jungian psychology]] ([[carl-gustav-jung]]'s 1902 dissertation examined a trance medium) descend from the magnetizers' rapport and somnambulism (Ellenberger 1970).
- **Exit 3 — mind-cure (into Christian Science, New Thought, and New Age).** Quimby's passage from magnetizer to mental healer carried the practice from fluid to mind; from his circle descend New Thought (Evans, the Dressers, Hopkins) and — by a contested route (see Disputes) — [[mary-baker-eddy]]'s Christian Science, whose own demonology of **"Malicious Animal Magnetism"** wears the ancestry in its name. [[william-james]] canonized "mind-cure" as America's "religion of healthy-mindedness" (*Varieties*, 1902); Hanegraaff (1996) and Albanese (2007) trace the [[tradition-new-age]] healing-"energy" vocabulary to this lineage.
- **The occultist absorption (a fourth, minor channel):** [[eliphas-levi]]'s "astral light" re-baptized the universal fluid for the French occult revival (McIntosh 1972), and [[helena-blavatsky]] defended mesmeric phenomena as recovered ancient magic in [[phase-7-005-isis-unveiled|Isis Unveiled]] vol. 1 (Godwin 1994) — feeding [[tradition-theosophy]].

## Cross-tradition significance

Mesmerism's significance in the vault is that it is a **transmission bottleneck of the same kind as the Golden Dawn, documented person-by-person**: Poyen lectures, Quimby attends; Levingston magnetizes Davis; Freud sits in Charcot's clinic. Nothing here rests on typological resemblance — the chains run through named people, dated events, and printed books, which makes this one of the strongest *transmission* (not convergence) cases in the modern wing of the Atlas. Its deeper interest is the **inversion it documents**: the standard modern story is religion giving way to science, but mesmerism ran the other way — a self-consciously scientific, materialist system was falsified by an exemplary scientific test (1784) and then progressively *re-enchanted*, ending as the technical substrate of séance religion, occult fluid-lore, and faith-healing. Honesty requires the converse note too: the same falsified system also seeded genuine science — blinded placebo methodology (via the commission that killed it) and the psychology of the unconscious (via the somnambulism it discovered). Upstream, its debt to [[paracelsus]] is real but generic (the magnetic-cure tradition of Paracelsus, van Helmont, Maxwell, and [[athanasius-kircher]] made "magnetic medicine" an available idiom), while its *proximate* source was Newtonian medicine — a distinction the Disputes section preserves.

## Disputes

- **Mesmer's sources: Paracelsian heir or Newtonian plagiarist?** The older historiography (and Mesmer's own Vienna critics) placed him in the Paracelsus–van Helmont–Maxwell magnetic-medicine lineage; Ellenberger (1970) surveys these precursors. Frank Pattie (1956; 1994) showed that Mesmer's 1766 dissertation borrows extensively and without acknowledgment from Richard Mead's *De imperio solis ac lunae* (1704) and argued the genuine framework is Newtonian, with the Paracelsian resemblance a convergence of idiom. Mesmer himself insisted on his originality against both. The Atlas wires both upstream edges and marks the Paracelsian one as lineage-of-idiom rather than demonstrated reading.
- **What 1784 actually proved.** The commissions disproved the fluid; they did not show the treatments were inert — the report itself attributes real effects to imagination. Whether the episode should be narrated as "science kills mesmerism" (the Enlightenment telling; Darnton 1968 complicates it) or as "the discovery of the placebo-shaped remainder" (Kaptchuk 1998) is a live historiographical difference of emphasis. Jussieu's minority report is the contemporary dissent.
- **Continuity vs. rupture at Braid.** Gauld (1992) and Winter (1998) discuss whether hypnotism is mesmerism renamed (continuity of practice, rupture of theory) or a genuinely new object; Braid's own polemic insisted on rupture. Ellenberger and Crabtree treat the practice-lineage as continuous — the position this node adopts, flagged as interpretive.
- **The Quimby–Eddy debt.** Whether Christian Science derives from Quimby's mesmerism-descended mind-cure (the Dresser/New Thought claim, pressed via the *Quimby Manuscripts*, 1921) or was Eddy's independent discovery (her own and her church's position) is the sharpest dispute in the American lineage; Gill (1998) gives the balanced account. The vault records the influence edge with this dispute attached rather than silently taking a side.
- **Minor:** the tradition that the young Mozart's *Bastien und Bastienne* premiered in Mesmer's Vienna garden theater (1768) is weakly documented; Mozart's parody of the "mesmeric stone" in *Così fan tutte* (1790) is, by contrast, secure — a datum for mesmerism's celebrity, not its doctrine.
