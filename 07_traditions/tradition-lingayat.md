# Lingayat / Vīraśaiva Tradition (12th c. CE onward)

A South Indian Shaiva reform movement that emerged in the 12th c. CE in northern Karnataka under the leadership of **[[basavanna]]** (Basava, ~1131–1167) and a circle of *vacanakāras* (poet-saints) including **`allama-prabhu`** and **`akka-mahadevi`**. The tradition is theologically Shaiva-monotheistic (Shiva as the sole supreme reality, worshipped via the personal *iṣṭa-liṅga* — a small portable lingam worn on the body), socially anti-caste-anti-Brahmanical-anti-temple, and philosophically distinct from the older Shaiva Siddhanta + Kashmir Shaivism traditions.

## Core practice — the *iṣṭa-liṅga*

The defining ritual practice: every Lingayat initiate (regardless of caste or sex) wears a small portable lingam — the *iṣṭa-liṅga* — on the body. Worship is direct (between the person and the linga); no temple, no Brahmin intermediary required. This is a radical structural break from temple-Brahmanical Hinduism — the body becomes the temple.

## The *vacanas*

The Lingayat literary corpus is the *vacanas* — short prose-poetic devotional utterances in Kannada (a Dravidian language, vernacular not Sanskrit) composed by Basavanna, Allama Prabhu, Akka Mahadevi, and others. The *vacanas* are anti-elitist (vernacular not Sanskrit), anti-temple, anti-caste, and erotically-charged in their devotional expression. Ramanujan's 1973 *Speaking of Śiva* is the canonical English translation.

## Cross-tradition position

Lingayatism is one of the cleanest South Indian *bhakti* movements with a documented anti-caste theological-political program. It parallels (without contact):
- The later **[[guru-nanak]]** + Sikh tradition (15th-16th c.) — Punjabi anti-caste vernacular devotional movement
- The Kabir + Sant tradition of north India — vernacular anti-caste devotional poetry
- The Lutheran Reformation in Europe (16th c.) — vernacular + anti-clerical + anti-intermediary

The structural parallel — bhakti devotionalism + vernacular language + anti-intermediary theology + anti-caste/anti-class — is one of the strongest cross-tradition convergences in medieval religious-reform history.

The contemporary political-legal status of Lingayatism (whether it should be classified as Hindu or as a separate religion under Indian law) is contested; the 2018 Karnataka state recommendation that Lingayats be classified as a separate religion was not implemented by the central government.
