# Lakota Religion

## Overview
The religious tradition of the Lakota people (one of the three dialectal-political divisions of the Oceti Sakowin — Seven Council Fires; the others are Dakota and Nakota), centered on the Great Plains of North America. The Lakota arrived in the Great Plains approximately 1500–1700 CE, migrating westward from the Great Lakes region following European pressure on eastern tribes; earlier forms of Lakota religion likely date further. Their worldview centers on *Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka* — the Great Mystery or Great Sacred — and the sacred geography of *Pahá Sápa* (the Black Hills of present South Dakota and Wyoming), which are regarded as the center of the world, the "Heart of Everything That Is."

Lakota religion is not a religion of creed or text but of *rites, relationships, and recognition*. The sacred (wakȟáŋ) is not located above the world in a transcendent deity but distributed through the world as a quality of being — animate in the sun, the earth, the rock, the wind, the bear, and ultimately organized in Wakan Tanka as their structured totality. This is one of the most striking features of Lakota theology from a cross-tradition perspective.

## Historical phases
1. **Pre-Plains substrate (pre-1500 CE)** — Woodland period Siouan religion; mound-building traditions; eastward-origin traditions preserved in oral narrative.
2. **Great Plains florescence (~1500–1850)** — westward migration; horse acquisition (~1750, transforming Plains culture); bison-centered economy and religion; sun dance elaboration; full development of the Seven Sacred Rites.
3. **Contact and conflict (~1851–1890)** — Fort Laramie Treaty 1851; gold rush invasion of the Black Hills 1874; Great Sioux War 1876–77; Crazy Horse killed 1877; Sitting Bull returns from Canada 1881. Black Elk participates in Buffalo Bill's Wild West Show (1886–89) and witnesses European religious traditions.
4. **Ghost Dance and Wounded Knee (~1889–1890)** — [[event-ghost-dance-wounded-knee-1890]]: Wovoka's syncretic Ghost Dance movement spreads among Lakota; US Army massacre at Wounded Knee Creek (December 29, 1890) kills ~250–300 Lakota. A catastrophic rupture.
5. **Reservation period and Christian overlay (~1890–1960)** — forced assimilation; boarding schools; Black Elk's complex Catholic-catechist / Lakota-holy-man dual identity (DeMallie 1984 recovers the Lakota interior). Lakota ceremonial life persists underground.
6. **American Indian Movement and revitalization (~1970s–present)** — AIM founded 1968; Wounded Knee occupation 1973; Lakota ceremonial revitalization; legal fights for Black Hills (still unresolved under Sioux Nation v. United States 1980 — the Supreme Court ruled the seizure unconstitutional and awarded $105 million; Lakota refused the money, demanding land return).

## Core texts
- [[phase-8-021-black-elk-speaks]] — Heȟáka Sápa / Black Elk's visions and the Seven Sacred Rites as narrated to John G. Neihardt (1932); foundational for outsiders but requires DeMallie 1984 correction.
- [[phase-8-022-the-sacred-pipe]] — Brown's 1953 compilation of Black Elk's teaching on the Seven Sacred Rites.
- Walker (1980) *Lakota Belief and Ritual* — interview transcripts with Oglala holy men: the closest available primary-ethnographic documentation of the classical Sixteen Wakan Beings system.

## Pantheon / Sacred Beings — The Sixteen Wakan
Walker's interviews (1980) document a structured hierarchy of *wakȟáŋ* (sacred) beings organized under *Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka*:

**Superior (Kte Wakan):**
- [[wakan-tanka]] — the totality of all that is sacred; not a personal god but the great field of sacredness itself
- Wí — the Sun (male; primary agent of order and time)
- Skáŋ — the Sky (motivating force; source of movement and soul)
- Makȟá — the Earth (mother; generative ground of life)
- Iŋyaŋ — the Rock (the primordial, that from which everything proceeds)

**Associate (Tob Kin):**
- Haŋwí — the Moon (Wí's consort)
- Tȟáŋte — the Wind (messenger)
- Wohpé — the Falling Star (mediator; became [[white-buffalo-calf-woman]])
- Whirlwind

**Subordinate (Wakan Kolakiciyapi):**
- Tatȟáŋka — the Buffalo
- Húŋkayapi — the Bear
- Tȟáŋka — the Four Winds
- Yumní — the Whirlwind

**The Seven Sacred Rites** (brought by [[white-buffalo-calf-woman]] with the sacred pipe *čaŋnúŋpa*):
1. **Inípi** — Sweat Lodge (purification; the first rite)
2. **Haŋbléčheyapi** — Vision Quest (*crying for a vision*; solitary encounter with the sacred)
3. **Wiwáŋyaŋg Wačhípi** — Sun Dance (communal sacrifice; cosmic renewal; the sun dance was outlawed by the US government 1883–1934)
4. **Huŋkápi** — Making of Relatives (kinship extension across clan lines)
5. **Išnáthi Awíčhalowaŋpi** — Keeping of the Soul (soul-retention and release; delayed mourning)
6. **Tȟápa Waŋkáyeyapi** — Throwing of the Ball (cosmic game; representing Wakan Tanka's gift to humanity)
7. **Naǧí Glúhapi** — Coming of Age (female puberty rite)

## Defining doctrines
- **Wakan Tanka as distributed sacredness** — the Great Mystery is not a person but the sum and field of all sacred powers; every being participates in wakȟáŋ to some degree
- **The *čaŋnúŋpa* (Sacred Pipe)** — the primary mediating technology; the pipe connects earth and sky, human and sacred; the smoke is prayer made visible
- **Sacred geography** — *Pahá Sápa* (Black Hills) as *the* sacred center; the world is structured concentrically from this point
- **Mitákuye Oyásʼiŋ** — "All My Relations" / "We are all related" — the relational fabric of all living beings; a liturgical phrase and a cosmological claim
- **Wičháša wakȟáŋ** — the holy man (and woman) as specialist in navigating the wakȟáŋ; not a priest (no clergy class) but a gifted mediator

## Relations to neighbors
- vs. [[tradition-haudenosaunee]]: both Great Plains / Woodlands Indigenous American traditions; both organize political life through religious-cosmological frameworks; structurally distinct (Lakota is bands-and-vision; Haudenosaunee is confederacy-and-longhouse).
- vs. [[tradition-navajo]]: both desert/plains North American traditions with a central cosmic-order concept (*wakȟáŋ* / *hózhó*); both non-text-based ceremonial systems with specialist practitioners.
- vs. [[tradition-christianity-canonical]]: post-1850 contact; Black Elk's Catholic catechism alongside Lakota ceremony is the vault's most documented individual case of dual religious identity. Ghost Dance as syncretic response.
- vs. [[tradition-australian-aboriginal]]: structurally parallel — sacred geography (Dreaming / *Pahá Sápa*) as the substrate of religious life; law-as-landscape; oral transmission by specialist practitioners.

## MASSIVE WIN — Wakan Tanka as Distributed / Immanent Sacredness: Independent Discovery Across Five Traditions

**The claim (investigative, not assertion):** Multiple religious traditions independently arrived at the insight that the sacred is not located above the world in a personal deity but *within* the world as a quality of being. Wakan Tanka is the most clearly documented case in a non-Western context uncontaminated by Mediterranean philosophical influence.

| Tradition | Concept | Form | Source |
|-----------|---------|------|--------|
| Lakota | *Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka* — the Great Mystery; totality of all sacred powers distributed through beings and places | Non-personal; structured hierarchy of wakan beings; approached through rites, not prayer to a person | Walker 1980 (DeMallie ed.); Powers 1977 |
| Shinto | *Kami* — sacred quality present in all things (mountains, rivers, ancestors, exceptional objects); not creator-deities above the world but sacred presences within it | Non-personal; millions of kami; no single supreme god in the oldest stratum | Ono 1962; Breen/Teeuwen 2000 |
| Daoist | *De* — the virtue/potency of *Tao* as distributed through all things; the Tao itself is "the valley spirit" and "the mother of ten thousand things" — immanent, not transcendent | Non-personal; *wu-wei* is participation in the immanent pattern, not obedience to a transcendent will | Daodejing ch. 6, 16, 28; LaFargue 1992 |
| Stoic | *Logos spermatikos* — the generative rational principle distributed through all matter as fire-reason; "God" is the immanent rational soul of the world, not a person above it | Philosophical; pantheism explicitly stated (Chrysippus): God is the cosmos | SVF II.310; Long/Sedley 1987 |
| Spinoza | *Deus sive Natura* — "God or Nature" (Ethics I, Prop. 14); the one substance expressed in infinite attributes; the traditional personal God is impossible | Philosophical; labeled "atheism" by contemporaries; structurally identical claim to Stoic and Wakan Tanka function | Ethics 1677 |

**Investigative finding:** Wakan Tanka, Shinto *kami*, Daoist *De*, Stoic *Logos*, and Spinoza's *Natura* all represent a coherent structural option in religious-philosophical thought: the sacred as *the quality of being in the world*, not a personal agent above it. These arose in complete independence from each other across five continents and three millennia. This is not transmission — it is independent convergence to what may be a structural feature of mystical-religious cognition: the recognition that the sacred is *here*, not elsewhere.

**What Wakan Tanka adds that the others lack:** The most explicit documentation that this is a *distributed* system with a clear internal hierarchy (the Sixteen Wakan Beings), not mere pantheism. Walker's interview record shows Lakota holy men could articulate the system with philosophical precision comparable to Neoplatonic emanation schemes — but derived entirely from experiential observation of the Plains world.

**Scholarly caution:** Walker's early-20th-c. interviews were conducted through interpreters and may reflect individual informants' systematizations rather than universal Lakota theology. DeMallie's editorial work (1984) and Powers' independent research (1977) corroborate the core structural features. The Wakan Tanka concept predates Christian contact and is not a product of missionary influence — the post-contact identification of Wakan Tanka with the Christian God is the later folk-syncretism (DeMallie 1984 p. 86).
