Siberian shamanism is the root complex from which many Central Asian, Mongolian, and indigenous North Asian spiritual traditions derive. Its defining feature is the shamanic journey — a trained practitioner's ritual descent or ascent through cosmic layers using altered states of consciousness, typically achieved through drumming. The drum is both the vehicle of the journey (the "shaman's horse") and a cosmological map: its surface represents the world-tree axis, and the rhythmic beating at 4–8 Hz induces the theta-wave states documented in modern neuroscience.

The tradition spans a geographic range from the Altai Mountains to the Arctic Circle, incorporating dozens of distinct ethnic traditions (Tuvan, Buryat, Yakut, Evenk, Nenets) that share structural features while varying in local deity taxonomies and ritual forms. Mircea Eliade's *Shamanism* (1964) synthesized the comparative record; its claim that Siberian shamanism is the archaic substrate of all shamanic traditions remains influential if contested.
