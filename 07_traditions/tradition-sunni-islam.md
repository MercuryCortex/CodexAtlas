# Sunni Islam

## Overview
**Sunni Islam** — *ahl al-sunna wa'l-jamāʿa*, "the people of the prophetic practice and of the community" — is the majority branch of [[tradition-islam|Islam]], roughly 85–90% of some 1.9 billion Muslims. Its defining commitments are three, and they hang together: that the **sunna** of [[muhammad-prophet|the Prophet]], recovered through hadith transmitted by the Companions, stands beside the [[phase-4-034-quran|Qurʾān]] as a source of law and doctrine; that the **jamāʿa**, the historical community and its consensus (*ijmāʿ*), is the vehicle through which that inheritance is preserved and religious authority is exercised; and that the succession of the four *Rāshidūn* caliphs — [[abu-bakr]], [[umar-ibn-al-khattab]], [[uthman]], [[ali-ibn-abi-talib]] — was legitimate in that order.

There is no Sunni church, no ordination and no magisterium. Authority is scholarly and plural: it lives in the *ʿulamāʾ*, is exercised through the schools of law, and settles disputes by weight of transmitted opinion rather than by ruling. This is the single most consequential structural fact about the tradition, and the reason a stable four-school pluralism has been the Sunni norm rather than an anomaly.

## Two datings, kept apart
**As the tradition tells it,** Sunnism begins in 632: the community gathers at Saqīfa on the day of the Prophet's death, accepts [[abu-bakr]] as *khalīfa*, and the *jamāʿa* holds. Everything after is preservation.

**As historians reconstruct it,** no self-conscious "Sunni" party existed for two centuries. The label *ahl al-sunna wa'l-jamāʿa* emerges in the 9th century, and the hard Sunnī/Shīʿī bifurcation in the 10th (Berkey 2003). The 7th-century disputes — [[event-saqifa-bani-saida-632|Saqīfa]], the [[event-first-fitna-656-661|First Fitna]], [[event-karbala-680|Karbala]] — are real, but they were fought between factions, not between the two confessions that later claimed them. Sunnism is best described as the position that consolidated out of the 8th–9th-century *ahl al-ḥadīth* ("partisans of hadith") milieu and then read itself backwards into the founding generation.

Both accounts belong in the record. The vault reports the first as the tradition's own self-understanding and the second as the scholarly reconstruction, and does not let either erase the other.

## Historical formation
1. **The unsettled succession (632–692).** [[event-saqifa-bani-saida-632|Saqīfa]], the Rāshidūn caliphates, the [[event-first-fitna-656-661|First Fitna]] and [[uthman|ʿUthmān]]'s murder, the Kharijite secession, [[event-karbala-680|Karbala]] and the Second Fitna. What later becomes the Sunni position takes shape here as a practical one: accept the historical community as legitimate, refuse to excommunicate its members over the civil wars, prefer imperfect order to renewed *fitna*.
2. **The Umayyad and early Abbasid milieu (661–833).** Under [[abd-al-malik]] the movement acquires public confessional form — the [[dome-of-the-rock-jerusalem|Dome of the Rock]] (692), an Arabic epigraphic coinage, an administrative state. Legal and pious scholarship is regional and informal: [[hasan-al-basri|al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī]] in Basra, Mālik ibn Anas in Medina, Abū Ḥanīfa in Kufa.
3. **The *miḥna* and the traditionalist victory (833–848).** The Abbasid caliph al-Maʾmūn imposes the [[doctrine-mutazila-rationalist-kalam|Muʿtazilī]] doctrine that the [[doctrine-quran-createdness|Qurʾān is created]] and demands assent from the scholars. [[ibn-hanbal|Ibn Ḥanbal]] refuses, is imprisoned and flogged, and becomes the symbol of scholarly authority independent of the state. Al-Mutawakkil abandons the inquisition around 848–851. **This is the hinge**: it establishes that the caliph does not define doctrine, that the *ʿulamāʾ* do, and that the criterion is transmitted *sunna* rather than reasoned theology — the three planks of Sunni identity (Hurvitz 2002; Zaman 1997; Melchert 1997).
4. **Canon and codification (9th–11th c.).** The hadith collections are compiled and then, over two further centuries, canonized: [[phase-5-007-sahih-al-bukhari|al-Bukhārī]] (d. 870) and [[phase-5-042-sahih-muslim|Muslim]] (d. 875) become the *Ṣaḥīḥayn*, joined by four *Sunan* as the *Kutub al-Sitta* (Brown 2007). [[al-tabari|al-Ṭabarī]] (d. 923) fixes the exegetical and historical inheritance.
5. **Classical synthesis (11th–13th c.).** [[al-ghazali|al-Ghazālī]] (d. 1111) integrates Sufi interiority into mainstream Sunni piety in the *[[phase-5-015-al-ghazali-ihya|Iḥyāʾ]]* and, in the *[[phase-5-045-al-ghazali-tahafut-al-falasifa|Tahāfut]]*, marks the boundary against the philosophers — answered by [[ibn-rushd|Ibn Rushd]]'s *[[phase-5-046-ibn-rushd-tahafut-al-tahafut|Tahāfut al-Tahāfut]]*. Al-Māwardī gives the caliphate its constitutional theory. The *madrasa* institutionalizes the whole system.
6. **Empire and reform (13th–19th c.).** [[ibn-taymiyya|Ibn Taymiyya]] (d. 1328) opens the traditionalist counter-current against saint-veneration, kalām and taqlīd. The [[tradition-islam-ottoman|Ottoman]] and [[tradition-islam-mughal|Mughal]] states institutionalize Ḥanafism; [[shah-wali-allah|Shāh Walī Allāh]] (d. 1762) in India and Muḥammad ibn ʿAbd al-Wahhāb (d. 1792) in Najd launch the 18th-c. revivalist wave, the latter in the alliance with the Āl Saʿūd that produces the modern Wahhābī–Salafī current.
7. **Modern (19th c. onward).** The Ottoman caliphate is abolished in 1924, leaving Sunnism without even a symbolic centre. [[muhammad-abduh|Muḥammad ʿAbduh]] and [[tradition-islamic-modernism|the modernists]] argue for reopened *ijtihād*; [[tradition-islamism-political|Islamist movements]] recast the tradition as political programme; Salafism globalizes a scripturalist reading; and traditional madhhab-based scholarship continues alongside all of it.

## The four schools of law (*madhāhib*)
Sunni jurisprudence recognizes four surviving Sunni schools, mutually accepted as valid:

| School | Eponym (d.) | Classical heartland | Distinctive emphasis |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ḥanafī** | Abū Ḥanīfa (767) | Iraq → Ottoman lands, Central + South Asia | Broadest scope for reasoned inference (*raʾy*, *istiḥsān*); the Ottoman and Mughal state school |
| **Mālikī** | Mālik ibn Anas (795) | Medina → Maghrib, al-Andalus, West Africa | The living practice of Medina (*ʿamal*) as evidence; see [[tradition-islam-maliki]] |
| **Shāfiʿī** | al-Shāfiʿī (820) | Egypt → East Africa, Southeast Asia | Systematized *uṣūl al-fiqh* in *al-Risāla* and subordinated local practice to prophetic hadith; see [[tradition-islam-shafii]] |
| **Ḥanbalī** | [[ibn-hanbal|Ibn Ḥanbal]] (855) | Baghdad → Najd, modern Arabia | Narrowest role for speculative reasoning; hadith and Companion-report first |

Two points of scholarly correction to the traditional account. First, **the schools were not founded by their eponyms**: Melchert 1997 shows them consolidating as scholarly guilds across the 9th–10th centuries, with the eponym retrospectively installed as founder. Second, **al-Shāfiʿī's role is the pivot of the whole system** — his argument that a sound hadith from the Prophet overrides both local custom and jurists' discretion is what makes *sunna* the second source of law in the form Sunnism inherits, and Joseph Schacht (1950) built his sceptical history of Islamic law around exactly this transition.

## Creed (*ʿaqīda*)
Sunni theology settled into three accepted positions rather than one:

- **Atharī / traditionalist** — the Ḥanbalī line: affirm the revealed statements about God *bi-lā kayf*, "without asking how", and refuse speculative construction. See [[ibn-hanbal]], [[ibn-taymiyya]].
- **[[doctrine-asharism-traditionalist-kalam|Ashʿarī]]** — al-Ashʿarī (d. 936): use the dialectical tools of kalām *in defence of* the traditionalist content. Carries [[philosophy-asharite-occasionalism|occasionalism]] — every event caused directly by God, secondary causation denied — into Islamic metaphysics.
- **[[doctrine-maturidism-hanafi-kalam|Māturīdī]]** — al-Māturīdī (d. 944): the Ḥanafī-aligned creed of Central Asia and later the Ottoman lands; a somewhat larger role for unaided reason than Ashʿarism allows.

The defeated position, the [[doctrine-mutazila-rationalist-kalam|Muʿtazila]], remains doctrinally important as the school against which the others defined themselves — and its central thesis, the [[doctrine-quran-createdness|createdness of the Qurʾān]], is the exact point on which Sunni orthodoxy is negatively defined. On [[doctrine-tawhid|tawḥīd]] itself all Sunni schools agree; they differ on what may be said about the divine attributes.

## Defining commitments
- **Qurʾān and *sunna*** as the two revealed sources; **[[moral-islamic-sharia|sharīʿa]]** derived from them via *ijmāʿ* (consensus) and *qiyās* (analogy), with *ijtihād* as the jurist's independent effort — the classical four *uṣūl*.
- **The legitimacy of the Rāshidūn sequence** and refusal to condemn the Companions, against the Shīʿī reading of the same events.
- **Caliphate as communal obligation, not divine designation** — the caliph is chosen by the qualified (*ahl al-ḥall wa'l-ʿaqd*), holds no interpretive infallibility, and is owed obedience short of sin; a strong quietist preference for order over rebellion.
- **The Five Pillars**: *shahāda*, [[ritual-salat-five-prayers|ṣalāt]], [[ritual-zakat-charity|zakāt]], [[ritual-ramadan-sawm|ṣawm]], [[ritual-hajj-pilgrimage|ḥajj]].
- **Sufism as licit but contested** — mainstream from al-Ghazālī onward ([[tradition-islam-sufi]], [[tradition-sufism]]), attacked by Ibn Taymiyya and by modern Salafism.

## MASSIVE wins — cross-tradition edges
- **The hadith canon as a canon-formation process inside recorded history.** [[phase-5-007-sahih-al-bukhari|Bukhārī]] and [[phase-5-042-sahih-muslim|Muslim]] compiled private collections in the 9th century; by the 11th they functioned as second scripture. Brown 2007 documents each step — the transmission networks, the commentary tradition, the polemical uses. Canon formation in Judaism and Christianity has to be inferred from its results; here it can be watched happening, which makes the Sunni case the best available control for how a scriptural canon is actually made.
- **Fiqh ↔ halakha: two scholar-made legal systems, no clergy.** Sunni law and rabbinic law share an unusual architecture — revealed text plus a transmitted oral supplement, worked by independent jurists, plural schools, no institution empowered to close a question (Hallaq 2009; Neusner & Sonn 1999). Filed as structural parallel, not transmission: the resemblance is in the shape of the authority, and both traditions developed it under conditions of dispersed community and absent central power.
- **The *miḥna* ↔ the Christological councils: two ways of settling a doctrinal dispute.** In 325 the Roman state convened bishops and enforced a creed ([[event-council-of-nicaea-325]]); in 833 the Abbasid state imposed a doctrine and lost. The Islamic outcome permanently separated caliphal from doctrinal authority, which is why Sunni Islam has schools where Christianity has churches, and no institution that can excommunicate.
- **The Greek inheritance re-exported.** The [[event-translation-movement-baghdad|Baghdad translation movement]] runs through the same milieu and generations as the Sunni schools' formation; the resulting [[falsafa]] tradition, and the [[tahafut-controversy|Tahāfut controversy]] that marked its limits, is the channel by which Aristotle reaches Latin scholasticism — Avicenna and Averroes into Aquinas.

## Relations to neighbours
- **[[tradition-shia-islam]]** — the defining sibling. Divided on the succession, on the authority of the Companions, on whether religious authority is designated or consensual. Both are 9th–10th-c. consolidations of a 7th-c. quarrel.
- **[[tradition-sufism]] / [[tradition-islam-sufi]]** — not a separate confession but the interior discipline of the same tradition, largely inside Sunni orthodoxy after al-Ghazālī and largely outside it for modern Salafism. The vault keeps it as a separate node for graph clarity.
- **[[tradition-ismailism]], [[tradition-alevi-bektashi]], [[tradition-druze]]** — traditions that branch from the Shīʿī side of the divide and are regarded as outside the Sunni *jamāʿa*.
- **[[tradition-judaism]], [[tradition-christianity-canonical]]** — the *ahl al-kitāb*, "People of the Book", with a legal status (*dhimma*) and a long polemical and philosophical exchange running from [[john-of-damascus|John of Damascus]] through the Baghdad *kalām* debates to the Andalusian translations.
- **[[tradition-pre-islamic-arabian]]** — the displaced parent milieu of the Hijaz.
- **[[tradition-islamic-modernism]] / [[tradition-islamism-political]]** — the two principal modern reformulations, both operating on Sunni materials and both contested inside the tradition.
