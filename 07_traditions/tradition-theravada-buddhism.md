# Theravāda Buddhism

## Overview
Theravāda ("Doctrine of the Elders") is the southern transmission of Buddhism, the only surviving school of the early "Eighteen Schools" stratum. It preserves the Pali Tipiṭaka — the only complete early-Buddhist canon extant in an Indian language — and treats it as a closed, fully-authoritative scriptural collection. Theravāda is the dominant form of Buddhism in Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Thailand, Cambodia, and Laos, and the doctrinal substrate of the modern global *vipassanā* and mindfulness movements. Its self-understanding is conservative: it claims to preserve what the Buddha actually taught, against the Mahāyāna polemic that characterizes it as *Hīnayāna* (Lesser Vehicle). Modern Theravāda scholarship (Gombrich, Bronkhorst, Schopen) supports the school's broad claim to early-Buddhist textual authenticity while contesting some of its later sectarian narratives.

## Historical phases
1. **Aśokan transmission (~-247)** — Mahinda (Aśoka's son or junior contemporary) brings Buddhism to Sri Lanka under King Devānaṃpiyatissa; Mahāvihāra monastery at Anurādhapura founded.
2. **Sri Lankan canonical period (~-247 to +400)** — oral transmission of the Pali canon by *bhāṇaka* reciter-lineages; first writing-down at Aluvihāra (~-29 BCE) under King Vaṭṭagāminī Abhaya; consolidation of the Mahāvihāra orthodoxy against the Abhayagiri / Jetavana fraternities.
3. **Buddhaghosa's classical synthesis (~5th c.)** — [[buddhaghosa]] arrives at the Mahāvihāra and composes the *Visuddhimagga* (*Path of Purification*) plus commentaries on most of the Pali canon, establishing the systematic doctrinal framework still definitive for the school.
4. **Southeast Asian transmission (~11th–13th c.)** — King Anawrahta of Pagan (Burma) adopts Theravāda ~1057; Sukhothai (Thailand) under Ramkhamhaeng ~13th c.; Khmer Cambodia and Laos follow. Sri Lankan ordination lineages re-seed the region multiple times after persecutions.
5. **Colonial-modernist period (~1850–1950)** — encounter with Western Christianity (Sri Lankan "Buddhist modernism" via Anagārika Dharmapāla); Olcott's *Buddhist Catechism* 1881; reformist re-engagement with the canon as anti-colonial cultural assertion.
6. **Modern global *vipassanā* movement (~1950 onward)** — Mahāsi Sayadaw and Sayagyi U Ba Khin in Burma; S.N. Goenka's lay teaching; Western reception via Joseph Goldstein, Sharon Salzberg, Jack Kornfield (the Insight Meditation Society, 1975); MBSR (Jon Kabat-Zinn, 1979) extracts secularized vipassanā into clinical psychology.

## Core texts
- [[phase-2-016-early-buddhist-suttas]] — Sutta Nipāta (Aṭṭhakavagga, Pārāyanavagga) — earliest stratum.
- [[phase-2-029-dhammapada]] — the verse anthology; most-translated Buddhist text.
- The full **Pali Tipiṭaka** in three baskets:
  - *Suttapiṭaka* — five Nikāyas (Dīgha, Majjhima, Saṃyutta, Aṅguttara, Khuddaka)
  - *Vinayapiṭaka* — monastic discipline
  - *Abhidhammapiṭaka* — systematic philosophy (seven books)
- [[phase-4-076-visuddhimagga]] — Buddhaghosa's *Path of Purification*; THE Theravāda systematic synthesis.
- Aṭṭhakathā commentaries (Buddhaghosa, Dhammapāla) on most canonical books.
- *Milindapañha* (the Theravāda Pali version — see [[phase-3-029-milindapanha]]).

## Key figures (founder + chief disciples + canonical transmitters)
- [[siddhartha-gautama-buddha]] — founder; the Theravāda canon's authority claim is direct from him.
- [[ananda-disciple]] — reciter of the Sutta-piṭaka at the First Council.
- [[mahakasyapa]] — convened the First Council; presiding figure of canonical formation.
- [[shariputra]] — Abhidhamma-tradition associated him with this systematic stratum.
- [[asoka-maurya]] — patron-emperor; without his missions Theravāda would not exist outside India.
- [[buddhaghosa]] — 5th-c. systematizer.

## Defining doctrines
- **The Four Noble Truths** ([[four-noble-truths]]) and **Noble Eightfold Path** ([[noble-eightfold-path]]) as the diagnostic frame.
- **Three Marks of Existence** — *anicca* (impermanence), *dukkha* (suffering), *anatta* ([[anatman-no-self]]).
- **Dependent origination** ([[pratitya-samutpada]]) — twelvefold formula.
- **Theravāda Abhidhamma** — analysis of experience into momentary *dhamma*s (mind-and-matter events), causal conditioning (*paṭṭhāna*), and the seven Abhidhamma-piṭaka books.
- **Path-result map** — sotāpanna (stream-enterer) → sakadāgāmi → anāgāmi → arahant (the four "noble persons").
- **Arahant as soteriological goal** (vs. Mahāyāna's bodhisattva-ideal — though Theravāda also recognizes the bodhisattva path for those aspiring to full Buddhahood).
- ***Suñña* (emptiness) — recognized but interpreted as emptiness-of-self in phenomena, not the Mādhyamika emptiness-of-inherent-existence.**

## Relations to neighbors
- vs. [[tradition-mahayana-buddhism]]: Mahāyāna polemic dubs Theravāda "*Hīnayāna*" (Lesser Vehicle); Theravāda historically rejects the Mahāyāna sūtras as non-Buddhavacana. Modern relations are ecumenical (World Fellowship of Buddhists; shared global Buddhist identity).
- vs. early Sectarian Buddhism: Theravāda is the only continuously-extant school of the ancient "Eighteen Schools"; it claims direct continuity with the Sthavira / Vibhajjavāda lineage. The Sarvāstivāda, Mahāsāṅghika, Pudgalavāda, etc., are known mainly through Chinese and Tibetan translations and through hostile mentions in Theravāda commentaries.
- vs. [[tradition-vedic-hinduism]]: shared karma/rebirth framework; opposition on *anātman*, Vedic authority, brahmin caste-priority.

## The Pali Canon (Tipiṭaka) — the vault's primary early-Buddhist source

The Theravāda canon — *Vinayapiṭaka* (monastic rules), *Suttapiṭaka* (discourses, five Nikāyas), *Abhidhammapiṭaka* (philosophical analysis, seven books) — is the most complete early Buddhist canon surviving in any Indian language. Pali is not the language of the historical Buddha (who likely spoke Māgadhī or a close Prakrit dialect) but was adopted by the Sri Lankan redactors as the canonical medium, first committed to writing at Āluvihāra (~29 BCE). The choice of Pali created the equivalent of what Latin performed for the Western Church: a fixed sacred-language that stabilized the tradition across centuries and geography.

The Suttapiṭaka's five Nikāyas are the vault's primary source for the historical Buddha's actual teaching. The Dīgha Nikāya (long discourses), Majjhima Nikāya (middle-length discourses), Saṃyutta Nikāya (connected discourses), Aṅguttara Nikāya (numerical discourses), and Khuddaka Nikāya (minor works, including the Dhammapada, Sutta Nipāta, and Jātakas) constitute the foundational textual stratum. Modern text-critical scholarship (Gombrich, Bronkhorst, Analayo) broadly supports the Nikāyas as preserving early material, while acknowledging later accretions.

T.W. Rhys Davids (1843–1922) and the Pali Text Society (founded 1881, London) made this canon accessible to Western scholarship through critical editions and translations — the founding moment of Buddhist studies as an academic discipline. This is structurally parallel to the Masoretic scholars' preservation of the Hebrew Bible, or Byzantine copyists' preservation of the Greek philosophical corpus: a textual tradition's survival into modernity depends on specialized institutional guardianship.

## Arhat vs. Bodhisattva — the doctrinal partition of the Buddhist world

The defining structural difference between Theravāda and Mahāyāna:

| Axis | Theravāda | Mahāyāna |
|---|---|---|
| Highest ideal | *Arahant* (arhat) — achieves personal nibbāna | *Bodhisattva* — delays nirvāṇa to save all beings |
| Motivation | Personal liberation from *dukkha* | Universal compassion (*karuṇā*); return until all sentient beings are free |
| Polemic label | Called *Hīnayāna* ("Lesser Vehicle") by Mahāyānists — rejected | Self-designation: *Theravāda* ("Teaching of the Elders") |
| Scope of obligation | Self-liberation; monastic ideal primary | Universal liberation; laity fully included in bodhisattva vow |
| Textual authority | Pali Tipiṭaka alone | Pali Nikāyas + Sanskrit Mahāyāna sūtras |

The structural question this raises: is the highest spiritual ideal self-liberation or universal compassion? This maps onto debates in other traditions. In Christianity: personal salvation theology (Protestant evangelical tradition; "accepting Jesus as personal savior") vs. universal redemption theology (Universalism; Gregory of Nyssa; Origen's *apokatastasis*) vs. communal covenant theology (ecclesial salvation; baptism into the Body of Christ). In Judaism: individual repentance (*teshuvah* of the individual soul) vs. communal covenant obligation (*kol Yisrael* — all Israel is responsible for one another). The structural debate is widespread, but the Buddhist formulation is perhaps the most explicit and historically extended.

The Mahāyāna polemic calling Theravāda *Hīnayāna* is documented from approximately the 1st–2nd century CE and was a polemical move, not a neutral description. Modern ecumenical relations under the World Fellowship of Buddhists are more nuanced. The two streams are now engaged in inter-Buddhist dialogue, though deep doctrinal differences remain.

## MASSIVE WIN — Vipassanā → global mindfulness: the Theravāda transmission chain

Theravāda *vipassanā* (insight meditation) is the tradition's most consequential modern export. The transmission chain:

1. **Ledi Sayadaw** (1846–1923, Burma) — taught vipassanā to laypersons, not just monastics; pivotal democratization move
2. **Mahāsi Sayadaw** (1904–1982, Burma) — developed the noting technique; trained Sayama and thousands of teachers; his Meditation Centre in Rangoon became the primary training institution
3. **Sayagyi U Ba Khin** (1899–1971, Burma) — trained S.N. Goenka
4. **S.N. Goenka** (1924–2013) — secular 10-day residential retreats (Vipassana Research Institute, 1969); now 340+ centers worldwide; fully decontextualized from Buddhist ritual; the largest single vehicle of Theravāda contemplative practice in modern history
5. **Joseph Goldstein, Sharon Salzberg, Jack Kornfield** — all trained in Southeast Asia under Mahāsi Sayadaw lineage teachers; founded the **Insight Meditation Society** (IMS, Barre, Massachusetts, 1975); the primary institutional bridge into Western Buddhism
6. **Jon Kabat-Zinn** (1944–) — trained partly at IMS; extracted vipassanā techniques into **Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction** (MBSR, 1979, UMass Medical School); clinical secularization of Theravāda insight meditation; the founding document of the $4 billion global mindfulness industry

While Zen → Kabat-Zinn is the best-documented direct chain for MBSR specifically (Kabat-Zinn credits Zen teachers including Thich Nhat Hanh), the IMS / vipassanā stream runs in parallel at massive scale. Between them, Zen and Theravāda now collectively supply the contemplative techniques for the entire global mindfulness movement. The Theravāda stream is arguably *larger* in terms of retreat practitioners worldwide (Goenka's network alone dwarfs most Zen institutional presence).

## MASSIVE WIN — Vinaya ↔ Benedict's Rule: independent monastic regulation

The Theravāda *Vinayapiṭaka* regulates every aspect of monastic life for the *bhikkhu* (monk) and *bhikkhunī* (nun) communities: approximately 227 rules for monks (*Pātimokkha*), 311 for nuns. The rules cover sleeping arrangements, eating times, possession of property, clothing, dispute resolution, ordination procedures, confession and rehabilitation of rule-violations (*āpatti*). The *Mahāvagga* and *Cullavagga* sections add narrative and procedural context. The Vinaya is self-described as the foundation of the sāsana — the Buddhist dispensation — because without institutional structure the contemplative life dissolves.

Benedict of Nursia's *Regula Benedetti* (Rule of Saint Benedict, c. 530–560 CE, Italy) performs an identical function for Western Christian monasticism: 73 chapters comprehensively regulating community life (hours of prayer, eating, sleeping, silence, manual labor, obedience, punishment, hospitality, governance of the abbot). The Rule has governed Benedictine, Cistercian, and related communities for 1,400 years without major alteration. It is still the living constitutional document of hundreds of active monasteries.

| Axis | Theravāda Vinaya (~3rd c. BCE codified) | Benedict's Rule (c. 530–560 CE) |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | Complete regulation of monastic community | Complete regulation of monastic community |
| Tradition | Buddhist | Western Christian |
| Geographic origin | India / Sri Lanka | Central Italy |
| Historical contact | None | None — independent invention |
| Current status | Still binding in Theravāda monasteries | Still binding in Benedictine/Cistercian monasteries |
| Key insight | Contemplative life requires institutional structure | Contemplative life requires institutional structure |
| Duration in active use | ~2,300 years | ~1,400 years |

Both emerged independently; both discovered the same structural truth: sustained contemplative life in community requires written institutional regulation. The parallel is not genetic — there is no documented transmission. It is a case of convergent institutional design under shared functional pressure.

## MASSIVE WIN — Theravāda in the Axial Age: textual transmission and academic guardianship

Theravāda preserved what is currently the most complete surviving stratum of the Axial Age Buddhist teaching. Karl Jaspers' Axial Age (~800–200 BCE) produced the Buddha, Confucius, Socrates, and the Hebrew prophets within a few centuries of each other. The Theravāda tradition is the primary documentary witness to the Buddhist strand of this transformation.

The Pali Text Society (founded 1881, London, by T.W. Rhys Davids) created the first critical editions and scholarly translations of the complete Pali canon — parallel in function to the Masoretes (Hebrew Bible, 6th–10th c. CE) or the Byzantine scriptoria (Greek philosophical corpus, 9th–15th c. CE):

| Tradition | Textual guardian institution | Period | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Theravāda Buddhism | Pali Text Society (London, 1881–) | 19th–21st c. CE | Critical editions + translations of full Pali canon |
| Hebrew Bible | Masoretes (Babylon/Palestine) | ~6th–10th c. CE | Vowel-pointed Masoretic text; the basis of all OT translations |
| Greek philosophy | Byzantine scriptoria (Constantinople) | ~9th–15th c. CE | Survival of Plato, Aristotle, and Neoplatonists into the West |
| Sanskrit | Brahmin pandits + East India Company | 18th–19th c. CE | First Sanskrit-European translations; birth of Indology |

In all cases: a textual tradition's survival into global scholarship depends on specialized institutional guardianship operating across several centuries.
