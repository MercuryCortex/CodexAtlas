# Sephardic Judaism

**Sephardic Judaism** (*Sefardim* — from *Sepharad*, the Hebrew name for the Iberian peninsula, Obad. 1:20) designates the Jewish tradition that developed in al-Andalus and Christian Spain (roughly 700–1492 CE), was catastrophically disrupted by the 1492 Expulsion from Spain and the 1497 Expulsion from Portugal, and then reconstituted in diaspora across the Ottoman Empire, North Africa, the Netherlands, and the Americas.

At its height, the Iberian Jewish community was the most intellectually sophisticated Jewish diaspora in the world: it produced Maimonides (Cordoba, 1135–1204), Nachmanides (Girona, 1194–1270), Solomon ibn Gabirol (Málaga, c.1021–1058), Judah Halevi (Toledo, c.1075–1141), and Abraham ibn Ezra (Tudela, c.1089–1167). The philosophical, poetic, and kabbalistic achievements of the Spanish Jewish golden age are foundational for all subsequent Jewish thought.

The [[event-spanish-inquisition-1478|Spanish Inquisition]] (1478) targeted *conversos* (Jewish converts to Christianity suspected of crypto-Judaism); the formal Expulsion of 1492 forced the remaining ~200,000 Spanish Jews into exile. The Sephardic diaspora carried their Iberian Hebrew liturgy, the **Ladino** language (Judeo-Spanish, written in Hebrew script), and distinct legal and mystical traditions. Sephardic and Ashkenazi Judaism developed as parallel Jewish worlds — the Ottoman Sephardic communities remained a distinct cultural force into the 20th century.
