# Shintō

## Overview
The indigenous religious tradition of Japan, centered on the veneration of *kami* — sacred presences inhabiting natural features, ancestors, gods, and sometimes places of historical-political significance. Shintō is doctrinally minimal (no creed, no founder, no scriptural revelation of the Abrahamic sort) and ritually rich; the *jinja* (shrine) and its *matsuri* (festival) cycle structures local religious life. The two foundational mytho-historical texts — *Kojiki* (712 CE) and *Nihon Shoki* (720 CE) — narrate cosmogony (the heavenly *kami* generating the islands of Japan), the descent of Amaterasu's grandson Ninigi, and the imperial line as her descendants.

## Historical phases
1. **Ancient** (pre-700 CE) — village and clan *kami*-cult; the Yamato imperial line consolidating its descent claim from Amaterasu.
2. **Medieval** (8th–16th c.) — substantial fusion with Buddhism (*shinbutsu shūgō*); the *honji suijaku* doctrine (kami as local manifestations of universal Buddhas).
3. **Edo / Kokugaku** (17th–19th c.) — the "National Learning" school (Motoori, Hirata) recovers a purified pre-Buddhist Shintō ideology.
4. **State Shintō** (1868–1945) — Meiji imperial-restoration ideology separates Shintō from Buddhism (*shinbutsu bunri*), institutes State Shintō as imperial ideology; dissolved by Allied Occupation directive 1945.
5. **Post-war** — Shrine Shintō as private religious institution; ongoing cultural-religious presence.
