# Hussitism

**Hussitism** is the religious and political reform movement that emerged from the execution of Jan Hus at the Council of Constance (1415) and transformed Bohemia for two centuries. It is the most successful medieval religious challenge to Roman papal authority before the Lutheran Reformation — the Hussite armies repelled five papal crusades (1420–1431), and the movement produced the first negotiated religious peace with Rome (*Compactata of Basel*, 1436).

Hussite theology centered on the demand for **utraquism** — communion in both kinds (*sub utraque specie*, bread AND wine) for the laity, not just clergy. The chalice (*kalich*) became the symbol of the movement; it remains on the Czech coat of arms. Jan Hus's concerns also included preaching in Czech (vernacular), clerical reform, and anti-simony. His ideas drew heavily on John Wycliffe, making the Hussite movement the direct transmission channel of Wycliffite theology into continental Europe — and therefore a direct ancestor of Lutheranism.

**Cross-tradition significance**: The Hussite Wars (1419–1434) are the only successful medieval crusade-resistance by a religious reform movement. The radical wing (**Taborites**) created a proto-communitarian utopian community at Tábor that influenced later Anabaptist and Moravian Brethren movements. The moderate wing (**Utraquists**) survived as the Bohemian church until the Battle of White Mountain (1620), which ended Bohemian Protestant independence for 300 years.
