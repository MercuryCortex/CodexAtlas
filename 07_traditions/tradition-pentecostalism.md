# Pentecostalism

## Overview
Pentecostalism is the fastest-growing branch of Christianity — approximately 600–700 million adherents as of 2020 by conservative estimates, with some projections higher — and the dominant form of Christian practice across large parts of the Global South. It centers on the *baptism of the Holy Spirit* as a post-conversion "second blessing," distinguishable from water baptism, evidenced above all by *glossolalia* (speaking in tongues), along with the continuing operation of the charismatic gifts (*charismata*): prophecy, healing, discernment, and exorcism. Its genealogy runs from John Wesley's doctrine of entire sanctification through the 19th-century Holiness movement to [[charles-parham]]'s Bethel Bible School in Topeka, Kansas (1900), where student Agnes Ozman first spoke in tongues on January 1, 1901. Its global launching event was the [[event-azusa-street-revival-1906]] at 312 Azusa Street, Los Angeles, led by [[william-seymour]].

## Historical phases

### 1. Holiness antecedents (1830s–1900)
John Wesley's doctrine of "entire sanctification" — a second crisis experience after conversion, in which the believer is cleansed of inbred sin — generated the American Holiness movement of the mid-19th century (Methodist camp revivals, National Holiness Association, 1867). This movement created the theological infrastructure: a post-conversion grace-event distinct from initial salvation. Charles Finney's revivalism, Phoebe Palmer's "shorter way" holiness teaching, and Dwight L. Moody's urban evangelism all contributed to the expectational culture. [[charles-parham]], an independent Holiness preacher in Kansas, pressed the question further: if there is a second blessing, what is its biblical sign? His study circles at Bethel Bible School concluded from Acts 2 that the sign was *glossolalia*.

### 2. Azusa Street Revival (1906–1909)
The decisive catalyst. [[william-seymour]], a Black Holiness preacher who had studied under Parham, was invited to Los Angeles in 1906. Denied a pulpit at a Black Holiness church after preaching tongues, he moved services to a converted stable at 312 Azusa Street. The revival that erupted — characterized by uncontrolled ecstasy, speaking in tongues, falling, prophesying, healing, weeping, and interracial worship — ran 24 hours a day for months and attracted visitors from across the United States and international missionaries. The *Los Angeles Times* mocked it on April 18, 1906 (the morning of the San Francisco earthquake); the coverage spread the news nationally. Within months, missionaries carried the experience to Scandinavia, Britain, India, Chile, and West Africa.

### 3. Classical Pentecostal denominations (1914–1960s)
The Assemblies of God (1914) formed as the first large Trinitarian Pentecostal denomination. The Church of God in Christ (COGIC, originating with Charles Mason, an Azusa Street participant) became the largest predominantly Black Pentecostal body. The United Pentecostal Church (1945) represented the "Oneness" or "Jesus Only" movement — a theological split holding that baptism in Jesus' name alone, not the Trinitarian formula, is necessary, and that Father, Son, and Holy Spirit are three manifestations of one Person. This Oneness/Trinitarian division remains the central internal fracture.

### 4. Charismatic Renewal (1960s–1980s)
Pentecostal experience migrated into mainline Protestant and Roman Catholic churches. Episcopal priest Dennis Bennett's 1960 testimony of tongues-speaking at St. Mark's, Van Nuys, California, launched the Protestant charismatic renewal. The Catholic Charismatic Renewal began in 1967 at Duquesne University (Pittsburgh). This "Neo-Pentecostalism" distinguished itself from classical Pentecostalism by remaining within existing denominations. The Second Vatican Council's (1962–1965) openness to charismatic expression facilitated Catholic participation; see [[event-second-vatican-council-1962-1965]].

### 5. Global Pentecostalism and the "Fourth Wave" (1970s–present)
The center of gravity shifted decisively to the Global South. Brazil's Universal Church of the Kingdom of God (IURD, founded 1977 by Edir Macedo) and its competitors became massive media-and-healing empires. Korean Pentecostalism (Yoido Full Gospel Church, founded by David Yonggi Cho; at its peak the world's largest single congregation) demonstrated East Asian Pentecostal growth. Sub-Saharan African independent churches combined Pentecostal pneumatology with indigenous practices. Philip Jenkins's *The Next Christendom* (2002/2011) documented how these currents are shifting world Christianity southward and eastward — the typical Christian is now a young woman in sub-Saharan Africa or Latin America.

### 6. Word of Faith / Prosperity Gospel (1980s–present)
A contested offshoot associated with Kenneth Hagin Sr., Kenneth Copeland, and Joel Osteen: the "positive confession" or "name it and claim it" theology holding that faith-filled speech activates divine promises of health and wealth. Mainstream Pentecostal bodies (Assemblies of God) officially reject prosperity theology, but it has spread widely across independent charismatic networks and in the Global South.

## Defining doctrines

- **Baptism of the Holy Spirit** as a post-conversion crisis experience distinct from water baptism, conferring power for witness (Acts 1:8) and gifts for ministry.
- **Glossolalia** (speaking in tongues) as the "initial physical evidence" of Spirit baptism — the diagnostic sign. This is the key classical-Pentecostal distinctive; charismatic movements often accept tongues as one gift among many without requiring it as *the* evidence.
- **Charismata** (spiritual gifts): tongues, interpretation of tongues, prophecy, healing, word of knowledge, word of wisdom, discernment of spirits, miracles, faith (1 Cor 12–14).
- **Restorationism** (see [[restorationism]]): Pentecostalism understands itself as restoring the New Testament church's original supernatural power, lost during the "dark ages" of institutional Christianity and recovered at the end of the age.
- **Premillennialism**: most Pentecostals hold dispensationalist eschatology (J.N. Darby / C.I. Scofield framework). Spirit-baptism experience is linked to end-times urgency — the gifts are poured out in the last days (Joel 2:28–32, quoted in Acts 2).
- **Divine healing**: physical healing through prayer is part of the "full gospel" (*fourfold* or *fivefold gospel*: salvation, Spirit-baptism, divine healing, and the Second Coming; in fivefold versions, sanctification is added).

## MASSIVE-win cross-tradition edges

**1. Azusa Street and the racial boundary dissolved by Spirit-possession**
[[event-azusa-street-revival-1906]] was deliberately interracial in Jim Crow America. [[william-seymour]] — son of freed slaves from Louisiana — led white, Black, Latino, and Asian worshippers together. The shared ecstatic experience of Spirit-possession temporarily dissolved the racial hierarchies enforced by law and custom. This structural parallel to the Afro-diasporic possession-ritual traditions ([[possession-ritual]], [[tradition-vodou-haitian]], [[orisha-pantheon]]) is striking: in both cases, Spirit-possession becomes a social leveling mechanism, crossing the most rigid social boundaries. The Azusa Street phenomenon happened *within* one of the most racially segregated societies on earth; the leveling was conspicuous enough that Seymour's white critics attacked it as "contamination" from Black religious culture.

**2. Glossolalia as institutionalized altered states of consciousness**
Pentecostal *glossolalia* is the direct continuation of Acts 2 (the Pentecost event) understood as restoration. But the cross-tradition comparative frame is larger: Dionysian ecstasy (the Maenads' *enthusiasmos*, "god-within"), Sufi *dhikr* trance and *sama* in [[tradition-sufism]], Vodou spirit-possession in [[tradition-vodou-haitian]], Korean *mudang* shamanic trance — all represent institutionalized altered states of consciousness within a religious community framework. All involve (a) a community rather than solo practice, (b) prescribed ritual contexts, (c) bodily possession or override by a non-ego force, (d) interpretation by a specialist role, and (e) social legitimation. The structural parallel is cross-cultural and diachronically vast; no genetic connection is claimed, but the convergence illuminates what kind of human religious need the Pentecostal experience addresses.

**3. The Prosperity Gospel as soteriology inversion**
The Prosperity Gospel offshoot represents a theological inversion: from the suffering-servant Christ (katabatic soteriology — descent, kenosis, crucifixion as the saving act) to the victorious-abundance Christ (anabatic soteriology — resurrection power, dominion, material blessing as covenant right). This mirrors the broader shift from theologies of divine condescension to theologies of human elevation that appears in multiple traditions. Compare the Gnostic split between the humble *pneumatic* communities and the "psychic" majority; compare the medieval shift from Franciscan poverty theology to Renaissance humanism's dignification of the human; compare the Calvinist doctrine of providential prosperity as a sign of election. See [[katabasis-and-anabasis]].

## Relations to neighbors

- vs. [[tradition-christianity-canonical]]: Pentecostalism accepts the full biblical canon and standard Christological orthodoxy (mostly); its innovation is pneumatological (emphasis on the Spirit's post-Pentecost continuing work) and experiential (the second blessing).
- vs. [[tradition-protestantism]]: emerged from the Holiness wing of Methodism; retains Protestant sola-scriptura principle but adds experience as a second epistemological source of spiritual confirmation.
- vs. [[possession-ritual]]: structural parallels in the mechanics of Spirit-possession; no genetic connection. The Azusa Street moment is explicitly the sociological site where these structural parallels produced observable social effects (racial desegregation in a Jim Crow context).
- vs. [[tradition-vodou-haitian]] / [[orisha-pantheon]]: independent parallel development of possession-ritual as communal practice with specialist interpretation.
