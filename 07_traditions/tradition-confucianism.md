# Confucianism

## Overview
Confucianism is the Chinese tradition stemming from the teaching of Confucius (~551–479 BCE) and elaborated by his disciples and successors (Mencius, Xunzi) into a comprehensive ethical, political, ritual, and educational tradition. It centers on cultivating moral character (*rén* — humaneness; *yì* — righteousness; *lǐ* — ritual propriety; *zhì* — wisdom; *xìn* — faithfulness), exemplary persons (*jūnzǐ*), proper relationships (the Five Relationships — ruler-subject, parent-child, husband-wife, elder-younger, friend-friend), and a moral cosmic order grounded in *Tiān* (Heaven). It served as the official state ideology of imperial China from the Han dynasty (~+136 BCE) until 1911 and remained the foundation of East Asian moral-political culture in Korea, Japan, and Vietnam.

## Historical phases
1. **Classical / Spring-Autumn-Warring States (~-500 to -200)** — Confucius; Mencius (~-372 to -289) elaborating innate moral goodness; Xunzi (~-310 to -235) elaborating moral cultivation against innate human badness.
2. **Han Confucianism (-200 to +220)** — Establishment as imperial orthodoxy; Five Classics canonized; correlative cosmology synthesis with yin-yang and Five Phases (Dong Zhongshu).
3. **Six Dynasties decline (+220 to +600)** — Buddhism rises; Confucianism in tension.
4. **Tang revival (~+600 to +900)** — Han Yu's anti-Buddhist polemic; Classical revival.
5. **Neo-Confucianism (~+1000 to +1900)** — Synthesis with Buddhist and Daoist metaphysics; Zhu Xi (~+1130–1200) compiles the Four Books (Analects, Mencius, Great Learning, Doctrine of the Mean) as the canonical core; Wang Yangming (~+1472–1529) inward-turn.
6. **Modern (~+1900 onward)** — Attacked by reformers and revolutionaries; persecuted under Maoism; partial revival in contemporary China and global "New Confucianism".

## Core texts
- [[phase-2-015-analects-of-confucius]] — Analects (*Lunyu*).
- The Four Books (*Sìshū*, canonized by Zhu Xi ~1190): Analects, Mencius, *Great Learning* (*Dàxué*), *Doctrine of the Mean* (*Zhōngyōng*).
- The Five Classics (*Wǔjīng*): Book of Songs (*Shījīng*), Book of Documents (*Shūjīng*), Book of Changes (*Yìjīng* / *I Ching*), Book of Rites (*Lǐjì*), Spring and Autumn Annals (*Chūnqiū*). These became the basis of the imperial examination system.

## Defining doctrines
- **Ren** (仁, humaneness / co-humanity).
- **Li** (禮, ritual propriety as the channel of moral cultivation).
- **Junzi** (君子, the exemplary person — paradigm of moral cultivation).
- **Xiao** (孝, filial piety as root of all virtue).
- **Tian Ming** (天命, the Mandate of Heaven).
- **Self-cultivation through learning, ritual, and self-examination.**

## MASSIVE WIN: Cross-Tradition Edges

### The Jesuit Bridge → Enlightenment Europe (Confucianism's second life)
The most consequential cross-tradition transmission in the vault that is almost never named as such. The Jesuit China mission — especially Matteo Ricci (1552–1610) — systematically studied and translated Confucian texts as part of their *accommodatio* strategy: present Christianity as fulfilling rather than replacing Confucianism. Ricci's posthumous *Journals* (edited by Nicolas Trigault, 1615) reached every major European intellectual. Philippe Couplet's *Confucius Sinarum Philosophus* (1687) — the first Latin translation of the Four Books — circulated through every European court and university.

The Enlightenment uptake was immediate and transformative:
- **Leibniz** (1646–1716) read Couplet and wrote *Novissima Sinica* (1697), praising Chinese civilization as proof that rational moral governance is achievable without revealed religion; he saw in the Neo-Confucian *li-qi* framework a parallel to his monadology (substance as active-principle-in-material-medium).
- **Voltaire** (1694–1778) weaponized Confucianism against the Church explicitly: Chinese governance demonstrates that rational ethics requires neither a founding revelation nor an institutional clergy. Voltaire's *Orphan of China* (1755) made Confucius a hero of the *philosophes*.
- **The Physiocrats** — especially François Quesnay (called "the European Confucius") — drew on the Chinese mandarin model to design their theory of enlightened governance by a learned administrative class.
- **The British civil service examination** (1855) — the foundation of modern global bureaucratic meritocracy — was explicitly modeled on China's imperial examination system, itself based on Confucian learning for 600 years.

The chain is: *Confucius (−500) → Four Books → Zhu Xi's canonical curriculum (1190) → Jesuit transmission (1615–1700) → Leibniz/Voltaire/Physiocrats (1697–1760) → British civil service (1855) → global bureaucratic meritocracy (20th c.)*. Confucianism is one of the two or three traditions that most directly shaped modern institutional architecture — alongside Protestant Christianity and Roman law.

### Confucianism ↔ Thomas Aquinas — Synthesis Parallel
Zhu Xi's Neo-Confucian synthesis (absorbing Buddhist Huayan metaphysics into Confucian ethics while officially repudiating Buddhism) follows the same strategic logic as Thomas Aquinas's synthesis (absorbing Aristotelian-Islamic metaphysics into Christian theology while officially repudiating pagan philosophy). Both:
- Claim to be purifying a classical inheritance (*Confucius's original teaching* / *Scriptural Christianity*)
- Absorb rival-tradition metaphysics via terminological translation (*li-qi* ↔ Huayan *li-shi*; *forma/materia* ↔ Aristotelian Greek)
- Produce a synthesis so comprehensive that it becomes the state orthodoxy for the following 300+ years
- Are posthumously attacked as having contaminated the tradition they claimed to purify (Wang Yangming attacks Zhu Xi's Buddhist borrowings; Protestant Reformers attack Scholasticism's Greek philosophy)

See also: [[thomas-aquinas]], [[tradition-neo-confucianism]].

### Confucian *Ren* ↔ Christian *Agape* ↔ Buddhist *Karuṇā* — the other-centered ethics triptych
The three traditions independently converge on the same highest moral ideal: unconditional orientation toward the welfare of others as the highest expression of one's own deepest nature:
| Tradition | Concept | Formulation |
|---|---|---|
| Confucianism | *Rén* (仁) | Humaneness / co-humanity; the Analects ask "Can you love others?" as the core of the good life |
| Christianity | *Agape* (ἀγάπη) | "God is love" (1 Jn 4:8); the love of God and neighbor as the fulfillment of all law |
| Buddhism | *Karuṇā* (करुणा) | Compassion as bodhisattva-ideal; "may all beings be free from suffering" |

All three traditions hold that this other-orientation is not an external obligation but the natural expression of the deepest human (or cosmic) nature — *rén* as Heaven's nature in humans, *agape* as the divine essence, *karuṇā* as Buddha-nature expressing itself.

## Relations to neighbors
- vs. [[tradition-daoism]]: foundational Chinese pairing. Daoists ridicule Confucian ritualism; Confucians criticize Daoist quietism. Yet both traditions historically share scholarly milieu and personnel; the educated Chinese gentleman characteristically held Confucian outward, Daoist inward.
- vs. [[tradition-buddhism]]: tense; Confucians often attacked Buddhism as foreign, anti-familial (monastic celibacy), and economically parasitic. Neo-Confucianism quietly absorbed Buddhist metaphysics while polemicizing against it.
- vs. Legalism: classical rival; Confucians and Legalists fought for influence at Warring States courts. Legalism briefly triumphed under the Qin (~-221) and was then displaced by Confucianism under the Han.
