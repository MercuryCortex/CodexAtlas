# Yazidism

## Overview
A monotheistic religious tradition of the Kurdish-speaking peoples of northern Mesopotamia, centered on the cult of Melek Taus (the Peacock Angel) and the saint-founder Şêx Adî ibn Musafir (12th c. CE). The tradition synthesizes Ancient Iranian (Mithraic, Zoroastrian), Sufi Islamic, and other Near Eastern elements into a distinct system whose central narrative is the rehabilitation of Melek Taus — the chief of seven angels who refused to bow to Adam and was *blessed* rather than damned for this act (an inversion of the Iblis-shaitan narrative familiar from Islam). Yazidi theology is preserved primarily in oral tradition (the *qewls* — hymns) and a strongly endogamous caste-structured community (Sheikhs, Pirs, and Murids).

## Historical phases
1. **Şêx Adî** (12th c.) — Sufi shaykh from Lebanon who settled at Lalish in Iraqi Kurdistan; venerated as saint-founder.
2. **Consolidation** (13th–17th c.) — distinct community-formation; recurrent persecution by surrounding Sunni Muslim authorities, who often classified Yazidis as devil-worshippers (a hostile reading of the Melek Taus veneration).
3. **Modern** — substantial dislocation in the 20th c.; near-genocidal attacks by ISIS (2014, the Sinjar massacre) led to large-scale displacement and diaspora.

## Distinctive doctrines
- **Melek Taus**: the chief of the seven angels; tested by God to bow to Adam; refused on the ground of his prior loyalty to God alone; rewarded for his fidelity. The figure has been hostile-equated with Iblis/Shaitan by external observers; this equation is firmly rejected by Yazidis.
- **Strict endogamy**: marriage outside the community is forbidden; conversion is not accepted.
- **Iconography**: peacock-image (sanjaks); the shrine of Şêx Adî at Lalish is the central pilgrimage site.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **Melek Taus ↔ Lucifer/Morning Star (the vault's most systematically misread deity figure)**: The Peacock Angel who refused to bow to Adam is almost universally identified by hostile outsiders — medieval Muslim polemicists, Ottoman authorities, and modern ISIS — as Shaitan (Satan). The Yazidi narrative inverts this: Melek Taus was *rewarded* for his fidelity to God alone, not punished for defiance. This is the same narrative that Gnostic texts apply to the "rebel" divine figures — Sophia, the serpent in Eden, Prometheus — who are reread as vehicles of true knowledge rather than agents of evil. The Yazidi case is the only surviving Near Eastern religious community that maintained the pre-Muslim angelology in which the chief of the angels' refusal to bow to Adam is a praiseworthy act, not a punishable rebellion. Whether this reflects an older stratum of pre-Islamic Near Eastern angel-theology that the dominant Abrahamic traditions suppressed is one of the vault's most significant unresolved questions.
- **Melek Taus theology ↔ Zoroastrian angelology ↔ pre-dualized Near Eastern religion**: The Yazidi tradition may preserve a version of the "testing" narrative from pre-Zoroastrian Iranian angelology — before Zoroaster's radical bifurcation of the divine world into Ahura Mazda (wholly good) and Angra Mainyu (wholly evil). In the pre-Zoroastrian layer, the same divine being could be both challenging and faithful; the angelic hierarchy was not sorted into absolutely good and absolutely evil camps. If so, Yazidism is a window into the undivided pre-systematic Near Eastern angel-theology that underlies both Zoroastrian dualism and Islamic angelology — preserving a stratum older than both. See [[tradition-zoroastrianism]].
- **Sinjar massacre (2014) ↔ Cathar genocide (1209) ↔ Mandaean dispersion (persecution-as-existential-threat pattern)**: The 2014 ISIS genocide against the Yazidis at Sinjar — murder, enslavement, forced conversion, and mass displacement of a community on the grounds that they are devil-worshippers — is the vault's most recent case of the recurring pattern: a small, endogamous, non-mainstream religious community accused of heresy/devil-worship by the dominant majority, subjected to organized violent suppression. The pattern runs from the Cathar genocide (the Albigensian Crusade, 1209–1229) through repeated Ottoman-era massacres of Yazidis (72 *firman* of destruction recorded, 1640–1918) to 2014. The Yazidi case demonstrates that the [[theme-persecution-as-legitimation]] dynamic — where defining a community as satanic justifies its elimination — has not ended with medieval history.
