# Bhakti Vaishnavism

## Overview
The complex, multi-regional, multi-linguistic Hindu tradition of devotional worship of Vishnu and his *avataras* (especially Krishna and Rama), emerging in South Indian Tamil Alvar poetry of the 7th–10th centuries and spreading throughout the subcontinent in successive waves through the early modern period. Bhakti as a devotional mode privileges direct, emotional, often ecstatic relationship with a chosen personal deity over ritual, learning, caste status, or asceticism; the *bhakti* poet-saints (Alvars, Nayanars, Tukaram, Mirabai, Tulsidas, Chaitanya, etc.) become the central religious figures of pre-modern Indian Hindu life. The "bhakti movement" framing (a single subcontinental movement) is a 19th-c.-onward historiographic construction (Hawley 2015) that obscures substantial regional and theological differences; nonetheless, the family of devotional traditions is real and constitutes one of the most consequential religious-cultural formations in world history.

## Historical phases
1. **South Indian Alvars** (~7th–10th c., Phase 5) — Tamil Vaishnava poet-saints; the foundation of *viraha-bhakti* in the female-devotee-yearning-for-divine-Beloved register.
2. **Ramanuja and the Sri Vaishnava tradition** (1077–1157, Phase 5) — theological systematization of qualified non-dualism (*Vishishtadvaita*).
3. **Madhva and the Dvaita tradition** (1238–1317, Phase 5) — Karnataka-based dualist Vedanta.
4. **North Indian bhakti reception** (15th c.+) — Ramananda's lineage opens bhakti to all castes; the Sant tradition (Kabir, Ravidas, Namdev — Phase 5/6) emerges alongside.
5. **Krishna-bhakti renaissance** (1500–1700) — Vallabhacharya's Pushti Marg in Braj; Chaitanya's Gaudiya Vaishnavism in Bengal; Mirabai in Rajasthan; Surdas (the leading Krishna-bhakti poet of Braj); the Radhavallabha and other Vrindavan-centered groupings.
6. **Ram-bhakti consolidation** (1500–1700) — Tulsidas's *Ramcharitmanas* (1574–1576) crystallizes North Indian vernacular Ram-bhakti; the Ramanandi sampradaya develops.
7. **Marathi Varkari tradition** — Tukaram (17th c.), Eknath, Namdev — Pandharpur-centered devotion to Vithoba (a Krishna form).
8. **Modern continuation and global expansion** — 19th–20th-c. continuation in India; ISKCON's 1966 founding and global Gaudiya Vaishnava expansion; the post-colonial Hindu religious-political reception of Tulsidas's *Ram-rajya*.

## Core texts
- Bhagavata Purana (Phase 5) — the principal Sanskrit Vaishnava theological narrative.
- Bhagavad Gita (Phase 2/3) — the foundational philosophical-devotional text.
- Jayadeva's *Gita Govinda* (Phase 5) — Radha-Krishna devotional poetics.
- Alvar Tamil corpus (Phase 5).

- Tukaram's Marathi Abhangas.
- Surdas's *Sur Sagar*.

## Defining doctrines
- *Bhakti* — devotional love as the supreme spiritual path, surpassing *karma* (action), *jnana* (knowledge), and *yoga* (discipline) when properly developed.
- *Avatara* — Vishnu descends in successive incarnations (ten major *avataras* in the standard list); Krishna and Rama are central.
- *Bhakti-rasa* — the aesthetics of devotion, systematized by Rupa Goswami (16th c.), with five primary forms (*shanta*, *dasya*, *sakhya*, *vatsalya*, *madhurya*).
- *Nama-sankirtana* — congregational chanting of the divine names as the supreme spiritual practice for the *Kali Yuga* (the current degenerate age).
- *Achintya-bheda-abheda* (Gaudiya) — the inconceivable simultaneous identity and difference of devotee and deity.
- Anti-caste, anti-ritualist, and (in some streams) anti-Sanskrit-elite-Brahmanism social posture.

## Relations to neighbors
- vs. Sant tradition (Kabir, Ravidas, Sikh Gurus): substantial overlap and exchange; both emphasize caste-defiance and devotional minimalism, but Vaishnava bhakti is *saguna* (form-attached) where Sant tradition is more *nirguna* (form-transcending).
- vs. [[tradition-sikhism]]: the Sant tradition that feeds into Sikhism overlaps with the Vaishnava bhakti tradition; the Adi Granth incorporates Vaishnava bhagats (Namdev) alongside Sants.
- vs. Hindu Shaivism and Shaktism: parallel devotional movements with their own histories.
- vs. [[tradition-sufism]]: parallel Indian Sufi devotional traditions had substantial cultural interpenetration with bhakti (especially in Bengali, Punjabi, and Dakhni contexts).
- vs. [[tradition-vedic-hinduism]] (foundational Vedic tradition): bhakti is theologically Vedic-derived but socially and devotionally transformative.
