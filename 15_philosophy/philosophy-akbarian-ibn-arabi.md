# Akbarian metaphysics (the school of Ibn ʿArabī)

## Identity

The **Akbarian school** (*al-madrasa al-Akbariyya*) is the systematic mystical metaphysics that grew out of the writings of **Muḥyī al-Dīn [[ibn-arabi|Ibn ʿArabī]]** (1165–1240), called *al-Shaykh al-Akbar*, "the Greatest Master" — hence *Akbarī*. It is the metaphysical-theosophical (*ʿirfān*) sibling to the two other great Islamic intellectual disciplines: *[[philosophy-falsafa|falsafa]]* (Aristotelian-Neoplatonic philosophy) and *[[philosophy-islamic-kalam-philosophy|kalām]]* (dialectical theology). Where the *faylasūf* reasons and the *mutakallim* defends dogma, the Akbarī reads the cosmos as a text of divine **self-disclosure** and systematizes what the realized mystic *witnesses*.

Its central doctrine is **[[wahdat-al-wujud|waḥdat al-wujūd]]** — "the oneness of being": there is, ultimately, only *one* being (*wujūd*), namely God, *al-Ḥaqq* (the Real, [[al-haqq]]); everything other than God has no being of its own and exists only insofar as the one Being discloses itself in finite, evanescent forms. (Diagnostic caution, following Chittick: Ibn ʿArabī himself does not use the nominal slogan "*waḥdat al-wujūd*"; the label is a coinage of his student **Ṣadr al-Dīn al-Qūnawī** (d. 1274) and the later commentators — Kāshānī, Qayṣarī, al-Jīlī, Jāmī — who turned the master's vast, unsystematic corpus into a technical school.)

The primary texts are Ibn ʿArabī's encyclopedic ***al-Futūḥāt al-Makkiyya*** ("The Meccan Openings," ~560 chapters, composed c. 1202–1231) and the concise, endlessly-commented ***[[phase-5-021-ibn-arabi-fusus-al-hikam|Fuṣūṣ al-Ḥikam]]*** ("The Bezels of Wisdom," c. 1230), 27 chapters each setting a "bezel" of divine wisdom in a prophet.

## The system

Four interlocking doctrines make the Akbarian school a *metaphysics* and not merely a devotional teaching:

- **Waḥdat al-wujūd — the oneness of being.** *Wujūd* (being/existence) has only one real referent, God. This is not Western pantheism (God is *not* identical with the cosmic totality) and its proponents insist it is *more radical* than pantheism: the cosmos has no being to be identified with God — only God *is*. It is better classed as *acosmic monism* or *theomonism*.

- **Tajallī — self-disclosure / theophany.** Creation is not an order of being *added* to God; it is God's Being manifesting itself in an unrepeating series of self-disclosures. Ibn ʿArabī's axiom: God never discloses Himself in the same form twice, and never to two witnesses in the same form. This is the Akbarian transposition of Neoplatonic emanation into a *theophanic* key.

- **al-Aʿyān al-thābita — the immutable (fixed) entities.** The archetypes of all things as objects of God's eternal knowledge. They possess *thubūt* (fixity in the divine knowledge) without *wujūd* (existence of their own); they receive existence through *tajallī* according to their innate predisposition (*istiʿdād*). Each thing is, in effect, what God knows it to be — which resolves divine foreknowledge and creaturely nature into a single act of self-manifestation.

- **al-Insān al-Kāmil — the Perfect Human.** The microcosm who reflects *all* the divine names at once and so serves as the *barzakh* (isthmus) between the Real and the cosmos — the polished mirror in which God contemplates God. Realized paradigmatically in the prophets, gathered in the **Muhammadan Reality** (*al-ḥaqīqa al-Muḥammadiyya*) and the Seal of the Saints (*khatm al-awliyāʾ*). ʿAbd al-Karīm **al-Jīlī**'s treatise *al-Insān al-Kāmil* (c. 1400) is its systematic exposition.

These are ordered by the schema of the **Five Divine Presences** (*al-ḥaḍarāt al-ilāhiyya al-khams*), fixed by al-Qūnawī and Qayṣarī: (1) the Essence in its absolute non-manifestation (*dhāt*, the unknowable), (2) the divine names and governing spirits (*jabarūt*), (3) the world of imagination (*ʿālam al-mithāl* / *malakūt*), (4) the sensory world (*ʿālam al-shahāda* / *mulk*), and (5) the Perfect Human who comprehends all four.

## Cross-tradition significance

The Akbarian school is a comparative-metaphysics *hub* precisely because its central claim — **that ultimate reality is one, and all multiplicity is its appearance** — recurs, independently, across traditions.

**Advaita Vedānta — convergence, not transmission.** The most-discussed parallel is with **Śaṅkara's [[philosophy-advaita-vedanta|Advaita Vedānta]]** (c. 800): only *nirguṇa* [[brahman-impersonal|Brahman]] is real, and the world of multiplicity is *māyā*, a superimposition on the one reality dissolved by correct knowing. Set beside *waḥdat al-wujūd* — only *al-Ḥaqq* truly *is*, and the cosmos is His self-disclosure — the structural agreement is striking, and **Reza Shah-Kazemi's *Paths to Transcendence*** (2006) develops the Śaṅkara–Ibn ʿArabī comparison at length. This parallel must be framed as **convergence**, never as genetic descent: there is **no documented historical channel** linking 8th-century South-Indian Vedānta to 13th-century Andalusian Sufism. Perennialist scholars (Schuon, Nasr, Shah-Kazemi) read the resemblance as evidence of a shared metaphysic; against them, the disanalogy is real — Ibn ʿArabī's Real is *theophanic and named*, balancing transcendence (*tanzīh*) with the self-manifesting divine names (*tashbīh*), and it never dissolves prophecy or the sharīʿa, whereas Advaita's *nirguṇa* Brahman is strictly attributeless. The convergence is genuine; the transmission is unproven and, most historians hold, unlikely.

**The Perfect Human ↔ the cosmic Anthropos.** *al-insān al-kāmil* fills the same cosmic-microcosm slot as the Gnostic Anthropos, the Manichaean Primal Man, the Vedic *Puruṣa*, and the Kabbalistic **[[adam-kadmon|Adam Kadmon]]** — the [[anthropos-myth|Anthropos pattern]] of the primal/perfect human who mirrors the whole. The parallel to Sefirotic Kabbalah's emanationist "supernal man" was noticed in medieval cross-tradition mysticism and is intensively studied in modern comparative scholarship (Wolfson), but it is best read as **convergence within a shared late-antique-to-medieval emanationist imaginaire**, not a demonstrated borrowing in either direction.

**Apophasis and emanation.** As a system that grounds all being in an unknowable Essence disclosed only through the names, Akbarian metaphysics belongs with the great **[[apophatic-mysticism|apophatic]]** traditions — Plotinus's One, [[the-one-plotinus|the One]], Pseudo-Dionysius, Eriugena, Meister Eckhart, and Kabbalah's *[[ein-sof|Ein Sof]]* — and Michael Sells treats Ibn ʿArabī among the masters of "unsaying" (*Mystical Languages of Unsaying*, 1994). Its Five Presences are the Islamic development of the Neoplatonic emanation ladder ([[divine-emanation|divine emanation]]; [[philosophy-neoplatonism-islamic|Islamic Neoplatonism]]), reached by radicalizing **Avicenna's** essence/existence distinction: where *[[philosophy-falsafa|falsafa]]* distinguishes necessary from contingent being, the Akbarīs fold all real being into the one *wujūd*.

**Perennialism.** In the 20th century the school became the Islamic centerpiece of the **[[perennial-philosophy|perennialist]]** thesis (Corbin, Izutsu, Nasr, Schuon) — read as an exponent of a trans-traditional wisdom. This reception is itself a contested interpretive move, resisted by historicist scholarship (Knysh 1999) that reads Ibn ʿArabī strictly within his Islamic polemical context.

## Disputes

- **Pantheism.** Whether *waḥdat al-wujūd* is pantheism in the strict Western sense (God = cosmos) is the perennial interpretive question. Chittick, Corbin, and Izutsu answer *no* — the doctrine is *more radical* than pantheism, since the cosmos has no being to identify with God. The school's critics answer *yes, in effect*.
- **Heresy polemics.** **Ibn Taymiyya** (d. 1328) and Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya wrote extensive refutations charging Ibn ʿArabī with *ittiḥād* (union) and *ḥulūl* (incarnation); modern Salafism inherits this critique. Knysh 1999 is the definitive study of the polemical afterlife.
- **The Advaita comparison.** The core firewall of this node: the wujūd-monism ↔ Advaita resemblance is **structural convergence**, not historical transmission. Naming it a "borrowing" in either direction is a category error the perennialist literature sometimes courts and the historical record does not support.
- **Waḥdat al-shuhūd.** Aḥmad Sirhindī (1564–1624) proposed *waḥdat al-shuhūd* (the oneness of *witnessing*) as a Naqshbandī reform: unity is what the mystic *experiences*, while creator and creation remain ontologically distinct. Whether this corrects or merely misreads Ibn ʿArabī is a standing debate in Indian-subcontinent Sufi historiography.
