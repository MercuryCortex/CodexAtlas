# Traditionalist Perennialism

The 20th-c. school holding that all authentic religious traditions originate from a single *primordial tradition* (*sophia perennis*) and converge in a single *transcendent unity* — distinct from but accessible through the orthodox exoteric forms of each tradition. Founded by René Guénon (1886-1951) in 1920s France; developed by Ananda K. Coomaraswamy (1877-1947) in 1930s-40s Boston; given its mature articulation by Frithjof Schuon (1907-1998) in *The Transcendent Unity of Religions* (1948). The school is sharply distinct from popular "all religions teach the same thing" pluralism — it insists on orthodox practice within an authentic tradition as the *only* access path to the transcendent unity (Sedgwick 2004).

## The three founders

- **René Guénon** — born Catholic in Blois, France 1886; passes through Theosophy and Hermetic-Masonic groups in 1910s Paris; encounters Hindu Vedanta via a network of Brahmin contacts; converts to Sufi Islam c. 1912 with reception into the Shādhilī order, emigrates to Cairo 1930 where he lives as Shaykh ʿAbd al-Wāḥid Yaḥyā until his death in 1951. *Introduction générale à l'étude des doctrines hindoues* (1921) and *La crise du monde moderne* (1927) inaugurate the school. The critique of modernity is severe and structural: the West has lost its tradition at the Renaissance; modern science, democracy, and individualism are degenerate phenomena.
- **Ananda K. Coomaraswamy** — Ceylon Tamil father, English mother; geologist-curator at Boston MFA from 1917 until his death in 1947. Vast scholarly output on Hindu and Buddhist art, metaphysics, and symbolism. *Hinduism and Buddhism* (1943) is a model of the Traditionalist comparative method. Less polemic than Guénon; more academic.
- **Frithjof Schuon** — Swiss-born 1907; converts to Sufi Islam in Algeria 1932; founds the Maryamiyya Sufi order (a branch of the Shādhilī ʿAlawiyya); emigrates to Bloomington, Indiana 1980 where he lives until 1998. *The Transcendent Unity of Religions* (1948) is the school's manifesto. *De l'unité transcendante des religions* distinguishes *exoteric* religion (the orthodox form, plural, mutually exclusive at the dogmatic level) from *esoteric* religion (the inner truth, single, accessible only through one's own orthodoxy).

## Cross-tradition resonance

The Traditionalist claim — all religions converge in a single transcendent unity — is structurally similar to the Vedantic [[philosophy-advaita-vedanta|Advaita]] claim that all is *Brahman*, with the religious traditions playing the role of multiple *māyā*-level paths to the same non-dual reality. Schuon explicitly draws this comparison. The school inherits from Renaissance Christian Kabbalists (Pico della Mirandola, Ficino) the *prisca theologia* (ancient theology) thesis — that pagan and Christian wisdom flow from a common source. Aldous Huxley's *The Perennial Philosophy* (1945) makes a parallel but less metaphysically rigorous claim and is often confused with Traditionalism.

The Traditionalist anti-modernism has had significant political reception. Sedgwick (2004) traces the influence of Traditionalism through Julius Evola (1898-1974) — far-right Italian Traditionalist — to mid-20th-c. fascist intellectual networks; and through contemporary figures like Aleksandr Dugin (b. 1962) to 21st-c. Russian Eurasianism. This political genealogy is contested by the religiously-oriented Traditionalist mainstream (Nasr, Lings, Smith), who view Evola as a heterodox dissident from Schuon's universalism. The school is a recurring touchstone in [[philosophy-phenomenology-of-religion|phenomenology-of-religion]] — Eliade was personally close to Coomaraswamy and engaged Guénon's work; both Eranos conferences and Traditionalist circles overlapped in 1930s-50s European intellectual networks.
