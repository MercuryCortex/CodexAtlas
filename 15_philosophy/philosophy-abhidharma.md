# Abhidharma

## Identity

**Abhidharma** (Sanskrit; Pali **Abhidhamma**) — variously glossed "the higher / further teaching" or "concerning the dharma" — is the systematic, analytical stratum of Buddhist thought that reworks the discursive teaching of the sūtras into an exhaustive, technical taxonomy of *dharmas*: the irreducible momentary constituents into which the whole of experience is analysed. It is the third of the Three Baskets (*Tripiṭaka* / *Tipiṭaka*) — Sūtra, Vinaya, **Abhidharma** — and it is widely regarded as the first properly *philosophical* system of Buddhism, in the sense of a self-conscious metaphysics, epistemology, and taxonomy built to make the Buddha's teaching precise and non-contradictory.

Abhidharma is not one school but a shared scholastic enterprise carried on within the mainstream (non-Mahāyāna, *śrāvakayāna*) traditions, above all by two: the **Sarvāstivāda** (in Kashmir and Gandhāra, composing in Sanskrit) and the **Theravāda** (in Sri Lanka, composing in Pali). In the Atlas it is placed among the philosophy schools as the **root against which the classical Mahāyāna systems react**: [[philosophy-madhyamaka|Madhyamaka]] and [[philosophy-yogacara|Yogācāra]] are both, in different ways, replies to the Abhidharma's picture of reality — a reply that presupposes, and could not exist without, the Abhidharma analysis it corrects.

## The core doctrine: dharma-theory

The Abhidharma's central move is a **two-level ontology**. At the level of *conventional truth* (*saṃvṛti-satya* / *sammuti-sacca*) there are persons, chariots, houses — the ordinary furniture of the world. At the level of *ultimate truth* (*paramārtha-satya* / *paramattha-sacca*) there are only *dharmas* (*dhammas*): the momentary, impersonal event-particulars — a flash of colour, a moment of contact, a pulse of aversion — that are the only things that *really* exist. A "person" is a mere designation (*prajñapti*) imposed on a stream of dharmas, exactly as "chariot" is a designation over pole, axle, and wheels (the [[phase-3-029-milindapanha|Nāgasena chariot-simile]]). The Abhidharma is therefore the technical, exhaustive form of the no-self doctrine ([[anatman-no-self|anātman]]): it dissolves the self by cataloguing everything that is *not* the self.

Each school produced a closed inventory of dharma-types:

- **Sarvāstivāda-Vaibhāṣika**: **75 dharmas** in five categories — 11 *rūpa* (material), 1 *citta* (mind), 46 *caitta* (mental factors), 14 *citta-viprayukta-saṃskāra* (conditioning forces dissociated from mind — e.g. possession, vitality, the marks of the conditioned), and 3 *asaṃskṛta* (unconditioned — space and two kinds of cessation).
- **Theravāda**: **82 ultimate realities** (*paramattha-dhamma*) — 1 category of *citta* (consciousness, resolved into 89 or 121 types), 52 *cetasika* (mental factors), 28 *rūpa* (materiality), and *nibbāna* (the single unconditioned).

Two further doctrines give the system its philosophical edge:

- **Momentariness** (*kṣaṇikavāda* / *khaṇavāda*): a dharma endures only for a *kṣaṇa*, an infinitesimal moment; the apparent persistence of things is a rapid succession of discrete, newly-arising momentary dharmas. Continuity is serial, not substantial.
- **Own-being** (*svabhāva* / *sabhāva*): each dharma has an intrinsic defining nature that makes it the kind of dharma it is and distinguishes it from every other. In the Sarvāstivāda this hardens into a strong realism (see below) — and it is precisely this *svabhāva* that the Mahāyāna doctrine of [[emptiness-sunyata|emptiness]] will later deny.

## The schools and their summae

**Sarvāstivāda** ("the doctrine that everything exists") takes its name from its signature and most controversial thesis: **dharmas exist in all three times** — past, present, and future are all real (*sarvam asti*). A dharma's *substance* (*dravya*) persists across the three times while only its *activity* (*kāritra*) is momentary and present. The system was codified in the *Jñānaprasthāna* of Kātyāyanīputra and its six auxiliary treatises (the *ṣaṭpādābhidharma*), then in the vast compendium *Mahāvibhāṣā* — whence the orthodox party is called **Vaibhāṣika**. Its greatest single text is Vasubandhu's *[[phase-4-077-abhidharmakosa|Abhidharmakośa]]* (4th–5th c.), which lays out the Vaibhāṣika system in verse and then, in the auto-commentary (*Bhāṣya*), criticises it from the rival **Sautrāntika** ("those who rely on the sūtras") standpoint — the Sautrāntikas rejected the three-times thesis and the reality of many dharmas, holding only the present moment real.

**Theravāda Abhidhamma** is organised in the seven books of the *Abhidhamma Piṭaka* — *Dhammasaṅgaṇī*, *Vibhaṅga*, *Dhātukathā*, *Puggalapaññatti*, *Kathāvatthu*, *Yamaka*, and the immense *Paṭṭhāna*, whose twenty-four *paccaya* (conditional relations) form the school's causal theory. Its classic synthesis is [[buddhaghosa|Buddhaghosa's]] *[[phase-4-076-visuddhimagga|Visuddhimagga]]* (5th c.); the standard later handbook is Anuruddha's *Abhidhammattha-saṅgaha*. The *Kathāvatthu* ("Points of Controversy"), traditionally ascribed to the Third Council under Aśoka, is a record of the doctrinal disputes that separated the early schools — including the refutation of the **Pudgalavāda** (the "personalists," who held that a *pudgala* is neither identical with nor different from the aggregates).

## Cross-tradition significance

**The Mahāyāna reply (transmission, not parallel).** For the Atlas, the key comparative fact about the Abhidharma is that it is the **thing the classical Mahāyāna philosophies were built to answer**. The Prajñāpāramitā literature and [[nagarjuna|Nāgārjuna's]] Madhyamaka extend the Buddha's no-self analysis from *persons* (*pudgala-nairātmya*) to *phenomena* (*dharma-nairātmya*): if the Abhidharma dissolved the self into dharmas that possess *svabhāva*, the Mahāyāna dissolves the dharmas too — they are [[emptiness-sunyata|empty]] of own-being. Nāgārjuna's target in the *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* is specifically the abhidharma *svabhāva*; [[philosophy-yogacara|Yogācāra]], for its part, keeps the abhidharma consciousness-analysis and rebuilds it around the *ālayavijñāna*. The bridge figure is [[vasubandhu|Vasubandhu]], traditionally credited with both the great abhidharma summa and, later, the founding Yogācāra treatises (though the single-author identity is contested — Frauwallner's two-Vasubandhus hypothesis). This is documented intellectual descent, not loose resemblance — the Mahāyāna schools quote, systematise, and refute the Abhidharma by name. The two-truths distinction (conventional / ultimate) that governs all later Buddhist metaphysics is itself an **Abhidharma invention**, inherited and reinterpreted by Madhyamaka.

**Reductionism about persons (structural convergence).** The Abhidharma's core claim — that a person is nothing over and above a bundle of impermanent, momentary constituents — is the canonical pre-modern analogue of the reductionist analyses of personal identity in **Hume** (the self as "a bundle or collection of different perceptions") and **Derek Parfit** (*Reasons and Persons*, 1984). Parfit and Mark Siderits (*Buddhism as Philosophy*, 2007) draw the comparison explicitly. No historical transmission is claimed in either direction; the parallel is a genuine structural convergence, and it has made the Abhidharma a standard reference-point in contemporary analytic philosophy of mind.

**Dharma-theory and atomism (typological, not genealogical).** The pairing of an exhaustive inventory of ultimate constituents with a doctrine of momentariness invites comparison with Greek [[philosophy-atomism-greek|atomism]] (Democritus, Epicurus). The comparison must be handled carefully: the abhidharma "atom" is not a persisting material corpuscle but a momentary **event- or quality-particular**, and mainstream scholarship posits **no contact** between the Indian and Greek systems. The resemblance is typological — two independent solutions to "what are the ultimate constituents of the world?" — not a case of borrowing. (The distinct Buddhist doctrine of material atoms, *paramāṇu*, is a separate, later development within the same schools and is closer in kind to the Greek discussion, though still independent of it.)

## Disputes

**Is the Abhidharma the word of the Buddha?** The Theravāda tradition holds that the Buddha himself taught the Abhidhamma — to his mother and the gods in the Tāvatiṃsa heaven — so that it is *buddhavacana*. Critical scholarship since **Frauwallner** (1995) instead treats the canonical Abhidharma as a **post-canonical scholastic development** of the 3rd–1st centuries BCE, grown out of the *mātṛkā* (mnemonic doctrinal lists) already embedded in the sūtras. On Frauwallner's influential thesis the Theravāda and Sarvāstivāda systems developed **independently and in parallel** from that common earlier list-layer, rather than descending from a single original Abhidharma — which is why their dharma-inventories differ (82 vs 75) while sharing a method.

**Does "everything exists" violate impermanence?** The Sarvāstivāda three-times thesis was the central inner-Buddhist controversy of the abhidharma age. Four Vaibhāṣika masters (Dharmatrāta, Ghoṣaka, Vasumitra, Buddhadeva) offered competing accounts of *how* a dharma can exist in past, present, and future without becoming permanent; Vasumitra's "activity" (*kāritra*) theory became orthodox. The Sautrāntikas, and after them the Mahāyāna, charged that a *dravya* persisting across the three times smuggles a hidden, quasi-eternal **substance** back into a system founded on impermanence — that the Sarvāstivāda *svabhāva* is, in effect, the very self-existence the Buddha denied. Whether the doctrine preserves or betrays impermanence is the question that the emptiness critique was designed to settle (Cox, *Disputed Dharmas*, 1995; Williams, *Buddhist Thought*, 2000).
