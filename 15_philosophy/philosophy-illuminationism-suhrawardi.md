# Illuminationism (Ḥikmat al-Ishrāq — Suhrawardī and the Ishrāqī school)

## Identity

Illuminationism — Arabic *ḥikmat al-ishrāq*, "the wisdom / philosophy of illumination" — is the school of Islamic philosophy founded by [[suhrawardi|Shihāb al-Dīn Yaḥyā al-Suhrawardī]] (1154–1191), the *Shaykh al-Ishrāq* ("Master of Illumination"), executed at Aleppo in 1191 and remembered as *al-Maqtūl*, "the executed one." Its charter text is the [[phase-5-018-suhrawardi-hikmat-al-ishraq|*Ḥikmat al-Ishrāq*]] (Philosophy of Illumination, completed 1186). The word *ishrāq* deliberately puns two senses: the *rising of light* (illumination as an epistemic event) and the *Orient* (*mashriq*), the symbolic East of Lights from which the soul descends and to which it returns. Illuminationism is widely regarded (Ziai 1990; Walbridge 2000) as the second great post-classical tradition of Islamic philosophy after Avicennan Peripateticism, and the sibling of [[philosophy-falsafa|falsafa]] out of which it grew — Suhrawardī wrote three Peripatetic systematic works before turning to the ishrāqī synthesis. It continues through the commentaries of Shahrazūrī and Quṭb al-Dīn al-Shīrāzī (13th c.), is absorbed into [[mulla-sadra|Mullā Ṣadrā]]'s transcendent wisdom (17th c.), and survives as a living current in the Iranian philosophical curriculum to the present.

## Core doctrine

**The metaphysics of light.** Suhrawardī re-founds first philosophy on *light* (*nūr*) as the most self-evident of all realities — that which is "manifest in itself and manifesting of others" and needs no definition. Being is graded luminosity. At the summit stands the **Light of Lights** (*Nūr al-Anwār*), the absolutely self-subsistent light, from which proceed, by a purely incorporeal self-diffusion, the **dominating lights** (*al-anwār al-qāhira*), then the **managing lights** (*al-anwār al-mudabbira*, i.e. souls), and finally the "dusky substances" (*barzakh*) of bodies. Crucially, **darkness (*ẓulma*) is privation** — the mere absence or weakening of light, not a coequal counter-principle; Illuminationism is therefore a **light-monism**, not a two-principle dualism (see [[light-and-darkness-dualism]] and Disputes). See [[divine-emanation]].

**The Platonic Forms as light-archangels.** The Illuminationist re-reading of the Platonic Forms is its signature move: the Forms become the *arbāb al-anwāʿ*, the **"lords of the species"** — self-subsistent dominating lights, each an incorporeal archangelic intelligence that is the paradigm and governor of a natural kind. Suhrawardī correlates them explicitly with the Zoroastrian **Amesha Spentas** (naming light-ranks after Ordibehesht = Asha Vahishta, Bahman = Vohu Manah). This fusion of Platonic Forms with angelology is the subject of Walbridge's *The Leaven of the Ancients* (2000). See [[forms-and-archetypes]].

**Critique of Peripatetic definition.** Against the Aristotelian–Avicennan theory of the essentialist definition (*ḥadd* by genus + differentia), Suhrawardī argues that a real definition is impossible: one can never establish that the enumerated constituents are *all* and *only* the essential ones, so a definition either presupposes what it should deliver or fails to capture the essence. This is, on Ziai's reading (1990), among the more developed internal critiques of Peripatetic essentialism in the Arabic tradition, analysed technically by Ziai (1990).

**Knowledge by presence.** In place of representational, definition-based knowledge, Suhrawardī grounds cognition in **knowledge by presence** (*al-ʿilm al-ḥuḍūrī*): the immediate, non-representational self-presence by which a luminous soul knows itself and, in illumination, apprehends the higher lights directly. Self-awareness is not a picture of the self but the self's own luminous presence to itself — a doctrine later central to [[mulla-sadra|Mullā Ṣadrā]] and revived in 20th-c. Iranian philosophy.

**The two lineages of ancient wisdom.** Suhrawardī frames his project as the recovery of a single "leaven" (*khamīra*) of wisdom transmitted along two lines — a **Greek** line (Pythagoras, Empedocles, Plato) and a **Persian Khusrawānī / Pahlawī** line of light-sages — converging in Illuminationism. This is his "Platonic Orientalism" (Walbridge 2001). See [[perennial-philosophy]].

## Cross-tradition significance

Illuminationism is one of the Atlas's clearest cases of a self-conscious, *authored* synthesis across traditions — and therefore a place where the transmission-versus-symbolic-borrowing distinction must be drawn carefully.

**Falsafa and Neoplatonism (secure, documented).** The downstream–upstream lines here are not in doubt. Illuminationism is a reform *within* the [[philosophy-falsafa|falsafa]] tradition: Suhrawardī knew and wrote the Avicennan corpus, and the light-hierarchy is a recoding of the Farabian–Avicennan [[divine-emanation|emanation]] scheme inherited through [[philosophy-neoplatonism-islamic|Islamic Neoplatonism]]. The identification of the [[forms-and-archetypes|Forms]] with self-subsistent lights is a genuine, text-documented Platonism (mediated through late-antique doxography rather than the dialogues themselves).

**Zoroastrian light-vocabulary (borrowing, not continuous transmission).** Suhrawardī deliberately imports Iranian religious language — the kingly/spiritual glory *kharra* / *khurra* ([[khvarenah|xᵛarənah / farr]]), the Amesha Spentas as names for his light-archangels, the "Khusrawānī sages" as an ancient Persian sapiential lineage. This is the "explicit Platonic-Zoroastrian synthesis" for which the school is famous. But the honest verdict, argued at length by **Walbridge (2000)**, is that this is a *symbolic appropriation* — Suhrawardī had no access to a living Avestan-Zoroastrian theology and drew his "Persian" material largely from Islamic-era doxography and his own Platonic framework; the Zoroastrian content is thinner and more reconstructed than **Corbin (1971)**, who read Suhrawardī as the reviver of a genuine "spiritual Iran," maintained. The Atlas therefore wires [[khvarenah]] as an *appropriation* edge, not a continuous-transmission one. Relatedly, the light/darkness idiom invites comparison with Zoroastrian [[light-and-darkness-dualism|dualism]], but Suhrawardī's system is a graded **monism** of light in which darkness is mere privation — the opposite metaphysics under a shared vocabulary.

**A medieval ancestor of perennialism.** Suhrawardī's doctrine of one *khamīra* of wisdom behind Greek and Persian sages is a genuine pre-modern precedent for the Renaissance *prisca theologia* / [[perennial-philosophy|philosophia perennis]] — a contributor to, not the origin of, the later Ficinian and modern perennialist claim.

## Disputes

**Transmission vs. symbol (Corbin vs. Walbridge/Ziai).** The central scholarly divide is over what Suhrawardī's "Oriental" material *is*. **Henry Corbin** read Illuminationism as the recovery of an authentic Zoroastrian angelology and visionary theosophy — "the Platonists of Persia" as heirs of pre-Islamic Iranian spirituality. **John Walbridge** and **Hossein Ziai** read Suhrawardī primarily as a *philosopher* working within the Greek tradition, whose "Orient of Lights" is a Platonic construction ("Platonic Orientalism") and whose Zoroastrian references are learned symbolism rather than transmitted doctrine. The mainstream current position leans to the Walbridge/Ziai reading while granting that the Zoroastrian *imagery* is deliberate and structurally important.

**Philosophy or theosophy?** Whether *ḥikmat al-ishrāq* is a rigorous demonstrative philosophy (Ziai's emphasis on its logic and epistemology) or an essentially mystical–visionary "theosophy" requiring inner unveiling (Corbin's and, in part, Nasr's emphasis) is contested — a tension Suhrawardī himself invites by insisting the true philosopher unites discursive reasoning (*baḥth*) with direct illumination (*taʾalluh*).

**Break or reform?** How radical the rupture with Avicennan Peripateticism really is remains debated: the critique of definition and the light-ontology mark a genuine systematic break, yet the earlier Peripatetic works and the retained emanational architecture show substantial continuity, so some read Illuminationism as a reform *within* falsafa rather than an independent school.
