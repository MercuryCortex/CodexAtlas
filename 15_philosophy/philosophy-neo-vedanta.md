# Neo-Vedanta

## Identity

Neo-Vedanta (also "Neo-Hinduism," "modern Vedanta," "practical Vedanta") is the 19th–20th-century reformist reframing of [[philosophy-advaita-vedanta|Advaita Vedanta]] as the perennial essence of all religions and the spiritual core of a modern, universal Hinduism. Its reformist headwater is Rammohan Roy and the Brahmo Samaj (founded 1828); its mature doctrinal form is the synthesis of [[swami-vivekananda|Swami Vivekananda]] (1863–1902), principal disciple of the Bengali mystic [[ramakrishna|Ramakrishna]] (1836–1886); its most prominent 20th-century academic voice is Sarvepalli Radhakrishnan (1888–1975). Where Shankara's classical Advaita is a scholastic tradition of Upanishadic commentary, Neo-Vedanta is a modern, missionary, and self-consciously comparative program: it presents non-dualism not as one Hindu *darshana* among six but as the underlying truth toward which all faiths ascend.

Its signature theses are four: (1) the **harmony of religions** — all religions are true, partial paths to the one reality; (2) the **potential divinity of the soul** — Vivekananda's formula that "each soul is potentially divine," and that religion is the manifestation of that divinity already within; (3) **practical Vedanta** — non-dualism as an ethic of service (*seva*) and social uplift rather than world-renouncing contemplation alone; and (4) the primacy of **direct experience** (*anubhava*, *samadhi*) as the verification of religious truth. In the Atlas it sits as the modern transformation of Advaita and as a principal modern feeder into the [[perennial-philosophy|perennial-philosophy]] and [[non-duality|non-duality]] clusters.

## In the movement and its texts

The emblematic public birth of the movement is Vivekananda's address to the World's Parliament of Religions in Chicago (11 September 1893), which opened with an appeal for the end of "sectarianism, bigotry, and ... fanaticism" and presented Vedanta as a tolerant, universal spirituality. He founded the Vedanta Society in New York (1894) and the Ramakrishna Mission in India (1897), institutionalizing a monastic order committed to both contemplation and social service. His lecture cycles — *Raja Yoga* (1896), *Jnana Yoga*, *Karma Yoga*, and *Bhakti Yoga* — recast the classical four yogas as complementary universal methods, with Raja Yoga in particular systematizing meditative experience for a global audience.

Ramakrishna supplies the movement's experiential authority: his reported practice of Christian and Islamic as well as Hindu devotions, and his conclusion *yato mat, tato path* ("as many faiths, so many paths"), is the lived warrant for the harmony-of-religions doctrine that Vivekananda then theorized. Radhakrishnan carried the program into the Western academy and public life (Spalding Professor at Oxford; later President of India), arguing in *An Idealist View of Life* (1932) and *Eastern Religions and Western Thought* (1939) that Vedanta is a universal, experientially grounded idealism compatible with — and corrective to — modern science and Western philosophy. [[sri-aurobindo|Sri Aurobindo]] represents a distinct, evolutionary wing of the same modern Vedantic impulse, transforming Advaita into a metaphysics of spiritual evolution (*The Life Divine*, 1939–40).

## Cross-tradition significance

Neo-Vedanta is one of the principal channels by which non-dual metaphysics entered global religious modernity. Its harmony-of-religions thesis is a modern statement of the [[perennial-philosophy|perennial philosophy]] — the claim that beneath the exoteric surface of the traditions lies a single mystical truth — and Vivekananda's Vedanta directly fed the 20th-century perennialist current running through Aldous Huxley (whose *The Perennial Philosophy* quotes Vivekananda and Ramakrishna). Its relation to the [[philosophy-perennialism-traditionalist|Traditionalist school]] of Guénon, Coomaraswamy, and Schuon is more oblique and partly antagonistic — Guénon drew his non-dualism from Śaṅkara directly and was critical of the Ramakrishna–Vivekananda movement, even as the two currents converge on a shared transcendent-unity thesis (Sedgwick 2004). The convergence is structural: both universalize a single reality accessed through the plural traditions, though Neo-Vedanta arises from Hindu reform and Traditionalism from a Western esoteric-then-Islamic base.

The honest historical question is **transmission versus modern construction**. On one reading (Paul Hacker; Wilhelm Halbfass) the universalist, experience-first, socially engaged Vedanta of Vivekananda is not a straightforward continuation of Shankara but a **modern transformation**, shaped in the colonial encounter and partly read through European categories — Schopenhauer's and Paul Deussen's Romantic reception of the Upanishads, and the idealist identification of *Brahman* with an Absolute of the sort found in [[philosophy-german-idealism|German idealism]]. On this view the parallel between Neo-Vedanta and Western perennial idealism is not a discovery of a pre-existing sameness but partly a **product of the same 19th-century intellectual milieu** working on both sides. This is a genuine dependence claim, not mere convergence, and it is contested (see Disputes). The deeper structural kinship with [[non-duality|non-dual]] mysticism worldwide — Plotinian *henosis*, Sufi *wahdat al-wujud*, Mahayana emptiness — is the older Advaitic inheritance that Neo-Vedanta universalizes and exports, and belongs to the broader convergence debate carried by the non-duality hub rather than to any single line of transmission.

## Disputes

The central scholarly dispute is whether Neo-Vedanta is a **modern invention** or a **legitimate development** of the classical tradition.

- **The "invented modern Vedanta" position** (Paul Hacker, *Philology and Confrontation* 1995; Wilhelm Halbfass, *India and Europe* 1988): Neo-Vedanta departs from Shankara on substance, not just idiom. Shankara subordinates ethics and action to renunciatory knowledge and treats *sruti* (scripture) as the sole *pramana* for Brahman; Vivekananda's "practical Vedanta" makes service a spiritual path and, crucially, makes **direct experience** the final authority. Anantanand Rambachan (*The Limits of Scripture* 1994) documents this precise inversion: for Vivekananda *anubhava* / *samadhi* verifies Brahman-knowledge, where for Shankara the verbal testimony of the Upanishads is the irreplaceable means. Jyotirmaya Sharma (*A Restatement of Religion* 2013) presses the political edge — that Vivekananda's universalism doubles as a source of muscular Hindu self-assertion.

- **The "pre-colonial roots" counter-position** (Andrew Nicholson, *Unifying Hinduism* 2010): the unification of the disparate Hindu *darshanas* into a single tradition with Vedanta at its apex is not a colonial fabrication but has demonstrable pre-modern precedent, especially in the doxographies of Vijnanabhiksu (c. 16th c.). On this view Neo-Vedanta continues an indigenous unifying trajectory rather than inventing one under Western pressure — qualifying, without dissolving, the Hacker–Halbfass thesis.

The defensible middle position: the **experiential and universalist reframing is genuinely modern and partly shaped by the Western encounter**, while the **impulse to unify Hindu thought under Vedanta has older Indian roots** — so Neo-Vedanta is neither a pure colonial artifact nor a seamless continuation of Shankara, but a real modern transformation of an already-consolidating tradition.
