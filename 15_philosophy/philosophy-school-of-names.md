# School of Names (Míngjiā 名家, the Logicians / Disputers)

The **School of Names** (*Míngjiā* 名家, literally "School/Family of Names") is the modern label for a current of Warring States (Zhànguó, 475-221 BCE) Chinese thought preoccupied with the relation between **names** (*míng* 名) and the **objects/actualities** they pick out (*shí* 實), and above all with the **paradoxes** that arise when names and things are prised apart. Like [[philosophy-sophism]] and [[philosophy-cynicism]], it is best treated as a **movement** rather than a doctrinal school with a common metaphysics or a succession of masters: its members shared a preoccupation and a method — public **disputation** (*biàn* 辯) — but no agreed teaching. In their own age they were called **the disputers** (*biàn zhě* 辯者); "Míngjiā" is a retrospective bibliographical classification, coined when Han-dynasty librarians sorted the pre-imperial thinkers into schools — one of **Sima Tan's six schools** (*liù jiā*) in the essay preserved in *Shiji* 130 (c. 110 BCE), and one of the ten schools of Ban Gu's *Hanshu* "Yiwenzhi" (1st c. CE).

Two figures anchor the movement:

- **Hui Shi** (惠施, also *Huizi* 惠子, c. 370-310 BCE) — a statesman (chief minister of King Hui of Wei/Liang) as well as a dialectician, and the friend, host and philosophical foil of **[[zhuangzi|Zhuang Zhou]]**. His thought survives almost entirely as a bare list of **ten theses** (the "ten points," *shí shì*) recorded in *Zhuangzi* chapter 33.
- **Gongsun Long** (公孫龍, c. 320-250 BCE) — of the state of Zhao, associated with the court of the Lord of Pingyuan, and author of the treatise *Gongsun Longzi* (公孫龍子), which carries the movement's most famous set-piece arguments.

## Core arguments

**Hui Shi's ten theses — the paradoxes of infinity and relativity.** Preserved only in *Zhuangzi* 33, the ten theses press the relativity of all magnitudes and distinctions toward a monistic conclusion. Among them: *"The greatest has nothing outside it — call it the Great One (dà yī); the smallest has nothing within it — call it the Small One (xiǎo yī)"*; *"The sky is as low as the earth; mountains are level with marshes"*; *"The sun at noon is the sun declining; the creature born is the creature dying"*; *"I set off for Yue today and arrived yesterday"*; and the concluding ethical turn, *"Love the myriad things without discrimination; Heaven and Earth are one body."* The theses dissolve fixed spatial, temporal and dimensional boundaries by showing every predicate to be relative to a frame, and end — like a paradox-logic turned to a mystical use — in the unity of all things.

**Gongsun Long's "white horse."** One of the best-known Chinese philosophical paradoxes, *"a white horse is not a horse"* (*bái mǎ fēi mǎ* 白馬非馬), argued in the *Bái mǎ lùn* ("On the White Horse"). The dialogue defends the thesis on the ground that the name "horse" (*mǎ*) selects for **shape**, while "white" (*bái*) selects for **colour**; "white horse" names shape-and-colour together, and so what "white horse" picks out is not what "horse" picks out — the two names have different denotations, hence "a white horse is not a horse." Whether this is a semantic point about the non-identity of a compound term with its head, a mereological claim about part and whole, or a sophistical trick has been argued ever since (see below).

**Hard and white.** The *Jiān bái lùn* ("On Hard and White") argues that the **hardness** and the **whiteness** of a stone are **separable** (*lí jiān bái* 離堅白): sight finds the white but not the hard, touch finds the hard but not the white, so hardness and whiteness are never given together and are, in that sense, "apart." The "hard and white" (*jiān bái*) and "same and different" (*tóng yì*) debates became the standing headings under which the whole disputation over names was conducted.

## In the sources and the later Mohist engagement

The evidence for the School of Names is thin, late and largely hostile. Hui Shi wrote a great deal — *Zhuangzi* 33 says "his writings would fill five carts" — but none of it survives except the ten theses embedded in that same, unsympathetic, chapter. Gongsun Long is known from the transmitted *Gongsun Longzi*, a short text whose received six chapters are partly a later reconstruction (see Disputes). The Confucian **Xunzi** devotes part of his "Rectifying Names" (*Zhèng míng*) chapter to refuting the disputers' paradoxes as verbal confusions that "disorder names."

The most substantial technical engagement comes from the **Later Mohist Canons** (*Mòjīng* 墨經, chapters 40-45 of the *Mozi*) — the rigorous logical-scientific stratum of [[philosophy-mohism]]. The Canons take up the disputers' own vocabulary of "hard and white" and "same and different," build a theory of naming (*míng*) tied to objects (*shí*), lay down rules of disputation (*biàn*), and argue **against** Gongsun Long's separations — holding that the properties of a single thing are mutually pervasive rather than "apart." A. C. Graham's reconstruction (1978) showed that the Mohist dialecticians and the School of Names are the two poles of one Warring States culture of argument, contending over the same name-object problem.

## Cross-tradition significance

**Engaged-by the Later Mohists (secure, internal to China).** The clearest and best-attested relation is the technical dispute with the [[philosophy-mohism|Mohist]] *Canons*, which quote and rebut the "hard-white" and "same-different" positions and constitute the nearest thing pre-imperial China produced to a formal logic and semantics. This is a documented philosophical engagement, not a typological guess.

**Foil-of Philosophical Daoism (secure, biographical).** Hui Shi is [[philosophy-daoism-philosophical|Zhuangzi]]'s named friend and antagonist, and the *Zhuangzi* is our sole witness to his ten theses. Zhuangzi borrows the disputers' relativizing paradoxes but turns them against fixed distinction altogether: the *Qiwulun* equalizes the very disputes (*biàn*) the School of Names lived by. The relation is intimate and internal to Chinese thought.

**Independent convergence with Eleatic and sophistic Greece (transmission excluded).** The School of Names is the standard comparative counterpart to the Greek paradox-logicians. Hui Shi's theses of the infinitely large and infinitely small, and of the traveller who "arrives yesterday," sit beside [[philosophy-eleaticism|Zeno of Elea]]'s paradoxes of magnitude and motion; the whole *biàn zhě* culture of professional disputation sits beside the Greek [[philosophy-sophism|sophistic]] movement, down to the shared fate of surviving only through their enemies. In every case there was **no contact** between 4th-century-BCE China and Greece: these are textbook cases of typological convergence — two independent argumentative cultures generating structurally similar paradoxes about the One and the Many and about relative predication. Comparativists (Graham, Harbsmeier) draw the Zeno comparison with care; mainstream sinology and Presocratic scholarship treat the traditions as unconnected in origin, which is exactly what makes the parallel evidentially interesting.

## Disputes

- **Authenticity of the *Gongsun Longzi*.** The transmitted treatise is not a clean early text. Graham's "Three Studies of Kung-sun Lung" (in Graham 1990) argues that only the "White Horse" and part of the "Pointing to Things" dialogues descend from the historical Gongsun Long, the remaining chapters being a later (post-Han) reconstruction assembled from doxographic scraps. Reconstructing the movement's original doctrine is therefore genuinely uncertain.

- **What "a white horse is not a horse" means.** The interpretations diverge sharply. Fung Yu-lan (1952) read it Platonically, as a distinction between the universal "horseness" and the compound universal "white-horseness." Chad Hansen (1983, 1992) rejects the universals framework entirely, reading classical Chinese nouns as mass-terms and the argument as a mereological point about part-and-whole. Harbsmeier (1998) and others treat it as a semantic argument about the non-identity of a compound term's reference with its head-term's reference. There is no consensus on whether the argument is a serious logical insight or an eristic trick — the same charge levelled at Zeno.

- **Was there a "School of Names" at all?** Because "Míngjiā" is a Han librarians' category and the *biàn zhě* shared no common doctrine, some scholars stress that Hui Shi (a relativist-monist mystic close to Zhuangzi) and Gongsun Long (an analytic separator of terms) actually point in opposite philosophical directions, so that the "school" names a loose family of disputers rather than a unified teaching. This node types them, accordingly, as a **movement** — matching the Atlas's treatment of Sophism and Cynicism.

- **Person-nodes still owed.** No dedicated Atlas nodes yet exist for **Gongsun Long** or **Hui Shi**, the two principal figures; both are recorded here as demand (see Refs and self-flags) rather than wired as dead links. Hui Shi appears in the Atlas only inside the [[zhuangzi]] material as Zhuangzi's interlocutor, not yet as his own node.
