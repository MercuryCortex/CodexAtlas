# Roman Stoicism

The practical-ethical phase of the Stoic school in the early Roman imperial period (-100 to 180 CE), preserved in the four major Roman-era source-corpora: Seneca the Younger's *Letters to Lucilius* + *De Tranquillitate Animi* + the tragedies (-4 to 65); Musonius Rufus's lecture-fragments (c. 30-101); Epictetus's *Discourses* + *Enchiridion* (transcribed by his student Arrian c. 108-135); and Marcus Aurelius's *Meditations* (180, addressed to himself rather than published). Roman Stoicism focuses almost entirely on ethics and the practical question *how should one live* — the early Stoa's elaborate logic, physics, and Logos-theology recede into background commitments rather than central topics (Long 2002).

## Core practical doctrines

- **The dichotomy of control** (Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 1.1): some things are *in our power* (judgment, desire, aversion, action) and others *not in our power* (body, property, reputation, office). Wisdom consists in caring exclusively for what is in our power and treating everything else with equanimity. This is the single most-influential Stoic ethical position in subsequent Western reception.
- **Prosochê** (continuous attention) and **prokopê** (progress): the Stoic life is a practice of moment-by-moment vigilance over one's impressions (*phantasiai*) and assent (*sunkatathesis*). Marcus's *Meditations* exemplifies this — the emperor self-correcting before bed and on waking.
- **Premeditatio malorum** (Seneca, *Letters* 91, 107): the practice of imagining future evils (exile, death, illness) in order to defuse their power. Modern cognitive-behavioral therapy borrows this directly.
- **Amor fati** (love of fate; Marcus, Epictetus): willing acceptance of what providence brings, recognizing one's role in the cosmic order.
- **Cosmopolitanism**: every rational being is a citizen of the cosmic city. Marcus famously: "as Antoninus, my city is Rome; as a human being, my city is the world."

## Historical context

Seneca was tutor and later effective regent to Nero; his suicide on Nero's orders (65 CE) becomes a paradigmatic Stoic death (preserved in Tacitus *Annals* XV). Musonius Rufus and his student Epictetus represent the freedman-and-exile articulation — Epictetus was born a slave in Hierapolis Phrygia, was freed, then exiled from Rome by Domitian c. 95 along with all philosophers, and re-established his school at Nicopolis in Epirus. Marcus Aurelius's *Meditations* — composed in Greek during his Danube campaigns 170-180 — is the unique surviving private spiritual journal of a Roman emperor and the most influential text of Roman Stoicism in subsequent reception.

## Cross-tradition resonance

Roman Stoicism's *prosochê* (continuous self-observation) and Epictetus's daily examination of impressions show structural parallel to Christian *examen conscientiae* — and the direct transmission is documented: Epictetus's *Enchiridion* was Christianized in the 5th-7th c. as paraphrases by Nilus of Ancyra (*Encheiridion Christianum*) and by Maximus the Confessor's circle, becoming required reading in Eastern Orthodox monastic formation. The Stoic doctrine of the *logos spermatikos* (seminal Logos) is absorbed by Justin Martyr (*Apology* II.13) and runs into Christian Logos-Christology. Roman Stoic *cosmopolitanism* (universal citizenship of rational beings) provides a structural template for Paul's *Galatians* 3:28 universalism — Paul was a citizen of Tarsus, a major Stoic center where Athenodorus and Posidonius's students taught (Engberg-Pedersen 2000 *Paul and the Stoics*).

Beyond Christianity, Roman Stoic practical ethics parallel Indian *Yoga-sūtras* (Patañjali c. 200-400 CE) on attention-discipline (*saṃyama*) and Buddhist *sati* (mindfulness) — the structural convergences are documented but the *Yoga-sūtras* and the *Discourses* are roughly contemporaneous with no plausible direct contact. The *Bhagavad-gītā*'s [karma-yoga] (action without attachment to fruits) is a recurring Stoicism-parallel comparison in modern Indo-European philosophical work (Hadot 1995 *Philosophy as a Way of Life*). The 20th-c. revival of Stoicism via Pierre Hadot, Martha Nussbaum, William Irvine, Massimo Pigliucci has produced a substantial "modern Stoicism" movement explicitly drawing the Christian, Buddhist, and CBT parallels.
