# Buddhist Pramāṇavāda

## Identity

**Pramāṇavāda** — the Buddhist "way of the means of knowledge," also called the Buddhist *logico-epistemological* school (Sanskrit *pramāṇa*, "means of valid cognition"; Tibetan *tshad ma*) — is the tradition of epistemology and logic founded by **Dignāga** (c. 480–540 CE) and brought to its classical summit by **Dharmakīrti** (c. 600–660 CE). It is among the most technically demanding philosophical enterprises in Indian Buddhism and, together with its great rival [[philosophy-nyaya-vaisheshika-realism|Nyāya]], the core of classical Indian epistemology. Its governing questions are: *how many valid means of knowledge are there, what does each of them reach, and what makes a cognition count as knowledge at all?* Its distinctive answers — exactly **two** means of knowledge, a **bare momentary particular** as the sole ultimately real object, meaning as **exclusion** rather than reference, and cognition as intrinsically **self-aware** — grew out of [[philosophy-yogacara|Yogācāra]] and became, through translation into Tibetan, the backbone of monastic philosophical training down to the present.

In the Atlas it is placed among the philosophy schools as a **major headwater** whose low modern name-recognition belies its importance: it is the Buddhist pole of the millennium-long Indian debate over perception, inference, and universals, and the direct ancestor of the *tshad ma* curriculum of Tibetan Buddhism.

## The two pramāṇas and the bare particular

Where the Naiyāyikas admitted four means of knowledge — perception, inference, comparison (*upamāna*), and verbal testimony (*śabda*) — Dignāga's *Pramāṇasamuccaya* ("Compendium of the Means of Knowledge") reduces them to **two**: **perception** (*pratyakṣa*) and **inference** (*anumāna*). Testimony and comparison, he argues, contain no new source of knowledge; they collapse into inference. The reduction is not arbitrary: it follows from a doctrine of **object–instrument correlation** (*pramāṇa-vyavasthā*). There are exactly two kinds of object, so there are exactly two means of grasping them.

- The **particular** (*svalakṣaṇa*, "own-characteristic"): the unique, momentary, non-repeatable *this-here-now*. It alone is **causally efficacious** (*arthakriyā-samartha*) — able to do work, produce effects — and therefore, for Dharmakīrti, it alone is **ultimately real** (*paramārtha-sat*). It is the object of **perception**, which Dignāga defines as cognition **free of conceptual construction** (*kalpanāpoḍha*); Dharmakīrti adds that it must also be **non-deceptive** (*abhrānta*).
- The **universal** (*sāmānya-lakṣaṇa*, "general characteristic"): the repeatable, shareable general character — *cow*, *blue*, *impermanent*. It is a conceptual construction, only **conventionally real** (*saṃvṛti-sat*), and it is the object of **inference** and of all thought and language.

Dharmakīrti's *Pramāṇavārttika* ("Commentary on the Means of Knowledge") welds this to the Buddhist doctrine of **momentariness** (*kṣaṇikatva*): *whatever exists is momentary* (*yat sat tat kṣaṇikam*), demonstrated by his celebrated **inference of momentariness from existence** (*sattvānumāna*). The svalakṣaṇa is thus the epistemological face of the [[philosophy-abhidharma|Abhidharma]] *dharma* — the momentary event-particular — now recast as *the thing perception actually touches* before thought overlays it with enduring, shareable universals.

## Apoha: meaning as exclusion

If only bare momentary particulars are real, how can a word like "cow," which applies to many things across time, mean anything — without smuggling back the real universals the school denies? Dignāga's answer is the theory of **apoha** (*anya-apoha*, "exclusion of the other"): a concept or word does **not** pick out a positive shared essence but operates **negatively**, by excluding everything the term does not apply to. "Cow" means *not-non-cow*. The unity that thought and language impose on a scattered manifold of unique particulars is a **constructed, negative** unity, not a real universal inhering in things.

Apoha is the Buddhist form of **nominalism** (or, on some readings, conceptualism): it lets the school have general terms and inference while keeping an ontology of nothing but momentary particulars. It was the single most-attacked Buddhist doctrine in classical India — the [[philosophy-mimamsa-purva-ritual-metaphysics|Mīmāṃsaka]] Kumārila Bhaṭṭa devoted the *Apohavāda* chapter of his *Ślokavārttika* to refuting it (how can pure negation be cognised, or ground positive experience, without a prior positive concept?), and the Naiyāyikas pressed the same charge. Later Buddhists — Śāntarakṣita, Jñānaśrīmitra, Ratnakīrti — revised apoha into subtler "positive-and-negative" versions in reply.

## Svasaṃvedana: cognition's self-awareness

The school's third signature doctrine is **svasaṃvedana** (*svasaṃvitti*, "reflexive awareness" or "self-cognition"): every cognition, in the very act of cognising its object, also **cognises itself**. Awareness is *self-luminous* (*svaprakāśa*) — it does not need a second, higher cognition to be known, on pain of regress. Svasaṃvedana grounds our immediate knowledge of our own mental states and, on the highest (Yogācāra) level of Dharmakīrti's analysis, becomes the point at which external objects drop away entirely and only self-aware cognition remains. It is the epistemological hinge between the pramāṇa school and its Yogācāra parent, and one of the most-debated theses in Buddhist philosophy: the Prāsaṅgika Madhyamaka of Candrakīrti rejects it, while Śāntarakṣita's Yogācāra-Madhyamaka synthesis defends it.

## The school and its texts

Dignāga's *Pramāṇasamuccaya* founds the tradition; Dharmakīrti's **seven treatises** (*sapta-grantha*) — headed by the *Pramāṇavārttika*, the *Pramāṇaviniścaya*, and the compact *Nyāyabindu* — become its classics. A dense commentarial lineage follows: **Devendrabuddhi** and **Śākyabuddhi**, then **Dharmottara** (whose *Nyāyabinduṭīkā* is the standard gloss), **Prajñākaragupta** (*Pramāṇavārttikālaṅkāra*), and the 10th–11th-century masters **Jñānaśrīmitra** and **Ratnakīrti**, who defend the system against a matured Nyāya. With the destruction of Nālandā and Vikramaśīla around 1200, the living Indian tradition ends — but by then it had been carried to **Tibet**, where, translated by rNgog Blo-ldan-shes-rab and systematised by **Sa-skya Paṇḍita** in the *Tshad ma rigs pa'i gter* ("Treasury of Reasoning"), it became *tshad ma*, the epistemology-and-logic core of the monastic curriculum, studied and debated to this day.

## Cross-tradition significance

**The Nyāya debate (documented intra-Indian polemic).** The primary comparative fact about pramāṇavāda is that it is one half of a **named, documented, centuries-long controversy** with [[philosophy-nyaya-vaisheshika-realism|Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika]]. This is not loose resemblance but explicit mutual refutation: Uddyotakara's *Nyāyavārttika* answers Dignāga by name; Dharmakīrti answers the Naiyāyikas; Vācaspati Miśra, Jayanta and Udayana answer Dharmakīrti; the Buddhists answer again. The two schools divide on every axis — **two pramāṇas vs four**, **apoha-nominalism vs real universals**, **efficacy-tested (extrinsic) validity vs intrinsic validity** — and their exchange constitutes the classical age of Indian epistemology.

**Apoha and the problem of universals (structural convergence).** The apoha theory addresses the same problem — *what, if anything, do general terms name?* — that drives the Western **realism–nominalism** debate over universals, from Porphyry and Boethius through Abelard to Ockham's *nominalism*. The parallel is genuine and much-discussed (the *Apoha* volume of 2011 sets Dignāga beside Frege, prototype theory, and modern semantics), but it is a **typological convergence**, not a case of transmission: two independent traditions reaching for a non-realist account of meaning. The comparison is illuminating precisely because the Buddhist route — *meaning by exclusion* — has no exact Western counterpart.

**Svasaṃvedana and theories of consciousness (convergence).** The doctrine that awareness is intrinsically self-aware runs parallel to Brentano's "inner perception," to **self-representational** and **higher-order** theories in contemporary philosophy of mind, and to phenomenological accounts of pre-reflective self-consciousness; Christian Coseru (*Perceiving Reality*, 2012) and others draw the comparison in detail. Again the relation is structural convergence and mutual illumination, with no historical contact.

**Transmission to Tibet (documented descent).** In contrast to these convergences, the passage of the entire system into Tibetan *tshad ma* is **documented intellectual transmission** — translated texts, named translators, an unbroken commentarial and debating tradition — and it is there that the school remains most fully alive.

## Disputes

**Realist or idealist?** Whether Dignāga and Dharmakīrti are ultimately **external realists** (the Sautrāntika reading: real, if imperceptible, external particulars represented in cognition) or **idealists** (the Yogācāra reading: only self-aware cognition) is the central interpretive question. Dunne's "sliding scale of analysis" (2004) reads Dharmakīrti as arguing on **both** levels, the lower corrected by the higher; Dreyfus (1997) shows how the Tibetan schools split over exactly this — the Sa-skya tradition reading svalakṣaṇa and universals anti-realistically, the Ge-luk reading them realistically.

**Dharmakīrti's dates.** Frauwallner (1961) placed Dharmakīrti c. 600–660; the dating remains contested, with arguments (from his relation to Kumārila Bhaṭṭa and to Bhāviveka) for pushing him earlier into the mid-6th century. The order of the Kumārila–Dharmakīrti exchange — who answered whom — is not fully settled.

**Can apoha work?** Whether a purely negative, exclusion-based semantics can actually ground positive cognition without covertly presupposing a positive concept was the standing objection of Kumārila and the Naiyāyikas, and drove the successive Buddhist reformulations (negative apoha → "positive-and-negative" apoha). The coherence of the theory is still argued in the modern literature (Siderits, Tillemans & Chakrabarti 2011).
