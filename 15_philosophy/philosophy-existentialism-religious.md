# Religious Existentialism

The 19th-20th-c. existentialist tradition's religious wing — sharply distinct from the atheist existentialism of Sartre and Camus by retaining the central existentialist commitments (priority of existence over essence; anxiety, freedom, and authenticity as constitutive; rejection of system-philosophy as evasion of concrete existence) while affirming the religious dimension of human existence as primary. Founded by Søren Kierkegaard (1813-1855) in 1840s Copenhagen; developed by Gabriel Marcel (Catholic), Martin Buber (Jewish), Nikolai Berdyaev (Russian Orthodox), Karl Jaspers, and Paul Tillich (Protestant) in the early- and mid-20th c. (Kaufmann 1956; Macquarrie 1972).

## Kierkegaard: the leap of faith

Kierkegaard's *Either/Or* (1843) sets up the choice between the *aesthetic* and the *ethical* stages of existence; *Fear and Trembling* (1843) introduces the *religious* stage via Abraham's binding of Isaac as the *teleological suspension of the ethical* — the moment Abraham obeys God's command to sacrifice his son, violating universal ethical norms in the name of an absolute relation to the Absolute. The act is incomprehensible to universal reason; Abraham is a "knight of faith" precisely because he *cannot* explain his obedience. The *leap of faith* (*springet*) — the existential commitment that cannot be justified by prior rational argument — is the school's signature concept. *Sickness Unto Death* (1849) develops the doctrine of *despair* as the existential condition before God: every form of inauthentic self-relation is a form of despair, and only the self-grounded-in-God escapes it.

## Marcel: the I-Thou and the mystery of being

Marcel's *Journal métaphysique* (1927) and *Le Mystère de l'être* (1951) develop the distinction between *problem* (something I can stand outside of and solve technically) and *mystery* (something in which I am inescapably implicated and which cannot be solved but only entered). The religious dimension belongs to mystery. Marcel's *présence* — the qualitative immediacy of personal encounter — anticipates Buber's *I-Thou* and Levinas's ethics-of-the-other.

## Buber: I and Thou

Buber's *Ich und Du* (1923) distinguishes the two primary attitudes the human being can take: *I-It* (objectifying, manipulative, instrumental) and *I-Thou* (relational, mutual, present). All genuine human relations are *I-Thou*; the religious relation to God is the *eternal Thou* that every limited *Thou* points toward. Buber's articulation has had vast influence in 20th-c. Jewish and Christian theology, in pastoral theology, and in 20th-c. religious education (Paulo Freire, John Macmurray, Maurice Friedman).

## Cross-tradition resonance

Religious existentialism's emphasis on *the leap* and on the *qualitative* shift from one mode of existence to another (Kierkegaard's stages) shows structural parallel to the Buddhist *enlightenment-as-rupture* tradition — particularly Chan/Zen *kenshō* and *satori* — and to Wang Yangming's [[philosophy-neoconfucianism-wang-yangming|*liangzhi*-awakening]] doctrine. Kierkegaard's polemic against system-philosophy (Hegel as the principal target) parallels the Buddhist [[philosophy-madhyamaka|Mādhyamika]] refusal of metaphysical system-building in favor of soteriological-existential transformation.

Buber's I-Thou doctrine runs parallel to several non-Western traditions of relational ontology — particularly the Hindu *bhakti* (devotional) traditions where the relation to *Iṣṭa-devatā* is constitutive of the self, and the Sufi *ʿishq* (loving relation to the Divine Beloved). The Confucian relational anthropology — the self constituted through the *wǔlún* five relations — is a structurally adjacent position from outside the I-Thou framework. Emmanuel Levinas's ethics-of-the-Other (1961+) inherits and Judaizes both Buber's relational ontology and Husserlian phenomenology, becoming the principal 20th-c. extension of the religious-existentialist tradition into contemporary continental philosophy.

The school's anxiety about *authenticity* and *inauthenticity* — taken up secularly by Heidegger and Sartre — has a striking structural parallel in the Daoist *zhenren* (true person) and the Confucian *junzi* (exemplary person) ideals; the qualitative distinction between authentic and inauthentic existence is one of the most recurring cross-tradition philosophical concerns.
