# Legalism (Fǎjiā 法家)

The Warring States Chinese school of political philosophy that systematized statecraft as the rigorous application of impersonal law (*fǎ* 法), administrative technique (*shù* 術), and the calculated use of position-power (*shì* 勢). Legalism is the ideology that produced the brief but transformative Qin unification of China (-221 to -207) and the administrative apparatus that — despite the official Han-dynasty repudiation of the school — remained the operative theory of Chinese imperial government for two millennia.

## Principal figures

- **Shāng Yāng** (Wèi Yāng, -390 to -338): chief minister of the state of Qin under Duke Xiao; the principal *practical* legalist. His reforms (-356 and -350) transformed Qin from a peripheral western state into the most administratively efficient power in the Warring States system: nationwide household registers, collective punishment of associated households, abolition of hereditary aristocratic privilege, standardized weights and measures, the *qiānmò* land system. Executed -338 by the successor king who had been alienated by Shang Yang's enforcement of penalty against the prince during his youth.

- **Shēn Bùhài** (-400 to -337): minister in the state of Han; developer of *shù* (administrative technique) — the use of impersonal selection-and-promotion procedures + the *xíngmíng* (form-and-name) doctrine of checking actual performance against title-claims.

- **Shèn Dào** (c. -350 to -275): philosopher of *shì* (position-power) — that authority is the product of position, not personal capacity; a mediocre ruler in a powerful position can govern a state of capable individuals.

- **Hán Fēi** (-280 to -233): the principal *theoretical* legalist. A noble of the small state of Han, his synthesis of Shang Yang's *fǎ*, Shen Buhai's *shù*, and Shen Dao's *shì* is the canonical Legalist text. He died in a Qin prison after the king of Qin, having read his writings with deep admiration, was nonetheless persuaded by Han Fei's school-fellow Li Si (the future Qin chancellor) to imprison and execute him.

## The synthesis

The Legalist program rests on a sharply non-Confucian anthropology: humans are self-interested and respond to incentives + threats. Confucian appeals to *rén* (humaneness), *yì* (rightness), or moral education are wishful; the ruler who tries to govern through moral example is incompetent. Effective government requires:

- **Fǎ (法)**: published, impersonal, universal law applying equally to all (with the explicit exception of the ruler). The doctrine that *the law applies to the chief minister and the gatekeeper alike* — a principle the Han codified.
- **Shù (術)**: administrative technique, particularly the systematic checking of subordinates' performance against their nominal duties.
- **Shì (势)**: the systematic use of the structural advantages of the ruler's position — surveillance, monopoly on punishment + reward, control of agricultural surplus + military force.

## The Qin unification and its aftermath

The Qin dynasty applied Legalist doctrine in undiluted form: standardized script, standardized axle-widths, mass conscription for the Great Wall, the *fenshu kengru* book-burning of -213 and the burying of scholars. The dynasty collapsed within fifteen years of its founding. The Han dynasty repudiated Legalism as the explicit doctrine and adopted Confucianism — but preserved virtually all of the Qin's administrative-legal apparatus. The longstanding Chinese commentary that imperial Chinese government was "Confucian on the outside, Legalist on the inside" (*wài Rú nèi Fǎ* 外儒內法) is a serious historical reading.

## Cross-tradition connections

- **[[philosophy-confucianism]]**: the principal philosophical opponent; the historical synthesis is the operative doctrine of Han + post-Han imperial China.
- **[[philosophy-mohism]]**: shared rejection of Confucian moral exemplarity, opposite tone on universal benefit vs ruthless statecraft.
- **[[philosophy-daoism-philosophical]]**: complex overlap — Han Fei wrote a commentary on the Daodejing, and the Legalist + Daoist schools share the doctrine of *wu wei* (non-interference), though they interpret it very differently.
