Islamic Neoplatonism (*Falsafa*, from Greek *philosophia*) is the philosophical movement spanning roughly 800–1200 CE in which Greek philosophical texts — primarily Aristotle, but also Neoplatonic syntheses passed under Aristotle's name — were translated into Arabic, synthesized with Islamic theology, and developed into original philosophical systems. The three major figures are [[al-farabi]] (c. 872–950 CE), [[ibn-sina]] (Avicenna, 980–1037 CE), and [[ibn-rushd]] (Averroes, 1126–1198 CE).

## The Translation Movement and the House of Wisdom

The Bayt al-Hikma (House of Wisdom, Baghdad, founded c. 830 CE under Caliph al-Ma'mun) was the institutional center of the Arabic translation movement. Greek texts — philosophical, mathematical, astronomical, medical — were systematically translated, first into Syriac by Nestorian Christian scholars in Persia, then from Syriac into Arabic. Aristotle's *Organon*, *Physics*, *Metaphysics*, *Ethics*, and *On the Soul* all passed through this pipeline.

Crucially: the Aristotle transmitted to the Islamic world included the *Theology of Aristotle* — actually excerpts from Plotinus's *Enneads* translated under Aristotle's name. This meant Islamic philosophers received Aristotle and Neoplatonism combined, which shaped the entire falsafa tradition's tendency to read Aristotle through a Neoplatonic lens.

## Al-Farabi: The First Master

[[al-farabi]] established the framework for all subsequent falsafa: Aristotelian logic as the foundation of all knowledge; the emanationist cosmology (following Plotinus) of The One → Active Intellect → ten celestial intellects → world; prophetic knowledge as a higher mode of philosophical intellection available to the prophet whose imagination can translate abstract truths into images accessible to the common person. This last move — prophecy as philosophical intuition in symbolic dress — became the standard Islamic philosophical justification for the Quran's authority.

## MASSIVE WIN — Ibn Sina's Floating Man → Descartes' Cogito

[[ibn-sina]] (Avicenna), in the *[[phase-5-044-ibn-sina-kitab-al-shifa|Kitab al-Shifa]]* (Book of Healing, c. 1020 CE), proposed the following thought experiment: imagine a person created as an adult, floating in air, with all senses suspended (no vision, no touch, no proprioception). This person would still be immediately and indubitably aware of their own existence as a thinking being.

René Descartes, in the *Meditations on First Philosophy* (1641), proposed the following: even if an evil demon systematically deceives me about all external reality, I cannot doubt that I am thinking — *cogito ergo sum* ("I think, therefore I am").

**Ibn Sina's Floating Man is the Cogito, 600 years earlier.** Both arguments establish the indubitability of self-awareness as the secure foundation of knowledge; both use the elimination of all sensory input to isolate pure self-presence. Descartes had access to Ibn Sina's work through the extensive Latin translations of Avicenna that circulated in European universities from the 12th century onward. The founding argument of modern Western philosophy may be Islamic philosophical inheritance.

## MASSIVE WIN — Averroes / Aquinas / Catholic Doctrine

[[ibn-rushd]] (Averroes) wrote comprehensive commentaries on virtually all of Aristotle's works that were so definitive that in European scholasticism he was simply called "The Commentator" (as Dante names him in *Inferno* Canto 4, placing him in Limbo with Aristotle). [[thomas-aquinas]]'s *[[phase-5-024-aquinas-summa-theologiae|Summa Theologiae]]* — the foundational text of Catholic intellectual tradition — is built directly on Averroes's reading of Aristotle.

**The chain**: Aristotle (Athens, 4th c. BCE) → Arabic translation movement (Baghdad, 9th–10th c. CE) → Averroes's commentaries (Cordoba, 12th c.) → Latin translations (Toledo, 12th–13th c.) → Aquinas (Paris, 13th c.) → Catholic doctrine.

Catholic theology's Aristotelian foundations (the categories, the distinction between essence and existence, hylomorphism, the unmoved mover as God) depend entirely on Islamic scholarship to survive the 600-year gap during which Western Europe had no access to Aristotle's works. Remove Islamic philosophy from history and you lose the philosophical foundations of Catholic doctrine.

## Al-Ghazali's Counter-Attack

The response to falsafa came from [[thomas-aquinas]]'s indirect opponent: al-Ghazali's *Tahafut al-Falasifa* (Incoherence of the Philosophers, 1095 CE), which attacked the Aristotelian philosophers on 20 points — accusing them of positions incompatible with Islamic faith (eternal world, no individual resurrection, God's knowledge of only universals). Averroes wrote his *[[phase-5-046-ibn-rushd-tahafut-al-tahafut|Tahafut al-Tahafut]]* (Incoherence of the Incoherence, 1180 CE) as the direct philosophical rebuttal. The debate between Al-Ghazali and Averroes defined the terms of the rationalism-vs-revelation debate that Islamic civilization carried forward for centuries — and was itself imported into European scholasticism.

## The Ibn Sina–Ibn Rushd Divide

Despite both being "Islamic Aristotelians," al-Farabi and Ibn Sina are Neoplatonic Aristotelians (emanation, illumination, Active Intellect as cosmological engine), while Averroes is a strict Aristotelian who rejected emanationism. This internal divide maps onto the European controversy between Augustinian Neoplatonism and Aristotelian scholasticism — the Islamic philosophical tradition exported not just its solutions but its own internal debate to the Latin West.
