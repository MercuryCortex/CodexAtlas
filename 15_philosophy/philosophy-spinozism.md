# Spinozism (substance monism — Deus sive Natura)

## Identity

**Spinozism** is the metaphysical system of [[baruch-spinoza|Baruch (Benedictus) de Spinoza]] (1632–1677) and the tradition of *substance monism* it founded — the thesis that there exists exactly **one substance**, which is *God, or Nature* (*Deus sive Natura*), of which all finite things are *modes*. It is one of the boldest products of seventeenth-century Dutch rationalism and, on the standard reading, the most rigorously deductive metaphysics in the Western canon, argued *more geometrico* (in the geometric manner of Euclid) in the posthumous *Ethica, Ordine Geometrico Demonstrata* (1677). This node covers the doctrine and its tradition; the biography, works and *cherem* (excommunication) of the man are treated at the person-node [[baruch-spinoza]].

The system stands beside the other early-modern rationalisms — Cartesianism and Leibnizian monadology — but breaks with both on the decisive point: where Descartes admits thinking and extended substances and a transcendent creator, and Leibniz an infinity of monads, Spinoza admits **one** substance only, and identifies it with the whole of nature.

## Core doctrine

**One substance — *Deus sive Natura*.** A substance is that which is *in itself* and *conceived through itself* (Ethics I def3). Spinoza argues that there can be only one such thing, absolutely infinite, and that it is God (I p11, I p14: "Except God, no substance can be or be conceived"). This single substance he names indifferently **God or Nature** (*Deus sive Natura*, Ethics IV Preface) — not God *and* nature, and not God *in* nature, but their strict identity. He distinguishes *natura naturans* (nature *naturing* — substance and its attributes, the active productive order) from *natura naturata* (nature *natured* — the modes that follow from it).

**Infinite attributes; thought and extension.** The one substance has *infinitely many* attributes, each expressing its eternal essence (I def6), but the human intellect knows only **two**: *thought* (*cogitatio*) and *extension* (*extensio*) (II p1–2). Crucially, these are two *equal* expressions of the same reality — Spinoza is therefore **not** a metaphysical idealist (mind is not prior to matter) nor a materialist (matter is not prior to mind) but a *neutral* substance-monist: mind and body are "one and the same thing, expressed in two ways" (II p7 scholium), which is his *parallelist* solution to the mind–body problem.

**Modes and conatus.** Finite things — a stone, a person, a thought — are *modes*: affections of the one substance, "that which is in another and conceived through another" (I def5). Each mode strives to persevere in its own being: this striving (*conatus*) *is* the actual essence of the thing (III p6–7), and in human beings, understood under the attribute of thought, it appears as desire, the root of Spinoza's naturalistic psychology of the affects.

**Necessitarianism.** Nothing is contingent: everything follows from the divine nature with geometric necessity, "in the same way as from the nature of a triangle it follows... that its three angles are equal to two right angles" (I p17 scholium; I p29, I p33). There is no free will (II p48); freedom is redefined as acting from the necessity of one's own nature, i.e. from *adequate ideas*.

**Amor Dei intellectualis.** The *Ethica*'s summit (Part V) is the **intellectual love of God** (*amor Dei intellectualis*): the mind's adequate understanding of things "under the aspect of eternity" (*sub specie aeternitatis*) is itself a part of the infinite love with which God loves himself (V p32–36). Human blessedness (*beatitudo*) is not the reward of virtue but virtue itself (V p42) — the serene comprehension of one's place in the necessary order of nature.

## In the *Ethica* and the tradition

The doctrine is developed across Spinoza's corpus — the early *Korte Verhandeling* (Short Treatise, c. 1661), the anonymously-published and immediately-banned *Tractatus Theologico-Politicus* (1670, with its naturalistic biblical criticism and defence of free thought) — but its definitive form is the *Ethica*, composed through the 1660s–70s and published in the *Opera Posthuma* the year Spinoza died (1677). Its Euclidean architecture — Definitions, Axioms, Propositions, Demonstrations, Corollaries, Scholia — is a deliberate wager that metaphysics and ethics, stated as theorems, can compel rational assent the way geometry does.

Condemned in its own century as atheism (the word *Spinozist* was for a century a synonym for *atheist*), the system nonetheless became, in Jonathan Israel's account (2001), the clandestine engine of the **radical Enlightenment**. Its public rehabilitation came through the *Pantheismusstreit* of 1785 and the Romantic-Idealist generation that followed.

## Cross-tradition significance

Spinozism sits at a junction where **documented transmission** and **independent convergence** must be told apart with care.

**Documented transmission (downstream, secure).** The line into German Idealism is one of the best-attested in modern philosophy. F. H. Jacobi's *Über die Lehre des Spinoza* (1785), by reporting Lessing's confession of Spinozism, ignited the *Pantheismusstreit* that made Spinoza unavoidable for Goethe (who called himself "a decided Spinozist"), Herder, Schelling and Hegel. Hegel's verdict — that "you are either a Spinozist or not a philosopher at all" (*Lectures on the History of Philosophy*) — registers the debt: the Idealist **Absolute** that unfolds itself through finite modes is a self-consciously post-Spinozist figure ([[philosophy-german-idealism]]; Beiser 1987). Downstream again, the chain reaches nineteenth-century scientific naturalism and Einstein's public creed — "I believe in Spinoza's God, who reveals himself in the lawful harmony of the world" (1929) — and Deleuze's twentieth-century revival. These are inheritances carried by named texts, not resemblances.

**Documented transmission (upstream).** The substance-monist frame is itself *received*: through the medieval Jewish Neoplatonist tradition (Ibn Gabirol's *Fons Vitae*, the kabbalistic *Ein Sof*) and Spinoza's acknowledged predecessor [[giordano-bruno|Giordano Bruno]] (God = the infinite universe), the "one substance with infinite attributes" descends from [[philosophy-neoplatonism|Plotinian]] metaphysics. Spinoza's decisive innovation is to **flatten the emanationist hierarchy into pure immanence** (Yovel 1989): no chain of hypostases, no world beneath God — the world simply *is* God's necessary self-expression.

**Independent convergence (structural, contested).** The pantheist-monist alignment with **Advaita Vedānta** (*brahman* as the sole reality, the world its dependent expression) and with the **Dao** (the one nameless source of the ten-thousand things) is the classic cross-civilisational parallel — and it is **convergence, not contact**. Spinoza read no Sanskrit and no Chinese; there was no textual channel between seventeenth-century Amsterdam and the Upaniṣadic or Daoist schools. The honest verdict is that two (indeed three) traditions reached a structurally homologous move — reality is ultimately one, and the manifold is its modes/appearances — by independent routes (rational deduction in Spinoza's case, contemplative realisation in the others). The parallel must further be *qualified*: Spinoza makes an **ontological substance-claim** (all is literally one substance), whereas the [[non-duality]] the hub tracks is typically a *soteriological-experiential* recognition that does **not** assert a single substance (the hub explicitly files "Monism... (Spinoza, Parmenides)" as a *neighbouring but distinct* type; Loy 1988). Spinozism is thus the near-neighbour of non-duality, not an instance of it — a substance-monist convergence with Advaita and the Dao rather than a shared mysticism.

## Disputes

**Pantheism vs. *Deus sive Natura*.** Whether Spinoza is best called a *pantheist* (God = the world) or something subtler turns on the substance–attribute–mode reading. Most current scholarship (Della Rocca 2008; Garrett; Curley) reads him as strictly identifying God and Nature; a minority reads the infinite substance as exceeding the totality of its modes, closer to *panentheism*. Melamed (2013) defends the priority of substance over modes without softening the identity.

**Idealist, materialist, or neutral?** Because thought and extension are *equal* attributes, Spinoza resists both the idealist and the materialist label; the standard reading (Nadler 2006) is a *neutral monism* with mind–body parallelism — which is exactly why the Atlas does **not** file Spinozism under metaphysical idealism despite its surface "all is one" affinity.

**Continuity with Jewish thought.** Whether Spinoza is *post*-Jewish (Strauss, Yovel) or retains substantial Jewish-rationalist content from [[maimonides]] and the kabbalah (Wolfson, Melamed) is contested — the same dispute recorded at the person-node.
