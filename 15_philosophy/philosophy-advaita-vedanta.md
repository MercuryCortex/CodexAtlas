Advaita Vedanta ("Non-Dual Vedanta") is the philosophical school systematized by [[shankara]] (Adi Shankaracharya, 788–820 CE) in his commentaries on the Brahma Sutras (*[[phase-5-005-shankara-brahma-sutra-bhasya]]*), the principal Upanishads, and the Bhagavad Gita. Advaita = "not-two": there is only one reality (Brahman), and the individual self (Atman) is identical with it.

## Core Doctrine

**The identity formula**: *Aham Brahmasmi* ("I am Brahman"), *Tat tvam asi* ("That art thou," Chandogya Upanishad 6.8–16), *Prajnanam Brahma* ("Consciousness is Brahman"). These *mahavakyas* (great sayings) articulate the core claim: the individual consciousness (Atman) is not a fragment of Brahman but is ontologically identical with it. Apparent multiplicity is **Maya** — not illusion in the trivial sense ("the world doesn't exist") but superimposition (adhyasa): we project the categories of multiplicity, limitation, and selfhood onto a reality that is undivided.

The Vedantic method is sravanam (hearing the scriptures), mananam (rational reflection on their meaning), and nididhyasanam (sustained meditative inquiry until the identity is known directly, not merely believed).

**Two levels of Brahman**: Nirguna Brahman (Brahman without qualities — ultimate reality as it is in itself, beyond predication) and Saguna Brahman (Brahman with qualities — the personal God, Ishvara, worshipped through devotion). Both are Brahman; Saguna is Brahman seen from within the conventional frame of Maya.

## Cross-Tradition Connections

### MASSIVE WIN — Advaita and Neoplatonism: Same Destination, Opposite Origins

Neoplatonism (Greek rational ascent): through philosophical contemplation, the soul recognizes its identity with The One; the One is not personal but is absolute unity.

Advaita Vedanta (Upanishadic revelation + rational systematization): through inquiry into scriptural testimony and meditative investigation, the individual self recognizes its identity with Brahman; the absolute is not personal (Nirguna) but is pure consciousness.

**The destination is identical**: the individual is not separate from ultimate reality — the appearance of separation is the problem; its dissolution through correct knowing is the solution. Shankara and Plotinus arrive at the same conclusion from diametrically opposite methodological starting points: one from Greek rationalism, one from Vedic revelation.

### Advaita and al-Hallaj's "Ana'l-Haqq"

[[al-hallaj]] (858–922 CE) was executed for the mystical proclamation "Ana'l-Haqq" — "I am the Truth/Real" (al-Haqq is a name of God). This is the Sufi equivalent of *Aham Brahmasmi*: the mystic who has achieved fana (annihilation of the ego-self in God) speaks from the station of identity, not as a claim of the individual ego but as a recognition that only the Real exists. The execution of al-Hallaj for this statement parallels the institutional hostility Shankara faced from Buddhist opponents — both were charged with collapsing the boundary between creature and creator/ultimate reality in ways their respective orthodox establishments found threatening.

### The Pracchanna-Bauddha Charge

Shankara's critics (particularly Ramanuja, who developed Vishishtadvaita) called him a *pracchanna-bauddha* — a "crypto-Buddhist." The accusation had real philosophical weight: Shankara absorbed Buddhist epistemology (especially Dignaga's theory of perception and inference), Buddhist logic (the Nyaya method), and Buddhist soteriological urgency (the centrality of direct experience over ritual) while rejecting Buddhist metaphysics (no-self, no permanent Atman). The result was a system that looked Buddhist from the outside while insisting on the Atman that Buddhism denied. This appropriation drove Buddhism out of India more effectively than persecution — Advaita gave Hindus all the philosophical rigor of Buddhism without conceding its metaphysical core.

### Maya and the Epistemology of Illusion

Shankara's doctrine of adhyasa (superimposition): we project the qualities of one thing onto another — just as we mistake a rope for a snake in dim light, we mistake the multiplicity of the world for ultimate reality. Compare to:
- Plato's cave allegory: the prisoners mistake shadows for reality
- Descartes' evil demon: systematic misperception of the real
- Buddhist dependent origination: we misperceive phenomena as having inherent existence

All four propose that ordinary perception is structurally deceptive, and that liberation/truth requires a correction of the perceptual framework, not merely better information within the existing framework.
