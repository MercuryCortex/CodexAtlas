# Jaina darśana

The systematic philosophy of Jainism — a metaphysical **realism**, an ontological **pluralism**, and a substance **dualism** of *jīva* (the living, conscious) and *ajīva* (the non-living), organised around a distinctively materialist theory of karma and a thoroughgoing perspectival theory of truth. It is one of the two surviving *nāstika* (Veda-rejecting) *śramaṇa* darśanas — the heterodox schools that, alongside [[philosophy-carvaka|Cārvāka]] and the vanished [[philosophy-ajivika-determinism|Ājīvikas]] and the Buddhist schools ([[philosophy-abhidharma|Abhidharma]], [[philosophy-madhyamaka|Madhyamaka]], [[philosophy-yogacara|Yogācāra]]), reject the authority of the Veda and the āstika presupposition of a creator or an impersonal Brahman. Where the [[philosophy-carvaka|Cārvāka]] materialists denied the soul outright and the [[philosophy-ajivika-determinism|Ājīvikas]] denied the efficacy of action, the Jains built among the most emphatically soul-affirming and effort-affirming of the heterodox systems. Its founding teacher is the historically attested [[mahavira|Mahāvīra]] (c. -500), but its philosophy is a scholastic construction reaching from Umāsvāti's *Tattvārtha-sūtra* (c. 2nd–5th c. CE) through the medieval logicians (Dundas 2002; Jaini 1979).

## Core doctrines

- **Jīva / ajīva — the fundamental dualism.** Reality divides exhaustively into the *living* (*jīva* — souls, of which there is an infinite plurality) and the *non-living* (*ajīva*). Each *jīva* is eternal, uncreated, individually distinct, and intrinsically endowed with infinite knowledge, perception, energy, and bliss (*ananta-catuṣṭaya*); its worldly condition is a *masking* of these innate powers, never their absence.
- **The five astikāyas (extended substances) and six dravyas.** The *ajīva* is analysed into substances alongside *jīva*: **pudgala** (matter), **dharma** (the medium that makes motion possible), **adharma** (the medium that makes rest possible), and **ākāśa** (space) — these five, with *jīva*, being the *astikāyas* ("extended bodies"); **kāla** (time) is added as the sixth *dravya*. The positing of *dharma* and *adharma* as impersonal media enabling and stabilising motion and rest is a distinctive Jain contribution with no close analogue in the other darśanas — a proto-field ontology.
- **Jaina atomism.** *Pudgala* (matter) is atomic: indivisible *paramāṇu*s combine into aggregates (*skandha*), and the sensible world, sound, and even karmic particles are all forms of *pudgala*. This aligns the school with the atomism of [[philosophy-nyaya-vaisheshika-realism|Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika]] and, at a distance, [[philosophy-atomism-greek|Greek atomism]].
- **Karma as subtle matter.** The school's signature doctrine (developed at length in the theme node [[jain-karma]]): karma is not a moral bookkeeping or a causal law but *literal fine matter* (*karma-pudgala*) that flows into (*āsrava*) and binds (*bandha*) the soul, colouring it (the *leśyā* doctrine) and obscuring its innate omniscience. Because binding follows from any disturbance of life, even unintentional harm binds — grounding the absolute *ahiṃsā* ethic.
- **Anekāntavāda, nayavāda, syādvāda.** The Jain epistemology of **non-absolutism** (developed in the theme node [[anekantavada]]): reality is many-sided (*anekānta*); every assertion is made from a partial standpoint (*naya*) and must be qualified by *syāt* ("in some respect"); the full analysis yields the seven-fold conditional predication (*saptabhaṅgī*: *is / is not / is-and-is-not / inexpressible /* and their combinations). This is the epistemic counterpart of the *utpāda-vyaya-dhrauvya* metaphysics — every real *is*, *is not*, and *persists* under different aspects, so no one-sided (*ekānta*) description can be complete.
- **The path and kevala-jñāna.** Liberation requires **saṃvara** (stopping fresh karmic inflow) and **nirjarā** (burning off accumulated karma through austerity), advancing up the fourteen *guṇasthāna* (stages of purity). When knowledge-obscuring karma is fully shed, the soul manifests **kevala-jñāna** — unconditioned, omniscient, all-at-once cognition (the state of a *kevalin* / *Tīrthaṅkara*). The fully liberated soul (*siddha*), now weightless and pure, rises to the apex of the universe (*siddha-śilā*) and abides there forever.

## In the classical texts

The philosophy is first stated as a system in **Umāsvāti's *Tattvārtha-sūtra*** (c. 2nd–5th c. CE) — the single work revered by both the Digambara and Śvetāmbara sects — whose opening aphorism defines the path as right faith, knowledge, and conduct, and whose ten chapters lay out the *tattvas*, the *dravyas*, the karma theory, and the stages to liberation. The Digambara mystical tradition runs through **Kundakunda's *Samayasāra*, *Pravacanasāra*, and *Niyamasāra*** (traditionally 2nd c., dated by most scholars to c. 8th c.), with their two-truths framework of *niścaya-naya* (the standpoint of the pure soul) and *vyavahāra-naya* (the conventional standpoint). Jain **logic and epistemology** are built by Siddhasena Divākara (*Sanmati-tarka*, *Nyāyāvatāra*), Samantabhadra (*Āptamīmāṃsā*), and above all Akalaṅka and Vidyānanda (7th–9th c.), who defended *syādvāda* against Buddhist and Brahmanical logicians; Māṇikyanandi's *Parīkṣāmukha* becomes the standard manual. Haribhadra's ***Ṣaḍdarśanasamuccaya*** (8th c.) and later Jain doxographies survey all six schools — and, like the Buddhist and Vedāntic doxographies, preserve much of what we know of the vanished materialist and determinist rivals. The polymath **Hemacandra** (12th c.) systematises the whole in the Śvetāmbara west.

## Cross-tradition significance

The Jaina darśana occupies a set of distinctive comparative slots.

**Against Buddhism — the jīva/anātman axis.** Jainism and Buddhism are the two continuously living *śramaṇa* traditions, and their disagreement is the cleanest self/no-self contrast in world thought: the Jaina permanent, individually real *jīva* against the Buddhist [[philosophy-abhidharma|Abhidharma]] *anātman*; Jaina karma-as-matter (binding even without intention) against Buddhist karma-as-*cetanā* (volition, so intention is decisive). The dispute is documented from within Jain scripture (the *Sūtrakṛtāṅga*) and is one of the earliest recorded philosophical debates anywhere. On the epistemic side, Jaina *anekāntavāda* and Nāgārjuna's [[philosophy-madhyamaka|Madhyamaka]] *śūnyatā* are twin anti-absolutisms that ran in opposite directions — additive versus dissolving — and attacked each other precisely over that difference (Matilal 1981; Ganeri 2001). These are structural parallels and live polemics within a shared milieu, **not** relations of transmission.

**Against Sāṃkhya — two pluralist soul-metaphysics.** With [[philosophy-samkhya|Sāṃkhya]] the Jains share a plurality of eternal conscious selves and a bondage-through-matter / liberation-through-disentanglement structure; but the Sāṃkhya *puruṣa* is a pure inactive *witness* that is never truly touched, whereas the Jaina *jīva* is an active agent literally weighted by matter it attracts — so Jain liberation is a genuine physical purification rather than a mere recognition of pre-existing separateness.

**The soul-immortality slot.** Within the cross-tradition study of [[soul-immortality|soul immortality]], Jainism supplies the maximal *jīvavāda* — an infinite plurality of eternal, uncreated, intrinsically omniscient souls — the direct antithesis of the *anātman* option carried in that theme and a soul-metaphysics more robust even than the Platonic or Upaniṣadic. Its karma-as-matter doctrine also generates the "luminous soul weighed down by heavy matter" structure that maps onto the Gnostic *pneuma*-in-*hylē* drama (developed in [[jain-karma]]) — a structural convergence, with the Jain difference that the matter is *self-attracted* rather than imposed by external archons.

**Syādvāda and non-classical logic.** Modern logicians (from J.B.S. Haldane onward, and in the Matilal–Ganeri–Priest discussions) have asked whether the seven-fold *saptabhaṅgī* anticipates a many-valued or paraconsistent logic. The responsible reading (Matilal 1981) treats it as a **contextual / perspectival** qualification of assertion rather than a formal system that literally asserts contradictions — a caution against over-eager formalisation.

## Disputes

- **Digambara vs. Śvetāmbara.** The two sects divide over the authenticity of the Śvetāmbara Āgama canon, over whether the omniscient *kevalin* still takes food, and over *strī-mokṣa* (whether a woman can attain liberation in her present body — Digambaras deny it, since their ideal ascetic is nude and, they hold, a woman cannot practise total *aparigraha*). Umāsvāti's *Tattvārtha* is the shared ground across the divide.
- **The date and authority of Kundakunda.** Traditionally placed in the 2nd c. CE, Kundakunda is dated by most modern scholars to around the 8th c.; his *niścaya/vyavahāra* two-truths reading of Jain metaphysics is central to Digambara mysticism but its antiquity is contested (Dundas 2002).
- **Is anekāntavāda coherent?** Buddhist logicians (Dharmakīrti) and the Advaitin Śaṅkara (*Brahma-sūtra-bhāṣya* 2.2.33–36) pressed the charge that *syādvāda* asserts contradictions and so is self-refuting. Jain thinkers (Akalaṅka, Vidyānanda, and in modern terms Matilal) reply that the *syāt* operator relativises each predication to an aspect, so no genuine contradiction arises. Whether the reply fully succeeds is a standing question in the philosophy of the *saptabhaṅgī*.
