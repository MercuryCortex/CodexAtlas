# American Pragmatism

The principal indigenous American philosophical tradition, originating in the 1870s "Metaphysical Club" of Cambridge, Massachusetts (Peirce, James, Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr., Chauncey Wright) and articulated in its mature form by William James (1842-1910) and John Dewey (1859-1952). Pragmatism's core thesis: the *meaning* of a concept is the practical difference its truth would make to experience (Peirce's "pragmatic maxim"); the *truth* of a belief is its workability in inquiry and action (James, Dewey). Applied to religion, pragmatism produces both an empirically rigorous *psychology of religion* (James) and a *religious naturalism* (Dewey) that treats religion as a function of experience rather than as a special metaphysical domain (Putnam 1995; Menand 2001).

## William James's psychology of religion

James's *Varieties of Religious Experience* (1902, the Gifford Lectures of 1901-1902) is the founding work of the modern academic study of religion. Methodologically: James brackets the *truth* of metaphysical claims and examines the *experience* of religious states empirically. Substantively: he identifies the *four marks* of mystical experience (ineffability, noetic quality, transiency, passivity); distinguishes the *healthy-minded* religion (Whitman, Emerson, Liberal Protestantism) from the *sick soul* religion (Augustine, Bunyan, Tolstoy) and argues both have epistemic dignity; and concludes — in his "Postscript" — with a *piecemeal supernaturalism* that defends the cognitive value of religious experience without endorsing any particular theology.

## John Dewey's religious naturalism

Dewey's *A Common Faith* (1934, the Terry Lectures) distinguishes *religion* (institutional, doctrinal, supernaturalist) from *the religious* — the qualitative experience of relating to encompassing values and to the natural-social whole. The religious can be cultivated naturalistically: *God* on Dewey's reading is "the unity of all ideal ends arousing us to desire and actions". Dewey is not anti-religious but anti-supernaturalist; he saves the function of religion for a naturalist age. *Experience and Nature* (1925) gives the metaphysical framework: experience is not a private mental affair but the transactional relation between organism and environment.

## Cross-tradition resonance

James's *Varieties* is the single most-cited source in 20th-c. comparative religion and the *psychology of religion* sub-discipline (Stace, Forman, Hood, Newberg all build on James). The *four marks* of mystical experience are still the analytic standard for cross-tradition mysticism studies. James's deliberate empirical-bracketing of metaphysical truth — taking the *experience* seriously without endorsing the *theology* — founds what becomes [philosophy-phenomenology-of-religion|phenomenology of religion] (Otto, van der Leeuw) and the academic religious-studies discipline as such.

Dewey's religious naturalism runs structurally parallel to the Buddhist [[philosophy-madhyamaka|Mādhyamika]] refusal of metaphysical commitment combined with affirmation of practical-ethical reality; to the Roman [[philosophy-stoicism-roman|Stoic]] *amor fati* recovery of the religious dimension of natural-cosmic order; and to the Confucian ([[philosophy-confucianism]]) treatment of *tian* (heaven) and *li* (ritual) as immanent rather than transcendent realities. The 20th-c. neo-pragmatists Hilary Putnam, Cornel West, Jeffrey Stout extend the program to religious ethics and democratic theory. Pragmatism's influence on contemporary religious naturalism (Stuart Kauffman, Ursula Goodenough, Loyal Rue) and on the "spiritual but not religious" framing of 21st-c. American religiosity is direct.
