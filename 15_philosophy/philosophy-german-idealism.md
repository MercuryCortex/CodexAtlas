# German Idealism

The half-century philosophical movement opened by Kant's *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781) and effectively closed by Hegel's death (1831), comprising the systematic projects of Immanuel Kant (1724-1804), Johann Gottlieb Fichte (1762-1814), Friedrich Wilhelm Joseph Schelling (1775-1854), and Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel (1770-1831). The school takes its name from the post-Kantian conviction that the structure of reality is, in some sense, *ideal* — knowable only through the categories of self-conscious thought and at the limit identical with the activity of self-conscious thought. It is the most ambitious post-Christian metaphysical synthesis in Western philosophy and the principal philosophical source-bed for Marx, Kierkegaard, the existentialists, Russian religious philosophy, 20th-c. Continental theology, and the contemporary Pittsburgh-school revival.

## Kant: the transcendental opening

Kant's *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781, 2nd ed. 1787) inaugurates the movement by drawing what is later called the *transcendental* distinction: between *phenomena* (things as they appear under the structuring activity of the cognitive subject — space, time, the twelve categories) and *noumena* (things-in-themselves, of which we can know nothing in principle). The cognitive subject is no longer a passive mirror of given reality; the subject's *a priori* structures constitute the form of any possible experience. The "Copernican revolution" of the first *Critique* — that objects must conform to the structure of cognition, not the reverse — is the move on which everything subsequent in the school depends.

The *Critique of Practical Reason* (1788) grounds morality in the autonomous self-legislation of reason (the categorical imperative); the *Critique of the Power of Judgment* (1790) treats aesthetic experience and biological organism as domains where the structures of phenomenal nature display purposive form. The three *Critiques* together set the agenda the successors inherit.

## Fichte: the *Wissenschaftslehre*

Fichte (most influentially the 1794-95 *Wissenschaftslehre* and its many revisions) argues that the Kantian distinction is unstable: the *thing-in-itself* is either inside or outside the structure of cognition — if inside, it isn't a *thing-in-itself*; if outside, it can't be cognized, named, or posited. Fichte's solution is to dissolve the noumenon entirely and ground the entire phenomenal world in the activity of the *I* (the absolute self), which posits itself, posits the not-I as its limit, and progressively works through the not-I in moral and cognitive activity. This is the most *subjectivist* moment in the school and the principal target of the later Schelling/Hegel reframings.

## Schelling: *Naturphilosophie* and the absolute

Schelling, in his prolific 1790s-1820s output, develops in stages: an early Fichtean phase; a *Naturphilosophie* (philosophy of nature) phase in which nature is the visible expression of an underlying productive activity isomorphic with the self-conscious I; an identity-philosophy phase in which subject and object are identical in an *Absolute* knowable only through intellectual intuition; and a late *positive philosophy* of revelation and mythology in which the contingency of existence is no longer derivable from logical necessity. Schelling's late thought is the most explicit channel through which the [[jakob-boehme|Boehmian]] theosophical tradition — *Ungrund*, divine self-realization through opposition, the dark ground in God — enters academic philosophy.

## Hegel: the dialectical system

Hegel's *Phenomenology of Spirit* (1807) and the mature *Science of Logic* (1812-16) and *Encyclopedia* (1817, 1827, 1830) present the most systematic version of the movement's central claim: that the structure of reality is identical with the self-development of *Geist* (Spirit, Mind) through a *dialectical* process in which each determination produces, by its own internal logic, the contradiction that drives it forward to a more adequate determination. The end-state is the Absolute knowing itself as the totality of the process it has traversed — at once the logical structure of being, the structure of the natural world, and the structure of human history (as the progressive self-actualization of freedom).

## The four divisions of Hegel's mature system

- *Logic* — the categorical structure of pure thought (being → essence → concept).
- *Philosophy of Nature* — the externalization of the logical idea in spatial-material form.
- *Philosophy of Spirit (subjective)* — the structures of individual consciousness, psychology, and cognition.
- *Philosophy of Spirit (objective + absolute)* — the structures of right, morality, ethical life (*Sittlichkeit*: family, civil society, state), and ultimately art, religion, and philosophy as the highest forms of Spirit's self-knowing.

## Cross-tradition connections

### MASSIVE WIN candidate — Boehme → Hegel transmission

Hegel himself wrote that [[jakob-boehme]] is "the first German philosopher" and devoted a substantial section of his *Lectures on the History of Philosophy* to Boehme's *Ungrund*, divine self-opposition, and theogonic doctrine of the seven *Eigenschaften*. Magee (2001, *Hegel and the Hermetic Tradition*) argues that the entire dialectical-Trinitarian-theogonic shape of Hegel's mature system is a secularized + philosophized Boehmian theosophy: the Absolute that goes out of itself, encounters itself as other, and returns to itself enriched is recognizably the Lutheran-theosophical pattern of God's self-realization through self-opposition. Schelling's debt to Boehme is even more direct and explicit (the 1809 *Freiheitsschrift* names Boehme). German Idealism is, on this reading, the academic-philosophical absorption of a heterodox Protestant theosophical tradition.

### Lurianic Kabbalah parallel

The Lurianic [[tzimtzum]] (divine self-contraction creating the space for the world), *shevirat ha-kelim* (the breaking of the vessels), and *tikkun* (the restoration through human action) form a structurally similar three-moment cosmic-dialectical pattern. The German-Idealist absolute that contracts to make room for finite reality, fragments in finite reality, and is restored in the historical process of Spirit's self-knowing is recognizably the same three-moment shape. The historical genealogy runs through Boehme's reading of Christian Kabbalah and through the wider Renaissance hermetic-Kabbalistic tradition; Christoph Schulte and others have documented direct Lurianic engagement in Schelling and in the Russian sophiological reception.

### Advaita Vedānta parallel

Hegel's Absolute knowing itself through the totality of the process is structurally parallel to [[philosophy-advaita-vedanta|Advaita Vedānta]]'s brahman knowing itself through the *līlā* of finite manifestation. Schopenhauer (a contemporary anti-idealist) drew the parallel explicitly with reference to the Upaniṣads. The convergence is one of the most-discussed comparative-philosophy questions of the 19th-20th c. and is independently striking even before any genealogical claim is made.

### The Marxian inversion

Marx's "inversion" of Hegel — keeping the dialectical structure but replacing Spirit with material productive forces as the moving subject of history — is the single most-consequential subsequent development. Without the Hegelian dialectical apparatus there is no Marxism in its received form. The school is thus the philosophical-genealogical ancestor of both 20th-c. existentialism (via Kierkegaard's anti-Hegel reaction) and 20th-c. Marxism (via Marx's Hegel-inversion).

### Continuing institutional presence

The Pittsburgh-school revival (Robert Brandom, John McDowell, the late Wilfrid Sellars) has rehabilitated Hegel as a philosopher of inferential normativity within Anglophone analytic philosophy. The Russian sophiological tradition (Solovyov, Bulgakov, Berdyaev) is the principal 20th-c. theological continuation. The Frankfurt School and contemporary Critical Theory descend through Marx.
