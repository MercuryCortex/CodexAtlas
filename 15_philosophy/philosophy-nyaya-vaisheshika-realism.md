# Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika

The merged classical Indian school of *atomistic realism + epistemological-logical inquiry* — the principal articulation of metaphysical realism and one of the most sophisticated formal-logical traditions in pre-modern world philosophy. The Vaiśeṣika component (Kaṇāda, c. -150) develops a categorial ontology built on six *padārthas* (categories of being) — substance, quality, motion, universal, particular, inherence — and a physical atomism: material reality consists of indivisible atoms (*paramāṇus*) of four kinds (earth, water, fire, air) that combine to form macroscopic objects. The Nyāya component (Akṣapāda, c. 200 CE) develops the epistemological-logical side: *pramāṇa* theory (means of knowledge — perception, inference, comparison, testimony) and a sophisticated theory of *anumāna* (inference) (Matilal 1985; Halbfass 1992).

## Vaiśeṣika atomism

Kaṇāda's *Vaiśeṣika-sūtra* (c. -150) proposes that material objects ultimately decompose into indivisible *paramāṇus* (atoms) of four substance-kinds. Atoms are partless, eternal, and individually imperceptible; they combine in pairs (*dvyaṇuka*) and then triads of pairs (*tryaṇuka*) — the *tryaṇuka* being the smallest perceptible entity. The cosmogonic schedule: at the start of each cosmic cycle, atoms begin combining under the supervisory will of *Īśvara* (added in the post-classical theistic Vaiśeṣika); at the end of the cycle they dissociate and rest until the next combination. The atomism is not materialist in the modern sense — there are also five non-material substances (ether, time, space, soul, mind) in the nine-substance ontology.

## Nyāya epistemology and the five-membered syllogism

Akṣapāda's *Nyāya-sūtra* articulates the Indian-classical theory of inference. The standard five-membered Indian syllogism (*pañcāvayava*):

1. *Pratijñā* (thesis): "The mountain has fire."
2. *Hetu* (reason): "Because it has smoke."
3. *Udāharaṇa* (example): "Wherever there is smoke there is fire, as in the kitchen."
4. *Upanaya* (application): "And there is smoke on the mountain."
5. *Nigamana* (conclusion): "Therefore the mountain has fire."

The school developed an elaborate fallacy-theory (*hetvābhāsa*: deviating reason, contradictory reason, etc.) and a sophisticated theory of universal-instantiation (*vyāpti*).

## Navya-Nyāya (New Nyāya)

Gaṅgeśa's *Tattvacintāmaṇi* (c. 1325) initiates Navya-Nyāya — a precision-formalist refinement of Indian logic that produces an extraordinarily rigorous technical vocabulary (around 100 terms with strictly-defined formal roles) and analytic methods that some scholars (notably J. F. Staal, B. K. Matilal) have compared in sophistication to early-modern European logic. The Navya-Nyāya technical apparatus becomes the universal Sanskrit-philosophical analytic tool from the 14th c. forward, deployed by Mīmāṃsakas, Vedāntins, Vyākaraṇa-grammarians, and even Jain philosophers across school boundaries.

## Cross-tradition resonance

The Vaiśeṣika atomism predates and runs structurally parallel to the [[philosophy-atomism-greek|Greek atomism]] of Leucippus-Democritus (c. -440), with no documented direct transmission. The two schools converge on the indivisible-atom principle and divergence over the soul/non-material substances issue (Greek atomism is materialist; Vaiśeṣika is dualist). The structural convergence is one of the standard cases in comparative philosophy. Whether c. -500 to -200 Persian-Mediterranean philosophical exchange could have mediated the convergence is contested (McEvilley 2002 *The Shape of Ancient Thought* argues yes; Bronkhorst 2007 argues independent emergence).

The Navya-Nyāya formal-logical sophistication runs parallel to and precedes the late-medieval European *terministic logic* (Buridan, Ockham, late-13th c.) by roughly the same amount that the Greek *Organon* precedes Indian classical logic. Jonardon Ganeri (1999, 2001) has formalized substantial Navya-Nyāya results in modern symbolic-logical notation, demonstrating non-trivial pre-modern Indian logical results.

The Nyāya epistemology — particularly the doctrine of *śabda-pramāṇa* (verbal testimony as a valid source of knowledge irreducible to perception or inference) — runs structurally parallel to medieval Latin scholastic *fides ex auditu* arguments and to the [[philosophy-mimamsa-purva-ritual-metaphysics|Pūrva Mīmāṃsā]] doctrine of testimony. The school's Īśvara-arguments (Udayana's *Nyāyakusumāñjali*, c. 1000 CE) anticipate aspects of the [[philosophy-thomism|Thomist]] *Five Ways* — particularly cosmological and design arguments for God's existence — and arrive independently at structurally similar conclusions.
