# Ashʿarite Occasionalism

The dominant classical Sunni kalām school, founded by Abū al-Ḥasan al-Ashʿarī (-874 to -935) after his break with the Muʿtazila c. 912 (Frank 1992). The school's most striking and influential philosophical position is *occasionalism*: the doctrine that there is no true causation in created reality — God is the sole causal agent, and what appear as natural causes are merely the *occasions* on which God directly produces effects according to *ʿāda* (custom). The doctrine is grounded in a kalām atomism: time, matter, and accidents are all composed of discrete atoms continuously re-created by God at each instant.

## The five distinguishing positions

- **Atomism + occasionalism**: matter consists of discrete atoms (*jawāhir*) and accidents (*aʿrāḍ*); accidents do not persist for two consecutive instants — God re-creates the universe continuously. The "burning of cotton" in al-Ghazālī's *Tahāfut* example involves no causal nexus between fire and cotton; God produces blackness, heat, and disintegration simultaneously with the fire's presence (Frank 1992).
- **Divine attributes as eternal but not separate** (against the Muʿtazilite *taʿṭīl*): God's seven essential attributes (knowledge, power, will, life, hearing, sight, speech) are eternal realities subsisting in His essence — neither identical with the essence nor separate from it.
- **The uncreated Qurʾān**: God's eternal speech is uncreated (against the Muʿtazilite doctrine of the created Qurʾān); the *recited* or *written* Qurʾān is the created vehicle.
- **Acquisition** (*kasb*): a middle position on free will. God creates all human acts; humans "acquire" them — incur moral responsibility — without being their efficient cause.
- **Bilā kayf** ("without [asking] how"): anthropomorphic Qurʾānic descriptions of God (hand, face, sitting on throne) are to be affirmed as the Qurʾān states without rationalizing or denying them.

## Al-Ghazālī's articulation

The most famous Ashʿarite argument against Avicennan natural causation is al-Ghazālī's 17th Discussion in the *Tahāfut al-falāsifa* (*The Incoherence of the Philosophers*, c. 1095): there is no observable necessary connection between cause and effect — only constant conjunction. The fire and the cotton: we observe heat *with* burning, but never the *causation*. God could create cotton without burning even in the presence of fire (and does so in the Qurʾānic miracle of Abraham in the furnace, Q 21:69). Al-Ghazālī's epistemological skepticism about causation anticipates Hume's nearly 700 years (1739) and Malebranche's late-17th-c. Christian occasionalism by 600 years.

## Cross-tradition resonance

The Ashʿarite occasionalist analysis of cause has remarkable structural anticipation of David Hume's *Treatise* (1739) sceptical analysis of causation — Hume reading no necessary connection in repeated observation; only constant conjunction and the mind's habit. Whether al-Ghazālī's Latin transmission (via the *Maqāṣid al-falāsifa*, translated as *Logica et Philosophia Algazelis Arabis*) reached Hume is contested; structural convergence is undisputed (Frank 1992). Nicolas Malebranche's Christian occasionalism (*De la recherche de la vérité*, 1674-75) — created beings do not causally interact; God is the sole efficient cause; secondary causes are *causes occasionnelles* — runs structurally identical to the Ashʿarite position, though direct transmission is not established.

Within Indian philosophy, the Yoga-school *Īśvara* doctrine and certain readings of Madhva's *Dvaita* Vedānta articulate similar all-divine-causation positions; the [philosophy-ajivika-determinism|Ājīvika] doctrine of *niyati* (fate) is the most-thoroughgoing Indian causal-determinist analogue. The Buddhist [[philosophy-madhyamaka|Mādhyamika]] critique of *svabhāva* (intrinsic nature) shares the Ashʿarite skepticism about real causal powers in created entities — though its constructive resolution is voidness, not divine omnicausality. In contemporary philosophy of religion, the *divine concurrence* and *divine conservation* debates ([[philosophy-thomism]] articulates a *secondary-causality* alternative to occasionalism) inherit the same conceptual problem.
