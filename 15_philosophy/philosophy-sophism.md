# Sophism (the Sophistic movement)

The Sophists were the first paid, professional teachers of aretē/rhetoric in the Greek cities — itinerant experts who, for a fee, taught *aretē* (excellence, especially the civic excellence that wins arguments, offices and lawsuits) in the democratic cities of the fifth century BCE, above all Periclean Athens. *Sophistēs* originally meant simply "expert" or "sage" (it could be applied to poets, seers, even the Seven Sages); it acquired its pejorative sense — a clever arguer who "makes the weaker argument the stronger" and traffics in mere appearance for money — through the sustained hostility of Plato and Aristotle, whose usage fixed the English word "sophistry." Like [[philosophy-cynicism]], Sophism is best treated as a **movement** rather than a doctrinal school: its members shared a profession, a set of concerns (rhetoric, relativism, the *nomos*-*physis* debate, the human origin of religion and law) and a method (antilogic — arguing both sides), but no common metaphysics or succession of scholarchs.

Its leading figures — none of whose works survive whole — were Protagoras of Abdera (c. 490-420 BCE), Gorgias of Leontini (c. 483-375 BCE), Prodicus of Ceos, Hippias of Elis, Thrasymachus of Chalcedon and Antiphon the Sophist. The anonymous *Dissoi Logoi* ("Twofold Arguments," c. 400 BCE) preserves the antilogical method in the raw. The Roman-era "Second Sophistic" (a term coined by Philostratus, 3rd c. CE) is a separate later revival of epideictic oratory and is not part of the classical movement dated here.

## Core positions

The Sophists left no shared doctrine, but several load-bearing positions recur:

- **Relativism — "man the measure."** Protagoras opened his lost work *Truth* with the most famous sentence of the movement: *pantōn chrēmatōn metron anthrōpos* — "of all things the measure is man, of the things that are that they are, and of the things that are not that they are not" (DK 80 B1, quoted at Plato *Theaetetus* 152a and Sextus, *Adv. Math.* VII.60). On the usual reading, what appears to each perceiver *is* true for that perceiver: there is no perceiver-independent fact of the matter. Plato's *Theaetetus* is the great attempt to refute it (including the "self-refutation" argument at 171a-c: if all beliefs are true, then the belief that Protagoras is wrong is also true).

- **Agnosticism about the gods.** Protagoras' *On the Gods* began: "Concerning the gods I am unable to know either that they exist or that they do not exist, nor what they are like in form; many things prevent knowledge — the obscurity of the matter and the shortness of human life" (DK 80 B4). Prodicus advanced a naturalistic account of religion's origin: early humans deified the things useful to life — sun, rivers, bread (Demeter), wine (Dionysus) — an early "euhemerist-before-Euhemerus" theory (DK 84 B5). The *Sisyphus* fragment (ascribed to Critias or to Euripides) makes religion a clever ruler's invention to police behavior the law cannot see.

- **Rhetoric and the power of the *logos*.** Gorgias treated persuasive speech as a quasi-physical force: in the *Encomium of Helen* he argues that *logos* (speech) is "a great potentate" that works on the soul as drugs work on the body, so that Helen, if persuaded by speech, is as blameless as if forced. Gorgias' showpiece *On Nature, or On the Non-Existent* (*Peri tou mē ontos*), preserved in Sextus (*Adv. Math.* VII.65-87) and the pseudo-Aristotelian *MXG*, argues three descending theses — nothing exists; if anything exists it cannot be known; if it can be known it cannot be communicated — a parody-inversion of Eleatic (Parmenidean) metaphysics that dissolves the very possibility of true speech about being.

- **The *nomos*-*physis* debate.** The signature controversy of the age: are the norms of justice, law and social station grounded in *physis* (nature) or are they merely *nomos* (convention/custom, and so alterable, even arbitrary)? Hippias declares in Plato's *Protagoras* (337c-d) that "law, being the tyrant of men, forces many things against nature." Antiphon's papyrus *On Truth* pushes toward a natural human equality beneath conventional distinctions ("we all breathe the air through mouth and nostrils alike"), while Thrasymachus in *Republic* I gives the debate its cynical edge: justice is simply "the advantage of the stronger."

## In the ancient sources

The catastrophe of the Sophistic evidence is that the Sophists' own books are almost entirely lost; we know them through their enemies. Plato is the fullest and most hostile witness — the dialogues *Protagoras*, *Gorgias*, *Theaetetus* and *Sophist* stage the leading Sophists as interlocutors and systematically discredit them, opposing sophistic *rhetoric* (persuasion aimed at winning) to Socratic *dialectic* (inquiry aimed at truth). Aristotle codified the polemic in the *Sophistical Refutations* (*Sophistici Elenchi*), defining sophistic as reasoning that only *appears* valid. Aristophanes' *Clouds* (423 BCE) satirizes the new education — though it pins the caricature on Socrates, conflating him with the paid teachers he in fact opposed. The direct fragments and testimonia are gathered as Diels-Kranz chapters 79-90 (English in Sprague's *The Older Sophists*).

## Cross-tradition significance

**Defined-against Platonism (secure, internal to Greece).** The clearest relation is agonistic: [[philosophy-platonism]] is in large part *built* against the Sophists. Plato's distinction between knowledge and opinion, his theory of Forms as perceiver-independent reality, and his defense of an objective justice all take shape as replies to Protagorean relativism and Gorgianic rhetoric. Because Plato both preserved and prosecuted the Sophists, the movement reaches us pre-interpreted by its prosecutor — the root of the modern historiographical dispute below.

**Ancestor of Greek skepticism (genealogical-within-Greek).** Protagorean relativism and Gorgias' dissolution of knowable, sayable being are recognized tributaries of later [[philosophy-pyrrhonian-skepticism]]: Sextus Empiricus engages both directly (*PH* I.216-219; *Adv. Math.* VII.60-87), while carefully distinguishing the skeptics' suspension of judgment (*epochē*) from Protagoras' positive doctrine that each appearance is true. The antilogical method — arguing opposed cases with equal force, exhibited in the *Dissoi Logoi* — is a direct methodological ancestor of the skeptics' balancing of arguments (*isostheneia*).

**Independent convergence with Indian materialism.** The Sophists invite comparison with [[philosophy-carvaka]] / Lokāyata, the materialist-skeptical current of classical India, on two specific points: a naturalistic critique of inherited religion (Protagoras' agnosticism and Prodicus' theory of deified benefactors beside Cārvāka's denial of afterlife and ritual efficacy), and the shared source-catastrophe — both movements survive almost solely through the polemics of their enemies (the Sophists through Plato and Aristotle; Cārvāka through Buddhist, Jain and Brahmanical doxographies). There was no contact between fifth-century Greece and early Indian materialism; this is a textbook case of typological convergence, valuable precisely because transmission is excluded. The pairing is the Atlas's structural comparison, not a claim advanced in any single monograph.

**Contemporary Abderite milieu (not descent).** Ancient doxography made Protagoras a pupil of Democritus, both being from Abdera — placing Sophism next to [[philosophy-atomism-greek]]. Modern scholarship regards the discipleship as chronologically doubtful and probably a later construction; the two are recorded here as a shared local milieu, not a doctrinal filiation.

## Disputes

- **Was there a "Sophistic movement" at all?** Scholars dispute whether the Sophists form a unified movement with shared doctrines or merely a loose profession of paid teachers with divergent views. Kerferd (1981) defends a real common core (relativism, *nomos*-*physis*, antilogic); others stress the heterogeneity of Protagoras, Gorgias and Prodicus. This node types them as a **movement**, matching the treatment of Cynicism.

- **The rehabilitation of the Sophists.** For two millennia "sophist" was a term of abuse, taking Plato's verdict at face value. From the nineteenth century a major revision set in: G. W. F. Hegel (*Lectures on the History of Philosophy*) read the Sophists as the Greek Enlightenment — the discovery of subjectivity and the turn to the human — and George Grote's *History of Greece* (1846-56, esp. vol. VIII ch. 67) mounted a famous defense of them as honest democratic educators traduced by an aristocratic Plato. The debate over how far to trust Plato's portrait remains live (Kerferd 1981; de Romilly 1992).

- **Person-nodes still owed.** No dedicated Atlas nodes yet exist for Protagoras or Gorgias, the two principal Sophists; both are recorded here as demand (see Refs / self-flags) rather than wired as dead links. Critias — the *Sisyphus*-fragment author sometimes grouped with the Sophists — appears in the Atlas only as the disputed dialogue-speaker of [[critias-younger]], a different identification and so not wired from here.
