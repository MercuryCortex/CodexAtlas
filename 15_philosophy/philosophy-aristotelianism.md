# Aristotelianism (the Peripatetic school)

## Identity

**Aristotelianism** is the philosophical tradition founded by [[aristotle|Aristotle]] (384-322 BCE) and taught at the **Lyceum**, the school he established in Athens c. 335 BCE. Its members were called the **Peripatetics** (*hoi peripatētikoi*, "those of the walkway," after the covered walk, *peripatos*, where they taught). The tradition is defined less by a single doctrine than by a comprehensive, integrated system spanning logic, physics, biology, psychology, metaphysics, ethics, and politics — and by a distinctive method of beginning from observed phenomena and reputable opinions (*endoxa*) and working toward first principles.

Its signature theses are: **hylomorphism** (every physical substance is a compound of *matter*, *hylē*, and *form*, *morphē*/*eidos*); the **four causes** (material, formal, efficient, and final — the *why* of a thing answered four ways, *Physics* II.3); the correlative pair **actuality and potentiality** (*energeia* / *dynamis*) that explains change; the doctrine of **substance** (*ousia*) and the ten **categories** as the fundamental modes of being; **teleology**, the primacy of the final cause or *telos* in nature ("nature does nothing in vain"); the **unmoved mover** of *Metaphysics* Λ as the cosmic final cause and "thought thinking itself"; the **soul as the form of a living body** (*De Anima* II) rather than a separable substance; **virtue ethics**, in which the good life is *eudaimonia* achieved through virtues understood as a mean between excess and deficiency (*Nicomachean Ethics* II); and the **syllogistic**, the first formal logic (the *Prior Analytics*), which governed the discipline until the nineteenth century. In the Atlas this node is the **root** from which [[philosophy-falsafa|falsafa]], [[philosophy-scholasticism|scholasticism]], and [[philosophy-thomism|Thomism]] descend, and the source-node behind the comparative theme-hubs [[unmoved-mover]], [[teleology]], and [[chain-of-being]].

## In its corpus and the history of the school

### The Lyceum and the corpus (c. 335 - 60 BCE)

Aristotle's surviving works are not the polished dialogues he published in his lifetime (now lost) but the *pragmateiai* — the treatises or lecture-courses used inside the school: the logical *Organon* (*Categories*, *De Interpretatione*, *Prior* and *Posterior Analytics*, *Topics*, *Sophistical Refutations*), the *Physics*, *De Caelo*, *De Anima*, the biological works, the *Metaphysics*, the *Nicomachean* and *Eudemian Ethics*, the *Politics*, the *Rhetoric*, and the *Poetics*. On Aristotle's death (322 BCE) the school passed to **Theophrastus** (its greatest botanist and doxographer) and then to **Strato of Lampsacus** ("the Physicist"). The corpus as now studied was edited and arranged c. 60 BCE by **Andronicus of Rhodes**, whose ordering — placing the treatises "after the *Physics*" — gave the *Metaphysics* its name.

### The commentators (c. 200 - 550 CE)

The tradition's second life is exegetical. **Alexander of Aphrodisias** (fl. c. 200 CE), "the Commentator" *par excellence*, produced the authoritative naturalistic readings of the *Metaphysics* and *De Anima* and developed the doctrine of the **Active Intellect**. From the third century the Neoplatonist school took over the commentary tradition — **Porphyry's** *Isagoge* (an introduction to the *Categories*) became the standard logic primer for a thousand years, and **Ammonius**, **Simplicius**, and **John Philoponus** wrote the great late-antique commentaries. Their project of **harmonising Aristotle with Plato** produced the "Neoplatonised Aristotle" that would be inherited by Islam and the Latin West (Sorabji 1990).

### Falsafa: the Arabic Aristotle (c. 800 - 1200)

Through Syriac intermediaries and then the Baghdad **Graeco-Arabic translation movement** (Gutas 1998), the corpus entered Arabic. [[philosophy-falsafa|Falsafa]] is Aristotelianism in Islamic dress: **al-Fārābī** is honoured as "the Second Teacher" (*al-muʿallim al-thānī*) after Aristotle himself; [[ibn-sina|Ibn Sīnā (Avicenna)]] builds one of the most comprehensive Aristotelian-Neoplatonic systems of the period in the *Kitāb al-Shifāʾ*; and [[ibn-rushd|Ibn Rushd (Averroes)]] writes the Short, Middle, and Long **Commentaries** ([[phase-5-057-ibn-rushd-aristotle-commentaries]]) that would make him simply "the Commentator" to the Latins. (A telling accident of transmission: the [[phase-5-048-theology-of-aristotle-arabic|*Theology of Aristotle*]], hugely influential in this tradition, is in fact a paraphrase of Plotinus's *Enneads*, not Aristotle at all.)

### Scholasticism and the Latin recovery (c. 1150 - 1500)

The twelfth- and thirteenth-century Latin translations — from the Arabic and then directly from the Greek by **William of Moerbeke** — returned the full Aristotle to Europe, where he became the curricular spine of the new universities (Hasse 2016). The reception was fought over: the Aristotelian libri naturales were repeatedly **condemned at Paris** (1210, 1231, and the sweeping condemnation of 1277). Out of this contest came [[philosophy-scholasticism|scholasticism]] and, above all, [[philosophy-thomism|Thomas Aquinas's]] synthesis, in which Aristotle is simply *the Philosopher*. **Renaissance Aristotelianism** then flourished at Padua (Pietro Pomponazzi, Jacopo Zabarella) even as the new mechanical philosophy of the seventeenth century began to displace the four causes and substantial forms.

### The modern revival

Aristotelianism was never wholly extinguished, and the twentieth century brought a deliberate return. **G. E. M. Anscombe's** "Modern Moral Philosophy" (1958) and **Alasdair MacIntyre's** *After Virtue* (1981) revived **virtue ethics** as a live alternative to Kantian and utilitarian theory; hylomorphism has been reclaimed in the philosophy of mind and biology; and the immanent-teleology reading of Aristotle (Lennox 2001) has reshaped the history of the life sciences.

## Cross-tradition significance (transmission vs. convergence)

The reach of Aristotelianism is overwhelmingly a matter of **documented historical transmission, not independent convergence**. There is a single traceable textual pipeline — Aristotle's treatises → the ancient commentators → the Baghdad translations → [[philosophy-falsafa|falsafa]] → the Toledo and Moerbeke Latin versions → [[philosophy-scholasticism|scholasticism]] and [[philosophy-thomism|Thomism]] → Renaissance Padua → and, after a mechanical-philosophy interregnum, the modern revival. Every stage is attested by surviving manuscripts and named translators (Gutas 1998; Sorabji 1990; Hasse 2016). Accordingly the comparative theme-hubs this node roots — [[unmoved-mover]], [[teleology]], and, in part, [[chain-of-being]] — are **genealogical lineages**, not convergent parallels: the four-cause apparatus, the unmoved-mover argument, and the *scala naturae* are Greek philosophical inventions that were carried, translated, and argued over, not reinvented, across the Mediterranean and Near-Eastern philosophical *koine*.

The one important **convergent** (genuinely independent) parallel is with Indian [[philosophy-nyaya-vaisheshika-realism|Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika]]. Classical Vaiśeṣika's scheme of **categories** (*padārtha*) — substance (*dravya*), quality (*guṇa*), motion (*karma*), universal (*sāmānya*), particular (*viśeṣa*), and inherence (*samavāya*) — is a realist category-ontology strikingly comparable to Aristotle's *Categories* and his substance-and-attribute metaphysics, and it was developed with **no demonstrable contact** with the Greek tradition. Wilhelm Halbfass's controlled comparison (*On Being and What There Is*, 1992) is careful about exactly this point: the resemblance is real and illuminating, but it is a case of two cultures independently building realist ontologies of substance and category, not of borrowing. This is the honest contrast — Aristotelianism's Mediterranean-Islamic-Latin reach is a **transmission chain**, while its Indian analogue is a **convergence**.

## Disputes

- **How much of "Aristotelianism" is Aristotle's?** The school's most influential doctrines are partly the commentators' constructions. The doctrine of the Active Intellect as a single separate substance is Alexander's and Averroes's development, not unambiguously Aristotle's own; and the "Neoplatonised Aristotle" harmonised with Plato (Porphyry, Simplicius) is the form in which the tradition was actually received — arguably a hybrid rather than the historical Aristotle (Sorabji 1990).
- **Is Aristotelian teleology theological?** The older and much of the medieval reading takes the final cause to imply a designing cosmic mind, continuous with natural theology; the dominant modern reading (Lennox 2001; Johnson 2005) holds that Aristotle's ends are **immanent** to natural kinds and that the unmoved mover does not design or care for the cosmos — the providentialist reading being a Stoic and Christian overlay. (See [[teleology]] for the full debate.)
- **The unity of the unmoved mover.** *Metaphysics* Λ 8 argues, against the rest of the book, for as many as fifty-five movers answering to the celestial spheres; whether this is consistent with Λ 7's single mover, or a later revision, is a perennial crux (Frede & Charles 2000).
- **One or many "Aristotelianisms"?** The Arabic Averroist Aristotle (eternal world, single intellect) and the Latin Christian Aristotle (creation in time, individual immortal soul) draw incompatible conclusions from the same texts; whether these are one tradition or several rival appropriations is a matter of framing.
