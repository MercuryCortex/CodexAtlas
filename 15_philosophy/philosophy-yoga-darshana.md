# Yoga darśana (Pātañjala Yoga)

## Identity

The **Yoga darśana** is one of the six *āstika* (Veda-affirming) schools of classical Hindu philosophy — the school that takes the *[[phase-3-023-yoga-sutras-of-patanjali|Yoga-Sūtra]]* of [[patanjali-yoga-sutras-author|Patañjali]] (c. 325–425 CE) as its founding text and organizes a systematic metaphysics of consciousness, matter and liberation around a disciplined meditative practice. It is the *philosophical* Yoga — a *darśana* ("view / system"), a theory of what exists and how the self is freed — and is to be distinguished at the outset from modern postural yoga (the *āsana*-centred global practice, which descends far more from medieval *[[phase-5-035-hatha-yoga-pradipika|Haṭha]]* traditions than from Patañjali).

Yoga is traditionally paired with **[[tradition-samkhya-yoga|Sāṃkhya]]**: the two share almost their entire metaphysic, and the classical formula is that *Sāṃkhya supplies the theory and Yoga the practice*. The one substantive metaphysical addition Yoga makes is **Īśvara** (a "special *puruṣa*"), which is why the school is often labelled *seśvara Sāṃkhya* — "Sāṃkhya-with-God" — against Sāṃkhya's *nirīśvara* ("godless") system (Larson 1979). Its foundational definition of yoga is the second aphorism of the text: *yogaś citta-vṛtti-nirodhaḥ* — "yoga is the stilling of the fluctuations of the mind-field" (YS 1.2).

## Core doctrine

**Puruṣa and prakṛti.** With Sāṃkhya, Yoga is an uncompromising **dualism** of two eternal, irreducible principles: *puruṣa* — pure, contentless, passive consciousness (the seer, *draṣṭṛ*) — and *prakṛti* — primordial unconscious materiality, out of which everything experienceable evolves, including the mind (*citta*/*buddhi*) itself. Crucially the school is **pluralist**: there are *many* puruṣas (one per sentient being), not a single universal Self — the sharpest doctrinal break from Advaita's one Brahman. Prakṛti is structured by three *guṇas* (*sattva*, *rajas*, *tamas* — lucidity, activity, inertia), whose disequilibrium unfolds the twenty-five *tattvas* (evolutes) of the Sāṃkhya scheme. Bondage is the mistaken conflation (*saṃyoga*) of puruṣa with the mental activity of prakṛti; liberation is their separation.

**Chitta-vṛtti-nirodha.** The mind (*citta*) is itself a product of prakṛti. Its *vṛttis* — cognitive fluctuations — are what puruṣa mistakes for its own experience. The whole path is the progressive **cessation (*nirodha*) of the vṛttis**, so that "then the seer abides in its own form" (YS 1.3). Standing in the way are the five *kleśas* (afflictions, YS 2.3): ignorance (*avidyā*), egoism (*asmitā*), attachment (*rāga*), aversion (*dveṣa*) and clinging-to-life (*abhiniveśa*).

**Aṣṭāṅga — the eight limbs.** The practical core is the *aṣṭāṅga-yoga* (YS 2.29), eight progressive "limbs": *yama* (restraints), *niyama* (observances), *āsana* (posture — a single sūtra, "steady and comfortable," with no posture catalogue), *prāṇāyāma* (breath-regulation), *pratyāhāra* (withdrawal of the senses), *dhāraṇā* (concentration), *dhyāna* (meditation) and *samādhi* (absorption). The last three together constitute *saṃyama*, the integrated meditative instrument that yields the *vibhūtis* (yogic powers) of the third *pāda* — powers the text explicitly warns are obstacles to liberation, not its goal.

**Īśvara-praṇidhāna.** Among the *niyamas*, and again as an independent fast path to *samādhi* (YS 1.23), stands *Īśvara-praṇidhāna* — devotion/surrender to **Īśvara**. Īśvara is defined narrowly (YS 1.24) as a distinct *puruṣa* untouched by the kleśas, karma and its fruits — the teacher of the ancient sages, "denoted by *praṇava*" (OM, YS 1.27). Notably Īśvara is **not** a creator god and does not grant liberation by grace in the theistic sense; devotion functions as one especially effective object of one-pointed concentration.

**Kaivalya.** The goal is *kaivalya* — "isolation / aloneness": the final, permanent disentanglement of puruṣa from prakṛti, in which consciousness rests in its own nature, luminous and free, and the karmic mechanism that produced rebirth ceases. The climactic state is *dharma-megha-samādhi* ("cloud-of-virtue absorption," YS 4.29), after which the guṇas resolve back into their ground and the freed puruṣa is no longer entangled in any further experience.

## Relation to Sāṃkhya and the six darśanas

Yoga's independence as a darśana is real but thin. It inherits Sāṃkhya's twenty-five *tattvas*, its guṇa-theory, its account of bondage-by-misidentification and its liberation-by-discernment (*viveka-khyāti*) essentially unchanged; its distinctive contribution is (a) the systematic *practice* — the eight limbs and the analysis of *samādhi* — and (b) the addition of Īśvara. Some scholars accordingly treat "Sāṃkhya-Yoga" as effectively a single tradition with two wings (theoretical and applied), which is why the Atlas houses the darśana under [[tradition-samkhya-yoga]]. Alongside it in the six-*darśana* scheme stand [[philosophy-nyaya-vaisheshika-realism|Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika]], Pūrva-[[philosophy-mimamsa-purva-ritual-metaphysics|Mīmāṃsā]] and Uttara-Mīmāṃsā ([[philosophy-advaita-vedanta|Vedānta]]).

## Cross-tradition significance

**Spirit–matter dualism (convergence, not transmission).** The puruṣa/prakṛti scheme is one of the most systematically developed *two-principle* metaphysics in the Indian traditions, and it occupies the same comparative slot as the Platonic–Gnostic **[[dualism-spirit-matter|spirit/matter dualism]]** of the Mediterranean: conscious, luminous spirit set over against unconscious materiality, with liberation figured as the spirit's release. But the resemblance is **structural and convergent**, and the differences are as instructive as the likeness. In Sāṃkhya-Yoga prakṛti is *real*, *eternal* and morally *neutral* — it exists precisely in order to serve puruṣa's eventual liberation — so there is no evil demiurge, no cosmic fall, no contempt of the body as such; bondage is a cognitive error, not a metaphysical crime, and its cure is *discernment* (viveka-khyāti), not rescue from a hostile cosmos. There is no documented historical link; the honest reading (Larson 1979) is independent convergence on the two-principle form.

**The Hellenistic parallels (contested).** A durable comparative literature — most fully McEvilley 2002 — sets the Yogic cultivation of attention beside **[[philosophy-stoicism|Stoic]]** *prosochē*/*prokopē* and **[[philosophy-pyrrhonian-skepticism|Pyrrhonian]]** *epochē*/*ataraxia*, with Neoplatonic *henōsis* as a third term. The one hard contact datum is Pyrrho's reported meeting with Indian "gymnosophists" on Alexander's campaign (Diogenes Laertius 9.61), and the Gandhāran/Bactrian zone (c. 300 BCE – 200 CE) offers a plausible channel. But the direction and even the reality of influence are unsettled: McEvilley reads deep mutual exchange, Beckwith (2015) reads Pyrrho as the *borrower* from Indian sources, and the Indological mainstream (Larson, Whicher, Halbfass) treats the parallels as structurally suggestive but historically unproven. The Atlas records the parallel as a live hypothesis, not a settled borrowing.

**Buddhist meditative technology (absorbed).** Running the other way, the Yoga-Sūtra's own vocabulary of *dhyāna* and *samādhi*, and much of the graded absorption analysis, is detectably continuous with earlier **Buddhist** *dhyāna* discourse; Larson & Bhattacharya (2008) treat the Buddhist debt as substantial. The distinctive move of the darśana is to re-house this meditative machinery inside a Sāṃkhya-Vedic, *ātman*-affirming frame — the exact opposite metaphysical commitment to the Buddhist sources it draws on.

**Contemplative exemplar.** In the comparative study of **[[mysticism|mysticism]]**, Yogic *samādhi* is the standard "Hindu" exemplar invoked in the perennialism-vs-constructivism debate (James, Stace, Zaehner, Katz) — the paradigm case of a technically-analysed, staged unitive discipline.

## Disputes

- **Is the Yoga darśana genuinely theistic?** Īśvara is a *special puruṣa*, not a creator and not a grace-giving redeemer; several scholars read Īśvara as primarily a soteriological aid and object of concentration rather than a robust deity, leaving Yoga only marginally more theistic than atheist Sāṃkhya (Bryant 2009; Larson & Bhattacharya 2008).
- **World-negating or world-transforming kaivalya?** The traditional reading takes kaivalya as the puruṣa's total *isolation* from prakṛti — a world-exiting terminus. Whicher (1998) mounts the principal revisionist case that classical Yoga aims instead at a transformed, non-afflicted mode of embodied engagement, not annihilation of world-experience.
- **Independent school or "applied Sāṃkhya"?** Because Yoga borrows nearly the whole Sāṃkhya metaphysic, its status as a *separate* darśana is contested; some treat Sāṃkhya-Yoga as one tradition.
- **Text and date.** Maas (2013) argues the Yoga-Sūtra and the Vyāsa-*Bhāṣya* are a single authored work (the *Pātañjala-Yoga-Śāstra*, c. 325–425 CE), overturning the older picture of an aphorism-text with a much later independent commentary; the "three Patañjalis" conflation (grammarian, yoga-author, medical) is a traditional identification undone by modern philology.
- **Homonym trap.** The Yoga darśana must not be conflated with Buddhist **[[philosophy-yogacara|Yogācāra]]** ("yoga-practice / Mind-Only"): despite the shared word, that is an idealist, no-self Mahāyāna school, metaphysically the inverse of Yoga's realist plural-puruṣa dualism.
