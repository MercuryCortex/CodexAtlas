# Sautrāntika

## Identity

**Sautrāntika** (Sanskrit *sautrāntika*, "one who relies on the sūtras / sūtra-end") is a Buddhist śrāvakayāna philosophical school — better described as a doctrinal standpoint — that arose as the reforming, sūtra-loyal dissent within the [[philosophy-abhidharma|Abhidharma]] scholasticism of northwest India. Its defining move is a refusal of authority: where the dominant **Sarvāstivāda-Vaibhāṣika** grounded its metaphysics in the seven canonical Abhidharma treatises, the Sautrāntikas held that only the **sūtras** (*sūtrānta*) are the word of the Buddha, and that the Abhidharma treatises are a later human systematisation whose more extravagant theses may be rejected. In the earlier sources this current appears under the name **Dārṣṭāntika** ("those who teach by way of examples / similes"), and the traditional founder is **Kumāralāta**; the mature position is associated with the teacher **Śrīlāta** and, above all, is the standpoint from which [[vasubandhu|Vasubandhu]] criticises the Vaibhāṣika in the auto-commentary (*bhāṣya*) of his *[[phase-4-077-abhidharmakosa|Abhidharmakośa]]*.

In the Atlas, Sautrāntika is placed among the philosophy schools as the **hinge between the Abhidharma and the Mahāyāna**: it is a distinct sibling of the Abhidharma — sharing its analytical method while rejecting its ontology — and, through its seed-theory and its representationalism, widely read as the immediate philosophical antecedent of [[philosophy-yogacara|Yogācāra]]. It should never be folded into the Abhidharma node: it is precisely the internal dissent *against* the Vaibhāṣika Abhidharma.

## The core doctrines

Four positions define the school, each a subtraction from Sarvāstivāda realism:

- **Against "everything exists" (rejection of *sarvāstitva*).** The Sarvāstivāda took its name from the thesis that dharmas exist in all three times — past, present, and future are all real, a *substance* (*dravya*) persisting while only its *activity* is momentary. The Sautrāntika rejects this as a betrayal of impermanence: **only the present, momentary dharma is real**; the past is what *has* been and the future what *will* be, not existent things. This is the school's signature and the charge the later Mahāyāna inherits — that a substance persisting across the three times smuggles a hidden self back into the system.

- **Radical momentariness (*kṣaṇikavāda*).** The Sautrāntika pushes momentariness to its limit: a dharma has no duration-phase at all — it arises and perishes in the very same instant, so that a thing's "ceasing" needs no separate cause (von Rospatt 1995). Continuity is a rapid causal succession of wholly discrete momentary events, never the endurance of anything.

- **Ontological parsimony (dharmas as *prajñapti*).** Against the Vaibhāṣika inventory of 75 real dharmas, the Sautrāntika demotes whole categories to **mere nominal designation** (*prajñapti*): the *citta-viprayukta-saṃskāras* (the fourteen "forces dissociated from mind" — possession, vitality, the marks of the conditioned, and the like) are not real entities but ways of speaking about the mental continuum, and the *asaṃskṛta* (unconditioned) dharmas — space, cessation — are absences, not existents. Only momentary mind, mental factors, and material atoms remain as truly real.

- **Seed-theory (*bīja*).** Having discarded the Sarvāstivāda machinery of *prāpti* ("possession") that tied past deeds to a present person, the Sautrāntika explains karmic retribution and memory by **seeds** (*bīja*): potentials deposited by an action in the momentary mental **continuum** (*santāna*), which mature (*pariṇāma*) into future results. This is a purely causal, ownerless account of continuity — and it is the doctrine Yogācāra rebuilds as the store-consciousness.

- **Representationalism (*sākāravāda*).** In epistemology the Sautrāntika is a **representative realist**: the external object exists but is never directly perceived. Because a strictly momentary object has already perished by the time cognition of it arises, what a cognition actually bears is a **mental image / aspect** (*ākāra*) *caused by* the object; the external thing is **inferred** (*bāhyārthānumeya*) as the image's cause, not grasped in itself. Perception is thus mediated by a representation — a "veil of ideas" between mind and world.

## The school and its sources

The Sautrāntika is unusual among the Atlas philosophy schools in that **no independent Sautrāntika treatise survives**, and it may never have been a separate ordination lineage (*nikāya*) at all — it is a doctrinal party, largely reconstructed from the writings of its opponents and from a single sympathetic source. Its positions are known from: the **Mahāvibhāṣā** (c. 2nd c. CE), which already records and refutes named **Dārṣṭāntika** masters; the **Abhidharmakośa-bhāṣya** of Vasubandhu, whose verses state the Vaibhāṣika system and whose commentary repeatedly overturns them "according to the Sautrāntika"; and **Saṅghabhadra's *Nyāyānusāra***, a vast Vaibhāṣika counter-attack that quotes and rebuts the Sautrāntika (and Śrīlāta) at length — the principal witness translated in Cox (1995). A separate, later **"Sautrāntika" strand** is identified within the epistemological (*pramāṇa*) tradition of Dignāga and Dharmakīrti, whose "Sautrāntika" tier posits external objects known through representations, below the "Yogācāra" tier that dispenses with them (Dreyfus 1997).

## Cross-tradition significance

**The bridge to Yogācāra (transmission, not parallel).** The decisive comparative fact about the Sautrāntika is that it is the **doctrinal ancestor of [[philosophy-yogacara|Yogācāra]]**, and the descent is documented, not resemblant. Two Sautrāntika doctrines cross the bridge, both carried by [[vasubandhu|Vasubandhu]], who voices the Sautrāntika critique in the *Kośa-bhāṣya* and is traditionally the same author of the founding Yogācāra treatises. First, the **seed-continuum** (*bīja* in the *santāna*) is refashioned as the **store-consciousness** (*ālaya-vijñāna*) — a seed-bearing stream that carries karmic potential across momentary lives (Park 2014). Second, the **representationalism**: once cognition is granted access only to its own image (*ākāra*) and the external object is a mere inference, the inference can be judged idle — and Yogācāra takes that step, declaring the object **representation-only** (*vijñapti-mātra*). Sautrāntika is thus representative realism poised one move away from idealism.

**Representationalism East and West (structural convergence).** That last move recapitulates, independently, a famous Western sequence: **Locke's** representative realism (we perceive "ideas" from which we infer external bodies) gives way to **Berkeley's** idealism (the inferred material substratum is superfluous and incoherent — *esse est percipi*). The Sautrāntika→Yogācāra slide is the Indian analogue of the Locke→Berkeley slide, and the "veil of perception" problem is common to both. The convergence is **typological, not genealogical**: mainstream scholarship posits no contact between the Buddhist and early-modern European discussions, and the resemblance is a case of two traditions independently discovering that a mediating representation, once posited, threatens to swallow the world it was meant to represent. (The internal Buddhist descent, by contrast, is genuine transmission.)

**Direct vs. representative realism in India (documented debate).** Within the Indian arena the Sautrāntika representationalism is the standing foil of [[philosophy-nyaya-vaisheshika-realism|Nyāya]] **direct realism**: the whole *sākāra* / *nirākāra* controversy — is cognition "with image" or "without image," is the object perceived or inferred — pits the Buddhist epistemologists against the Naiyāyikas across centuries of live, mutually-citing dispute (Matilal 1986; Dreyfus 1997). This is inter-school argument, not loose analogy.

## Disputes

**Was there ever a Sautrāntika "school"?** It is contested whether the Sautrāntika was an actual monastic lineage or only a doctrinal label. Many scholars hold that "Sautrāntika" names a **position within (or beside) the Sarvāstivāda**, continuous with the earlier **Dārṣṭāntika**, rather than a separate *nikāya* with its own Vinaya; the very name may be a relatively late coinage crystallising a family of anti-Vaibhāṣika views (Cox 1995; Dhammajoti 2009). The relation of *Dārṣṭāntika* to *Sautrāntika* — whether they are the same current under two names, or successive stages — is itself part of the problem.

**Is the *Kośa*'s "Sautrāntika" really Sautrāntika?** Because the school survives mainly through Vasubandhu, the identity of his dissenting voice is a live question. **Robert Kritzer** (*Vasubandhu and the Yogācārabhūmi*, 2005) argues that many positions the *Kośa-bhāṣya* labels "Sautrāntika" are in fact drawn from the **Yogācārabhūmi** — i.e. that the Kośa's dissent is already crypto-Yogācāra, and that "Sautrāntika" as reconstructed from Vasubandhu may be a partly Yogācāra construct read back onto an Abhidharma-era school. Others (Park 2014, working from Śrīlāta via Saṅghabhadra) recover a Sautrāntika doctrine that is independently attested outside Vasubandhu, and so resist collapsing the two. The honest state of the question: the Sautrāntika **standpoint is real and independently witnessed** (in the Vibhāṣā and the Nyāyānusāra), but **how much of the received "Sautrāntika system" is Vasubandhu's own Yogācāra-inflected reconstruction** remains debated.
