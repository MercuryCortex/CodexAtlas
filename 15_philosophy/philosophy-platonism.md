# Platonism (classical — Plato and the Academy)

## Identity

Platonism, in its classical sense, is the philosophy of [[plato]] (c. 428–348 BCE) and the school he founded — the [[ideal-state-myth|Academy]] in Athens, established c. 387 BCE and running (with changes of doctrine) for nearly nine centuries. This node covers the *classical and Middle-Platonist* phase of the tradition — Plato's own dialogues, the Old Academy of his immediate successors (Speusippus, Xenocrates), and the revived dogmatic Platonism of c. -90 to +220 (the "Middle Platonists," Plutarch, Alcinous, Numenius) — and is kept deliberately distinct from [[philosophy-neoplatonism]], the late-antique system of Plotinus and his successors (204–529 CE), which the Atlas treats as a separate school. It is the sibling of the other classical Greek schools already in the Atlas — [[philosophy-stoicism]], [[philosophy-atomism-greek]], [[philosophy-pyrrhonian-skepticism]] — and, on Whitehead's much-quoted verdict, the trunk of which the later European tradition is "a series of footnotes."

## Core doctrine

**The Forms.** The centre of classical Platonism is the theory of Forms (*eidē*, *ideai*): eternal, immaterial, unchanging *paradigms* that the shifting sensible particulars merely *participate in* (*metechein*) or *imitate*. The Form of Beauty is what makes beautiful things beautiful; the sensible cosmos is "a moving image of eternity" (*Timaeus* 37d). The Forms are argued most fully in the *Phaedo* (74a–75d), the *Republic* (the images of the Sun, the Divided Line and the Cave, 507a–521b), and the *Symposium* (the ascending ladder to Beauty itself, 210a–212a). See [[forms-and-archetypes]].

**The Good.** Above the Forms stands the Form of the Good (*to agathon*), "beyond being in dignity and power" (*Republic* 509b) — the source that makes the Forms intelligible as the sun makes things visible. This "beyond being" formula is the seed of every later Western doctrine of a first principle above all predication.

**The soul: immortality and tripartition.** The soul (*psychē*) is immortal by its own nature — the *Phaedo* argues this from its kinship with the changeless Forms (78b–80b) and from its being essentially a life-bearer (105c–107a). In the *Republic* (IV, 435b–441c) and the *Phaedrus* (the chariot, 246a–254e) the soul is *tripartite*: reason (*logistikon*), spirit (*thymoeides*), and appetite (*epithymētikon*), with justice consisting in their right ordering. See [[soul-immortality]].

**Anamnesis and metempsychosis.** Learning is *recollection* (*anamnesis*): the soul knows the Forms from before birth and "recovers" that knowledge, demonstrated by the slave-boy's geometry in the *Meno* (81a–86b) and grounded in the soul's pre-existence in the *Phaedo*. This is bound to the doctrine of transmigration (*metempsychosis*) that Plato inherited from Pythagoras and Orphism and dramatised in the Myth of Er (*Republic* 614b–621d), the *Phaedrus*, and the *Gorgias* and *Phaedo* judgement-myths. See [[reincarnation]].

**The Demiurge and the cosmos.** In the *Timaeus* the [[demiurge|Demiurge]], a divine craftsman, orders pre-existing chaos into a cosmos by looking to the eternal Forms as his model — a *formation* of matter, not creation from nothing. The World-Soul is built from harmonic ratios, and the four elements from the regular solids ([[mathematics-platonic-solids|the Platonic solids]]), fusing Platonic metaphysics with the [[tradition-pythagorean|Pythagorean]] mathematics Plato absorbed through Archytas.

## Cross-tradition significance

Classical Platonism sits at a junction where **documented transmission** and **independent convergence** must be told apart carefully.

**Documented transmission (secure).** The downstream line is one of the best-attested in the history of ideas. The Forms and the Good pass through Middle Platonism into [[philo-of-alexandria|Philo]] (who lodges the Forms as *logoi* in the divine mind and reads them into Genesis 1), then into the Christian [[logos-cosmic-reason|Logos]] and the exemplarist theology of the Fathers; and through [[plotinus|Plotinus]] into all of [[philosophy-neoplatonism|Neoplatonism]] and thence into [[ein-sof|Kabbalah]], [[ibn-arabi|Sufi]] metaphysics, and Christian mysticism. Dillon (1977) documents the Middle-Platonist bridge; Kraut (1992) the ancient reception. That Christian and Islamic mysticism "think in Platonic categories" is not a resemblance but an inheritance carried by named texts and translators.

**Upstream transmission.** Two of the school's signatures are themselves *received*: the mathematical metaphysics and the metempsychosis come to Plato from [[pythagoras|Pythagoras]] and Orphism (Guthrie 1975). The Atlas therefore wires the [[reincarnation|reincarnation]] hub to Platonism as a *contributor*, not an originator — Plato systematises a doctrine older than himself.

**Independent convergence (typological, contested).** The alignment of the Forms/idealism with **Advaita Vedānta** — the intelligible One real, the sensible world a lower-grade appearance — is the classic case in the [[idealism-metaphysical|metaphysical-idealism]] hub. Here the honest verdict is convergence, not contact: [[philosophy-advaita-vedanta|Śaṅkara's]] *māyā* and Plato's "moving image" are structurally parallel articulations of consciousness-as-primary, but there is no documented textual channel between classical Athens and the Upaniṣadic schools, and the parallel is best read as two civilisations reaching the same metaphysical move (cf. the apophatic convergence catalogued at [[philosophy-neoplatonism]]: [[the-one-plotinus|the One]] / [[brahman-impersonal|nirguṇa Brahman]] / [[emptiness-sunyata|śūnyatā]] / the Dao). The Platonic–Indian *reincarnation* parallel is likewise structural: the Indo-Greek contact zone opened only after Alexander (c. 323 BCE), well after Plato, so shared Axial-Age development, not borrowing, is the mainstream reading.

## Disputes

**The "unwritten doctrines."** The Tübingen School (Hans Joachim Krämer, Konrad Gaiser) argues that Plato's deepest teaching was an *unwritten* doctrine of first principles — the One and the Indefinite Dyad — reported by Aristotle and the Old Academy but kept out of the dialogues; on this reading classical Platonism is already crypto-Neoplatonic. The dominant Anglophone tradition (Vlastos 1981; Cherniss) rejects the reconstruction as over-reliant on hostile Aristotelian testimony and insists the dialogues are the doctrine. The Atlas keeps the classical/Neoplatonic split precisely because how much "systematic metaphysics" to read back into Plato is exactly what is contested.

**The Forms' own problems.** Plato himself mounted the sharpest objections — the *Parmenides* (130a–135c) raises the regress now called the "Third Man" and the difficulty of participation — so whether the mature Plato still held the "classical" theory of Forms unchanged is a live developmental question (Vlastos 1981).

**Boundaries of the phase.** Where "Platonism" ends and "Neoplatonism" begins is a matter of scholarly convention; Middle Platonism (Numenius especially) already anticipates Plotinus. This node draws the line at Dillon's c. 220 CE terminus and hands the tradition to [[philosophy-neoplatonism]].
