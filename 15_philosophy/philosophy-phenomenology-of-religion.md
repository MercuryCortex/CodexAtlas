# Phenomenology of Religion

The 20th-c. methodological school treating religion as an irreducible *phenomenon* — a special quality of experience and structure that must be described on its own terms before any reductive (sociological, psychological, naturalist) explanation is attempted. Three principal articulators: Rudolf Otto (1869-1937), Gerardus van der Leeuw (1890-1950), and Mircea Eliade (1907-1986). Methodologically the school adapts Husserlian phenomenology (*epoché* — bracketing the question of metaphysical truth — and attentive description of essential structures) to the comparative study of religious materials worldwide (Pals 1996; Sharpe 1986).

## The three principal figures

- **Rudolf Otto** — *Das Heilige* (1917, ET *The Idea of the Holy* 1923). Isolates the *numinous* (*sui generis* religious quality irreducible to ethics or rationality) and its experiential signature: *mysterium tremendum et fascinans* (the awesome-fascinating mystery), encountered as *the Wholly Other*. Otto's *creature-feeling* — the sense of one's nothingness before the numinous — is the affective core of religious experience across traditions.
- **Gerardus van der Leeuw** — *Phänomenologie der Religion* (1933). Systematic classification of religious manifestations (*Erscheinungen*) around the central category of *power* (*Macht*). Distinguishes the *subject* (the religious person), the *object* (the sacred / power), and the *relation* between them; each is classified into typologies (sacred persons, sacred objects, sacred places, sacred times, sacred actions).
- **Mircea Eliade** — *The Sacred and the Profane* (1957), *Patterns in Comparative Religion* (1949), *The Myth of the Eternal Return* (1949). The most-influential 20th-c. comparative religionist. Central categories: *hierophany* (manifestation of the sacred), *axis mundi* (the cosmic center), *illud tempus* (the strong time of myth), homo religiosus, sacred space vs profane space. Eliade headed the Chicago Divinity School History of Religions program 1957-1986, training generations of comparative-religion scholars.

## Cross-tradition resonance and the critique

Phenomenology of religion's cross-tradition method — describing religious phenomena from different cultures in a common typological framework — generates the comparative-religion vocabulary still in use ([axis-mundi], [hierophany], [sacred-time], [creation-as-return]). The school's claim that religious experience is *sui generis* (irreducible to other explanations) provided 20th-c. comparative religion with its disciplinary self-justification against reductive psychology and sociology of religion. Eliade's "patterns" (sky god, earth mother, deluge, dying-rising god) became the comparative-religion lingua franca.

The school has been subjected to severe critique since the 1980s. Jonathan Z. Smith (*Map Is Not Territory* 1978; *To Take Place* 1987) argued that Eliade's "patterns" are scholarly *constructions* projected onto disparate materials, not pre-existing structures. The post-1980 critical-religious-studies generation (Russell McCutcheon, Tim Fitzgerald, Ivan Strenski) has argued phenomenology of religion is a covert theological program — *sui generis* religion is an apologetic claim, not a methodological neutral. Modern religious studies departments now teach phenomenology of religion historically rather than methodologically — as the dominant 1920-1980 paradigm subsequently superseded. Yet its categorical vocabulary remains in use; Otto's *numinous* and Eliade's *hierophany* survive in working comparative discourse.

The school converges with [[philosophy-pragmatism-james-dewey|William James's *Varieties of Religious Experience*]] (1902) in foregrounding *experience* rather than *doctrine*; James and Otto both work from the same Schleiermacherian Romantic-Protestant heritage of religion-as-feeling. Eliade's homo religiosus runs parallel to Carl Jung's archetypal psychology (Eliade and Jung corresponded extensively; both were participants in the Eranos conferences at Ascona 1933+).
