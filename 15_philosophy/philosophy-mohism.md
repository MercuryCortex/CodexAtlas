# Mohism (Mòjiā 墨家)

The principal alternative to Confucianism during the Warring States period of Chinese philosophy (-475 to -221) and arguably the most internally rigorous of the pre-imperial Chinese schools. Founded by Mòzi (Mò Dí, c. -470 to -390), Mohism articulated a consequentialist ethics, a rigorous logic + epistemology, a distinctive theology, and a state-organizational program that put it in direct programmatic opposition to the Confucian-Mencian project.

## The ten doctrines

The Mohist program is organized as ten paired chapters (each given in three versions in the surviving Mozi text — the *Three Books* arrangement):

- **Jiān ài (兼愛)** — *universal love* / *impartial concern*. The principal Mohist ethical doctrine: one should benefit others impartially, not preferentially according to family proximity. Direct rejection of the Confucian doctrine of graded love (*qīnqīn*).
- **Fēi gōng (非攻)** — *against offensive war*. Wars of aggression are massively harmful; the Mohist organization specialized in defensive military assistance to small states under attack.
- **Shàng xián (尚賢)** — *exalting the worthy*. Government office should be allocated by capacity, not hereditary status.
- **Shàng tóng (尚同)** — *exalting unity*. Authority structures should produce uniform standards of right + wrong, with the Son of Heaven as the apex.
- **Tiān zhì (天志)** — *the will of Heaven*. There is a moral will inhering in Heaven; Heaven loves all people impartially; punishments + rewards reflect Heaven's response to action.
- **Míng guǐ (明鬼)** — *understanding ghosts/spirits*. Spirits and ghosts are real, observe human conduct, and reward + punish accordingly.
- **Fēi yuè (非樂)** — *against music*. State expenditure on music + ritual is wasteful; the resources should be redirected to general welfare.
- **Fēi mìng (非命)** — *against fatalism*. Human action makes a real difference; fate is a destructive doctrine that excuses inaction.
- **Jié yòng (節用)** — *moderation in use*. State + personal expenditure should be minimized to what produces real benefit.
- **Jié zàng (節葬)** — *moderation in burial*. Confucian elaborate-burial practice is wasteful; simple burials should be the standard.

## The Later Mohist Canons

A distinct strand of Mohist thought — the *Later Mohist Canons* (Mòjīng 墨經), preserved in chapters 40-45 of the received Mozi — develops one of the most rigorous logical and epistemological traditions of pre-imperial China. The Canons treat definitions, deductive structure, optics, mechanics, and arithmetic. The optical chapter contains the earliest known camera-obscura description; the mechanical chapter treats levers and balance. The Later Mohists are unique among the pre-imperial schools in pursuing a logical-scientific program for its own sake.

## The Han suppression and the modern revival

Mohism declined sharply under the imperial unifications. The Han adoption of Confucianism as state ideology + the Qin-Han military monopoly removed the social conditions for the Mohist movement (small-state defense, anti-aristocratic merit hiring). The school had no surviving institutional carrier through late imperial China; it was preserved only in the Mozi text. The modern revival — late-Qing scholars (Sun Yirang), Republican-period philosophers (Hu Shi, Fung Yu-lan), and post-1949 PRC historiography (which valued Mohism as a proto-egalitarian, anti-aristocratic, anti-ritualistic tradition) — has restored Mohism to a major position in the study of Chinese philosophy.
