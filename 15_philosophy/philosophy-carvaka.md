# Cārvāka / Lokāyata

The materialist, anti-Vedic, sense-empiricist school of classical Indian philosophy — the only continuously-attested materialist tradition in pre-modern South Asian thought. The school's principal sūtra — the *Bṛhaspati-sūtra* (attributed to the deified guru Bṛhaspati) — survives only in fragments quoted hostilely by opponents. The school is consequently known almost entirely through what its rivals (Buddhist, Jain, Mīmāṃsā, Vedānta) said about it.

## The doctrines

The reconstructed Cārvāka positions:

- **Only the four elements** (earth, water, fire, air) constitute reality. There is no fifth element (ākāśa is denied), no soul (ātman), no liberation (mokṣa), no afterlife, no karma.
- **Consciousness is a product of elemental combination** — analogous to the intoxicating power that arises when grain, water, and yeast are combined to produce alcohol (the famous Cārvāka analogy, paraphrased in multiple hostile sources).
- **Perception is the only valid means of knowledge** (*pratyakṣa-pramāṇa*). Inference (*anumāna*) is rejected as logically infirm; testimony (*śabda*) is rejected as inheriting the unreliability of its sources; the Vedas in particular are dismissed as the work of priests' self-interest.
- **The Vedas were composed by knaves and fools** — the most-quoted Cārvāka aphorism. The threefold designation of Brahmin priests as *thieves*, *fools*, and *demons* recurs in opponents' citations.
- **The only proper end of life is pleasure** in this world — there being no other world to consider. Wealth, kāma (sense-enjoyment), and the avoidance of suffering exhaust the appropriate human aims.

## The peculiar visibility

The school is paradoxically well-attested precisely because its rivals devoted detailed refutations to its positions. Every major doxographical text — Mādhava's *Sarva-darśana-saṃgraha* (14th c.), Haribhadra's *Ṣaḍ-darśana-samuccaya* (Jain), the Buddhist *Tattvasaṃgraha* — opens with an extended refutation of Cārvāka. The level of philosophical sophistication required to refute Cārvāka contributed substantially to the development of Indian epistemology (*pramāṇa*-theory) — the school's challenge that inference is unreliable forced the rival schools to develop rigorous accounts of valid inference.

## Why no surviving texts

The most likely explanation for the loss of the *Bṛhaspati-sūtra* is the asymmetry of manuscript preservation in pre-modern India: continuous Brahmanical and Jain monastic scribal traditions preserved the texts that supported their respective communities; no comparable institution preserved Cārvāka. The school had no monastic order, no temple complex, no patron-noble lineage. When the last private Cārvāka teacher died, the manuscript line ended.
