Stoicism was founded by [[zeno-of-citium]] (c. 334–262 BCE) in Athens and systematized by [[chrysippus]] (c. 279–206 BCE), who gave the school its mature logical and physical framework. Its later Roman expressions — [[epictetus]], [[seneca]], and [[marcus-aurelius-emperor]] — represent the ethical tradition at its most practical and personally demanding.

## Core Doctrine

The Stoic Logos (rational principle/divine reason) pervades and orders all reality — it is simultaneously the physical law governing the cosmos, the principle of rational thought in human minds, and the ethical standard by which the good life is measured. Logos is active, material, and immanent: it is the fire/breath (pneuma) that gives form and coherence to all things.

The Stoic sage (sophos) is one who has achieved complete alignment with the Logos — who desires nothing the Logos does not provide, who judges correctly about what is truly good (virtue alone) versus what is "preferred indifferent" (health, wealth, reputation). The four Stoic passions (pleasure, pain, desire, fear) are distortions produced by false judgments; they are eliminated by correct understanding, not repression.

**Cosmopolitanism**: Because all rational beings participate in the same Logos, all are citizens of a single rational order (kosmopolis). This is the first articulation of universal human dignity grounded in shared rational nature.

## Cross-Tradition Connections

### MASSIVE WIN — The Stoic Logos → Johannine Logos

[[philo-of-alexandria]] (c. 20 BCE–50 CE) synthesized the Stoic Logos with the Hebrew account of creation (Genesis 1, "And God spoke..."). In Philo, the Logos is the divine mind's first speech-act — the blueprint through which the world was made. This Philonic synthesis entered the Johannine prologue directly: "In the beginning was the Logos, and the Logos was with God, and the Logos was God" ([[logos-johannine]]).

The [[logos-stoic]] and the Johannine Logos are not merely analogous — one is the philosophical ancestor of the other, via Philo. Stoic natural philosophy was baptized and became the theological foundation of the Gospel of John and thus of orthodox Christology. Catholic doctrine that Christ is the eternal Word/Reason of God is Stoic natural philosophy in theological dress.

### Cosmopolitanism → Modern International Law

Stoic cosmopolitanism (all humans as co-citizens of one rational order) → Roman Stoic jurists' concept of *ius gentium* (law of peoples) → Hugo Grotius (1625) grounding international law in natural reason → the Enlightenment concept of natural rights → the UN Declaration of Human Rights (1948). The philosophical foundation of modern international law is Stoic.

### The Stoic Sage and the Buddhist Arhat

The Stoic ethical ideal — the sage who achieves perfect equanimity (ataraxia) through correct rational judgment and is no longer moved by external events — is structurally identical to the Buddhist arhat who achieves nirvana through right understanding and is no longer moved by craving. Both ideals were codified within roughly 50 years of each other (Stoicism: c. 300 BCE; early Buddhism: c. 250 BCE). Both insist that the emotions that disturb tranquility are not given facts of life but products of false belief/ignorance that correct understanding dissolves.

The Indo-Greek kingdoms (after Alexander's campaigns, c. 323 BCE onward) created the first documented zone of direct Greek-Indian philosophical contact. The Buddhist monk Nagasena debated King Milinda (Menander, Greek Bactrian king) in the *Milindapanha* — direct philosophical dialogue. It is not established that Stoic and Buddhist thinkers influenced each other, but they were contemporaries, were aware of each other's civilizations, and arrived at the same ethical destination.

### The Four Passions and the Brahmaviharas

The Stoic four passions (pleasure, pain, desire, fear) and their correction through equanimity maps structurally onto the Buddhist four Brahmaviharas (lovingkindness, compassion, equanimity [upekkha], sympathetic joy) — both are frameworks for achieving the same affective transformation, one through reason, one through cultivation. The parallel may reflect the Indo-Greek contact zone.

## [[moral-stoic-virtue-ethics]]

The [[moral-golden-rule-cross-tradition]] appears in Stoicism in its cosmopolitan form: if all persons share in the Logos, harm to any is a violation of the rational order one participates in. Marcus Aurelius: "We are made for cooperation, like feet, like hands, like eyelids" (*Meditations* 2.1).
