# Epicureanism

## Identity

Epicureanism is the Hellenistic philosophical school founded by [[epicurus]] of Samos (-341 to -270) in the garden he purchased on the outskirts of Athens c. -307 — the *Kepos* ("the Garden"), which gave the school its name and its communal character. One of the four great Hellenistic schools (with the Stoa, the Academy, and Pyrrhonian Skepticism), it is the principal ancient articulation of a fully **materialist, anti-providential, therapeutic** philosophy: a physics of atoms and void put wholly in the service of a single practical goal — the removal of the fears and false beliefs that disturb human life.

Its structure is a single argument in three stages. The **physics** ([[philosophy-atomism-greek]], inherited from Democritus and given its complete surviving exposition in Lucretius's *De Rerum Natura*, c. -55) establishes that the cosmos is atoms moving in the void, without creator, providence, or purpose. The **canonic** (epistemology) grounds knowledge in sensation, prolepsis (preconception), and feeling. On this base the **ethics** builds the school's telos: *ataraxia* (freedom from mental disturbance) and *aponia* (absence of bodily pain), reached by disciplined desire and by dissolving the two great fears — of the gods and of death.

The school compressed its therapy into the **tetrapharmakos** ("four-part cure"), preserved in Philodemus's Herculaneum papyrus and summarizing the first four *Kyriai Doxai* (Principal Doctrines): *the god is not to be feared; death is nothing to worry about; what is good is easy to get; what is terrible is easy to endure.* The "Epicurean" popular stereotype of luxurious sensual indulgence is an ancient hostile caricature: the historical Garden taught a modest, communal, disciplined life and admitted women and slaves as members — unusual for an Athenian school.

## In the doctrine of the Garden

**Pleasure as the absence of disturbance.** Epicurus is a hedonist in that pleasure is the criterion and end of the good life, but the highest pleasure is *katastematic* (the settled state of tranquility and painlessness), not *kinetic* (the pleasures of active gratification). Once pain and fear are removed, pleasure cannot be increased, only varied (*Kyriai Doxai* 3, 18; Cicero, *De Finibus* 1-2, a hostile but informative witness). Desires are sorted into the natural-and-necessary (to be satisfied), the natural-but-unnecessary (moderated), and the empty/vain (to be extinguished) — the discipline that makes "what is good easy to get."

**Death is nothing to us.** Because the soul is a mortal atomic compound that disperses at death, death is not an experienced state: "when we exist death is not present, and when death is present we do not exist" (Letter to Menoeceus 124-125; *Kyriai Doxai* 2). The fear of death — and of post-mortem punishment — is therefore groundless, and its removal is the centerpiece of Epicurean therapy. This is the school's most-polemicized doctrine in antiquity.

**The gods in the intermundia.** Epicurus did not deny the gods; he relocated them. They are blessed, immortal, and perfectly self-sufficient, dwelling in the *metakosmia* (Latin *intermundia*, the spaces between worlds), and precisely because they are perfectly happy they neither made the cosmos nor govern it (*Kyriai Doxai* 1; Letter to Herodotus). The position is **anti-providentialism, not atheism**: piety is retained, providence denied. Fearing the gods' anger is the second great source of needless disturbance.

**Friendship and withdrawal.** "Of all the things that wisdom provides for the blessedness of the complete life, by far the greatest is the possession of friendship" (*Kyriai Doxai* 27). The Garden was a friendship-community that counselled political withdrawal — *lathe biosas*, "live unnoticed" — as the condition of tranquility, in deliberate contrast to the Stoic and civic ideal of public engagement.

**Transmission.** Of Epicurus's roughly 300 rolls almost nothing survives directly except three letters, the *Kyriai Doxai*, and the *Vatican Sayings* (all via Diogenes Laertius, *Lives* X), plus the carbonized Herculaneum rolls of Philodemus and fragments of Epicurus's own *On Nature*. The complete system survives only in Lucretius's Latin poem, recovered by Poggio Bracciolini in 1417 and transmitted into early-modern materialism through Gassendi, Hobbes, and Spinoza.

## Cross-tradition significance

Epicureanism sits at three cross-tradition slots, and in each the honest reading is different.

**Anti-providentialism — a genuine polemical opposition, not a parallel.** The Epicurean indifferent gods and the Stoic providential Logos ([[logos-stoic]]) are the two poles the entire Hellenistic providence-debate ([[providence-and-fate]]) is organized around. This is not a case of convergence or transmission but of a live, self-conscious rivalry: the two schools defined themselves against each other in Athens for centuries. Epicureanism thereby became, in later Western thought, the ancestral name for every denial of providence — the reason "Epicurean" hardened into a byword for impiety in patristic literature (Paul disputes "Epicurean and Stoic philosophers" at Athens, Acts 17:18) and, most consequentially, the philosophical seed recovered by Gassendi and the Enlightenment *philosophes*. Marx's doctoral dissertation (1841) is precisely on the Democritus-Epicurus difference.

**Mortalism — independent convergence.** The Epicurean denial of the immortal soul places the school on a cross-tradition axis with Indian [[philosophy-carvaka]] (materialist Lokayata), with the Sadducean denial of resurrection, and, on a very different metaphysical basis, with Buddhist [[anatman-no-self]]. These are **independent confrontations**, not a transmitted doctrine: the Epicurean version is materialist and physiological (soul-atoms disperse), the Carvaka version likewise materialist, the Buddhist version anti-substantialist and soteriological. The shared move — rejection of a persisting personal soul — is real; the metaphysics behind it is not the same, and no historian claims a line of descent among them.

**Ataraxia — shared Hellenistic goal, contested wider parallel.** The therapeutic goal of *ataraxia* is common to all three Hellenistic schools (Epicurean, Stoic, Pyrrhonian) and structurally mirrors the equanimity (*upekkha*) of Buddhist and the tranquillity of other Indian liberation-traditions. But the transmission case that exists for **Pyrrhonism** — Pyrrho travelled to India with Alexander and is argued (Beckwith 2015) to have absorbed a Buddhist method — has **no counterpart for Epicurus**, who did not travel east and whose ataraxia is derived internally from atomist physics. For Epicureanism the Buddhist parallel is best read as convergence within the shared "philosophy as therapy of the soul" idiom of the Axial-to-Hellenistic world (Nussbaum, *The Therapy of Desire*, 1994), not as contact.

## Disputes

- **Relation to [[philosophy-atomism-greek]] — one node or two?** The physics of atoms, void, the swerve (*clinamen*), and the mortal soul is treated in the atomism node, which spans the whole lineage Leucippus → Democritus → Epicurus → Lucretius. This node covers the *school* — the ethical, theological, and communal system the Garden built on that physics. The division follows the standard scholarly distinction between Democritean natural philosophy and the Epicurean ethical reframing (the swerve, the intermundia theology, ataraxia); the two nodes are reciprocally wired. If the project later prefers a single "Epicurean" hub, this node and the atomism node's Epicurean sections should be merged.
- **The "Epicurean trilemma" belongs to the theodicy hub, not here.** The argument "is God willing to prevent evil but not able? …" survives only in Lactantius (*De ira Dei* 13, 4th c. CE), a hostile source, and its attribution to Epicurus is genuinely uncertain (it may be Lactantius's reconstruction or a Skeptical argument retrojected). It is handled at [[theodicy]] under [[epicurus]] and is deliberately not asserted here as a secure Epicurean fragment.
- **The *apikoros* etymology.** The Talmudic/Mishnaic *apikoros* (Mishnah Sanhedrin 10:1 — the heretic excluded from the world to come) is popularly derived from Epicurus's name, and the semantic fit (denial of providence and the afterlife) is close. But some scholars derive it from the Aramaic/Hebrew root *p-q-r* ("to be licentious / throw off restraint") rather than directly from the philosopher; the derivation is disputed and is flagged, not asserted.
