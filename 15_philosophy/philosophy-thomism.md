# Thomism

The philosophical-theological school deriving from Thomas Aquinas (1225-1274), whose *Summa Theologiae* (1265-1274) and *Summa Contra Gentiles* (c. 1259-1265) achieved the most influential single synthesis of Aristotelian philosophy with Catholic Christian doctrine in the Latin West (Kerr 2002). Distinguishing positions: the *Five Ways* of natural theology; the real distinction between essence and existence (*esse*) in finite beings; the analogy of being (*analogia entis*) governing predication of God; the *quinque viae* + *quia* proofs of God's existence; hylomorphic anthropology + the rational soul as substantial form of the body; natural law as participation of human reason in eternal law; the doctrine of the beatific vision as supernatural end of human nature.

## Historical development

Aquinas's synthesis emerged from the 12th-13th c. Latin recovery of Aristotle — itself mediated through the Arabic translation movement (see [[philosophy-falsafa]]) — and from his Dominican training under Albertus Magnus at Cologne. Within four years of his death (1274), Aquinas's positions were officially condemned by the Bishop of Paris (1277 Tempier condemnations, including 16 theses Aquinas had defended) — yet Aquinas was canonized in 1323 and the condemnations rescinded for him in 1325. The Dominican Order adopted Thomism as its official doctrine (1278); the school became one pole of the medieval *via antiqua* (Thomist + Scotist realism) against the *via moderna* (Ockhamist nominalism).

The Council of Trent (1545-1563) elevated the *Summa Theologiae* to the second place of honor on the altar beside Scripture during deliberations. *Second Scholasticism* — the Iberian Jesuit-Dominican Thomism of Vitoria, Soto, Cajetan, Suárez (c. 1500-1700) — produced the international-law tradition that founds modern law-of-nations theory. Leo XIII's encyclical *Aeterni Patris* (1879) made Neo-Thomism the official philosophical-theological standard of Catholic seminaries until Vatican II (1962-1965), producing 20th-c. figures Maritain, Gilson, Garrigou-Lagrange, Bernard Lonergan, Elizabeth Anscombe, Alasdair MacIntyre, John Finnis (Kerr 2002).

## Cross-tradition resonance

Aquinas's *Five Ways* show structural parallels to Ibn Sīnā's *burhān al-ṣiddīqīn* (proof of the truthful) and to al-Ghazālī's modal-cosmological arguments. The third way (contingency-necessity) is closest to Ibn Sīnā's necessary-vs-possible-being argument as transmitted via the Latin translations of the *Kitāb al-Shifā'* through Toledo. The doctrine of *esse* (the act of being) — Aquinas's most original metaphysical contribution — adapts Ibn Sīnā's essence/existence distinction but inverts its Avicennan accidentality reading. *Analogia entis* answers the same predication problem the [[philosophy-mutazila]] addressed with their negative-attributes doctrine: how do finite predicates apply to an infinite God without compromising divine unity?

The Aristotelian hylomorphic doctrine of the soul-as-substantial-form runs structurally parallel to Hindu Sāṃkhya's *puruṣa-prakṛti* and to certain Buddhist [[philosophy-yogacara]] readings of consciousness-mind-form. The Thomist *natural law* tradition — human practical reason participating in eternal law — has structural cousins in Stoic *koinos logos* (universal reason: [[philosophy-stoicism]]) and in Confucian *li* as cosmically-grounded normativity (see [[philosophy-confucianism]]). The 20th-c. Maritain-Gilson reception built natural law into the 1948 [document-udhr-universal-declaration-of-human-rights|UDHR] drafting through Jacques Maritain's UNESCO involvement.
