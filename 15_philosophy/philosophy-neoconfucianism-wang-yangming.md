# Wang Yangming Neo-Confucianism

The principal Ming-dynasty (1368-1644) philosophical alternative to Cheng-Zhu *lixue*, founded by Wang Shouren (1472-1529), known by his style-name Yangming. Where [[philosophy-neoconfucianism-zhu-xi|Cheng-Zhu]] *lixue* (study of principle) seeks moral cultivation through gradual *gewu* (investigation of things) in the external world, Wang's *xinxue* (study of mind/heart) locates the source of moral knowledge *internally*: every human being possesses *liangzhi* (良知, innate knowledge / pure knowing), an immediate intuitive moral awareness that needs only to be *uncovered* by clearing away the obstructions of selfish desire (Tu 1976; Ivanhoe 2002).

## The biographical turning point

Wang Yangming's youth (chronicled by Tu 1976) is a dramatic intellectual passage: trained in Cheng-Zhu orthodoxy, he famously spent seven days "investigating" a bamboo plant in his father's garden — attempting Zhu Xi's *gewu* on the principle (*li*) of the bamboo — and collapsed in illness without insight. After successive Daoist and Buddhist experiments, the breakthrough came in 1508 during political exile in Longchang (Guizhou): the awakening that "*xin ji li ye*" — *mind is principle*. The principle one seeks externally is already present in the mind; *gewu* is not investigation of external things but *rectification of mind's relation to them*.

## Three defining theses

- **Mind is principle** (*xin ji li ye*): the source of moral knowledge is not external pattern requiring research but the mind's own *liangzhi*. The Cheng-Zhu external-investigation program rests on a category error.
- **Innate knowledge** (*liangzhi*): every person possesses an immediate intuitive knowledge of moral right and wrong, independent of formal study. (The term derives from Mencius 7A.15: "what one knows without reflection is *liangzhi*".) Liangzhi is identical with the original mind (*benxin*) and with the Confucian "heart" (*xin*).
- **The unity of knowledge and action** (*zhixing heyi*): genuine moral knowledge is constitutively action-oriented; one cannot truly *know* a moral truth and fail to act on it. The Cheng-Zhu sequence ("study first, then act") splits what is metaphysically a single act.

## Transmission and impact

In late-Ming China, Wang Yangming's school — particularly the Taizhou branch (Wang Gen 1483-1541, Li Zhi 1527-1602) — produced radical egalitarian and individualist articulations of the *liangzhi* doctrine that destabilized the Cheng-Zhu examination-state orthodoxy. The school survived Qing-era marginalization and re-emerged through 20th-c. Confucian revival figures (Liang Shuming, Xiong Shili, Mou Zongsan). In Korea, Hwadam Sŏ Kyŏngdŏk (1489-1546) developed a related *xinxue*. In Japan, *Yōmeigaku* (Yōmei = Yangming) was transmitted by Nakae Tōju (1608-1648) and Kumazawa Banzan (1619-1691); Ōshio Heihachirō's 1837 Osaka rising and the Meiji Restoration loyalists' justification of imperial-restoration action both drew on the unity-of-knowledge-and-action doctrine.

## Cross-tradition resonance

The *liangzhi* doctrine — moral knowledge as immediate intuitive uncovering rather than discursive acquisition — runs structurally parallel to the [[philosophy-pratyabhijna-recognition|Kashmir Śaiva *pratyabhijñā* (recognition)]] doctrine: liberation is recognition of what one already is, not attainment of new content. Plato's *Meno* doctrine of *anamnesis* (knowledge as recollection of what the soul already possesses) makes the third pillar of this cross-tradition convergence. Wang's polemic against Zhu Xi's gradualist *gewu* parallels the Zen *sudden enlightenment* (dunwu, kenshō) critique of gradualist Buddhist cultivation; Wang explicitly drew on Chan/Zen meditation in his youth and the school's enemies regularly accused it of crypto-Buddhism.

The unity-of-knowledge-and-action thesis prefigures certain modern pragmatist positions ([philosophy-pragmatism-james-dewey|James-Dewey]: belief is fundamentally a habit of action) and aspects of Existentialist authenticity (Heidegger, Sartre: ungenuine knowing-without-being-able-to-act is a category mistake). The 20th-c. Kyoto School (Nishida Kitarō, Nishitani Keiji) drew on Yōmeigaku in articulating their absolute-nothingness ontology. Some recent comparative scholarship (Slingerland 2014 *Trying Not to Try*) reads *liangzhi* as a Confucian articulation of *wuwei* (effortless action) shared with [[philosophy-daoism-philosophical|Daoist]] and Zen traditions.
