# Ājīvika

The "vanished Indian religion" — the strict-determinist *śramaṇa* school founded by Makkhali Gosāla (Sanskrit Maskariputra Gośāla, c. -540 to -480), contemporary with the Buddha and Mahāvīra, attested in the Buddhist *Sāmaññaphala-sutta* (Dīgha Nikāya 2) as one of the six heterodox teachers of late-Vedic-period Magadha. The school's defining doctrine: *niyati*, absolute cosmic fate. Every event — including every act of every being — is fixed by an impersonal cosmic determination; no human or divine effort can alter the predetermined course of any being's transmigratory career (Basham 1951).

## The core doctrine

Gosāla's *niyati* doctrine — reported through hostile Buddhist and Jain doxographies (Ājīvika primary sources do not survive) — states:

> *Natthi hetu, natthi paccayo sattānaṃ saṃkilesāya, ahetū apaccayā sattā saṃkilissanti.*
> "There is no cause, there is no condition for the defilement of beings; without cause, without condition, beings are defiled. There is no cause, there is no condition for the purification of beings; without cause, without condition, beings are purified." (Sāmaññaphala-sutta, DN 2.20)

Every being is destined to traverse a fixed number of births (Buddhist sources report 8,400,000 *mahākalpa* aeons in Gosāla's scheme) and then attain liberation automatically — neither karma nor effort can shorten or extend the cycle. The metaphor: the soul is like a ball of yarn that, once thrown, will unroll completely — no act can stop it short or extend it.

## Historical trajectory

The school enjoyed substantial state patronage under the Maurya emperor Aśoka's grandfather Bindusāra (3rd c. BCE) and under Aśoka's son Daśaratha (Barabar caves epigraphy 261 BCE). It maintained recognizable presence in north India through the Gupta period and migrated south, surviving as a Tamil-speaking school through the medieval Tamil philosophical tradition into the 14th c. CE. Substantial Tamil philosophical works (now lost) and references in Chola-era inscriptions document continued existence. Tamil Cīvaka literature suggests a Tamil-Ājīvika philosophical fusion. After the 14th c. the school disappears as a self-identifying community (Basham 1951; Bronkhorst 2007).

## Cross-tradition resonance

The Ājīvika *niyati* doctrine is the most-thoroughgoing causal-determinist position in classical Indian philosophy and one of the most-thoroughgoing in world philosophy. Its structural close relatives are the [[philosophy-asharite-occasionalism|Ashʿarite occasionalism]] (Islamic, 10th c.+) — where every event is directly produced by God's eternal will; the [[philosophy-stoicism|Stoic]] cosmic *heimarmenē* (fate) doctrine — where every event is determined by the providential cosmic Logos; and certain readings of Spinoza's 17th-c. *Ethics* — where every event is necessitated by the eternal nature of substance. The Calvinist double-predestination doctrine (16th c.) is the closest Christian theological cousin: every soul's eternal destiny is fixed by divine decree from before the foundation of the world.

The Buddhist *Sāmaññaphala-sutta* preserves the most-detailed account of the doctrine precisely *because* the Buddha rejected it as a denial of the moral efficacy of action that grounds the Buddhist program. The Jain dispute with Ājīvika is even more central — Mahāvīra reportedly broke with Gosāla over the question (Jain *Bhagavatī-sūtra*). The early Buddhist preservation of the Ājīvika position thus generates a remarkable feature of the comparative-religion record: the systematic preservation of a heterodox school by its principal polemic opponents. The contemporary problem of free will and determinism in [[philosophy-process-theology|process theology]] and in compatibilist analytic philosophy (Dennett, Frankfurt) inherits the same conceptual problem the Buddha and Mahāvīra faced.
