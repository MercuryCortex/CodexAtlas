# Analytic theism (the analytic revival of philosophy of religion)

## Identity

**Analytic theism** — more precisely the *analytic revival of philosophy of religion* — is the movement, beginning in the anglophone philosophical world around 1960 and continuing today, in which the tools of **analytic philosophy** (formal and modal logic, probability theory, post-foundationalist epistemology, careful conceptual analysis) were turned to the *defense* of theistic belief and the *reconstruction* of natural theology. It is not a single doctrine or church but a **methodological movement** with a shared toolkit and a cluster of signature results, centered on **Alvin Plantinga** (1932–2024, Calvin College / Notre Dame), **Richard Swinburne** (b. 1934, Oxford), and later **William Lane Craig**, **Peter van Inwagen**, **Eleonore Stump**, and **Robert M. Adams**.

The movement's origin is *dialectical*. Mid-century analytic philosophy was hostile to theology: A. J. Ayer's logical-positivist verification principle (*Language, Truth and Logic*, 1936) held that "God exists" is literally *meaningless* (unverifiable), and Antony Flew's "Theology and Falsification" (1950) pressed that religious assertions die "the death of a thousand qualifications." The volume that crystallized this challenge, **Flew & MacIntyre's *New Essays in Philosophical Theology* (1955)**, is the movement's negative starting point. The revival is what happened when — after positivism's own verification principle collapsed as self-refuting — analytic *theists* took the same rigorous tools and used them constructively. Plantinga's **"Advice to Christian Philosophers"** (his 1984 inaugural Notre Dame address) is its manifesto: Christian philosophers should work with intellectual autonomy and not accept the naturalist's framing of the questions.

## In the analytic-philosophy-of-religion corpus

The movement is best identified by its signature results rather than a creed:

- **Reformed epistemology** (Plantinga, Nicholas Wolterstorff, William Alston). The claim, launched in *Faith and Rationality* (1983), that belief in God can be **"properly basic"** — rationally held *non-inferentially*, without the support of arguments — just as belief in other minds, the past, or the external world is basic. This attacks the **"evidentialist objection"** (Clifford, Flew): the assumption that theistic belief is irrational unless propped on evidence. In *Warranted Christian Belief* (2000) Plantinga completes the program: warrant is proper cognitive function aimed at truth, and if theism is *true*, belief in God (via a *sensus divinitatis*, borrowed from Calvin) is very likely warranted — so the *de jure* challenge ("even if true, it's irrational") cannot be sustained independently of the *de facto* question of truth.

- **The free-will defense** (Plantinga, *God, Freedom, and Evil*, 1974). Against the *logical* problem of evil (that "God is omnipotent and wholly good" and "evil exists" are jointly inconsistent), Plantinga argues it is *possible* that God could not create a world with moral good but no moral evil, because every creaturely essence God might instantiate might suffer **"transworld depravity."** The defense is modest by design — it establishes only *possibility*, hence *consistency*, not the actual reason for evil — but it is widely credited with ending the logical form of the argument from evil as a live threat (see [[theodicy]]).

- **The modal ontological argument** (Plantinga, *The Nature of Necessity*, 1974). A rehabilitation of Anselm using possible-worlds semantics and S5 modal logic: *if* it is *possible* that a being of "maximal greatness" (necessary existence + maximal excellence in every world) exists, then it exists necessarily and so actually. Plantinga concedes the argument does not *compel* the skeptic (its key premise — that maximal greatness is *possibly* instantiated — can be denied) but holds it establishes theism as rationally acceptable.

- **Probabilistic / Bayesian natural theology** (Swinburne, *The Existence of God*, 1979). Rather than deductive proof, a **cumulative inductive case**: each traditional argument (cosmological, teleological, from consciousness, from providence, from religious experience) raises the probability of theism, and their conjunction, weighed by Bayes's theorem and a *simplicity* criterion (theism as the simplest possible hypothesis — one being of infinite power, knowledge, freedom), makes God's existence *more probable than not*.

- **The revived cosmological and design arguments.** Craig's recovery of the medieval **kalām cosmological argument** (*The Kalām Cosmological Argument*, 1979) — everything that begins to exist has a cause; the universe began to exist; therefore the universe has a cause — married to Big-Bang cosmology. And the **fine-tuning argument** (John Leslie, *Universes*, 1989; Robin Collins), that the improbably life-permitting values of the physical constants are evidence of design.

Institutionally the movement is carried by the **Society of Christian Philosophers** (founded 1978, often described as the largest single-field society within American philosophy) and its journal ***Faith and Philosophy*** (from 1984), together with *Religious Studies*, *Philosophia Christi*, and *The International Journal for Philosophy of Religion*.

## Cross-tradition significance

The comparative interest of analytic theism is precisely that it is a **transmission-and-revival** phenomenon inside the Western philosophical-theist stream, and a **contrast case** against its 20th-c. siblings.

- **Transmission (a genuine line of descent).** Unlike a convergent theme such as [[theodicy]], analytic theism is *self-consciously continuous* with older natural theology: it reconstructs Anselm's ontological argument, the Thomist and Leibnizian cosmological arguments (see [[philosophy-thomism]], [[philosophy-scholasticism]]), Paley's design argument, and — via Craig's kalām — a specifically **Islamic *kalām*** inheritance (al-Kindī, al-Ghazālī; see [[philosophy-islamic-kalam-philosophy]]). This is documented textual descent, not typological resemblance. What is new is the *instrument* (modal logic, Bayesianism, post-foundationalist epistemology), not the *arguments*, which are medieval.

- **Contrast with process theology** (see [[philosophy-process-theology]]). Both are 20th-c. rehabilitations of theism after the modern challenge, but by *opposite* strategies. Process theology (Whitehead, Hartshorne, Cobb) *revises* classical theism — surrendering omnipotence for a dipolar, persuasive God — to escape the problem of evil. Analytic theism instead *defends* a broadly classical omniGod and answers the problem of evil argumentatively (the free-will defense). The two are, in effect, the analytic-Anglo and the metaphysical-revisionist wings of the same recovery.

- **Contrast with Continental phenomenology of religion** (see [[philosophy-phenomenology-of-religion]]). The other great 20th-c. philosophical rehabilitation of religion works on the opposite methodological bank: it *describes* the structures of religious experience (Otto's numinous, Eliade's hierophany, the sacred) in the Husserlian lineage, where analytic theism *argues* over propositions and evidence. There is a real point of convergence — reformed epistemology and phenomenology both treat the awareness of God as *non-inferential and immediate* — but the frameworks (properly-basic *belief* vs pre-reflective *experience*) are distinct.

- **Polemical foil for the New Atheism** (see [[event-new-atheism-2004-2010]]). Analytic theism is the standing philosophical interlocutor the New Atheist bestsellers (2004–2010) largely bypassed; the analytic philosophers replied that Dawkins's central objection (an ordered designer is more improbable than what it explains) misunderstands the technical natural-theology literature it does not engage.

## Disputes

- **"Analytic theism" vs "analytic philosophy of religion."** The two are not co-extensive. *Analytic philosophy of religion* is a neutral subfield that includes prominent atheists (J. L. Mackie, *The Miracle of Theism* 1982; Quentin Smith; Graham Oppy; Paul Draper). This node covers specifically the **theistic revival** within that subfield — the constructive program of Plantinga, Swinburne, Craig et al. — while noting the field also contains its analytic critics. A reviewer could reasonably prefer a broader neutral node with the theistic revival as a section; kept focused here because the *revival* is the demand signal.

- **How much is "classical" theism actually defended?** Some analytic theists (Swinburne) explicitly *modify* the classical attributes (a temporal rather than timeless God, contingent omniscience), so labeling the movement a defense of *classical* theism is imprecise; "modest/perfect-being theism" is the more careful description. Brian Davies and analytic Thomists argue Swinburne's "theistic personalism" has already abandoned classical (divine-simplicity) theism.

- **Does reformed epistemology license too much?** The standard objection (the "Great Pumpkin" / religious-diversity objection) is that if belief in God can be *properly basic*, so can any deeply-held religious (or fanatical) belief; the debate over whether Plantinga's warrant condition and *sensus divinitatis* block this is unresolved.
