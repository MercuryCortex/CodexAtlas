# Scholasticism

The dominant philosophical tradition of the medieval Latin Christian universities — characterized by its distinctive *method* (the *quaestio*: pose a question, set out arguments for an apparent answer, set out arguments for the opposite, give a determination, reply to the rejected arguments), its principal source-set (Aristotle as received through Latin translations of the Arabic [[philosophy-falsafa|Falsafa]] tradition, plus the Bible, the Church Fathers, and Augustine), and its institutional locus (the great medieval universities, founded c. 1100-1250 at Paris, Bologna, Oxford, Cambridge, Padua, Salamanca, and elsewhere).

## Periodization

- **Early Scholasticism** (c. 1050-1200): cathedral-school philosophy. Anselm of Canterbury (1033-1109) — the ontological argument; *Cur Deus Homo* on the atonement. Peter Abelard (1079-1142) — the sic et non method; the early universals controversy. Hugh of St Victor + Peter Lombard (the *Sentences*, which became the standard theology textbook for ~400 years).
- **High Scholasticism** (c. 1200-1350): the great university synthesis after the recovery of Aristotle through Arabic mediation. Thomas Aquinas (1225-1274) — the *Summa Theologiae* + *Summa Contra Gentiles*; the Aristotelian-Christian synthesis. Bonaventure (1217-1274) — the Franciscan Augustinian alternative. John Duns Scotus (c. 1266-1308) — the *univocity of being* doctrine, *haecceitas* (this-ness). William of Ockham (c. 1287-1347) — nominalism, *Ockham's razor*, the via moderna.
- **Late Scholasticism** (c. 1350-1500): the via antiqua (Thomism, Scotism) vs the via moderna (Ockhamism) controversies; the Wegestreit at the universities.
- **Second Scholasticism** (c. 1500-1700): the Iberian revival — Francisco de Vitoria (1483-1546), Domingo de Soto (1494-1560), Francisco Suárez (1548-1617). Vitoria's lectures on natural law and the rights of conquered peoples (*De Indis*, 1539) are foundational texts of international law.
- **Neo-Thomism** (c. 1879-present): the revival sparked by Leo XIII's *Aeterni Patris* (1879); continues in Catholic philosophy departments globally.

## The method

The scholastic *quaestio* (question) is the signature methodological move. A scholastic treatment of any philosophical or theological topic follows:

1. The question is posed.
2. *Videtur quod sic* — arguments are given that appear to require an affirmative answer.
3. *Sed contra* — arguments are given that appear to require a negative answer.
4. *Respondeo dicendum* — the master's determination, with reasoning.
5. *Ad primum*, *ad secundum*, etc. — replies to each of the arguments not adopted in the determination.

The method produced enormous quantity (Aquinas's *Summa Theologiae* alone runs to 3,000+ articles) and exposed every philosophical claim to its strongest opposing arguments before resolution. It is one of the most powerful argumentative methodologies in any tradition.

## The Aristotelian recovery

The 12th-13th c. recovery of Aristotle was not a Greek-to-Latin process but a Greek-to-Arabic-to-Latin process. Toledo (capital of newly-Christian-reconquered Castile, c. 1150-1300) was the principal translation center; Gerard of Cremona, Dominicus Gundissalinus, and others translated dozens of Aristotelian + Arabic-philosophical works into Latin. The "Aristotle" of the 13th-c. Latin universities was an Aristotle filtered through Ibn Sīnā and Ibn Rushd. The 13th-c. "Averroist" controversies are direct consequences of this Arabic mediation.
