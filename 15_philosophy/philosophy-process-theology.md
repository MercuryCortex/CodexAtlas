# Process theology

The 20th-century philosophical-theological school inaugurated by Alfred North Whitehead's *Process and Reality* (1929) and developed by Charles Hartshorne, John B. Cobb Jr, David Ray Griffin, and others. Process theology re-conceives the central theological vocabulary — God, creation, action, redemption — in terms of a process metaphysics in which the ultimate constituents of reality are not substances but momentary *actual occasions* in continuous becoming.

## Whiteheadian metaphysics

The core Whitehead positions:

- **Actual occasions / actual entities** are the ultimate things; they are not substances persisting through change but momentary *concrescences* (coming-togethers) of prior data into a determinate experiencing-event. The world is a continuous succession of such occasions.
- **Prehension**: each actual occasion takes account of (prehends) the prior occasions in its causal past. Prehensions can be either *physical* (taking-in prior actual occasions) or *conceptual* (taking-in eternal objects = abstract forms).
- **Eternal objects**: abstract forms (universals, qualities, relations) that are ingressed into actual occasions; they are the equivalent of Platonic forms in the process framework.
- **God** has two natures: the *primordial nature* (God's eternal envisagement of the entire realm of eternal objects, providing each actual occasion with its initial subjective aim) and the *consequent nature* (God's prehension of every actual occasion as it happens, the world thus being permanently in God).

## The theological positions

Process theology departs from classical theism in three principal ways:

- **Panentheism**, not classical theism: the world is *in* God (God's consequent nature) but God is *more than* the world.
- **Dipolar God**: God has both an eternal-changeless aspect (primordial) and a developing-temporal aspect (consequent). God's primordial nature is the classical-theistic God; God's consequent nature is genuinely affected by what happens in the world. God *suffers with* the world.
- **Persuasive, not coercive, divine action**: God acts in the world by offering each actual occasion an *initial subjective aim* — a possibility of optimal becoming. The actual occasion is free to accept or modify or refuse this lure. God cannot determine what occurs; God can only entice.

## The problem of evil

Process theology offers one of the most-discussed contemporary responses to the problem of evil. Because God's action is persuasive and not coercive, God is not directly responsible for actual occasions' decisions; evil is the consequence of actual occasions' partial or full refusal of the divine lure. The cost is that God is not omnipotent in the classical sense — God cannot prevent any specific evil. The gain is that the problem of evil is genuinely (rather than verbally) resolved: God does what God can do, but cannot determine the world to be otherwise.

## Continuing institutional presence

The Center for Process Studies (founded 1973 at Claremont School of Theology) is the principal continuing institution. The *Process Studies* journal (founded 1971) is the discipline's standard. Process theology has been influential in interfaith dialogue with Buddhism (its non-substantialist metaphysics resonates with Madhyamaka and Yogācāra positions), in liberation theology, and in some ecological theology.

## Cross-tradition connections

- **[[philosophy-madhyamaka]]**: non-substantialist metaphysics; the Cobb-Abe dialogues (John Cobb and Masao Abe, 1970s-1990s) explored the Buddhist-process convergence.
- **[[philosophy-yogacara]]**: the consciousness-stream model.
- **[[philosophy-stoicism]]**: distant comparative axis as immanentist theology.
- **[[doctrine-incarnation]]**: process theology offers a distinctive Christological framework — Christ as the maximally-accepted divine initial aim.
