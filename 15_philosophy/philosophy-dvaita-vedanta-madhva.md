# Dvaita Vedānta

Dvaita Vedānta ("dualist Vedānta"; *dvaita* = "two"), also called **Tattvavāda** ("the doctrine of reals"), is the theistic-realist school of Vedānta systematised by [[madhva|Madhva]] (Ānanda-tīrtha / Pūrṇaprajña, traditionally 1238-1317 CE) on the Karnataka coast. It is the third of the three principal classical Vedāntic positions alongside Śaṅkara's [[philosophy-advaita-vedanta|Advaita]] (strict non-dualism) and Rāmānuja's [[philosophy-vishishtadvaita-vedanta|Viśiṣṭādvaita]] (qualified non-dualism), and it is the most thoroughgoing rejection of monism in the tradition: Brahman — identified specifically with [[vishnu|Viṣṇu]]-Nārāyaṇa — and the souls and world are *eternally distinct realities*, related not by identity but by the absolute dependence of the latter on the former. (Its systematic theology has its own Atlas node at [[doctrine-dvaita-dualism-madhva|Dvaita as doctrine]]; this node is the school / darśana.)

## Core doctrines

- **The *pañca-bheda* (five eternal distinctions)** are the structural centre: God ↔ soul, God ↔ matter, soul ↔ soul, soul ↔ matter, matter ↔ matter. These are not conventional truths or appearances to be sublated (as Advaita would have them) but *real, eternal, intrinsic* features of reality.
- **Svatantra / paratantra (independent vs. dependent reality)**: Brahman alone is *svatantra* (self-dependent, wholly independent); everything else — souls and matter — is *paratantra* (dependent), sustained moment-by-moment by the divine will. Difference plus asymmetric dependence, not difference plus identity, is the whole of the metaphysics.
- **Saguṇa personal Brahman**: Madhva's absolute is not the attribute-less *nirguṇa* Brahman of Advaita but Viṣṇu with eternal auspicious qualities (*kalyāṇa-guṇas*), with [[lakshmi|Lakṣmī]] as his eternal consort — highest among dependent beings and, uniquely, never bound and so never "liberated."
- **The *tāratamya* (eternal gradation of souls)**: souls are eternally ranked, and eternally sorted into the *mukti-yogya* (fit for liberation), the *nitya-saṁsārin* (eternally transmigrating), and the *tamo-yogya* (eternally destined for the lowest darkness). This threefold predestination — including a genuine doctrine of *eternal* damnation (tamo-yogya) — is distinctive within Hinduism.
- **Realist epistemology**: the world is real as perceived; *pratyakṣa*, *anumāna* and *āgama* are the three valid *pramāṇas*, yielding true knowledge of a real world. Liberation is not *jñāna*-identity but *bhakti* and grace — eternal, individuated, graded loving service to Viṣṇu in Vaikuṇṭha.

## In the Dvaitin corpus

The foundational texts are Madhva's [[phase-5-022-madhva-brahma-sutra-bhasya|*Brahma-sūtra-bhāṣya*]], its versified expansion the *Anuvyākhyāna*, the *Gītā-bhāṣya* and *Gītā-tātparya*, the *Viṣṇu-tattva-vinirṇaya* (the systematic statement of the five-fold difference), and the ten *prakaraṇa* treatises on epistemology, ontology and polemics. The school was given its scholastic apparatus by **Jayatīrtha** (c. 1356-1388) in the *Nyāya-sudhā*, and reached its polemical peak with **Vyāsatīrtha** (c. 1460-1539), whose *Nyāyāmṛta* ("Ambrosia of Logic") drew the great Advaitin reply of Madhusūdana Sarasvatī's *Advaita-siddhi*. **Raghavendra Tīrtha** (1595-1671) is the late-classical synthesist. Valerie Stoker (2016) has shown how the 14th-16th-c. Vijayanagara court patronised the Advaita-Dvaita dispute as an instrument of sectarian-political legitimation, complicating any purely philosophical reading of the debate's intensity. The Udupi Kṛṣṇa monastery founded by Madhva remains the living centre of the Mādhva sampradāya.

## Cross-tradition significance

Dvaita's most-discussed cross-tradition feature is its *tāratamya* doctrine of **eternal threefold predestination**, which is one of the closest pre-modern *non-Abrahamic* structural parallels to Calvinist *praedestinatio gemina* (alongside Vallabha's fourfold soul-gradation) (double predestination): some souls are eternally elected, others eternally reprobated, by divine sovereignty rather than by their own merit. B. N. K. Sharma (1962) flagged the comparative-theological singularity, and recent comparative work (Buchta 2014) has examined the parallel with the Reformed tradition with care. The honest framing is **convergence, not transmission**: Madhva (13th c., coastal Karnataka) and Calvin (16th c., Geneva) belong to unrelated traditions with no plausible contact, yet both derive from the premise of absolute divine sovereignty the same structurally identical conclusion — the heterogeneity of eternal destinies. The parallel is typological, not genealogical.

A second, subtler resonance is that Dvaita's **saguṇa personal-God realism** and its insistence on real, eternal distinction-in-relation between God and souls made it one of the Hindu theologies most readily legible to Western Christian comparative theology — taken up by 19th-c. Indologists and most systematically by 20th-c. comparativists reading Madhva as a non-Western theistic-realist interlocutor whose personal God, real world and eternally individuated souls map onto Christian theistic realism far more readily than the Advaitin absolute does. Here too the relation is structural rather than historical; there is no evidence that Christian contact shaped Madhva's system, and the tāratamya doctrine is fully derivable from his own *pañca-bheda* premises.

Within India, the school's realist epistemology allies it structurally with [[philosophy-nyaya-vaisheshika-realism|Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika]] pluralist realism against the shared Advaitin opponent, while its eternal individuated *jīva* makes it the strongest classical assertion of the [[soul-immortality|soul's intrinsic immortality]] on the Indian side of that comparative theme.

## Disputes

The persistent scholarly question is Madhva's **use of sources**. Critics from Advaita onward, and modern philologists, have noted that Madhva cites a large body of *smṛti* and Purāṇic passages — and some texts (e.g. the "Brahma-tarka") — that cannot be located in any independently attested work, raising the charge that some of his scriptural warrants were either lost, otherwise unknown, or in disputed cases fabricated to support the Dvaitin reading. Sarma (2003) treats the question directly and cautiously, distinguishing genuinely lost texts from the polemically motivated accusation; the matter remains unresolved and is the standard critical caveat in Dvaita studies. Second, the historicity of the miracle-laden traditional hagiography (the *Madhva-vijaya* of Nārāyaṇa Paṇḍitācārya) is set aside by modern scholarship, which relies on the doctrinal corpus rather than the *vijaya* for the historical Madhva. On the comparative side, the tāratamya-Calvinism parallel is sometimes overstated in popular writing; the careful literature (Sharma 1962; Buchta 2014) treats it strictly as structural convergence and warns against reading either doctrine as evidence for the other.
