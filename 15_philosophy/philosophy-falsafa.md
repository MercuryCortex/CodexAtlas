# Falsafa

The Islamic philosophical tradition that emerged from the Bayt al-Ḥikma (Baghdad, c. 800-900) Greek-to-Arabic translation movement and developed into the dominant intellectual program of the Islamic Golden Age — an Aristotelian-Neoplatonic synthesis pursued by a series of philosopher-scientists from al-Kindī through Ibn Rushd. The name *falsafa* is the Arabic transliteration of Greek *philosophia*; the practitioner is the *faylasūf*.

## The principal figures

- **Al-Kindī** (c. 800-870, "The Philosopher of the Arabs"): the founding generation; integrated Aristotelian metaphysics with Quranic monotheism; mediated Plotinian-derived material (the so-called *Theology of Aristotle*, in fact a paraphrase of Plotinus's *Enneads* IV-VI) under the misattribution that shaped the Islamic-Aristotelian reception.
- **Al-Fārābī** (c. 872-950, "the Second Teacher" — after Aristotle): the architectural systematizer; his *Virtuous City* applies Platonic political philosophy to Islamic governance; his logic textbooks established the standard Arabic Aristotelian logic curriculum.
- **Ibn Sīnā** (Avicenna, 980-1037): the great synthesizer; the *Shifāʾ* (the *Cure*) is the most-comprehensive Aristotelian-Neoplatonic system in any language. The *Canon of Medicine* (al-Qānūn fī al-Ṭibb) is the standard medieval medical reference for ~600 years.
- **Al-Ghazālī** (1058-1111): the great critic; his *Tahāfut al-Falāsifa* ("Incoherence of the Philosophers", c. 1095) argued that Aristotelian conclusions in metaphysics + cosmology were not philosophically necessary and that several were inconsistent with Islamic revelation. The work ended the dominant Sunni institutional position of falsafa, though it did not end the tradition.
- **Ibn Bājja** (Avempace, c. 1080-1138) + **Ibn Ṭufayl** (c. 1105-1185): the Andalusian generation; Ibn Ṭufayl's philosophical novel *Ḥayy ibn Yaqẓān* (the autodidact philosopher on a desert island) anticipates Defoe and Rousseau by half a millennium.
- **Ibn Rushd** (Averroes, 1126-1198): the great commentator; his comprehensive commentaries on Aristotle (the Short, Middle, and Long Commentaries) defined the Aristotelian reception in Latin Christendom for two centuries. His *Tahāfut al-Tahāfut* ("Incoherence of the Incoherence") replied to al-Ghazālī.

## The doctrines

The core Falsafa positions, as systematized by Ibn Sīnā:

- **The Necessary Being** (*wājib al-wujūd*): a being that exists necessarily and through which all contingent existence is necessitated. The Necessary Being is identified with God; the contingent universe necessarily emanates from the Necessary Being.
- **Eternal universe**: the world is co-eternal with God by necessity of God's nature, not created at a moment in time. This was one of al-Ghazālī's three principal accusations of un-Islamic doctrine.
- **Universal intellect** (*ʿaql al-faʿʿāl*): the Active Intellect is a separate Intelligence that actualizes potential intellects in human minds; human intellectual perfection is union with it. Some readings (Ibn Rushd's interpretation) make the Active Intellect numerically one — a position that read as denying personal immortality; this was the basis of the medieval Latin "Averroist" controversies.

## Transmission to the Latin West

Ibn Rushd + Ibn Sīnā were translated into Latin in 12th-13th c. Toledo and entered the European universities; "Averroism" became a major and controversial strand of 13th-c. Latin philosophy (Siger of Brabant + the Parisian arts faculty); Thomas Aquinas's *Summa Contra Gentiles* and *Summa Theologica* construct their theological project explicitly in dialogue with Averroes. The Latin-Christian Aristotelianism of the high Middle Ages is a direct dependent of falsafa.
