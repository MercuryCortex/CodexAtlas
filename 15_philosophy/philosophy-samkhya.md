# Sāṃkhya

Traditionally regarded as one of the oldest systematic schools (*darśana*) of Indian philosophy, and the foundational **dualist enumerationist** system — the name *sāṃkhya* means "enumeration" / "reckoning," because the school proceeds by counting out the ultimate constituents of reality into **twenty-five *tattvas*** (principles). At its root stand two irreducible realities: **puruṣa**, pure contentless witnessing consciousness, and **prakṛti**, the single unconscious material-and-mental substrate out of which everything else evolves. Every other āstika darśana in the vault has its philosophy node — [[philosophy-nyaya-vaisheshika-realism|Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika]], [[philosophy-mimamsa-purva-ritual-metaphysics|Pūrva Mīmāṃsā]], [[philosophy-advaita-vedanta|Advaita Vedānta]], [[philosophy-vishishtadvaita-vedanta|Viśiṣṭādvaita]] — and Sāṃkhya is the one that supplies the dualist pole of the classical Indian metaphysical field. Its canonical statement is Īśvarakṛṣṇa's *Sāṃkhya-kārikā* (c. 350–450 CE); its legendary founder Kapila is named in the *Mahābhārata* and the *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* but is not a datable historical figure (Larson 1979; Larson & Bhattacharya 1987).

## Core doctrines

- **Puruṣa** — pure consciousness: eternal, inactive, contentless, a mere *witness* (*sākṣin*). Crucially, puruṣa is **plural** — each liberated-in-principle self is a distinct puruṣa (SK 18, argued from the fact that births, deaths, and endowments are distributed, not shared). Puruṣa does nothing and undergoes nothing; it only *seems* to act because it is reflected in prakṛti's most luminous evolute, the intellect.
- **Prakṛti** — the single, eternal, uncaused **material cause** of everything non-conscious, including what we would call the mind. In its unmanifest state (*mūla-prakṛti* / *pradhāna*) it is an undifferentiated equilibrium of three strands.
- **The three guṇas** — prakṛti is constituted of **sattva** (lightness, luminosity, intelligibility), **rajas** (activity, passion, motion), and **tamas** (heaviness, inertia, obscuration). All manifest reality is a shifting proportion of the three; their equilibrium is unmanifest prakṛti, their disturbance is the world. The guṇa theory is Sāṃkhya's most widely borrowed contribution — it is absorbed into the *Bhagavad Gītā*, Āyurveda, and later Hindu thought at large.
- **Satkāryavāda** — the doctrine that the **effect pre-exists in its cause** (SK 9): oil is already latent in the seed, curds in the milk. Creation is therefore *manifestation* (evolution/transformation, *pariṇāma*) of what prakṛti already contains, not production out of nothing. This is the direct antithesis of the Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika *asatkāryavāda*.
- **The 25 tattvas** — from disturbed prakṛti evolves, in order: **mahat / buddhi** (cosmic intellect), **ahaṃkāra** (the I-maker / ego-principle), and from ahaṃkāra the **manas** (mind), the **five sense-capacities** (*jñānendriyas*) and **five action-capacities** (*karmendriyas*), the **five subtle elements** (*tanmātras*), and the **five gross elements** (*mahābhūtas*) — twenty-four evolutes of prakṛti, plus prakṛti itself (23 + 1), with **puruṣa** as the twenty-fifth, non-evolving principle.
- **Liberation (kaivalya)** — bondage is the beginningless *mis-identification* of puruṣa with the workings of prakṛti (above all with buddhi). Liberation is **discriminative knowledge** (*vivekakhyāti*) — puruṣa recognising "I am not this, this is not mine, this is not I" (SK 64). When prakṛti has been fully *seen*, she withdraws "like a dancer who ceases once she has been watched" (SK 59), and puruṣa abides in its own isolated purity: *kaivalya*, "aloneness."
- **Atheism (nir-īśvara)** — classical Sāṃkhya is **non-theistic**: it needs no creator-God, because an eternal prakṛti evolving under its own teleology accounts for the world. This is the sharpest doctrinal difference from its sister school Yoga, which inserts a special puruṣa, *Īśvara*, as an object of meditation (*seśvara* Sāṃkhya).

## In the classical texts

The system as reconstructed is that of Īśvarakṛṣṇa's **Sāṃkhya-kārikā** — seventy-two terse memorial verses that open by asking why, given ordinary and Vedic means of removing suffering, one should seek the "better" method of discriminative knowledge, and close with the claim that the whole teaching was handed down from Kapila through a line of teachers. The kārikā is glossed by the anonymous **Yuktidīpikā** (c. 7th c.), the richest classical commentary, and by Gauḍapāda's *Bhāṣya* and Vācaspati Miśra's **Sāṃkhya-tattva-kaumudī** (c. 9th–10th c.), which became the standard scholastic reading. A later revival works through the pseudo-Kapila **Sāṃkhya-sūtra** (c. 14th c.) and Vijñānabhikṣu's *Sāṃkhya-pravacana-bhāṣya* (16th c.), which tries to soften Sāṃkhya's atheism and pull it toward Vedānta. Sāṃkhya categories are also deployed narratively throughout the **Mokṣadharma** of the *Mahābhārata* and in the *Bhagavad Gītā* (esp. chs. 2–3, 13–14, where the guṇas and the field/knower-of-the-field distinction are Sāṃkhya in idiom), and its metaphysics is presupposed wholesale by the *Yoga-sūtra* of [[patanjali-yoga-sutras-author|Patañjali]] (see [[tradition-samkhya-yoga]]).

## Cross-tradition significance (the non-anti-cosmic dualism)

Sāṃkhya is the **Indian occupant of the consciousness-versus-matter slot** in the cross-tradition study of [[dualism-spirit-matter|spirit-matter dualism]]. A luminous, uninvolved awareness (puruṣa) is categorically distinct from an entangling material substrate (prakṛti); bondage is the awareness's entanglement, and salvation is its **recognition of its own difference** and consequent disentanglement — a "recognition → separation → return-to-self" structure strikingly homologous to the Gnostic drama in which a *pneuma* is caught in *hylē* and freed by *gnōsis*, and to the Platonic soul buried in body. This is one of the standard comparative pairings (the *Sāṃkhya-Yoga* tradition node already flags the Gnostic parallel).

But the honest framing requires the **decisive disanalogy**, and it is what makes Sāṃkhya valuable to the comparison rather than just another instance: in Gnostic and radical-Platonic dualism matter is *evil* or a *prison*, the work of an ignorant demiurge, and the world is something to be despised. In Sāṃkhya **prakṛti is not evil at all**. She is unconscious but benign, and she evolves *teleologically for the very purpose of* puruṣa's liberation — Sāṃkhya's most quoted images (SK 56–59) compare prakṛti to unconscious milk that flows for the calf's nourishment, and to a modest dancer who performs and then withdraws once she has been truly seen. There is no cosmic villain, no fall, no demiurge. Sāṃkhya is therefore the **great non-anti-cosmic dualism** — the control case demonstrating that a rigorous two-principle metaphysics of spirit and matter need not entail contempt for the material world. Any claim of historical transmission is unsupported: the Indo-Greek contact window (c. -300 to -100) makes speculation *possible*, but there is no textual evidence, and the mainstream scholarly view is **independent convergence** on a widely-available structural idea, with the divergence over matter's moral status marking the two developments as genuinely separate (Larson 1979; Burley 2007).

Within India, Sāṃkhya is above all the **dualist foil for [[philosophy-advaita-vedanta|Advaita]] non-dualism**: because it shares the Upaniṣadic vocabulary (ātman-like puruṣa, mokṣa) while reaching the opposite conclusion — two ultimate reals, not one — Śaṅkara devotes one of the most extensive polemics in his *Brahma-sūtra-bhāṣya* (the *pradhāna* refutation, BSBh 2.1–2.2) to demolishing the Sāṃkhya unconscious-first-cause. Against [[philosophy-nyaya-vaisheshika-realism|Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika]] it shares a pluralism of eternal selves but splits on causation (*satkāryavāda* vs *asatkāryavāda*); with [[philosophy-yogacara|Yogācāra]] Buddhism it shares an analytic stratification of the inner instrument (buddhi/ahaṃkāra/manas) and, through its sister school Yoga, a meditative vocabulary (*samādhi*) held in common with Buddhist *jhāna*.

## Disputes

- **Was there a historical Kapila, and a lost "original" Sāṃkhya?** The school claims descent from Kapila through Āsuri and Pañcaśikha, but no pre-kārikā systematic text survives. Scholars reconstruct a *ṣaṣṭitantra* ("sixty-topics") tradition behind Īśvarakṛṣṇa, but its content and date are conjectural (Larson & Bhattacharya 1987). The Atlas therefore hosts **no Kapila person-node** — he is a legendary tradition-founder, not a documented individual.
- **Is puruṣa really plural, and how can plural inactive witnesses differ?** Critics (Advaitins especially) press that contentless, attributeless witnesses could not be numerically many; Sāṃkhya answers from the *distribution* of birth, death, and experience across beings (SK 18). The coherence of a **plurality of featureless consciousnesses** remains the school's most-attacked doctrine.
- **How does an inactive puruṣa relate to prakṛti at all?** The system insists puruṣa never truly acts or changes, yet must somehow occasion prakṛti's evolution and suffer apparent bondage. The classical answer is *reflection* (puruṣa mirrored in the sattva of buddhi, producing a false sense of agency); whether this "proximity without contact" (*saṃyoga*) is intelligible is the core internal problem, and Burley (2007) reads the whole scheme as phenomenological rather than as a literal two-substance ontology to relieve it.
- **Original theism or original atheism?** Vijñānabhikṣu (16th c.) argued Sāṃkhya was never truly atheistic; most modern scholars hold that **classical Sāṃkhya is genuinely nir-īśvara** and that the theistic reading is a late Vedāntising harmonisation.
