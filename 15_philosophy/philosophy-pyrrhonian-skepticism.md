Pyrrhonian Skepticism is the philosophical method founded by [[pyrrho-of-elis]] (c. 360–270 BCE) and documented systematically by Sextus Empiricus (c. 160–210 CE) in the *Outlines of Pyrrhonism* and *Against the Professors*. The method: for any proposition, an equally convincing counter-proposition can be constructed (isostheneia, "equal strength"); the appropriate response is suspension of judgment (epoché); the unexpected result of suspension is mental tranquility (ataraxia).

## Core Doctrine

Pyrrho held that things are:
1. Indeterminate (not determinate as F or not-F)
2. Inapprehensible (we cannot reliably grasp their nature)
3. Such that non-assertion is the appropriate response

The Pyrrhonist does not assert that knowledge is impossible (that would be a dogmatic claim). They remain suspended — neither asserting nor denying — and find in that suspension the peace they were seeking through philosophical inquiry.

Sextus Empiricus preserved the tradition in systematic form, cataloguing the "Ten Modes of Aenesidemus" (ten ways in which sense impressions vary — by species, individual, sense organ, condition, position, distance, frequency, admixture, relativity, culture) and the "Five Modes of Agrippa" (disagreement, infinite regress, relativity, assumption, circularity) — showing that any claim to justified belief leads into one of these epistemic dead-ends.

## SURPRISING FIND — Pyrrho in India

Ancient sources — primarily Diogenes Laertius (*Lives of the Philosophers*, 3rd c. CE) and Eusebius — report that [[pyrrho-of-elis]] accompanied Alexander's Indian campaign (327–325 BCE) and there encountered Indian "naked philosophers" (*gymnosophists*). Ancient accounts name two: Kalanos (who accompanied Alexander back to Persia and burned himself alive) and Mandanis (who refused to come and reportedly debated Alexander).

The gymnosophists are typically identified as Jain monks (who practice radical non-attachment and are known for nudity as an ascetic practice), though the Ajivikas (determinists who held that liberation comes through the exhaustion of karma, not through action) are another possibility.

The structural parallel between Pyrrho's philosophical outcome (non-assertion → tranquility) and Jain/Buddhist/Ajivika spiritual practice (non-attachment → liberation) is striking. If Pyrrho's encounter with Indian philosophers contributed to his philosophical orientation, this would be **the earliest documented instance of Indian philosophical ideas influencing Greek philosophy** — predating by centuries the later Neo-Pythagorean and Neoplatonic traditions that scholars debate for Indian influence.

This is not established as transmission — it is a documented contact event with a plausible philosophical influence hypothesis. The methodological caution Pyrrho applied to all metaphysical claims applies equally to claims about his own origins.

## Cross-Tradition Connections

### MASSIVE WIN — Pyrrhonism / Madhyamaka: Same Method, Same Outcome

[[nagarjuna]]'s *[[phase-4-075-mulamadhyamakakarika|Mulamadhyamakakarika]]* (c. 150–250 CE) uses the prasanga (reductio ad absurdum) to systematically deconstruct all positive metaphysical positions. The outcome: [[emptiness-sunyata]] — not a new positive metaphysical position but the abandonment of the craving for metaphysical positions. The result: liberation.

Sextus Empiricus's Pyrrhonism uses the Modes to systematically demonstrate that no positive position can be held without contradiction. The outcome: epoché — not a new epistemic position but the abandonment of the craving for epistemic certainty. The result: ataraxia.

**Both use logical deconstruction of all positions to produce a state of mental openness and peace.** The mechanism is identical: it is not achieving the right position that liberates, but giving up the search for the right position. This is so unusual in philosophical methodology that the parallel is extraordinary. If Pyrrho's Indian contacts involved exposure to proto-Buddhist or Jain philosophical methods, the Pyrrhonian-Madhyamaka parallel may not be parallel-form but transmission-form — with Pyrrho as the original carrier.

### Three Traditions, Same Therapeutic Destination

The goal is mental tranquility (ataraxia / nirvana / wu wei) achieved by releasing fixed positions:
- **Pyrrhonism**: suspension of judgment → ataraxia
- **Madhyamaka Buddhism**: deconstruction of inherent existence claims → liberation
- **Philosophical Daoism**: release of fixed perspectives → alignment with Dao (wu wei)

All three arrived within 200–300 years of each other. All three frame philosophical method as therapeutic rather than merely theoretical — the point is not to know the right answer but to be freed from the compulsion to demand one.

### Pyrrhonism → Descartes → Modernity

Sextus Empiricus's works were rediscovered and translated into Latin in 1569 (by Henri Estienne). This directly triggered the "crisis of skepticism" in European philosophy — if Pyrrhonian skepticism could not be answered, all knowledge claims were in doubt. Michel de Montaigne's *Essays* (1580s) absorbed Pyrrhonism and made suspension of judgment into a philosophical life-stance.

Descartes' *Meditations* (1641) was written explicitly to answer the Pyrrhonist challenge: the Cogito (*cogito ergo sum*) is designed to find the one claim that even a Pyrrhonist cannot suspend judgment on. From that point, Descartes rebuilds knowledge on the foundation of self-awareness.

**The chain**: [[pyrrho-of-elis]] (c. 350 BCE) → Sextus Empiricus (c. 200 CE) → rediscovery 1569 → Montaigne → Descartes → Kant (answering Hume's skepticism) → modernity. The entire trajectory of modern Western epistemology is a response to Pyrrho's Indian encounter.
