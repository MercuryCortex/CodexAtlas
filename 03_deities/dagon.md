# Dagon

Dagon (better-attested form: **Dagan**) is a major Bronze Age Northwest Semitic deity, originally most prominent on the Middle Euphrates at cult centers like Tuttul and Mari (early third millennium onward) and later worshipped throughout Syria and into the Levant. In the Ugaritic [[phase-1-013-baal-cycle]] Dagan is the father of Baal — Baal's standard patronymic in the Ugaritic texts is *bn dgn*, "son of Dagan." This is an interesting genealogical inconsistency: El is also presented as the father of Baal in some Ugaritic passages, suggesting two distinct traditions imperfectly harmonized. The name's etymology is contested. The older popular etymology connected it with Hebrew *dāgān* "grain" and treated Dagon as a grain-fertility god (supported by his ancient agricultural-region cults); another older etymology connected it with Hebrew *dāg* "fish" and imagined a half-man, half-fish form (this is the Rashi-mediated medieval reading, perpetuated in Milton's *Samson Agonistes* and modern fantasy; modern scholarship rejects the fish-etymology, though "fish-Dagon" remains the popular image).

## Mythological role
Dagan's mythology is sparse. He has no surviving narrative texts of his own. The Mari tablets attest him as a major recipient of royal cult; the Ugaritic ritual texts list him second in the pantheon (after El) on some pantheon-list tablets, ahead even of Baal — a striking ranking that suggests his great age and prestige in the regional pantheon despite his thin mythological profile. The Hebrew Bible attests Dagon as the principal Philistine god: the Philistines capture the Ark of the Covenant and place it in the temple of Dagon at Ashdod (1 Sam 5); the image of Dagon falls before the Ark and breaks. The Samson narrative climaxes in the destruction of Dagon's temple at Gaza (Judg 16:23–30). This places Dagon firmly in Iron Age Philistine religion — though the Philistines were ethnically Aegean-derived "Sea Peoples," they had taken over Northwest Semitic religious patrons of the territory they settled.
