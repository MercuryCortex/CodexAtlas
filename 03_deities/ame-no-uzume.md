# Ame-no-Uzume

## Identity
Ame-no-Uzume-no-Mikoto ("Heavenly Alarming Female Spirit" or "Heavenly Whirling Female") is the Shinto goddess of dawn, festivity, and sacred performance. She is best known as the goddess whose ecstatic, comedic, and erotic dance before the sealed cave of [[amaterasu]] (the Ama-no-Iwato myth in the *Kojiki*) brought the sun-goddess out of her retreat and restored light to heaven and earth.

## The Ama-no-Iwato Myth
After [[susanoo]]'s destructive rampage — destroying rice paddies, defiling sacred spaces, and killing a weaving maiden — [[amaterasu]] withdrew in terror and sealed herself inside the Heavenly Rock Cave (*ama-no-iwato*). Without her, heaven and earth fell into darkness, crops withered, and malevolent spirits proliferated.

The eight million deities assembled before the cave. Various ritual attempts were made: sacred cocks crowed, the Yata no Kagami mirror and Yasakani no Magatama jewels were hung in the trees, prayers and liturgies were recited. Nothing worked.

Then **Ame-no-Uzume** climbed onto an overturned tub, and performed an ecstatic shamanic dance — stamping her feet, progressively loosening her garments, exposing her body — until she fell into a trance. The 800 assembled gods erupted in laughter at her comic-erotic performance. The sound of their laughter puzzled Amaterasu: *Why is everyone laughing when the world is dark?* She pushed the rock aside to look. At that moment, Ame-no-Koyane held up the mirror so that her own face shone back at her, and the god Tajikarao pulled her fully out.

This myth is remarkable for multiple reasons:
- **Comedy and erotic display** — not prayer, force, or negotiation — are the divine acts that restore the sun
- Uzume's trance-dance is an archaic shamanic performance (*kagura*) that remained central to Shinto ritual practice
- The assembly of gods' laughter is itself a cosmogonic act: collective joy restores the cosmos

## Role in Shinto religion
Ame-no-Uzume is the divine ancestor of the *miko* (shrine maidens) and kagura performance traditions in Shinto. Kagura — ritual dance performed at shrines — is explicitly traced to Uzume's Ama-no-Iwato performance. She is worshipped at Chichibu Shrine and Tsubaki Grand Shrine, among others.

Her folk forms **Okame** and **Otafuku** — the round-faced, laughing goddess of good fortune — are among the most universally recognized Japanese religious iconographic figures, present in almost every traditional home and festival.

## Cross-tradition significance
The Ama-no-Iwato myth belongs to a cross-cultural type of **solar withdrawal and restoration myths** where a solar deity's absence creates cosmic crisis and a non-solar deity's comedic or erotic performance restores them. The structural parallel with the Egyptian myth of Ra's withdrawal (where Hathor's display restores Ra's joy) suggests a shared mythological grammar even without documented transmission.

Uzume is also the paradigmatic **shamanic trickster** in Japanese religion: she achieves what conventional piety cannot through ecstasy, comedy, and boundary-transgression.
