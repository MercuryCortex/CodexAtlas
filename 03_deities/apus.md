# Apus

The **mountain-lords** of the Andes — each major peak is an *apu*, treated as a person with whom the surrounding community has an ongoing relationship of offering, consultation, and reciprocity. The most powerful apus are the snow-capped peaks (**Apu Ausangate**, **Apu Salkantay**, **Apu Huascarán**, **Apu Illimani**); lesser apus include all named hills within a community's territory. The peak IS the deity, not merely the deity's residence — this is one of the clearest cases in world religion of *landscape itself as personal divine being*.

**Apu/Pachamama complementarity.** Contemporary Andean ritual practice is structured around the binary of **Apus** (masculine, vertical, mountain) and [[pachamama]] (feminine, horizontal, earth-substrate). The *despacho* / *pago a la tierra* — the ritual offering bundle of coca leaves, seeds, fat, wine, and minerals — is addressed to both, often with explicit gendered division (offerings to Apus deposited upslope; offerings to Pachamama buried in earth). This complementarity is the structural backbone of lived Andean religion, far more central than the Inca state-pantheon ([[inti]], [[viracocha]]) which is now mostly historical-cultural identification.

The **continuous-practice** of Apu veneration through 500 years of Spanish and Catholic colonization is one of the most-documented cases of pre-Columbian religious survival anywhere in the Americas. Tourists climbing Ausangate or Salkantay today still encounter offering-bundles left by Quechua porters at high passes — the ritual logic is unchanged from the pre-Inca period.
