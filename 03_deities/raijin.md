# Raijin

## Identity
The Japanese god of thunder, lightning, and storms — one of the most visually distinctive deities in the entire East Asian pantheon. Depicted as a fearsome red or blue oni-featured demon surrounded by a ring of drums that he beats with sticks to produce the thunder. Paired with [[fujin]] (wind god) as an inseparable dyad, the two appearing together in some of the most celebrated works of Japanese art. In some mythological accounts, Raijin is born from the rotting corpse of [[izanami]] in Yomi (the land of the dead), making him a deity born from death itself — a chthonic origin for a sky-power.

## Earliest attestation
- *Kojiki* (712 CE) — the Yomi narrative: when [[izanagi]] illuminates Izanami's decaying body, he sees eight different thunder-deities (the *yakusa no ikadzuchi*) clinging to her. Raijin as composite thunder-being emerges from this sequence.
- *Nihon Shoki* (720 CE) — parallel accounts; thunder deity described as a son of Izanami.
- **Buddhist syncretic iconography** (Heian period, 794–1185 CE) — the fearsome demon form becomes canonical under Sino-Japanese Buddhist artistic influence; Leigong (Chinese thunder deity) iconography merges with native Shinto thunder-deity concept.
- **Tawara-ya Sōtatsu's screen** (c. 1620, Kennin-ji) — the definitive paired Fujin-Raijin iconography.

## Mythological role
Raijin's primary function is atmospheric violence in service of agricultural fertility: thunder was understood in Japanese agrarian tradition to fertilize rice crops. The deity's terrifying form thus doubles as protector of the harvest — the same paradox found in [[indra]] (warrior-god whose rain brings agricultural abundance) and [[baal-hadad]] (whose storm-combat delivers rain and harvest).

The **drums-of-thunder** motif: Raijin's ring of drums is the Japanese solution to the cross-cultural question of how the storm-god produces thunder. [[shango]] (Yoruba) uses the thunder-axe, [[teshub]] uses the bull-roar, [[thor]] uses Mjolnir. The drum is specifically significant: drums as world-creating, rhythm-giving instruments appear across traditions as more than sound — they are the heartbeat of order against chaos.

The **born-from-death** origin: Raijin emerging from Izanami's decomposing body in Yomi positions him as a deity of terrifying boundary-crossing — the thunder that comes from the region of death into the realm of the living. This parallels sky-power deities born from underworld contexts (Dionysus, dismembered and reborn; Osiris, resurrected to rule the dead).

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[thor]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Norse thunder deity who brings rain and battles chaos; thunder-deity archetype as warrior against serpentine disorder (Jormungandr / Yamata-no-Orochi parallel).
- **[[indra]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: the Vedic king of gods and master thunder-deity; Puhvel (1987) documents the thunder-god cluster as the most widely distributed deity type in world religion.
- **[[zeus]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Greek sky-and-thunder sovereign; the thunder-deity as supreme or near-supreme deity reflects the power of the storm as the most overwhelming natural force.
- **[[perun]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Slavic thunder deity who battles the underworld serpent Veles; storm combat mytheme paralleling Raijin's chthonic birth and atmospheric violence.
- **[[leigong]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Chinese thunder deity with drum iconography directly parallel to Raijin's; probable line of iconographic transmission via Sino-Japanese Buddhist art circuits.
- **[[teshub]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Hittite storm-king whose thunder-weapon battles chaos.
- **[[baal-hadad]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Canaanite storm deity who brings rain and fertility through combat; the storm-deity-as-agricultural-protector function is exact.
- **[[shango]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Yoruba thunder deity whose sacred drums are the voice of divine power; the drums-as-thunder-instrument is a profound cross-cultural convergence.
- **[[fujin]]** — *ancient-identification*: the wind-and-thunder divine dyad, theologically inseparable.

## Iconography
Fearsome red or blue oni-demon with wild black hair, naked or minimally clothed, surrounded by a circular arrangement of drums (typically depicted as 6–10 large circular drums arranged in a halo). Beats the drums with sticks. Often depicted in mid-air, leaping or flying. Appears at temple gates paired with [[fujin]], the two as guardian-deterrent figures.
