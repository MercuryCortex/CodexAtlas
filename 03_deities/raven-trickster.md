# Raven (Yéil / Gugwis)

## Identity

**Raven** (Tlingit: *Yéil*; Haida: *Gugwis*; Tsimshian: *Txamsem*) is the trickster-creator of the Pacific Northwest Coast — the most prominent mythological figure in the Haida, Tlingit, Tsimshian, Heiltsuk, and related traditions of coastal Alaska and British Columbia. Raven is simultaneously the agent of creation and its most disruptive force: he does not create from nothing by divine fiat but obtains, transforms, steals, and rearranges what exists. His most celebrated narrative — the theft of light from a box owned by a jealous chief — is both a creation story and a trickster story at once, distinguishing Pacific Northwest cosmogony from traditions that imagine light as already present or as a gift from a benevolent deity.

## Earliest attestation

- Raven narrative traditions belong to living oral literatures that predate European contact; archaeological evidence of raven symbolism on Northwest Coast artifacts (shaman's rattles, totem poles, regalia) reaches back several thousand years.
- First extensive European documentation: Franz Boas, *Indianische Sagen von der nord-pacifischen Küste Amerikas* (1895), collecting from Tlingit, Haida, Kwakwaka'wakw, and related nations.
- John Reed Swanton, *Haida Texts and Myths* (Smithsonian, 1905) — the foundational scholarly record of Haida Raven narratives.
- Bill Reid and Robert Bringhurst, *Raven Steals the Light* (1984) — the most widely read modern retelling from within the Haida tradition.

## Mythological role

**The theft of the boxes of light**: In the primary Haida narrative, the world is in total darkness because a wealthy chief hoards the sun, moon, and stars in a series of nested boxes. Raven, desperate and clever, transforms himself into a hemlock needle (or spruce needle) and drops himself into the drinking water of the chief's daughter. She swallows him; he is reborn as her baby grandson. As a cherished grandchild, Raven manipulates the old chief with tears and tantrums until he is allowed to play with each of the boxes. He releases the stars and moon, then seizes the box of the sun and transforms back into a raven, flying out the smoke-hole into the world and releasing light everywhere.

**Structural function**: Raven does not act from altruism — he acts from appetite, curiosity, and the pure desire to possess whatever is hidden. That the consequences happen to include illuminating the world for humanity is, structurally, incidental to Raven's motivation. This is the trickster's paradox: cosmic goods arrive through selfish transgression rather than through divine planning.

Claude Lévi-Strauss devoted extended analysis in *Mythologiques* (1964–71) to Raven's role as a **binary operator** — a figure who mediates the fundamental structural oppositions of Northwest Coast myth (light/darkness, life/death, culture/nature, the possessive chief/the generative release). Raven is one of the key figures through whom Lévi-Strauss argued that structurally equivalent mythological thinking appears independently across cultures.

## Cross-tradition identifications

- **[[coyote-navajo]]** — *structural-parallel*: the two paradigmatic North American trickster-creators; both drive creation narratives through amoral disruptive action; both are shapeshifters; both introduce the conditions of existence (light, death, limitation) through transgression rather than gift.
- **[[anansi]]** — *structural-parallel*: Anansi owns all stories; Raven steals all light — both tricksters acquire/release what was hoarded by a jealous possessor. Shapeshifters; necessary; creators through theft.
- **[[loki]]** — *structural-parallel*: both are transformer-tricksters who use metamorphosis as their primary tool; both drive mythological narratives that reshape cosmic conditions; Lévi-Strauss's structural analysis parallels the structural role scholars assign Loki in Norse myth.
- **[[prometheus]]** — *structural-parallel* (fire-theft archetype): Raven steals the boxes of light from a jealous possessor and releases light to the world; Prometheus steals fire from the gods and gives it to humanity. Same narrative structure: transgressor / jealous possessor / luminous/energetic substance / release for all / cosmic transformation. No transmission; the motif appears globally.
- **[[agni]]** — *structural-parallel*: both are associated with the concealment and disclosure of the fire/light principle; Agni hidden in the waters and wood until drawn out mirrors Raven releasing light from boxes.
- **[[eshu]]** — *structural-parallel*: crossroads trickster whose unpredictable intervention mediates between cosmic realms; both are amoral boundary-crossers essential to the cosmic order's function.
- **[[maui-polynesian]]** — *structural-parallel*: Maui snares the sun and steals fire from Mahuika; the most direct Polynesian parallel to Raven's theft of the boxes of light. Both are culture-heroes who transform cosmic conditions through trickery.
- **[[wakan-tanka]]** — *contextual*: the broader Plains sense of sacred creative power that pervades all things contextualizes the Northwest Coast figure; both locate creative power in unexpected, transgressive forms.
- **[[spider-grandmother-hopi]]** — *contextual*: another pan-Native American creator whose methods differ (Spider Grandmother creates through thought-song) but whose archetype as a non-anthropomorphic creator parallels Raven's animal-form creation.

## Iconography

- **Black raven**: always depicted in bird form or in mid-transformation (half-human, half-raven)
- **Raven rattle**: a ceremonial shaman's rattle carved in the shape of a raven — one of the most distinctive objects of Northwest Coast material culture
- **Totem poles**: Raven appears as the uppermost or central figure on many clan poles, representing the Raven moiety
- **Formline art**: Raven in the distinctive Northwest Coast two-dimensional style — ovoids, U-forms, split-U forms encoding the body as nested energy

## Disputes

The Lévi-Strauss structural analysis of Raven was enormously influential in 1960s-70s anthropology and mythological studies, but has been critiqued for:
1. Imposing a Western logical schema (binary opposition) onto traditions with different internal logics
2. Abstraction that erases the specific cultural and ceremonial contexts of Raven narratives within living Haida and Tlingit traditions
3. Treating oral-narrative traditions as static "myths" rather than ongoing living performances

Contemporary Indigenous scholars (Nora Marks Dauenhauer, Richard Dauenhauer on Tlingit; Masayuki Miyata on Haida) emphasize the performative and ceremonial dimensions that structural analysis cannot capture.
