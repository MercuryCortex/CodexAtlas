# Manasa Devi

## Identity

Manasa Devi — "the mind-born goddess" (*manasa* = "from the mind/consciousness") — is the Hindu goddess of snakes, serpent-bite, fertility, and the underground powers of the earth. She is primarily a folk deity of Bengal and Northeast India, her worship concentrated among agricultural and fishing communities where snakebite was a genuine mortal danger, and she is one of the most important regional deities of eastern South Asia who is not fully integrated into the pan-Hindu Sanskritic mainstream despite repeated attempts at Brahminic incorporation. Her mythology is preserved primarily in the Bengali *Manasa-Mangal Kavya* (Songs of Mangal for Manasa) — a genre of vernacular literary texts produced from the 13th to 17th centuries — which constitute major works of Bengali literature and document the complex, contested relationship between folk and Brahminic religious authority.

Manasa's theological signature is the narrative of *forced worship*: her central myth involves her compelling the richest merchant in the world, Chand Saudagar, to worship her after years of violent resistance in which she destroys his seven ships, kills his six sons, and finally kills his seventh son Lakshminder — a serpent-bitten death that his wife Behula reverses through devotion and a miraculous journey to the gods' realm. When Chand finally offers her worship — even with his left hand, in contempt — the myth is complete. This narrative of a goddess who cannot be ignored, who demands recognition through catastrophe, who is neither gentle nor assimilable, places Manasa in the tradition of fierce, autonomous feminine divine power in South Asian religion.

## Earliest attestation

- **Sanskrit Puranas** (various, ca. 300–1000 CE): Manasa appears in the Devi-Bhagavata Purana, Brahma Vaivarta Purana, and Skanda Purana as Shiva's daughter and the queen of serpents, indicating Brahminic awareness and partial incorporation of what was likely a much older folk deity.
- **Manasa-Mangal Kavya** (Bengali, 13th–17th c. CE): the primary literary tradition; the texts of Vijaya Gupta, Ketakadas Kshemananda, and others. The Chand Saudagar narrative is the central text.
- **Archaeological**: snake-goddess worship in the Indian subcontinent extends to the Indus Valley Civilization (ca. 2600–1900 BCE) in the form of serpentine imagery; whether any continuity with Manasa's specific cult exists is uncertain but structurally plausible.
- **Folk cult**: Manasa's worship in Bengal continues actively through the present; her festival (*Manasa Puja*) occurs in the monsoon months (July–August) when snakes are most active.

## Mythological role

The Manasa mythology revolves around two intertwined themes:

**1. The demand for recognition**: Manasa is Shiva's daughter, but she is illegitimate — born not of [[parvati]] but of Shiva's yogic power manifesting on a lotus. She is therefore not fully accepted in the divine hierarchy. Her entire career is structured as a campaign to gain worship from the world and thereby gain status in the divine order. She is not primarily a goddess of grace but a goddess of necessity: you worship her because she will destroy you if you don't, and she will protect you if you do. This is the theological logic of many folk deities who exist outside the Brahminic mainstream — they demand recognition from the margins.

**2. The serpent-as-earth-power**: Manasa's domain over snakes is simultaneously about literal snakebite protection and about the serpentine powers of the earth — the *naga* beings (divine serpent-humans) who control underground water, fertility, and agricultural abundance. Her worship secures both protection from literal snake venom and the goodwill of the naga powers who control rainfall, fertility, and the earth's productivity. She is thus a goddess of the relationship between human communities and the dangerous, fertile powers of the natural world.

The epic narrative of Behula and Lakshminder — Behula's preservation of her dead husband's body on a raft as she descends through the world seeking his resurrection, ultimately reaching the gods and earning his return — is one of the most remarkable narratives in Bengali literature, combining folk ballad energy with profound themes of spousal devotion (*sati* ideology in its most active, heroic form) and the power of human love to breach the divide between life and death.

## Cross-tradition identifications

- **[[kali]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Both are fierce Bengali goddesses who demand worship through catastrophe and cannot be domesticated; both represent the autonomous, unassimilable feminine divine power that makes claims on human attention through destruction rather than through beauty. The theological kinship between Manasa and Kali in Bengali Shakta tradition is real — they are neighboring nodes in the same religious ecosystem.
- **[[durga]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Durga defeats demons through martial power; Manasa defeats resistance through the deployment of serpentine forces. Both represent the goddess's ultimate sovereignty over the male world. Kinsley (1988) groups them in the broader category of "fierce goddesses who assert divine autonomy."
- **[[wadjet]]** (Egyptian) — *scholarly-parallel*: Wadjet, the cobra-headed protective goddess of Lower Egypt, and Manasa share the core function of serpent-goddess-who-protects. Both are among the oldest layers of their respective religious traditions. Both are associated with royal protection and agricultural fertility. No direct transmission — a deep structural parallel that suggests an ancient stratum of snake-goddess theology common to agricultural civilizations.
- **[[asclepius-greek]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Asclepius's healing power is mediated through serpents (the rod of Asclepius); Manasa's protection from snakebite is snake-goddess power deployed as medicine. Both represent the ambivalent healing-and-killing power of serpent knowledge.
- **[[vishnu]]** — *scholarly-parallel (via naga connection)*: Vishnu reclines on the cosmic serpent Shesha/Ananta between cosmic cycles; Manasa governs the nagas and serpentine powers of the earth. The serpent as cosmic substrate (Shesha) and the serpent as earth-power (naga/Manasa) are two aspects of the same theological understanding: serpents as the life-force underlying all creation.

### MASSIVE WIN — The Serpent Goddess Pattern

Snake goddesses appear across the ancient agricultural world with remarkable structural consistency:

- **Egypt**: [[wadjet]] (cobra goddess of Lower Egypt; royal protector)
- **India**: Manasa Devi (snake goddess of Bengal); Naga-worship more broadly
- **Crete/Minoan**: the famous Minoan Snake Goddess figurines (ca. 1600 BCE) — a goddess holding snakes in both hands, in a palace shrine context
- **Greece**: chthonic serpents associated with Demeter and Persephone; Asclepius's healing serpent
- **Hebrew**: the bronze serpent (*nehushtan*) made by Moses (Numbers 21:8–9) to protect Israelites from snakebite — directly structural with Manasa's protective function
- **Mesoamerica**: the Feathered Serpent (Quetzalcoatl) as cosmic creator-deity

The snake goddess represents the earth's generative and poisonous powers simultaneously — the serpent that can kill and the serpent that heals are the same serpent. This is one of the most consistent cross-cultural theological structures in the vault.

## Iconography

Manasa Devi is depicted seated on a lotus or on a coiled serpent, surrounded by snake imagery on all sides — cobras rise from her hair, wrap around her arms, encircle her body. She may hold a bowl of snakes or a cluster of serpents in her hands. In some depictions she has one eye (or is described as blind in one eye — a common folk-goddess asymmetry signaling partial divine access, or reflecting a mythological episode where Shiva's wife Parvati covered one of Shiva's eyes playfully and Manasa lost an eye in the resulting darkness). She is associated with the neem tree (*Azadirachta indica*) whose leaves are used in her worship and which has genuine antiparasitic properties. Her skin is sometimes depicted as green (serpentine) or dark (earth-power).

## Disputes

- The question of whether Manasa is a pre-Brahminic folk deity who was partially incorporated into Hinduism, or a Puranic goddess whose cult then spread into folk practice, is debated; the predominant scholarly view (Kinsley 1988; Dimock 1966) is the former.
- The relationship between Manasa and Shitala Devi (Hindu goddess of smallpox and disease — no node yet) is complex — both are folk goddesses of disease who protect from their own domain, and they are sometimes conflated in folk practice.
- Manasa's legitimacy within Shaiva tradition (whether she is truly Shiva's daughter, and how this claim was used to gain Brahminic recognition for a folk goddess) is a theological and political question the Manasa-Mangal texts themselves address directly.
