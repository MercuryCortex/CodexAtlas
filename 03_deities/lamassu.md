# Lamassu (šēdu)

## Identity
The *lamassu* (Akkadian *lamassu*, feminine; Sumerian *lamma*; the male counterpart *šēdu*) is the principal apotropaic protective-spirit of Mesopotamian religion, attested from the third millennium through the Neo-Assyrian period. In its most monumental form — the *aladlammû* colossi flanking the gates of Neo-Assyrian royal palaces (Nimrud, Khorsabad, Nineveh, Aššur) — it is the winged-bull or winged-lion with a human bearded male head, wearing the horned crown of divinity. It is not itself a high-tier god but a subordinate apotropaic genie in the service of the gods and of the king; its placement at thresholds and gates expresses the Mesopotamian theology of *boundary-protection* — the recognition that the danger of cosmic and political disorder is concentrated at thresholds and that ritual-iconographic technologies (the colossal protective figure, the foundation-deposit figurine, the apotropaic incantation) are required to ward it off (Wiggermann 1992; Reade 1983).

## Earliest attestation
- Cylinder-seal iconography of the Akkadian and Ur III periods (~-2300 to -2000) attests the small-scale protective-genie type.
- The Lamassu/šēdu of personal-religion (the protective spirit of the individual, addressed in *šuilla* prayers) is attested in second-millennium incantation literature.
- The **colossal *aladlammû* gate-guardians** become the canonical Neo-Assyrian form under Assurnasirpal II (Nimrud Northwest Palace, ~-865), Sargon II (Khorsabad, ~-720), and Sennacherib (Nineveh, ~-700). These are the iconic Lamassu of the British Museum, Louvre, Oriental Institute Chicago, and Metropolitan Museum collections.
- Apotropaic-incantation collections (*Bīt mēseri*, *Šurpu*, *Maqlû*) provide the ritual-textual frame for the lamassu cult (Wiggermann 1992).

## Mythological role
The lamassu has no narrative mythology. Its role is **ritual-protective**: it stands as the visible-figural form of the apotropaic theology, and as the recipient of personal-religion prayer. In Neo-Assyrian state religion, the Lamassu colossi enacted the protection of the royal palace and (by extension) the Assyrian state from inimical forces — both supernatural (demons, evil eye, malefic divination) and political (foreign invasion, internal rebellion). The *foundation-deposit figurine* practice — burying small clay or bronze apotropaic figurines beneath palace floors and thresholds — extends the lamassu-protection downward into the building's foundations (Wiggermann 1992).

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[ashur]]** — *cultic-state association*: the colossal Lamassu/aladlammû stand at the gates of the Aššur-state royal palaces; they are the visible apotropaic expression of Aššur-state theology, though they are themselves subordinate apotropaic spirits.
- **kərûbîm / cherubim** — *scholarly-parallel* (cognate): Akkadian *kerubu* and Hebrew *kərûb* are widely held to be cognate; the biblical cherubim of Ezekiel 1, 1 Kings 6–8, and the Ark of the Covenant are the West-Semitic continuation of the Mesopotamian winged-hybrid apotropaic-spirit tradition (Albright 1938; Wiggermann 1992; Mettinger 1979). The iconographic continuity is most clearly seen in the Ezekiel 1 four-faced living creatures (man, lion, bull, eagle) — a near-direct catalogue of the Neo-Assyrian apotropaic-creature inventory.

## Iconography
**The *aladlammû* type.** A winged bull or winged lion (the iconographic species is debated; Reade reads the Nimrud examples as winged bulls, the Khorsabad as winged human-headed bulls, and the Sennacherib as winged human-headed lions), with a human bearded male head wearing the horned crown. The five-legged convention is a Neo-Assyrian innovation: the figure must read as *walking* from the side (showing four legs in motion) and as *standing* from the front (showing two legs at rest, the rear two legs not visible) — combined, five legs are sculpted. This is a deliberate response to the architectural problem of representing the protective spirit in both modes simultaneously.

## Disputes
- **Bull vs. lion as the principal *aladlammû* species.** Reade 1983 argues the species is regionally variable; Wiggermann 1992 treats the species as iconographically interchangeable.
- **The cherubim-lamassu equation.** Some scholars (Mettinger, Wiggermann) accept the etymological cognate *kerubu* → *kərûb* and the iconographic continuity as straightforward; others (Mettinger in later work) qualify this and treat the Hebrew cherubim as a parallel-but-distinct West-Semitic adaptation. The cognate-relation is widely accepted; the iconographic-direct-transmission claim is more contested.
