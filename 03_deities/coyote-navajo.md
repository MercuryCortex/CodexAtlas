# Coyote (Mąʼii)

## Who he is

**Coyote** (Diné: *Mąʼii*, "the Roamer") is one of the Navajo Holy People — but profoundly different in character from the beneficent figures like [[changing-woman]] or [[spider-woman-navajo]]. Coyote is not evil but **necessary**: his disruptive, impulsive, often sexually-motivated actions introduce complications into cosmic order that the other Holy People must then resolve, thereby driving the creation narrative forward. He is the paradigmatic Native American trickster, and more broadly one of the most widespread mythological figures in North America — Coyote narratives appear across dozens of distinct traditions from the Southwest to the Pacific Northwest to the Great Plains, though with significant variation in character and role.

## The origin of death

The most consequential Coyote narrative in Navajo tradition: in the primordial world, people were debating whether the dead should return to life. This would mean no grief, no loss — but also no room for the living. Coyote settled the debate by throwing a **stone into the water** and declaring: if it sinks, death is permanent. It sank. Death entered the world through Coyote's impulsive act.

This narrative encodes the trickster's most profound function: **the introduction of finitude**. The conditions of human existence — death, scarcity, limitation, the irreversibility of time — are not the result of divine planning but of trickster disruption. Coyote's stone is the founding act of mortal existence. And yet the act is ambiguous: would an immortal world be better? The debate the Holy People were having was genuine. Coyote cut it short — recklessly, but perhaps correctly.

## Coyote's role in creation narratives

Across Navajo ceremony and myth, Coyote appears as a disruptive agent whose actions are:
- **Sexually motivated**: Coyote frequently disrupts sacred proceedings to pursue women, often causing catastrophic results
- **Impulsive**: he acts before thinking, where the other Holy People deliberate
- **Ultimately generative**: his disruptions create the conditions for new creation — problems that the other Holy People must solve, and in solving, produce the world's current form

In the **Blessingway** ceremony, Coyote's role is carefully managed: his presence is acknowledged because ignoring him would be more dangerous than including him. **If you dream of Coyote, a Coyote ceremony must be performed** — his attention, once attracted, must be ritually addressed rather than dismissed.

## The Coyote fire-theft (cross-tradition)

In several non-Navajo but related traditions (California tribes including Maidu, Miwok, and Karuk; various Great Basin traditions), **Coyote steals fire for humanity** — the same narrative as Prometheus. The functional parallel is striking:

- Coyote / Prometheus: a trickster figure steals a divine substance (fire/light) from those who possess it and gives it to humanity, suffering consequences
- The theft is not sanctioned by the divine order — it is transgression that becomes gift
- The cosmos RESISTS giving fire/light to humanity; the trickster bypasses the resistance

This is one of the clearest independent attestations of the fire-theft motif in geographically isolated traditions — and one of the strongest arguments for the fire-theft as a genuine cross-cultural archetype rather than a transmission.

## Cross-tradition MASSIVE WIN: Coyote ↔ Loki — the trickster-who-introduces-death

Among all cross-tradition trickster parallels, Coyote and Loki share the most specific structural element: both introduce death into the world through a specific act of trickery:

| Feature | Coyote | Loki |
|---|---|---|
| **Act** | Throws stone in water; declares death permanent | Engineers Baldr's death through mistletoe |
| **Target** | The consensus of the Holy People | The one being who was immune to death |
| **Motivation** | Impulsive impatience | Jealousy / mischief |
| **Result** | Death is irreversible for all | Death enters the world (Baldr, the deathless, dies) |
| **Cosmic consequence** | Finite mortal existence | Ragnarök set in motion |

Both tricksters are the hinge on which mortal existence turns. The parallel has no transmission — it reflects the narrative logic of the trickster archetype itself: the figure who introduces death is necessarily the transgressor of the cosmic categories that separate the living from the dead.
