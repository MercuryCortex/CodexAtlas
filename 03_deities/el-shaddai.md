# El Shaddai

## Identity and significance

El Shaddai (*ʾēl šaddai*) is the patriarchal divine name used throughout the Genesis narratives to designate the deity worshipped by Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. The Priestly source of the Pentateuch (P) frames the entire pre-Mosaic religious history of Israel under this name, explicitly distinguishing it from the Tetragrammaton in Exodus 6:2–3: "I appeared to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob as El Shaddai, but by my name Yahweh I did not make myself known to them." This verse is the hinge of the Pentateuch's theological history: it acknowledges that the patriarchs and Moses worshipped the same deity under different names — and implies, for critical scholarship, that those names may originally have designated distinct divine traditions that were retrospectively harmonized.

The name functions in the Hebrew Bible as: (1) a covenant-maker (*Gen 17:1* — "I am El Shaddai; walk before me and be blameless, and I will make my covenant between me and you"), (2) a blessing-deity associated with fertility and multiplication (*Gen 28:3*, *35:11*, *43:14*, *49:25*), and (3) a divine name used with particular frequency in the book of Job (31 occurrences of *šaddai* alone, without *ʾēl*), where it functions as a formal name for the deity of the ancient world before Israel's particular covenant framework.

## The etymology — a contested question

The meaning of *šaddai* remains the most debated aspect of the name. Scholarly proposals fall into five groups:

1. **"Mountain" (*šadū* / Akkadian *šaddāʾu*):** The most widely accepted modern etymology, favored by Cross (1973). Akkadian *šadû* = "mountain"; *šaddāʾu* = "mountain-dweller" or "one of the mountain." El Shaddai = "El of the Mountain" — connecting the deity to the cosmic mountain (*Ṣapan* in Ugaritic, *Sinai/Horeb* in Hebrew tradition, *Zion* in later theology).

2. **"Wilderness/field" (*šādeh*):** Ernst Knauf (1999, DDD) argues *šaddai* relates to Hebrew *śādeh* = "(uncultivated) field/steppe," yielding "God of the Wilderness/Steppe." This fits the geographic context of the semi-nomadic patriarchal narratives and the southern desert Yahweh traditions (Teman, Seir, Sinai).

3. **"Destroyer" (*šādad*):** A minority position connecting to the root *šdd* = "to destroy, devastate." Largely dismissed by comparative philologists.

4. **"Sufficiency" (*šaddai* = *šeˈ-dai*):** Popular theological etymology rendered as *pantokrátōr* / *omnipotens* ("Almighty") in LXX and Vulgate. Almost universally rejected by critical scholars as secondary folk etymology.

5. **"Breasted" (*šad*):** Albright's proposal — El Shaddai as "the Breasted One," a nurturing fertility deity. Now held by few scholars as primary, though it may inform the name's semantic range.

The current consensus (Cross, Knauf, Wyatt, Day) favors the mountain or field/wilderness etymology, with the mountain reading connecting most naturally to the Ugaritic-Canaanite background.

## Exodus 6:3 and the documentary hypothesis

Exodus 6:3 is one of the founding texts of the documentary hypothesis. The J and E sources use "Yahweh" freely throughout the patriarchal narratives (e.g., Gen 2:4, 4:1, 12:8); the P source systematically avoids "Yahweh" before the Exodus and uses "El Shaddai" instead. For Wellhausen and his successors, this is conclusive evidence of distinct source traditions: J and E assumed the patriarchs knew the Tetragrammaton; P preserved an older naming tradition indicating they did not.

The theological-historical implication is significant: the P source appears to preserve a tradition in which the patriarchal religion was an El-cult — specifically an El Shaddai cult associated with the Canaanite high god tradition ([[el-canaanite]]) — that was originally distinct from the Mosaic Yahweh covenant tradition. The Priestly editor's harmonization ("same god, different name") is itself an act of theological convergence of the kind Cross and Smith trace structurally across the biblical tradition.

## Shaddai in Job

The 31 occurrences of *šaddai* (alone, without *ʾēl*) in the book of Job are notable: Job is explicitly set in the land of Uz (possibly Edomite/Transjordanian) and involves non-Israelite characters who address God as *šaddai*. This signals that the name was understood by biblical authors as the divine name of the pre-Israelite, non-Yahwistic ancient Near Eastern world — the god of the great patriarchs before Israel's particular covenant. Job's use of *šaddai* in anguished complaint ("But the arrows of Shaddai are in me," Job 6:4) gives the name a darker coloring — more severe, less intimate than the covenant-warmth of Yahweh — that may preserve an older stratum of the name's semantic range.
