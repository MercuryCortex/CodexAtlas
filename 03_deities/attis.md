# Attis

## Identity
Attis is the consort (sometimes son, sometimes lover) of the Phrygian Great Mother **Cybele**, whose myth — castration, death under a pine tree, and annual ritual mourning and renewal — became the focus of one of the most prominent Roman imperial mystery cults. The Phrygian cult traveled to Rome with Cybele in -204; Attis was initially less prominent in the official Roman cult than the Magna Mater herself, but became increasingly central in the imperial period (1st–4th c. CE). The annual cycle of the Hilaria festival (March 22–25 in the Julian calendar) marked Attis's death and renewal.

## Earliest attestation
- Phrygian inscriptions name "Ates" (= Attis) from ~-700/-500; he appears as both a divine figure and the title of priests in the Phrygian theocratic city-states (Pessinus).
- Greek literary sources from the Hellenistic period.
- Catullus 63 (~-60) gives the most vivid Latin literary treatment: a man named Attis, in an ecstatic frenzy, castrates himself in service to Cybele and then awakens to lament his condition.
- Roman imperial-era literary and iconographic attestation is abundant (Lucretius, Ovid, the Naassene Sermon recorded by Hippolytus).

## Mythological role
Multiple variant myths circulated; the canonical Roman version (Ovid *Fasti* 4.221–246; Pausanias 7.17.10–12) tells:
- Attis vowed chastity in service to Cybele.
- He broke the vow with a nymph.
- Cybele struck Attis with madness; in his frenzy he castrated himself under a pine tree.
- He died (or was preserved in a state between life and death — variant).
- Cybele mourned; the gods granted that his body would not decay, his hair would continue to grow, and his little finger would continue to move — or, in other variants, he was transformed into the pine tree.

The annual ritual cycle:
- **March 15** — *Canna intrat* (the Reed enters): priests cut reeds from the Almo river.
- **March 22** — *Arbor intrat* (the Tree enters): a pine tree is brought into the temple, wrapped as a corpse — Attis's death.
- **March 24** — *Sanguis* (Blood Day): mourning, flagellation, self-mutilation by initiates and Galli; new Galli are initiated by castration.
- **March 25** — *Hilaria* (Joy Day): joyful celebration, traditionally interpreted as Attis's revivification.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[adonis]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Frazer's "dying and rising gods" classification grouped Attis, Adonis, Osiris, Tammuz; Mettinger (2001) revisits the category with more nuance.
- **[[osiris]]** — *scholarly-parallel* / *ancient-identification* (Plutarch DIO 69 makes the comparison): both involve mourning consort and ritual renewal.
- **[[dumuzi-tammuz]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Mesopotamian vegetation god mourned by Inanna/Ishtar; Levantine transmission possible but not direct.

## Iconography
- Phrygian cap (signaling Asian-mystery exoticism)
- Pine tree (sacred to him)
- Pan-pipes (shepherd's instrument)
- Sometimes shown with hand raised in the *adlocutio* gesture
- Late iconography shows him in a reclining "soft" pose suggesting both death and youth

## Disputes
- **"Dying and rising god"?** Frazer (1906–15) treated Attis as a paradigmatic "dying and rising god"; mid-20th-c. scholarship (Smith, Mettinger 2001) re-examined the category. Mettinger concludes Attis's "resurrection" is *ambiguous* — the texts emphasize preservation of body parts and transformation more than full physical resurrection.
- **Christian parallels.** Easter / Christ-resurrection parallels are often drawn in popular comparative-religion literature (the March timing, the dying-and-renewing motif). Modern specialists treat these as *parallel-motif* not direct dependence; the Attis Hilaria and Christian Easter overlap chronologically but the soteriological content differs sharply.
- **Self-castration.** Was the Galli's self-castration a genuine widespread practice or an exaggerated literary topos? Mainstream: a real practice, attested in inscriptions and material evidence (Galli funerary stelae show their distinctive ritual implements).
