# Set

Set is the Egyptian god of the desert, storms, foreign lands, and disorder — and the most ambivalent figure in the Egyptian pantheon. The "Set animal" depicted as his iconographic representation is a stylized canid-like creature whose real-world referent has never been identified; some scholars conjecture an extinct species, others a deliberately composite imaginary beast. Set's mythological character darkens over Egyptian history: in early texts (Pyramid Texts, Old Kingdom) he is a fierce but ambivalent god, frequently invoked as a protective and royal deity (Khasekhemwy of the 2nd Dynasty featured Set alongside Horus on his serekh); under the 19th and 20th Dynasties he is one of the great state-gods (Seti I and Setnakht bear his name); after the Third Intermediate Period he is increasingly demonized, and by the Late Period he is essentially a figure of evil — the murderer of [[osiris]], the rival of [[horus]], the patron of all things foreign and chaotic.

## Mythological role
In the Osirian narrative Set murders his brother Osiris, dismembers his body, contests Horus for the kingship, and is eventually defeated and assigned the desert; in the **Contendings of Horus and Seth** the tribunal sometimes favors his strength but ultimately rules against him. In a much earlier and theologically *positive* role, Set rides the prow of Ra's solar bark and defeats Apophis, the chaos-serpent that attacks the sun nightly — a striking example of Egyptian theology's capacity to assign chaos-fighting to a chaos-god. The Hyksos kings (Asiatic invaders, 15th–17th Dynasties) identified Set with the Levantine storm-god [[baal-hadad]]; this Set-Baal syncretism was continued by the Ramesside kings, who traced ancestry to Avaris and incorporated Set as a royal god. The progressive demonization of Set may reflect both the historical memory of the Hyksos disaster and the late-period theological tendency to clean up ambiguity by sorting deities into clearer good/evil categories. te Velde (1967) is the standard study.
