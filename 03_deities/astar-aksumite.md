# Astar (Aksumite)

## Identity
**Astar** (Sabaean / Aksumite *ʿṯtr*; Greek transliterations vary) is the **Venus-star deity** of the pre-Christian Aksumite pantheon and the Sabaean parent-tradition — and one of the most theologically interesting figures of pre-Christian Aksumite religion for its **gender-variation across the Semitic deity-system**. Where the East-Semitic Mesopotamian *Ishtar* and the Northwest-Semitic Canaanite/Phoenician *Astarte* / Ugaritic *Athtart* are **feminine**, the South-Semitic Sabaean/Aksumite Astar is **masculine**. The etymological root is identical (the Proto-Semitic ʿṯtr); the gender-variation reflects the South-Semitic linguistic-religious development that masculinizes several deities feminized further north.

In the pre-Christian Aksumite triad-or-quintet attested on Ezana's pre-conversion stelae, Astar appears alongside **[[almaqah]]** (the moon / Sabaean national high-god), **[[mahrem]]** (the war-god), **[[beher]]** (regional / sea-deity), and **[[meder]]** (earth-deity). Astar's role is the **astral / Venus-star** dimension of the pantheon.

## Earliest attestation
- **Sabaean inscriptions** of South Arabia from at least the 8th c. BCE.
- **Pre-Aksumite Damʾat-era inscriptions** (~700–400 BCE) at Yeha and other northern Ethiopian sites.
- **Pre-Christian Ezana stelae** — RIE 185 (vocalized Ge'ez), RIE 270 (Greek), RIE 271 — all invoke Astar alongside the other deities of the pre-Christian Aksumite pantheon.
- After Ezana's conversion c. 330, Astar disappears from royal inscriptions along with the rest of the pre-Christian pantheon.

## Mythological role
Astar in the pre-Christian Aksumite frame is the **astral-Venus** member of the royal pantheon. The Venus-as-morning-and-evening-star astronomical phenomenon — visible across the entire ancient Near East — is divinely-personified consistently across cultures, with gender-variation: feminine in Mesopotamia (Inanna/Ishtar), Levant (Astarte), Greece (Aphrodite-Ourania), Rome (Venus); masculine in South Semitic (Astar). The Aksumite Astar inherited this from the Sabaean cultural substrate.

Concrete mythological narratives around Aksumite Astar are not preserved (the documentation is epigraphic-cultic only); modern reconstruction depends on (a) the Sabaean inscriptional record, which is more extensive but still cult-oriented rather than narrative, and (b) comparison with the better-documented Mesopotamian and Levantine cognates.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[ishtar-akkadian]]** — *ancient-identification* (etymological): direct etymological cognate of the Proto-Semitic ʿṯtr root; gender-variation (feminine east, masculine south) is the principal divergence.
- **[[astarte-canaanite]]** (NW Semitic feminine; includes the Ugaritic Athtart form) — *ancient-identification* (etymological): Northwest-Semitic feminine cognates.
- **Athtar / ʿAttar** in pre-Islamic North Arabian and Ugaritic — *ancient-identification*: same masculine form attested elsewhere in South-Semitic and adjacent contexts.
- **[[venus-roman]]** / **[[aphrodite-greek]]** — *parallel-form*: Venus-star personification across cultural-religious systems.
- **[[inanna-sumerian]]** — *parallel-form*: pre-Semitic-language cognate Venus-deity.

## Iconography
Sabaean Astar is associated with the **ibex** in some iconographic contexts; not strongly standardized in Aksumite material culture.

## Disputes
- **Masculine vs. feminine.** The masculinization is well-attested in South-Semitic; some 20th-c. scholarship debated whether the Aksumite Astar was a *demoted* feminine deity or originally masculine. Modern Sabaean studies (Robin 2012) treats the masculinization as a primary South-Semitic feature.
- **Relationship to the Ugaritic ʿAttar.** The Ugaritic *ʿAttar* who unsuccessfully attempts to sit on Baal's throne (KTU 1.6) is the Northwest-Semitic *masculine* cognate (alongside the feminine Athtart) — establishing that the gender-variation is not a clean east-feminine / south-masculine binary but a fluid pattern.
