# Tian

## Identity
Tian (天, "Heaven") is the supreme cosmic-moral principle of Chinese religion from the early Zhou (~-1046) onward. Introduced by the Zhou dynasty as the cosmic legitimator of their conquest of the Shang — the *Mandate of Heaven* (*tian ming*) is the foundational political-theological doctrine of Chinese imperial ideology, surviving from the 11th c. BCE to the fall of the Qing in 1911. Heaven mandates rulers who maintain the virtue (*de*) appropriate to rule; when virtue is lost, Heaven withdraws the mandate and a new dynasty arises. This doctrine made Chinese kingship conditional in a way unparalleled in most ancient theocracies — and it gave rebellion a recognized theological grammar.

In the Analects, Tian appears as Confucius's primary religious referent: "Heaven has given me the virtue I have" (7.23), "I do not blame Heaven" (14.35). Whether Confucius's Tian is a personal moral deity, an impersonal moral order, or both — a question still debated. The Mohists treated Tian as personal and active (will of Heaven, *tian zhi*); the Mencians more morally interior; the Xunzians more naturalistic; the Daoists effectively cosmic-impersonal. The imperial cult of Heaven (the Altar of Heaven in Beijing) was the highest state ritual until 1911. Tian is functionally the closest Chinese equivalent to a "high God" while remaining non-anthropomorphic — a distinct alternative to both Mediterranean monotheisms and to fully polytheistic systems.
