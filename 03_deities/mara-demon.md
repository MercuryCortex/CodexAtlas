# Māra

## Identity
Māra — "the Killer," "the Evil One" — is the personification of temptation, illusion, and the binding force of Samsara in Buddhist cosmology. He is the ruler of Kāmadhātu, the Desire Realm, the lowest and most entrapping of the three cosmic planes. Māra is not simply a demon but a cosmological necessity: as long as beings are bound to desire, Māra has dominion. He is, structurally, the cosmic force opposed to liberation — the Buddhist equivalent of the satan-function in Abrahamic religion. Every Buddha must defeat Māra at the moment of enlightenment; Māra's defeat is not his destruction but his being transcended. In the Pāli Canon he appears repeatedly attempting to dissuade the Buddha from teaching, appearing to monks and nuns in disguise, and challenging the Buddha's right to occupy the earth.

## Earliest attestation
The temptation of the Bodhisattva under the Bodhi Tree is attested in the **Padhāna Sutta** (Sutta Nipāta 3.2) and the **Mahāparinibbāna Sutta** (Dīgha Nikāya 16), texts among the earliest strata of the Pāli Canon (~3rd c. BCE in their present form, preserving earlier oral tradition). Māra's three daughters — Taṇhā (Thirst), Arati (Aversion), and Ragā (Passion) — are named in the **Māra-saṃyutta** (Saṃyutta Nikāya 4). The *Buddhavamsa* (Chronicle of the Buddhas) establishes the pattern that all Buddhas must confront and defeat Māra as a universal prerequisite of Buddhahood.

## Mythological role
**The Temptation Under the Bodhi Tree.** Māra's paradigmatic act is the assault on the Bodhisattva Siddhārtha Gautama at the moment of his enlightenment. The sequence runs: (1) Māra sends his armies of demons to frighten the meditating Bodhisattva — all weapons thrown become flowers as they approach him; (2) Māra challenges his right to occupy the seat of enlightenment, to which the Bodhisattva responds with the *bhūmisparśa mudrā* (earth-touching gesture) — calling the earth to witness all his prior acts of compassion across countless lifetimes; (3) Māra sends his three daughters (Desire, Aversion, Passion) to seduce the meditator. The Bodhisattva sees through the illusion and achieves enlightenment. Māra withdraws — not destroyed, but defeated. This narrative is **the central mythos of Buddhist soteriology**: the moment liberation becomes possible is simultaneously the moment the forces of bondage reach maximum intensity.

**The Cosmic Adversary Throughout the Canon.** After the Enlightenment, Māra continues to harass the Buddha and his disciples. He appears to the nun Vajirā disguised as a questioner; to the monk Samiddhi; to the Buddha himself. In each case he is recognized and named — "I see you, Māra" — and immediately withdraws. The recognition of Māra is itself a soteriological act: Māra has no power over one who sees him clearly.

**Cosmological Role.** Māra governs the sixth and highest heaven of Kāmadhātu — the Paranirmitavaśavartin ("realm where others' emanations are controlled") — meaning that within the realm of desire, he is at the top. From this position he works to keep beings entrapped in the cycle of rebirth. In Mahāyāna thought Māra is sometimes treated as a teaching device — a bodhisattva who plays the role of adversary to deepen practitioners' realization.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[satan-christian]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: **MASSIVE cross-tradition parallel**. The three temptations of the Buddha (pleasure, power, fear — rendered through daughters, army, and challenge) structurally mirror Satan's three temptations of Christ in the wilderness (bread, kingdoms, proving divine status). Both are cosmic adversary figures confronting the founder-teacher at the threshold of his mission; both use desire, power, and fear as weapons; both are defeated through the teacher's spiritual mastery. The parallel is among the most-discussed in comparative religion (Boyd 1975, Ling 1962).
- **[[ahriman]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Both are the cosmic principle opposing salvific liberation — Māra opposes the Buddha's enlightenment as Ahriman opposes Ahura Mazdā's holy truth. Both command demonic hosts; both are conceived as ultimately transcendable. Gandharan Buddhist-Zoroastrian contact makes some historical cross-influence possible.
- **[[amitabha]]** — Māra's realm (Kāmadhātu / Samsara) is the condition that [[amitabha]]'s Pure Land offers escape from; Amitābha's compassion is the antithesis of Māra's entrapment.
- **[[avalokitesvara]]** — in Mahāyāna tradition, Avalokiteśvara's boundless compassion operates precisely within the realm Māra governs; the bodhisattva's vow to remain until all beings are liberated implicitly engages Māra's domain.
- **[[mahakala]]** — the wrathful protector-deity whose ferocity is directed against forces that obstruct liberation; structurally opposed to Māra.
- **[[tara]]** — the swift liberator who saves beings from the dangers Māra personifies.
- **[[yama-vedic]]** — the Vedic/Buddhist lord of death; some traditions associate or conflate Māra with the death-function (his name "Killer"); the two are distinct but adjacent figures in Buddhist cosmology.

## Iconography
Māra appears in art in two modes: (1) **the dark warrior** — a fearsome figure commanding armies of monstrous soldiers; (2) **the beautiful seducer** — in the form of his three daughters, he embodies attractive illusion. The *bhūmisparśa mudrā* (earth-touching gesture) of the seated Buddha directly depicts the moment of Māra's defeat and is the single most common mudra in Buddhist art across all traditions.

## Disputes
Whether Māra is a cosmological principle (a personification of ignorance and craving) or a literal being is debated within Buddhism itself. Theravāda tends toward treating Māra as a real deva-being; certain Mahāyāna interpretations treat him as a teaching construct, or even as a bodhisattva playing a necessary role in the cosmic drama of liberation. The pre-Buddhist Vedic figure Namuci (who holds back the rain / the cosmic good) is sometimes identified as Māra's precursor.
