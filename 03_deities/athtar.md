# Athtar

## Identity

Athtar (ʿAṯtar) is the Canaanite astral god of the **morning star** — the masculine counterpart of the feminine Venus-deity (Ishtar/Astarte) of the rest of the Semitic world. (In the South Arabian pantheon ʿAthtar was a major high god; at Ugarit he is a lesser but significant figure.) He is a son of [[el-canaanite]], associated with irrigation by wells and channels — the artificial watering of the land, set implicitly against [[baal]]'s gift of rain.

## Mythological role

Athtar's defining scene is in the **Baal Cycle**. When Baal has been killed by [[mot]] and his throne stands empty, the high god El asks the mother-goddess Athirat ([[asherah]]) to name a successor; she proposes Athtar. Athtar ascends to the heights of Mount Ṣapān and **sits on Baal's throne — but his feet do not reach the footstool and his head does not reach the top of the seat.** Too small to fill the storm-god's place, he climbs down and instead "rules over all the great earth." The episode is a study in the inadequacy of the pretender: the astral god who reaches for the supreme throne and cannot occupy it.

## Cross-tradition significance (the failed ascent)

Athtar is one of the two principal Canaanite backgrounds to the **morning-star-who-overreaches** motif of Isaiah 14. Where [[shahar]] supplies the name (Helel *ben Shahar*) and the dawn-light imagery, Athtar supplies the **narrative of the bright astral god who aspires to the high throne and fails to attain it** — the would-be usurper whose ascent ends in descent. Together they form the mythological substrate that, refracted through Isaiah's taunt against the king of Babylon and the Vulgate's "Lucifer," became the Western legend of the proud light-bearer cast down from heaven ([[lucifer]]). As the masculine Venus, Athtar is also the gender-mirror of the Mesopotamian [[ishtar-akkadian]], a vivid example of how one astral body could be divinized as goddess in one culture and god in its neighbor.
