# Nergal

## Identity
Nergal is the Mesopotamian god of plague, war, and the destructive aspect of the netherworld. His cult center is **Kutha** (the Emeslam temple); his Sumerian by-name Meslamtaea ("he who comes forth from the Meslam") preserves this association. He is the son of [[enlil]] and [[nin-lil-sumerian]] in the *Enlil and Ninlil* myth, where he is conceived during Enlil's third intercourse-disguise (as river-man). In the Akkadian *Nergal and Ereshkigal* myth he descends to the netherworld, becomes consort of [[ereshkigal]], and is established as co-ruler of the underworld. From the Old Babylonian period he is increasingly identified with [[erra-akkadian]] — the Erra Epic (Cagni 1977) is one of the most important Akkadian compositions, a meditation on civic destruction and theological reckoning.

## Earliest attestation
- Early Dynastic god lists; Meslamtaea is attested in the Fara god list.
- The Lagashite royal inscriptions invoke him as a destroyer of enemies.
- The Sargonic and Ur III periods see major Nergal temples at Kutha and elsewhere.
- *Nergal and Ereshkigal* in two recensions: Amarna (~-1400) and Sultantepe (~-700).
- The Erra Epic (~-800).

## Mythological role
- ***Nergal and Ereshkigal***: Nergal descends to the netherworld (in the Amarna version, after insulting Ereshkigal's messenger Namtar); seizes Ereshkigal by the hair to kill her; she pleads, offers herself in marriage; he remains as her consort. The myth etiologizes the dual rulership of the netherworld and explains the periodic plague-incursions of Nergal into the upper world.
- ***The Erra Epic***: Erra (Nergal) is roused to violence by his seven divine weapons; he tricks Marduk into temporarily leaving his throne; in Marduk's absence Erra unleashes destruction across Babylonia. The text addresses civic catastrophe — likely composed in the wake of a real Babylonian disaster.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[erra-akkadian]]** — *ancient-identification*: the syncretism is fully realized by the Old Babylonian period.
- *Scholarly parallel*: Greek Ares (war), Apollo (plague-arrows), and various plague-deities have been compared cross-culturally; these are *scholarly-parallel*, not ancient identifications.
