# Pariacaca

## Identity
Pariacaca is the **primary deity of the Huarochirí Manuscript** — the most extensively documented indigenous Andean deity in the colonial record — and an exemplar of the Andean theological concept of the **apu** (mountain deity): the sacred mountain is not a symbol or home of the deity but the deity itself. His cult center is the Pariacaca mountain in the central Andean cordillera (modern Junín region, Peru), and he embodies the storm, flood, and rain that the mountain sends down into the surrounding valleys. He is a deity of **regional sovereignty** — specifically the protector-ancestor of the Huarochirí people — who defeated the world's corrupted first inhabitants with a catastrophic flood and established the sacred order that governed the region until the Spanish conquest.

## Earliest attestation
- Primary source: **Huarochirí Manuscript**, compiled c. **1608 CE** by Francisco de Ávila from Quechua oral traditions in the Huarochirí region.
- Pariacaca occupies more narrative space in the manuscript than any other deity — his birth, flood, victories over adversaries, his festival, and his relationship with the Huarochirí people are described across multiple chapters.
- The Pariacaca mountain and its associated cult are documented by archaeological evidence (ceremonial platforms, offerings) from the pre-Inca period, suggesting the cult predates the Inca incorporation of Huarochirí.

## Mythological role
**Birth from five eggs**: Pariacaca was born from **five eggs** on the summit of a mountain. From the eggs emerged five falcon-like forms — his five simultaneous aspects — that together constitute his divine being. The five-fold structure is characteristic of Andean numerical symbolism (*quincunx*: center + four directions).

**The great flood**: Pariacaca's first act of sovereignty was to destroy the corrupt world of the first humans. He sent a devastating flood and storm that swept away the old people and their world, cleansing the landscape for a new sacred order. This flood myth is one of several in the Huarochirí corpus and belongs to the pan-Andean tradition of flood-as-world-renewal (compare the Viracocha tradition).

**Rain and irrigation**: After destroying the old world, Pariacaca brought the rains and controlled the irrigation waters that made coastal and highland agriculture possible. He is specifically credited with establishing the **yanan irrigation canals** that brought water from the highlands to the arid coast — a theological claim that encodes the real hydraulic engineering achievements of Andean societies.

**The festival race**: Pariacaca's annual festival included a ritual **running race of 500 participants** from the community to his mountain peak — one of the most physically demanding festival rituals documented in the Huarochirí Manuscript. The race is a form of pilgrimage-as-offering, the community's bodies ascending toward the deity.

**Battles**: Pariacaca defeated various adversarial deities and huacas, establishing his supremacy in the regional sacred hierarchy. His most significant victory was over **Huallallo Carhuincho**, a fire deity who demanded human sacrifice before Pariacaca's arrival; Pariacaca defeated him with storm and flood and forced him from the region.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[tlaloc]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: mountain-associated rain deity at the center of agricultural religion; both send the rains that sustain life and floods that punish violation. No transmission; convergent mountain-storm-rain deity structure from independent Mesoamerican and Andean developments.
- **[[teshub]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Hittite/Hurrian mountain storm deity whose power is expressed through storm and rain from elevated terrain. Pariacaca's five eagle-storm aspects / Teshub's mountain-storm theophany share the core equation: mountain peak = storm origin = divine sovereignty.
- **[[indra]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Vedic storm deity who releases the rain after defeating the drought-demon; both embody rain as divine gift essential to civilization. No transmission; parallel agricultural-deity logic.
- **[[zeus]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: storm sovereignty from a mountain height (Olympus / Pariacaca peak). Both express the equation: ruler of storms = ruler of the world.
- **[[illapa]]** — *tradition-internal*: the Inca imperial storm deity; Pariacaca and Illapa coexist within the Andean tradition, representing local/regional versus imperial-state levels of the same theological function.
- **[[marduk]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: flood as the weapon of the highest deity, used to destroy and reset the world. Pariacaca's flood / Marduk's defeat of Tiamat both use water as the supreme creative-destructive force.

## MASSIVE WIN: the flood that destroys the first world

Pariacaca's flood belongs to the most widely distributed mythological pattern in human religion — the **world-renewal flood**:

| Flood | Tradition | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Pariacaca's flood** | Andean (Huarochirí) | Huarochirí Manuscript, c. 1608 |
| **Utnapishtim's flood** | Mesopotamian | *Epic of Gilgamesh*, tablet XI |
| **Noah's flood** | Israelite | Genesis 6–9 |
| **Deucalion's flood** | Greek | Ovid, *Metamorphoses* I |
| **Manu's flood** | Vedic/Hindu | *Shatapatha Brahmana* |
| **The flood of the Popol Vuh** | K'iche' Maya | *Popol Vuh* |
| **Bergelmir's survival** | Norse | *Prose Edda* |

The flood that ends a corrupted age and allows a new, purified world to begin is so widely distributed that it appears in virtually every tradition with a developed cosmogony. Whether this reflects a real catastrophic flood in human prehistory, a shared narrative inheritance from a common ancestor population, or convergent theological logic (the world needs periodic cleansing) remains one of mythology's fundamental open questions.

## MASSIVE WIN: the apu tradition — the mountain IS the deity

Pariacaca exemplifies the Andean theological principle that sacred mountains (*apu*) are not homes or symbols of deities but **are the deities themselves**. This "substantive" divinity of landscape features recurs across traditions:
- **Andean apu**: Pariacaca IS the Pariacaca mountain; offerings go to the mountain-body
- **Shinto yorishiro**: kami dwell within natural features (rocks, trees, mountains) — the feature is the deity's body
- **Aboriginal sacred sites**: ancestor-bodies petrified in the landscape; Uluru is not where the ancestor went — Uluru IS the ancestor
- **Greek**: some mountains were themselves divine (Olympus as the residence of the gods shades toward the mountain being the divine domain)
- The most radical form: the land is not given to us by the gods; the land IS the gods

## Iconography
Pariacaca is largely aniconic — the mountain itself is his body. His five eagle-aspects are depicted in some oral descriptions as great birds of prey (falcons or condors) carrying storm clouds. Ritual offerings to him — coca leaves, chicha beer, animal blood — were deposited on his mountain peak.
