# Urizen

## The Figure

Urizen is Blake's great antagonist — the god of reason, law, abstraction, and material limitation. His name puns on "Your Reason" and "Horizon" — the reason that sets boundaries, that measures and divides, that mistakes its own finite categories for ultimate reality.

In Blake's cosmogony, Urizen is not a separate being but a *fallen aspect* of the primordial cosmic human **Albion** (= [[adam-kadmon]], the universal Human). When Albion falls into self-divided sleep, his four Zoas (faculties) dissociate. Urizen — the Reason-faculty — seizes power, attempts to rule alone, and in doing so *creates* the fallen material world as a prison of measurement and law.

**The Book of Urizen (1794)** narrates the cosmogony with devastating precision:

> "Lo, a shadow of horror is risen / In Eternity! Unknown, unprolific, / Self-clos'd, all-repelling: what Demon / Hath form'd this abominable void, / This soul-shudd'ring vacuum? Some said / 'It is Urizen.' But unknown, abstracted, / Brooding, secret, the dark power hid."

Urizen separates himself from the other Eternals, closes himself in a "void" of self-enclosure, and proceeds to *create* — not by emanation and love, but by division and law:

> "He form'd a line and a plummet / To divide the Abyss beneath. / He form'd scales to weigh, / He form'd massy weights, / He form'd a brazen quadrant..."

The image: God-as-geometer, God-as-lawgiver, God-as-Newtonian-mechanic. This is Blake's precise rejection of Enlightenment rationalism and Deism — the God of clockwork universe and moral law is Urizen, not the divine.

## The Gnostic Parallel (Independent Convergence)

Blake arrived at the Gnostic Demiurge narrative without direct access to Gnostic texts (the Nag Hammadi codices were not discovered until 1945; the only Gnostic texts available to Blake were hostile patristic summaries). His sources were:
- **Jacob Boehme's Wrath-principle** — the divine self-contradiction in which God's wrath and love separate, and the wrath-principle becomes tyranny
- **Milton's God** — whom Blake identified as a Urizenic projection: "Milton's God… is he who dwells in flaming fire. / Know that after Christ's death, he became Jehovah."
- **Newton's "God"** — the deist clockwork-universe deity whose laws Blake saw as the spiritualization of mechanical materialism
- **The Jehovah of conventional religion** — the rule-giving, punishing God of the Anglican establishment

The result is structurally identical to the Gnostic Yaldabaoth:

| Urizen (Blake, 1794) | Yaldabaoth (Apocryphon of John, ~180 CE) |
|---|---|
| Separates from the divine Unity | Separates from the Pleroma through Sophia's error |
| Creates a void of self-enclosure | Creates the kenoma / deficiency-space |
| Creates the material world by measurement and law | Creates the material world by imitating the divine forms |
| Believes himself the highest God | Declares "I am God and there is no other God beside me" |
| Identified with Jehovah / conventional religion | Identified as the God of Genesis 1 (by the Gnostics) |
| Imprisons humanity in bodies of law and reason | Imprisons divine sparks in material bodies |
| Los (Blake's counter-figure) works to liberate | Christ / Sophia descend to liberate the pneumatics |

Blake did not *copy* the Gnostic myth. He *reconstructed* it from first principles, working from the Bible, Milton, Boehme, and his own visionary logic. That the results are structurally identical is the MASSIVE WIN — it suggests both Blake and the 2nd-century Gnostics were independently mapping the same deep pattern of spiritual experience.

## Urizen and Modern Philosophy

Blake's critique of Urizen is simultaneously a critique of the philosophical tradition he saw as Urizenic:
- **Bacon**: empiricism as the reduction of reality to measurable sense-experience
- **Newton**: the universe as a mechanical clock governed by mathematical law
- **Locke**: the mind as a blank slate passively receiving sensory impressions — the denial of imagination and vision

> "Mock on, Mock on, Voltaire, Rousseau: / Mock on, Mock on; 'tis all in vain! / You throw the sand against the wind, / And the wind blows it back again."

Blake's Urizen is, in effect, the personification of what later thinkers called "the disenchantment of the world" (Weber), "the dialectic of Enlightenment" (Adorno and Horkheimer), and "onto-theology" (Heidegger) — the domination of Being by calculative reason. Blake diagnosed it in 1794.

## Visual Iconography

Blake's most famous image: **"The Ancient of Days"** (frontispiece to *Europe: A Prophecy*, 1794) — the white-bearded God kneeling in a circle of light, arm extended downward, deploying a compass to measure the abyss below. This image, which became iconic as a vision of divine creation, was intended by Blake as the opposite: it is **Urizen creating his prison**, the god of ratio measuring and limiting the infinite.

The image was so powerful it escaped its context and became a universal symbol of divine ordering — precisely demonstrating Blake's point about Urizenic religion's capacity to aestheticize its own tyranny.
