# Ọya

## Identity
Yoruba orisha of winds, storms, sudden change, the marketplace, and the cemetery — specifically the threshold between life and death. Ọya is the orisha of *transformation*: she brings change, sometimes violent (the tornado, the abrupt death), sometimes generative (the wind that turns the page, the marketplace's commercial transformation). She is the consort of Ṣàngó and rides with him in storms; he produces the lightning, she the wind.

## Earliest attestation
Cult on the Niger River (which Yoruba tradition associates with her); 19th-c. ethnographic attestation; Ifá corpus references.

## Mythological role
A narrative: Ṣàngó had three wives — Ọya, Ọ̀ṣun, and Obá — and was visited by them in rotation. Ọya alone went with him into battle, carrying his lightning-magic. Another narrative: Ọya guards the threshold of the cemetery; the dead must pass her to enter the *Ìlú-Ọkú* (City of the Dead). She is therefore invoked at funerals and at the threshold of major life transitions.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **Our Lady of Candelaria** — *folk-syncretism* (Cuban Lucumí): Candelaria's candle-fire iconography pairs with Ọya's lightning-storm dimension.
- **Saint Theresa** (some Cuban traditions) — *folk-syncretism*: regional variation.
- **Iansã (Brazilian Candomblé)** — *manuscript-transmission*: the same orisha in Bahia.

## Iconography
Multicolored / rainbow cloth (sometimes nine pieces); horsetail flywhisk; buffalo horns (Ọya's *avatar* animal is the buffalo, into which she transforms in a major narrative); the number nine.
