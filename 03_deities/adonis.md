# Adonis

## The myth

**Adonis** is born from the incestuous union of Myrrha and her father Cinyras (king of Cyprus) — Myrrha is transformed into a myrrh tree by Aphrodite; Adonis is born from the split trunk of the tree. He is a child of extraordinary beauty. Aphrodite (goddess of love) falls in love with the infant and entrusts him to **Persephone** (queen of the underworld) to raise. Both goddesses want him; Zeus mediates: Adonis will spend **one-third of the year** with Aphrodite, **one-third** with Persephone, and **one-third** as he chooses (he always chooses Aphrodite). 

Adonis grows into a young hunter of surpassing beauty. **Ares** (Aphrodite's consort, jealous) transforms himself into a wild boar and kills Adonis while hunting. Aphrodite rushes to him; his blood falls on a white rose, turning it red (the mythological origin of the red rose). Adonis descends to Hades; Aphrodite mourns. **Persephone** refuses to give him back. Zeus intervenes again: Adonis will spend **six months** above with Aphrodite and **six months** below with Persephone — the Hellenized version maps exactly onto the seasonal cycle.

From the blood of Adonis spring **anemones** (wind-flowers) — the short-lived wildflower as Adonis's emblem: brilliant, brief, beautiful, gone.

## Adonis and Tammuz: the Semitic origin

"Adonis" derives from Semitic *adon* (lord) — exactly the same root as Hebrew *Adonai*, the substituted pronunciation of YHWH. The Greeks received Adonis from **Phoenicia**, specifically from the cult at **Byblos** (modern Lebanon), where the annual mourning for Tammuz/Adonis was celebrated with women weeping on the rooftops — the same ritual condemned in **Ezekiel 8:14**: "there sat women weeping for Tammuz."

The Adonis cult is therefore the Greek reception of the **Sumerian Dumuzi/Tammuz** myth (c. -3000 BCE), the oldest documented dying-god narrative, transmitted through Phoenician-Semitic religious culture into the Greek world. The love triangle shifts: in Sumerian myth, **Inanna** and the underworld goddesses fight over Dumuzi; in Greek myth, **Aphrodite** and **Persephone** fight over Adonis. Aphrodite = Inanna/Ishtar (goddess of love and war); Persephone = Ereshkigal (queen of the underworld). The narrative structure is preserved through the cultural translation.

## The Adonia festival

The **Adonia** was celebrated in Athens from at least the 5th century BCE. Women climbed to the rooftops, lamented Adonis with crying and breast-beating, planted **"gardens of Adonis"** — shallow pots with fast-growing seeds (fennel, lettuce, wheat) that shot up quickly and died — and then cast the dead plants and clay figurines of Adonis into the sea. The festival lasted two days: mourning; then (in some versions) joyful celebration of his return.

The "gardens of Adonis" are Marcel Detienne's key structural evidence: the fast-growing-fast-dying plants are the **structural opposite** of the slow-ripening agricultural crops of Demeter — Adonis represents anti-Demeter, excess and premature consumption rather than disciplined cultivation. The contrast with the Eleusinian agricultural mystery is the Athens festival-system's way of mapping out the full range of the agricultural cycle's emotional register.

## Reception: Frazer and the dying-and-rising god category

James Frazer's **The Golden Bough** (1890, expanded to 12 volumes by 1915) made Adonis the paradigm case for the "dying and rising god" category that shaped a century of comparative religion. Frazer's thesis: agricultural religions universally worship a vegetation god who dies (autumn) and rises (spring); Adonis, Osiris, Attis, Dionysus, and Christ are all instances of this universal pattern.

Jonathan Z. Smith's revisionist critique (1987): the "dying and rising god" category is a modern comparative construct. Adonis does not clearly *rise* in most versions — he divides time between above and below; Osiris rises but only as king of the dead, not as a living being; the Greek and Near Eastern evidence is more varied than Frazer's schema admits. The scholarly position is now: the structural parallel is real but the category of "dying and rising god" was over-systematized by Frazer for apologetic purposes (to relativize Christian resurrection).

## The Adonis–Christ connection

Jerome (*Letter 58*, c. 395 CE) reports that a grove of Tammuz/Adonis had been planted in Bethlehem — over the cave traditional Christians venerated as the birthplace of Jesus. This may be: (a) a deliberate pagan-temple placement over a Christian holy site; (b) a later confusion; or (c) evidence that the cave already had pre-Christian sacred associations.

Early Christian apologists (Justin Martyr, Origen) explicitly compared Adonis and Christ to argue for Christ's superiority. The existence of the comparison in ancient discourse is well-attested; whether it represents transmission (Adonis cult → Christian resurrection) or independent development of a common ancient Near Eastern template remains debated.
