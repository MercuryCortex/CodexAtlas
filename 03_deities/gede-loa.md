# Gede — The Haitian Vodou Loa-Family of Death and Sexuality

The **Gede** (also **Ghede**, **Guédé**) are a **vast loa-family** of Haitian Vodou — dozens of named figures sharing a common identity as **the loa of death, the cemetery, the dead, and sexuality**. They are simultaneously the most fearsome (they are death itself) AND the most beloved (their obscene comedy makes them deeply approachable). Their dress code is **funeral-formal**: top hat, tailcoat, dark glasses (often with one lens deliberately missing), purple-black-white tricolor cloth. Their offering is **rum infused with twenty-one hot peppers** — too strong for any living person, demonstrating Gede's invulnerability.

## The Gede family structure

The Gede are not one figure but a clan, headed by [[baron-samedi]] (the great chief of the dead) and **Manman Brigitte** (his consort, the great mother of the cemetery). The family includes:

- **Baron Samedi** ("Baron Saturday") — the patriarch, the great chief
- **Baron Kriminel** — the executioner of the unjustly-dead
- **Baron La Croix** — the cross at the cemetery gate
- **Baron Cimitière** — the cemetery itself
- **Manman Brigitte** — the matriarch, mother of the cemetery
- **Gede Nibo** — the catamite-trickster, often portrayed as a young man who died violently
- **Gede Plumaj** — Gede of plumage / vanity
- And dozens of other named Gede with individual stories, deaths, and personalities

## MASSIVE-WIN: the Egungun substrate

The Yoruba **Egungun** masquerade-cult — in which the ancestors return to the community via masked dancers — is one of the substrates of the Haitian Gede. Yoruba Egungun performances are simultaneously **sacred AND deliberately comic**: the ancestors come back not only with solemn warnings but with humor, satire, and obscene jokes. The masked figures are the dead, but they are also street-performers; they bless and they lampoon.

This Yoruba pattern — **death-as-humor**, *ancestors-as-clowns* — travels across the Middle Passage and lands in Haiti as the Gede aesthetic: dressed for a funeral but drinking pepper-rum, lecturing children but cracking obscene jokes, holding court at the cemetery as a circus and a Catholic Mass simultaneously. Drewal 1992 (*Yoruba Ritual*) documents the original Egungun pattern; Brown 1991 traces the diaspora continuity.

## MASSIVE-WIN: Gede / Baron Samedi → Saint Gerard Majella (Òrìṣà-Saint syncretism)

The Catholic Saint Gerard Majella — an 18th-century Italian Redemptorist lay brother, patron saint of expectant mothers and the dying — became the iconographic stand-in for the Gede family in Haitian Vodou. The standard chromolithograph (Gerard in black Redemptorist habit, sometimes contemplating a skull) supplied a Catholic visual stable that allowed the African-derived death-cult to survive colonial Christianization.

(The Baron Samedi figure also picks up French colonial-era iconographic elements — top hat, frock coat, skull-face — from French Catholic funereal imagery, especially the All-Saints / All-Souls processions of November 1–2. The synthesis of Yoruba Egungun + Fon ancestor-cult + Saint Gerard + French funeral imagery + Saint-Domingue colonial graveyard culture is the full Gede portrait.)

## MASSIVE-WIN: the cross-tradition death-as-fertile complex

The Gede's combination of **death + sexuality + obscene humor + life-regeneration** is a recurring cross-tradition religious complex:

- **Medieval European Carnival** (Bakhtin 1965) — the inversive feast in which the dead are toasted with bawdy songs, the social hierarchy inverted, and the body's lower functions celebrated as fertile
- **Mexican Día de los Muertos** — the sugar-skull tradition, the dressed-up Catrina figure (death as elegant lady), the cemetery picnics
- **Egyptian Osiris cult** — the dying-rising god whose phallic resurrection generates [[horus]]; death is not the end but the matrix of new life
- **Aztec Mictlantecuhtli** — the death-god with sexual aspect, paired with Mictecacihuatl
- **Indo-Tibetan tantric charnel-ground iconography** — [[mahakala]] + the Cittipati skeleton-pair dancing on corpses; cremation ground as place of liberation

Bakhtin 1965 (*Rabelais and His World*) is the classic statement of the cross-cultural pattern. Brown 1991 specifically argues in her *Mama Lola* Gede chapter that the obscenity is a **theological statement**: death itself is fertile, the cemetery is where life is reborn, the proper response to mortality is bawdy laughter — and the Gede are the priests of that theology.

## Fèt Gede — November 1–2

The Gede festival, **Fèt Gede**, coincides with All Saints + All Souls (Nov 1–2) — the same dates as Mexican Día de los Muertos, and (not coincidentally) the same dates as the ancient Roman *Lemuria* and the Celtic Samhain (later All Hallows' Eve). Throughout Haiti, hundreds of practitioners fill the cemeteries of Port-au-Prince and other cities in **purple-black-white** costume, dark glasses, top hats, dancing the *banda* (the Gede's hip-thrusting characteristic dance) — a public theological statement that death is to be met with sex, music, rum, and laughter.
