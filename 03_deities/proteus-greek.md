# Proteus

## Identity

Proteus (Greek **Prōteus**) is a Greek sea-god — one of the two "**Old Men of the Sea**" (with [[nereus]]) — famed for two powers: endless **shape-shifting** and infallible **prophecy**. He herds the seals of [[poseidon]] and dwells (in the *Odyssey*) by the island of Pharos off the coast of Egypt. His name has given English the word "protean," for anyone or anything endlessly changeable.

## Mythological role

The defining Proteus episode is in *Odyssey* IV. Stranded in Egypt, Menelaus is told that only Proteus can reveal how he may get home — but the sea-god will not answer willingly. Menelaus and his men, disguised in sealskins, ambush the Old Man as he comes ashore at noon to count his seals, and **seize him**. Proteus instantly begins to transform — into a lion, a serpent, a leopard, a boar, running water, and a leafy tree — trying to break free. Only because Menelaus holds fast through every change does Proteus at last give up and return to his own form, whereupon he answers truthfully. The motif — that hidden truth can be wrung from a shape-shifting power only by one who refuses to let go — is one of the most memorable in Greek myth.

## Cross-tradition significance

Proteus crystallizes a widespread mythic type: the **shape-shifting water-spirit whose prophecy must be wrestled out of him**. He doubles with [[nereus]] in Greek tradition, and the "hold the transforming being fast to win its secret" pattern recurs across folklore worldwide (and is echoed in the biblical wrestling of Jacob with the night-being at the Jabbok, who likewise must be held until he yields a blessing). As a marine elder in Poseidon's service stationed off Egypt, Proteus also sits at one of the Greek–Egyptian contact points that produced so much cross-cultural mythology.
