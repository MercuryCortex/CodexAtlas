# Ninurta

## Identity
Ninurta is the Sumerian-Akkadian warrior-agricultural-storm god, son of [[enlil]] and [[nin-lil-sumerian]], whose principal cult center is Nippur (the Ešumeša temple, secondary to Enlil's Ekur). He is the cosmic warrior who defends the order established by his father Enlil against monstrous threats (the Asakku-demon in *Lugal-e*; the Anzu-bird in the *Anzu* epic); the agricultural god whose annual return is celebrated in the *Farmer's Almanac* and whose plow institutes ordered cultivation; the storm god whose thunderous voice is the rumble of the spring floods on the Tigris and Euphrates; the lion-headed mace (the *Šarur*) is his weapon, sometimes personified as his independent agent. By the Ur III period he is identified with [[nin-girsu]], the warrior-agricultural god of the Lagashite state, and the two figures are theologically identical from the Old Babylonian period forward. Ninurta is among the most cross-period attested Mesopotamian deities — Early Dynastic through Neo-Assyrian — and his royal-warrior persona shapes the entire ANE conception of divinely-mandated kingship.

## Earliest attestation
- **Early Dynastic Lagashite material (~-2500).** Ur-Nanshe's royal inscriptions and the early Lagashite cult attest Ningirsu (Ninurta's Lagashite form). The Stele of the Vultures (~-2450) depicts Ningirsu in giant scale with the Anzu-bird mace.
- **Sargonic and Ur III period (~-2300 to -2000).** Ninurta in Nippur cult attested at the Ešumeša; the Gudea Cylinders ([[phase-1-018-gudea-cylinders]], ~-2125) provide the most extensive theological description of the Ningirsu/Ninurta figure.
- **Old Babylonian period (~-1800).** Both major Ninurta myths — *Lugal-e* (*Ninurta and the Asakku* / *Ninurta and the Mountain*) and *Anzu* (*Ninurta's Recovery of the Tablet of Destinies*) — are stabilized in their canonical Sumerian and Akkadian forms.
- **Middle Babylonian / Kassite period.** Ninurta increasingly Akkadianized; the Anzu epic preserved in Akkadian recension.
- **Neo-Assyrian period (~-900 to -612).** Ninurta becomes the patron god of the Assyrian warrior-kings; Assurnasirpal II builds a major Ninurta temple at Kalhu (Nimrud); the Anzu-mythology becomes central to Assyrian royal ideology. The kings model themselves on Ninurta-as-cosmic-warrior.

## Mythological role
- ***Lugal-e* (*Ninurta and the Asakku*, *Ninurta and the Mountain* — full Sumerian title *Lugal-e ud me-lam-bi nir-ĝal* "The King, Storm, whose splendor is heroic").** Ninurta is summoned by his father Enlil to defeat the Asakku, a monstrous demon who has taken over the mountain and assembled an army of stones against the gods. Ninurta defeats the Asakku, judges the stones (some are blessed for human use, others are cursed), and institutes the order of irrigation: he raises a wall of stones (the *hursag*, the mountains) to divert the cosmic waters into the rivers that nourish Sumer. This is the foundational Mesopotamian myth of cosmic-agricultural-warrior order. Van Dijk 1983 is the standard edition.
- ***Anzu* (*Ninurta's Recovery of the Tablet of Destinies* — also titled *Anzû* in the Akkadian recension).** The Anzu-bird (the lion-headed eagle) steals the Tablet of Destinies from Enlil, threatening cosmic order. After several gods refuse the mission, Ninurta is sent to recover it; after a difficult battle (Ninurta initially loses ground, is advised by Ea, and ultimately strikes Anzu with a special arrow), he recovers the tablet and is rewarded with elevated status in the pantheon. The Akkadian version is preserved most fully in Ashurbanipal's library at Nineveh. Vogelzang 1988 is the bilingual edition.
- ***An-gim dím-ma* (*Ninurta's Return to Nippur*).** A processional hymn celebrating Ninurta's triumphant return to Nippur after his cosmic battles; he displays his trophies (the heads of defeated enemies) to his father Enlil. Cooper 1978 is the definitive edition.
- **Agricultural-cult role.** The Sumerian *Farmer's Almanac* (Hoes-and-Plow / *Geš-ĝal ĝiš-bar*) and the agricultural-festival hymns celebrate Ninurta as the divine plowman whose seasonal return brings the spring floods; the agricultural-irrigation dimension is balanced with the warrior dimension throughout the tradition.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[nin-girsu]]** — *ancient-identification*: from the Ur III period the syncretism is complete. Ningirsu is the local Lagashite cultic form; Ninurta is the pan-Sumerian Nippur-centered form. The same god under two cultic names. By the Old Babylonian period the names are used essentially interchangeably; Lugal-e is sometimes set at Girsu, sometimes at Nippur. Refs: Annus 2002 (ch. 1); Streck 2001 *RlA*.
- **The Akkadian/Babylonian warrior god (cf. Tishpak of Eshnunna; cf. eventually [[marduk]] in his cosmic-warrior dimension).** Marduk's elevation in Enuma Elish absorbs functions previously held by Ninurta — the warrior-against-chaos role, the recovery of cosmic order, the recipient of the Tablet of Destinies. Some scholars (Annus 2002) argue Marduk's narrative in Enuma Elish is directly modeled on the Ninurta-Anzu pattern; the Marduk-Ninurta relation is one of the central topics in Babylonian theological development.
- **Hellenistic-Jewish / Christian descent (contested).** Annus 2002 argues for substantive transmission of Ninurta-Anzu themes into the Jewish apocalyptic tradition (the Watchers and fallen-angels material in [[phase-3-004-1-enoch]]; the Michael-as-cosmic-warrior figure; the divine-warrior pattern in late biblical material). This is a strong-influence reading that mainstream biblical scholarship treats as suggestive rather than definitive.
- **The "Nimrod" identification.** Annus 2002 and earlier scholarship (Speiser, van der Toorn) argue that the biblical Nimrod (Gen 10:8–12, the "mighty hunter before YHWH" who founded the Mesopotamian cities) preserves a faint Hebrew memory of the Ninurta-warrior-hunter figure; the etymological-iconographic case is suggestive. *Scholarly-parallel*.

## Iconography
- The Anzu-bird (Imdugud / *anzûm*) — a lion-headed eagle, sometimes shown gripping bulls or stags — is Ninurta's principal emblem, especially in his Ningirsu form (the great Lagashite vases and stelae).
- Lion-headed mace (the *Šarur*); sometimes personified as Ninurta's herald-weapon.
- The plow, in agricultural-cult contexts.
- The double-bladed axe.
- In Neo-Assyrian royal art, Ninurta is sometimes shown pursuing a lion-dragon hybrid (the *mušḫuššu* or related composite), the iconography from which classical Saint-George-and-the-dragon imagery may distantly descend (Annus 2002).
- Astral identification: Ninurta is associated with the planet Saturn in late-Babylonian astronomical-astrological tradition (the *kakkab Nirgal* / "star of Ninurta" entries in EAE and related material).

## Disputes
- **Ningirsu vs. Ninurta origin.** Were Ningirsu and Ninurta originally independent local warrior-storm gods of Lagash and Nippur respectively, later identified in the Ur III period? Or is the syncretism much older? Mainstream view (Streck 2001 *RlA*; Annus 2002): originally distinct, with identification complete by Ur III; the iconographic identity of the Anzu-emblem across both cults supports very early syncretism but does not require originally-single-god identity.
- **The Marduk-Ninurta relation.** How much of Marduk's Enuma Elish narrative is directly modeled on the Ninurta-Anzu pattern? Annus 2002 argues for substantial direct dependence; some scholars treat it as parallel-development from shared ANE warrior-god patterns.
- **The Hebrew Nimrod identification.** Suggestive but not conclusive; the philological case (Heb. *Nimrōd* ↔ *Ninurta*) requires irregular phonological development, and the Genesis 10 context is not theologically straightforward.
- **Ninurta and Jewish apocalyptic.** Annus 2002's case for substantive Ninurta-mythology transmission into Jewish Enochic and apocalyptic literature is intriguing but reception-historically debated; mainstream Enoch-scholarship is generally cautious.
