# Isrāfīl (إسرافيل)

## Identity

**Isrāfīl** (إسرافيل) is the Islamic angel of the trumpet — the heavenly herald who will sound the **Ṣūr** (صور, the great cosmic trumpet or horn) at the **Yawm al-Qiyāma** (يوم القيامة, the Day of Resurrection / Last Day), initiating the destruction of the present world and the resurrection of all human souls for divine judgment. He is not named in the Quran itself but is extensively described in *hadith* (authenticated traditions of the Prophet) and is universally recognized in both Sunni and Shia Islamic theology as one of the four principal archangels alongside Gabriel (Jibrīl), Michael (Mīkā'īl), and the Angel of Death (ʿIzrā'īl / Malak al-Mawt).

His defining characteristic is a posture of **eternal readiness**: Isrāfīl stands with the Ṣūr pressed to his lips, his gaze fixed on the Divine Throne (*ʿArsh*), waiting — without rest or interruption — for the word of God to sound the blast. This posture of suspended anticipation makes him one of the most theologically charged figures in Islamic angelology: a being whose entire existence is defined by a single act that has not yet occurred.

## Earliest attestation

- The role of the trumpet angel at the Last Day is directly alluded to in the Quran, though Isrāfīl is not named: "And the Trumpet will be blown, and whoever is in the heavens and whoever is on the earth will fall dead except whom Allah wills. Then it will be blown again, and at once they will be standing, looking on." (Quran 39:68)
- The name Isrāfīl and specific attributes (four wings, body containing all languages, lips-to-trumpet posture) appear in the hadith literature — earliest reliable documentation in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim and Sunan Abī Dāwūd (9th century CE).
- Islamic eschatological theology was substantially developed during the first three centuries of Islam through hadith compilation, *tafsīr* (Quranic commentary), and theological treatises (*kalām*).

## Mythological role

**The two blasts**: Islamic hadith tradition describes Isrāfīl sounding the Ṣūr in **two blasts**:
1. **The first blast** (*Nafkhat al-Ṣaʿq* / "Blast of Swooning"): all living beings in the heavens and earth die or fall unconscious — the destruction of the present world order.
2. **The second blast** (*Nafkhat al-Baʿth* / "Blast of Resurrection"): after an interval (forty years in some traditions), all dead souls are raised from their graves to stand for divine judgment at the *Ḥisāb* (reckoning). The interval between the two blasts is a period of cosmic void.

This two-stage structure (death-interval-resurrection) closely parallels the Zoroastrian eschatological sequence (cosmic battle / renovation / final judgment in *Frashokereti*), and may reflect Iranian influence on Islamic eschatological development via the Sasanian cultural milieu of early Islam's geographic and intellectual context.

**Body of all languages**: An unusual attribute in hadith literature: Isrāfīl's body contains every human language, or he knows and can speak all languages. This attribute connects him to a function as a universal communicator — the divine message that the trumpet carries is comprehensible across all human speech.

**Angel of music / inspiration**: Some Sufi traditions identify Isrāfīl as the angel of music and divine inspiration, linking the trumpet-blast as the ultimate utterance to a more general association with sacred sound, divine breath (*rūḥ*), and the creative word (*kun*, "be!") through which Allah creates.

## Cross-tradition identifications

- **[[gabriel-archangel]]** — *shared-tradition*: Gabriel and Isrāfīl are complementary within Islamic angelology — Gabriel carries the prophetic revelation; Isrāfīl signals its eschatological culmination. Together they frame the full arc of divine communication: the Quran revealed to Muhammad (Gabriel) and the Last Day announced (Isrāfīl).
- **[[raphael-archangel]]** — *structural-parallel*: Raphael's position in the Judeo-Christian four-archangel schema parallels Isrāfīl's in the Islamic four-archangel schema; some sources conflate or identify them as the eschatological herald angel.
- **[[heimdall]]** — *structural-parallel* (suspended-trumpet motif): the most striking cross-tradition parallel — Heimdall stands at Bifröst with the Gjallarhorn waiting to blow at Ragnarök; Isrāfīl stands with the Ṣūr pressed to his lips waiting for Allah's command. Both figures are defined by **the cosmic pause** — the moment of suspended time before the end, personified as a herald who does not yet sound. No transmission; the parallel is structural.
- **[[saoshyant]]** — *structural-parallel*: Saoshyant (Zoroastrian end-times savior) triggers the *Frashokereti* (final renovation) at the end of time — paralleling Isrāfīl's eschatological role. The Iranian cultural substrate of early Islamic theology makes this not only structural but potentially genealogical.
- **[[metatron]]** — *structural-parallel*: Metatron in Jewish mystical tradition stands before the Divine Throne transmitting divine commands — paralleling Isrāfīl's posture of fixed attention on the ʿArsh (*Throne*) awaiting the divine command to blow.
- **[[allah]]** — *direct relationship*: Isrāfīl's trumpet-blast is entirely contingent on Allah's command; the gap between his held posture and the actual blast is the entire span of history — a theological statement about divine sovereignty over time and eschatology.
- **[[iblis-shaytan]]** — *contextual*: Iblis (Shaytan) refused to bow to Adam; Islamic eschatology involves the final defeat of Iblis's domain — the Last Day that Isrāfīl announces is the end of Iblis's permitted period of temptation.
- **[[nur-muhammadi]]** — *contextual*: the Nur Muhammadi (Muhammadan Light) as the first created thing is the divine template that Isrāfīl's trumpet-call calls back into ultimate union; both figures mark boundaries of cosmic time.

## Iconography

In Islamic tradition, the prohibition on depicting divine and angelic beings means Isrāfīl has limited visual iconographic tradition compared to Christian angelology. Where representations exist (in Persian miniature painting traditions, which developed their own iconographic vocabularies), Isrāfīl is typically shown:
- A winged figure of enormous size
- With a great horn or trumpet raised to the lips
- Surrounded by the clouds of the heavenly realm
- Gazing upward toward the Divine Throne

Persian manuscript traditions (particularly in Quranic illumination and eschatological poetry illustrations) provide the richest visual documentation.

## Disputes

- **Named or unnamed?** The scholarly debate on whether the Quran's trumpet angel is identical with the hadith figure Isrāfīl is ongoing. The Quran references the trumpet (Ṣūr) and the angelic blast multiple times without naming the angel; the name Isrāfīl appears only in hadith literature. Whether the hadith material accurately transmits early Muslim understanding or represents later theological elaboration is contested.
- **Zoroastrian influence**: the degree to which Islamic eschatological theology, including the trumpet-angel tradition, was shaped by Zoroastrian Iranian eschatology (the *Frashokereti* / renovation at the end of time) is a significant scholarly question. Most scholars accept significant influence via the Sasanian cultural milieu; the exact channels of transmission remain debated.
