# Atum

Atum is the self-emergent primordial creator of Egyptian Heliopolitan theology — the "Complete One" (the name *itm* derives from the verb "to be complete, to be perfect"). He emerges spontaneously from the primordial waters of [[nun]], stands alone on the first mound (the *benben*), and generates the first divine couple [[shu]] (air) and [[tefnut]] (moisture) by self-procreation — variously by masturbation (most famously in [[phase-1-002-pyramid-texts]] PT 600 and similar passages), by spitting them forth, or by sneezing (the variants coexist within the corpus). From Shu and Tefnut descend [[geb]] (earth) and [[nut]] (sky), and from them the second-generation gods [[osiris]], [[isis]], [[set]], and Nephthys — the Heliopolitan **ennead** (nine gods). Atum is paired and fused with [[ra]] as Atum-Ra: where Ra is the visible daily sun, Atum is the primordial creator and (in some texts) the sun at evening or the totality.

## Mythological role
Atum's mythology is more cosmogonic than narrative — he is a starting point, not a hero. He represents the Egyptian theological concept of self-emergent existence: the universe begins not by combat (contrast Mesopotamian chaoskampf) but by a self-actualizing principle differentiating itself out of undifferentiated potency. In Coffin Texts CT 76–80 and BD 175, Atum tells Osiris that at the end of cosmic time he will return all things to himself, leaving only himself and Osiris in the form of two snakes in the primordial water — the rare ancient articulation of an *eschaton*, a cosmic dissolution and reabsorption. The Atum cosmogony is one of three principal Egyptian creation accounts (alongside the Memphite Theology of [[ptah]] and the Hermopolitan Ogdoad); James P. Allen (1988) provides the standard philosophical analysis.
