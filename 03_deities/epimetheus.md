# Epimetheus

## Identity

Epimetheus (Greek **Epimētheus**, "Afterthought" or "Hindsight") is a Titan of the second generation, a son of [[iapetus]] and brother of [[prometheus]], [[atlas-titan]], and Menoetius. His very name marks him as the foil of Prometheus ("Forethought"): where his brother is the embodiment of foresight and cunning planning, Epimetheus is the one who acts first and understands too late.

## Mythological role

Two episodes define Epimetheus. In Plato's *Protagoras*, the brothers are charged with equipping all living creatures with their faculties; Epimetheus insists on doing the distributing himself but, true to his name, lavishes all the protective gifts (claws, fur, speed) on the animals and reaches humankind with nothing left — leaving Prometheus to steal fire and craft-skill to save the human race. In Hesiod, Epimetheus is the husband who **accepts Pandora**: Prometheus has warned him never to take a gift from Zeus, but Epimetheus, forgetting the warning, receives the beautiful Pandora, who then opens the jar that looses toil, disease, and every evil upon the world, leaving only Hope inside. Epimetheus thus becomes the agent through whom the ills of human life enter the world.

## Cross-tradition significance

The Prometheus–Epimetheus pair dramatizes the Greek meditation on **foresight versus hindsight** as the two faces of the human condition. Epimetheus's role as the one who accepts the fateful "gift" (Pandora) that releases the world's evils gives the Greek tradition a counterpart to the Near Eastern motif of an original act that admits suffering and death into the world — a structural parallel often drawn with the Eden narrative. As an Iapetionid Titan, he belongs to the lineage set in tension with the Olympian order.
