# Kali

## Identity
Kālī — "the Black One" / "She-who-is-Time" — is the fierce, dark, transgressive manifestation of the Hindu Great Goddess. Iconography: skin black or dark blue; tongue protruding (the moment of intoxicated battle-frenzy); naked or wearing a skirt of severed arms; garland of fifty severed heads (the fifty Sanskrit letters; the cycle of saṃsāric speech); standing or dancing on the supine corpse of Śiva (her transgressive transcendence of even male authority); four arms holding a severed head, a sword, a gesture of fear-removal (*abhaya-mudrā*), and a gesture of boon-giving (*varada-mudrā*). The first major literary appearance is in the [[phase-5-001-devi-mahatmya]] (~600 CE), where she emerges from Durgā's forehead to defeat the demon Raktabīja (whose every drop of spilled blood spawns a new demon — she drinks them all). Her Tantric development is central to the Krama school (~10th–12th c., Kashmir) and to subsequent Bengali Tantric Shaktism. In modern Bengal, Kālī becomes the central object of bhakti devotion under figures like Ramprasad Sen (~1750) and Ramakrishna Paramahamsa (~1880); the latter's mystical theology of Kālī as the ultimate reached global reception through Vivekananda.

## Earliest attestation
Earliest substantive literary appearance: *Devī Māhātmya* episode 3 (~600 CE). Tantric systematic treatment: Krama and Kālīkula Tantras (~9th–11th c.). Bengali bhakti renaissance: ~17th–19th c.
