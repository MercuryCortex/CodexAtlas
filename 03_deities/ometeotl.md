# Ometeotl

## Identity
Ometeotl ("Two-God" in Classical Nahuatl, from *ome* = two, *teotl* = god/divine force) is the supreme primordial dual deity of Nahua theology — the self-generating, self-sustaining source of all existence, dwelling in Omeyocan, the "Place of Duality," the highest of the thirteen heavens. Ometeotl is simultaneously masculine (Ometecuhtli, "Lord of Duality," also called Tonacatecuhtli, "Lord of Our Sustenance") and feminine (Omecihuatl, "Lady of Duality," also Tonacacihuatl). The duality is not a conflict between opposites but a generative union — the divine dyad is the precondition of all created being. Crucially, Ometeotl received no temples, no priests, no direct cult — this deity was a *first principle*, an unmanifest source, not a cult object. All the active gods of the Aztec pantheon — including [[quetzalcoatl]], [[tezcatlipoca]], [[huitzilopochtli]], and [[xipe-totec]] — are Ometeotl's children, its emanations into created reality.

## Earliest attestation
- Sahagún's *Florentine Codex* ([[phase-8-004-florentine-codex-sahagun]], ~1577) preserves the most systematic account of Ometeotl as the supreme self-generating deity dwelling in the thirteenth heaven. The *Codex Chimalpopoca* (Nahuatl manuscript, c. 1558) also references the primordial dual source. Iconographic attestations are rare precisely because Ometeotl was the unrepresented source — deity-images were made for the active, manifest gods.
- Miguel León-Portilla's *Aztec Thought and Culture* (1963) reconstructed the philosophical theology of Ometeotl from these sources, arguing it constitutes a genuinely abstract metaphysical system.

## Mythological role
Ometeotl does not act in mythology in the way that the other Aztec gods do — there is no myth of Ometeotl fighting, descending, or transforming. The deity is purely the primordial source. From Omeyocan, the 13th heaven, Ometeotl generated the four Tezcatlipocas (the four directional cosmic forces, including [[quetzalcoatl]] and [[tezcatlipoca]]) who then created the successive world-ages (the Five Suns). The four sons of Ometeotl are in constant creative conflict — their cosmic wrestling produces the destruction and re-creation of successive universes. Ometeotl itself remains above and beyond this drama. The Nahuatl concept *teotl* — often translated simply as "god" — in its deepest philosophical usage designates a single dynamic cosmic energy of which all beings are expressions, and Ometeotl is the name given to this energy in its dual, self-generating, undivided form.

## Cross-tradition identifications

- **[[ein-sof]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: The Kabbalistic Ein Sof ("without limit," the infinite divine ground before the Sefirot) is the closest structural parallel outside Mesoamerica. Both Ometeotl and Ein Sof are: (1) the infinite self-generating source, (2) entirely beyond the manifest gods/divine intermediaries, (3) never directly worshipped or approached, (4) the origin from which all subsequent divine emanations proceed. The parallel is not transmission — it is structural convergence between two traditions that independently developed an abstract metaphysical theology of the unmanifest infinite.

- **[[the-one-plotinus]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Plotinus's "The One" (Enneads, 3rd c. CE) — the ineffable first principle beyond Being, beyond intellect, from which Nous and then Soul and then Matter emanate — parallels Ometeotl structurally. Both are: the transcendent absolute, prior to all duality, the source of creation not by action but by overflow/self-generation. León-Portilla himself draws this comparison.

- **[[the-monad-gnostic]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: The Gnostic texts (Apocryphon of John; Gospel of Truth) describe the Monad as the supreme, self-knowing, invisible, perfectly unified first principle whose "thinking" generates the divine pleroma. Ometeotl's dual self-generation (Lord and Lady as one) parallels the Monad's internal self-contemplation as the ground of emanation.

- **[[tian-heaven]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: The Chinese *Tian* (Heaven) in its philosophical usage (esp. in Confucian and Neo-Confucian thought) is an impersonal supreme principle governing cosmic and moral order — similarly not directly worshipped with a cult image, similarly conceived as the ultimate ground of all things. Both represent the tendency in multiple traditions to posit an abstract, impersonal supreme above the personal/anthropomorphic gods.

- **[[brahma]]** — *thematic parallel*: Brahma as ultimate ground (not the personal creator deity Brahma but *Brahman* in its absolute aspect) shares the structure of impersonal source-of-all. The male/female dual unity of Ometeotl echoes the Ardhanarisvara concept (half-Shiva, half-Parvati, see [[shiva]]) — the divine as the coincidence of masculine and feminine principles.

## Iconography
Ometeotl has no cult image — this is theologically significant. What exists are symbolic references: the dual male-female pairing; the number two; Omeyocan as a conceptual map of the thirteenth heaven. The "deity bundle" motif in some Aztec manuscripts (a wrapped bundle standing in for an absent deity) may gesture toward Ometeotl's invisible presence.

## Disputes
- Ángel María Garibay Kintana first systematized the Ometeotl evidence in the 1950s; Léon-Portilla built on this in 1963, claiming Aztec religion had a genuine metaphysical theology of the One-Source. Some scholars (notably Alan Sandstrom) argue León-Portilla over-systematized scattered evidence and that "Ometeotl" was less a unified theological concept and more a cluster of epithets. The debate continues. The existence of the unmanifest dual-source concept, however, is not disputed — only its degree of systematization.
