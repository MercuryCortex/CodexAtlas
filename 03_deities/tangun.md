# Tangun

## Identity
Tangun (Dangun Wanggeom) is the semi-divine founder-ancestor of the Korean nation — born of the divine [[hwanung]] and Ungnyeo (a bear transformed into a woman), he established the kingdom of **Gojoseon** ("Old Joseon") in 2333 BCE according to traditional Korean chronology. He is simultaneously the founder of the first Korean state, the ancestor of all Korean people, and a shaman-king (*mu-wang*) who combines priestly and royal authority.

At the end of his 1,500-year reign, Tangun did not die but became the mountain deity (*sanshin*) of Asadal Mountain — a transformation rather than a death, preserving the divine-ancestor presence in the landscape.

## The founding narrative (Samguk Yusa)
Tangun is born from the union of:
- **[[hwanung]]** (son of the heavenly ruler [[hwanin]], civilizer of humanity) — the divine, civilizing, heavenly strand
- **Ungnyeo** (a bear who endured a 21-day cave ordeal to become human) — the chthonic, animal-totemic, patient-endurance strand

His origin thus combines the two poles of Korean cosmology: the heavenly-divine (from Hwanung and Hwanin) and the earthly-animal (from the bear). The bear as sacred animal reflects the bear-cult traditions of northern Eurasia (shamanic Siberian, Korean, and Japanese bear traditions share a common substrate).

Tangun's name: *Dan* (檀/단) = sandalwood/altar; *gun* (君/군) = lord/king. *Wanggeom* = "king" — the dual title (*Dangun Wanggeom*) may preserve the dual shaman-and-king function in his founding role.

## The shaman-king
Tangun's title and function suggest he was originally conceived as a **shaman-king** (*mu-wang*) — the figure who combines access to the spirit world (shamanic authority) with political sovereignty (royal authority). This dual function is characteristic of early Korean kingship and continues in the *mudang* (Korean shaman) tradition's claim to direct access to the founding spirits of the Korean cosmos.

## Modern political and religious reception
Tangun became the central symbol of **Korean national identity** in the 19th–20th centuries, when Korean religious and nationalist movements used him against Japanese colonial rule:
- **Cheondogyo / Donghak** (est. 1860 by Choe Je-u): syncretic Korean religion connecting Tangun's founding to a new revelation; positioned Tangun as proof of Korea's independent divine foundation
- **Daejonggyo** (est. 1909 by Na Cheol): religion that explicitly worships Tangun as god; the most explicit modern Tangun theology; drove anti-Japanese resistance
- **National Foundation Day** (*Gaecheonjeol*, October 3): South Korean national holiday commemorating Tangun's founding of Gojoseon in 2333 BCE — one of the few national holidays worldwide that commemorates a mythological founder as a historical date
- **North Korea**: the Kim dynasty claims its own quasi-divine founding narrative but also preserved a "Tangun tomb" (officially "rediscovered" 1993) at Kangdan — a political use of the founding myth for nationalist legitimacy

## Historicity debates
The *Samguk Yusa* (1281 CE) is the earliest text to record the Tangun myth in a coherent form; it was compiled by the Buddhist monk Iryeon during the Mongol occupation, possibly as a Korean nationalist assertion of independent cultural identity. Whether elements of the myth reflect pre-Buddhist Korean oral traditions from the Bronze Age or were substantially shaped by medieval Buddhist and Confucian frameworks is actively debated in Korean archaeology and historiography. The political stakes of the debate (national origin mythology as national identity) make it particularly sensitive.
