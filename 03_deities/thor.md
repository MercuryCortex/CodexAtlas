# Þórr

## Identity
Thunder-god of Norse and broader Germanic religion. Where Óðinn was the god of warriors-elite, kings, and the educated, Þórr was the god of *free farmers* — the broader Norse population's principal deity. Place-names with Þórr-elements (Tórshavn, Thorsten, etc.) and personal names (Þórsteinn, Þorgeirr) are far more numerous than Óðinn-elements; archaeological evidence (hammer-amulets, especially in the late Viking Age as Christian conversion advanced) shows Þórr was the popular cult-figure of late paganism. He defends gods and humans against the giants (*jötnar*) and is the principal active-protective deity.

## Earliest attestation
Tacitus *Germania* 9 identifies the Germans' "Hercules" — this is Þunor/Þórr. Bronze Age Scandinavian rock-art may show storm-god figures with hammers / axes. Old English *Þunor*; continental *Donar* (visible in *Donnerstag* "Thursday"). Old Norse literary attestation from skaldic poetry onward.

## Mythological role
Thor's principal mythic activity is fighting giants. The most famous narratives: the *Þrymskviða* (Thor recovers his hammer by cross-dressing as Freyja); the Útgarða-Loki episode (Thor humiliated by deceptive giant-magic); the Hymir-fishing-for-Miðgarðsormr (Thor catches the Midgard-Serpent, the central cosmic-combat scene, narrowly fails to kill it). At Ragnarǫk, Thor kills the Midgard-Serpent but dies from its poison.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **Jupiter / Zeus / [[indra]] / [[perun]] / Perkūnas** — *scholarly-parallel*: Indo-European storm-god comparative complex; Thor is the Germanic instantiation of a deep IE pattern. Perun is the Slavic cognate (cf. *Primary Chronicle* Rus'-Byzantine treaty oaths "by Perun their god"); Perkūnas is the Baltic cognate. See [[perun]] for the Slavic reconstruction.
- **"Hercules"** (Tacitus) — *ancient-identification*: Roman interpretatio of Germanic Thor as Hercules on the basis of strength + monster-fighting.

## Iconography
The hammer *Mjölnir* — the most distinctive attribute; goat-chariot; red beard; iron gloves and belt of strength. Hammer-amulets (Mjölnir pendants) survive in considerable archaeological number, especially from the conversion period (a counter-symbol to the cross).
