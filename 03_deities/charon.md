# Charon

## Identity

Charon is the ferryman of the dead in Greek myth — the grim, aged boatman who carries the souls of the deceased across the boundary rivers of the underworld (the Styx and the Acheron) into the realm of [[hades]]. He is reckoned a son of [[erebus]] (Darkness) and [[nyx-primordial]] (Night), fittingly born of the primordial gloom over which he plies his ferry.

## Mythological role

Charon admits to his ferry only the **properly buried** dead who can pay his fare — hence the Greek custom of placing a coin (an *obol*) in the mouth of the corpse, "Charon's obol." The unburied and the penniless are turned away to wander the near bank for a hundred years. He appears in the great underworld journeys of myth: Heracles, Orpheus, Aeneas, and Psyche all must deal with him to cross. Virgil's *Aeneid* VI gives the canonical portrait — squalid, fierce-eyed, eternally poling his rust-colored skiff. Though a minor deity with few myths of his own, Charon is one of the most vivid and enduring figures of the Greek underworld.

## Cross-tradition significance

Charon is the Greek instance of the widespread **psychopomp / death-crossing** motif — the figure who conducts or carries the soul across a water-boundary into the land of the dead. The image of the river-crossing into death, and of a fare or toll required for passage, recurs across many traditions, and "Charon" became so iconic that his name survives in Modern Greek (*Charos*) as a personification of death itself. He pairs with [[hermes-greek]] (the soul-guide) and [[thanatos-greek]] (Death) in the Greek division of the labors of dying.
