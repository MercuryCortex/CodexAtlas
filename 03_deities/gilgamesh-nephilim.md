# Gilgamesh (Nephilim — Book of Giants appearance)

## Identity
**This is the Watchers-tradition appearance of the Mesopotamian Gilgamesh** — the same figure as the canonical [[gilgamesh-king]] of the Sumerian-Akkadian Gilgamesh epic tradition, but appearing here in his **Second-Temple-Jewish apocalyptic Book of Giants context** as a *named Nephilim giant* in the pre-Diluvian Watchers-narrative.

The identification is documentary-textual and scholarly-consensual:

1. **Aramaic Qumran 4Q530** (Book of Giants fragment): the consonantal Aramaic name *glgmš* appears in a list of named giants. Józef Milik 1976 first identified this as Gilgamesh; Loren Stuckenbruck 1997 confirmed; the identification is now standard.

2. **Manichaean canonical Book of Giants** (Mani ~250 CE incorporated the Book of Giants into the seven canonical Manichaean scriptures): Gilgamesh appears by name in Middle Persian, Sogdian, Uyghur Turkic, Coptic, and Latin Manichaean witnesses — alongside **Humbaba** (the Mesopotamian-epic monster of the Cedar Forest, also a named Nephilim in the Manichaean Book of Giants). Reeves 1992 is definitive.

3. **The implications:** the Aramaic Book of Giants (~2nd c. BCE) and the Manichaean canonical reception (~3rd c. CE) both attest that **the Mesopotamian Gilgamesh and Humbaba were absorbed into the Second-Temple-Jewish Watchers-tradition as named Nephilim giants**. This is one of the most concrete cross-tradition tracing edges in late-Second-Temple-Jewish apocalyptic literature — a direct documentary linkage from the Bronze-Age Sumerian-Akkadian Gilgamesh hero (oral tradition ~2100 BCE; Standard Babylonian written version ~1300 BCE) into the Watchers-mythology that becomes Ethiopian-canonical via 1 Enoch.

## Earliest attestation
- **Sumerian Gilgamesh poems** (~2100 BCE oral; ~1800 BCE Old Babylonian written) — see [[gilgamesh-king]].
- **Akkadian Standard Babylonian Gilgamesh epic** (~1300 BCE, Sin-leqi-unninni recension) — the canonical Mesopotamian version.
- **Aramaic Qumran 4Q530** (~2nd–1st c. BCE) — the *glgmš* identification as Nephilim.
- **Manichaean canonical Book of Giants** (~3rd c. CE) — Gilgamesh + Humbaba as Nephilim.
- **Ethiopian-canonical 1 Enoch** (~5th c. CE Geʼez translation; continuous canonical use to present) — the parallel-and-source Watchers tradition that contextualizes the Book of Giants Gilgamesh.

## Mythological role
Within the Book of Giants frame, Gilgamesh-as-Nephilim is one among the named giants — appearing in the giant-council scenes alongside [[ohyah-nephilim]], [[hahyah-nephilim]], [[mahaway-nephilim]], and Humbaba. The Aramaic fragments are too brief to reconstruct a developed Gilgamesh-narrative within Book of Giants; the Manichaean reception is more elaborated and presents Gilgamesh and Humbaba as paired giant-figures.

The Mesopotamian Gilgamesh's *original* mythological role — king of Uruk, two-thirds divine and one-third human, friend of Enkidu, quester for immortality after Enkidu's death, recipient of Utnapishtim's flood-account — is *largely absent* from the Book of Giants frame. What the Second-Temple-Jewish and Manichaean compilers retained was the **name and the giant-stature**; the broader Mesopotamian narrative substance is essentially absent.

This makes the Gilgamesh-as-Nephilim a *case of cultural-religious appropriation under reframing*: the famous Bronze-Age hero is recruited into the Jewish Watchers-tradition as a named giant, with most of his original mythology suppressed.

## Cross-tradition identifications

- **[[gilgamesh-king]]** — *direct-identification*: the Mesopotamian-historical-mythological Gilgamesh is the same figure under his original cultural framing. This node is the *Watchers-tradition appearance*; [[gilgamesh-king]] is the *Mesopotamian-epic* presentation.
- **Humbaba / Hum-baba** (Mesopotamian Cedar Forest monster, also appearing in the Manichaean Book of Giants as a Nephilim) — *companion-figure*: paired with Gilgamesh both in the original epic and in the Book of Giants reception.
- **[[utnapishtim]]** (the Mesopotamian Flood-survivor; Gilgamesh-epic) — *related-thematic*: the Mesopotamian flood-tradition that the Book of Giants implicitly engages.

## Iconography
Not standardized in Watchers-tradition art. The Mesopotamian Gilgamesh iconography (king-of-Uruk imagery; the lion-clasping master-of-animals motif) is distinct from the Book of Giants framing.

## Disputes
- **The Milik identification.** Milik 1976 first proposed reading the Aramaic *glgmš* in 4Q530 as Gilgamesh; the identification was initially debated but is now scholarly consensus (Stuckenbruck 1997; Reeves 1992; Drawnel 2012).
- **Direct vs. mediated transmission.** Whether the Second-Temple-Jewish Book of Giants compilers had *direct* access to Mesopotamian Gilgamesh-epic material or knew the figure only through cultural-Mediterranean diffusion. The Aramaic context of the Book of Giants makes direct Akkadian-Aramaic transmission plausible (Aramaic was the Mesopotamian-imperial *lingua franca* in the Persian period); the precise transmission channel is not fully reconstructed.
- **Manichaean independent witness or derivative-from-Qumran?** Mani (~3rd c. CE) likely knew Aramaic Watchers-tradition material independently of the Qumran-sectarian preservation; the Manichaean reception is a *parallel* attestation, not strictly *derivative* (Reeves 1992).
