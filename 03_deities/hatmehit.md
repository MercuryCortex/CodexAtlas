# Hatmehit

## Identity

Hatmehit (also **Hatmehyt**; Egyptian *ḥꜢt-mḥyt*, conventionally rendered "**Foremost of the Fishes**") is the Egyptian **fish-goddess of Mendes** — the patroness of the city of Djedet (Greek **Mendes**, modern Tell el-Rubʿa) and of the sixteenth nome of Lower Egypt, whose very emblem was a fish. She is one of the very few Egyptian deities conceived in essentially **piscine** form: she is depicted as a woman crowned with a fish-standard, and in some representations with a fish upon her head or in wholly fish shape. The exact species behind her emblem is **not securely identified** — proposals include a Nile catfish, a *schilbe*, a *lepidotus*, and (on the strength of the later Mendes coinage) a dolphin (Hart 2005). Her name, taken as "foremost/chief of the fishes," marks her both as the leading fish-deity and as the tutelary power of a nome whose standard was itself a fish.

## In Egyptian religion (the cult of Mendes)

Hatmehit's home is the Mendesian nome of the eastern Delta, where she is the local goddess of **Djedet/Mendes**. In the developed theology of the cult-centre she is paired with the ram-god **[[banebdjedet]]** (the "Ram, Lord of Djedet"), whose consort she is, and the two stand at the head of the **Mendesian triad** together with their child — generally identified as **Harpocrates** (Har-pa-khered, "Horus the Child"). This father–mother–child grouping is the local Mendesian instance of the standard Egyptian divine triad, structurally parallel to the great triads of Osiris–[[isis-egyptian|Isis]]–[[horus|Horus]] and of Amun–Mut–Khonsu (Wilkinson 2003).

Mendes was a significant and long-lived cult-centre: Herodotus describes the nome and the veneration of the ram there in the fifth century BCE (*Histories* 2.42, 2.46), and the city remained important into the Ptolemaic period, when the **Mendes Stela of Ptolemy II Philadelphus** records royal attention to its cults. Hatmehit's relative prominence shifts over time — in the later record the ram-god Banebdjedet tends to dominate the theology of Mendes, with Hatmehit as the established consort and nome-patroness rather than the leading figure — but her identity as the fish-goddess and "Foremost of the Fishes" is stable across the attestations.

## Cross-tradition significance

Hatmehit is a **strongly local** deity: she is a nome-goddess of the Delta with no developed cult outside the Mendesian region and no ancient identification with a foreign deity in the surviving record. Her principal cross-cultural foothold is **indirect**, through her consort: Herodotus (2.46) identifies the **ram of Mendes** (Banebdjedet) with the Greek **[[pan-greek|Pan]]** by way of the shared caprid imagery, an *interpretatio graeca* move that places the Mendes cult — and Hatmehit as its goddess — within the Greek ethnographic horizon, even though the equation is made of the ram and not of the fish-goddess herself.

Two further points of comparative interest are worth stating carefully. First, Hatmehit belongs to the small set of **theriomorphic Egyptian goddesses defined by a single animal** — comparable in form (though not in domain or myth) to the frog-goddess **[[heqet|Heqet]]** or the lioness **[[sekhmet|Sekhmet]]** — and is among the rare Egyptian deities whose emblematic animal is a **fish**, an animal more often treated as taboo or ambivalent in Egyptian cult than as divine. Second, as **mother of the child-god in the Mendes triad** she participates in the broadly diffused Egyptian father–mother–child triad pattern; this is a **structural** parallel internal to Egyptian religion, not evidence of transmission to or from any other tradition. There is no scholarly claim of genetic connection between Hatmehit and fish-deities of other cultures (e.g. the Mesopotamian fish-cloaked sages or the Philistine Dagon, whose fish-association is itself disputed): the resemblance, where noted at all, is **typological**, and the responsible statement is that Hatmehit is an indigenous Egyptian nome-goddess whose fish-form is a local Delta development.

## Disputes

The chief unresolved question is **iconographic**: which fish lies behind Hatmehit's emblem. The ancient evidence is not decisive, and modern identifications range from a Nile catfish or *schilbe* to a *lepidotus* and, on the basis of the Roman-period Mendes coinage, a dolphin (Hart 2005); the Atlas follows the standard references in leaving the species **open**. A second, lesser point is the **changing balance** between Hatmehit and Banebdjedet at Mendes: some treatments emphasise an earlier period in which the fish-goddess was the senior local deity, later overshadowed by the ram-god, but the evidence for a clear chronological precedence is thin and is best presented as a tendency rather than an established sequence.
