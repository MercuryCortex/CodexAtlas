# Logos (Stoic)

## Identity
**Note: this is a concept-deity entry.** The Stoic *logos* is not a deity in the cultic sense — it is not the object of temple worship, sacrifice, or prayer in the way Zeus, Isis, or Mithras are. It is a **philosophical concept** that the Stoics treat with theological seriousness: the cosmos is a single living rational divine being, and the *logos* is the *substance* of that divinity. Because Stoicism's *logos* is one of the most consequential theological-philosophical concepts in the history of religion — feeding directly into Philo, Johannine Christology, Patristic theology, and the entire Christian-Trinitarian tradition — we treat it here in the deity register, flagged as a *concept-deity*.

## Earliest attestation
- The *logos*-concept enters Greek philosophy with **Heraclitus** (~-500), in fragments that the Stoics inherited and developed: "The logos is common, but most live as if they had a private wisdom" (DK B2); "all things come to pass according to this logos" (B1).
- **Zeno of Citium** (~-300), founder of the Stoa, articulates the doctrine: the cosmos is a single living being whose body is matter and whose soul/active principle is the *logos*-*pneuma*.
- **Chrysippus** (~-230) systematizes Stoic theology; his nearly 700 lost works expanded the logos doctrine in immense detail (SVF II).
- **Cleanthes' *Hymn to Zeus*** (~-260, surviving complete) prays to Zeus as the "ever-ruling, ever-guiding, hated by no one but the wicked" *logos* that "runs through all things."

## Mythological role
The Stoic logos is not narrative — it has no biography, no consort, no children. It is *cosmological* and *ethical*:
- **Cosmologically**: the logos is the active principle (*to poioun*) that informs passive matter (*to paschon*), making the cosmos rational, providential, and beautiful. It is *material* — Stoics are materialists; logos is fiery *pneuma* extending through everything.
- **Theologically**: identified with **Zeus** (Cleanthes, Chrysippus); identified with **fate / *heimarmenē***; identified with **providence / *pronoia***. The "civic" Zeus is read as a popular-religious expression of the philosophical truth.
- **Ethically**: human reason is a portion of the cosmic logos (*logos spermatikos* — "seminal logos", "seeds of the logos"). Virtue is rational accord with the cosmic logos. To live "according to nature" (*kata phusin*) is to live "according to logos."
- **Eschatologically**: at the end of each cosmic cycle, the universe is consumed in a great conflagration (*ekpyrosis*) and reborn — the logos remains continuous through these cycles.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[zeus]]** — *scholarly-parallel* (within Stoic theology): Stoics identify Zeus *philosophically* with the cosmic logos; this is interpretive theology, not a new mythic claim about civic Zeus.
- **[[logos-philonic]]** — *direct-influence*: Philo of Alexandria takes the Stoic immanent-cosmic-rationality logos and fuses it with the Platonic world of Forms, producing a *Logos as God's first-born son and architect of creation*. **Step 1 → Step 2 of the great Stoic → Philo → John transmission.**
- **[[logos-johannine]]** — *direct-influence*: John 1:1's "In the beginning was the Logos..." presupposes Philonic-Hellenistic-Jewish logos theology, which presupposes Stoic logos.
- **[[heraclitus]]** — *direct-influence* (ancestor): Heraclitus is the philosophical ancestor of the Stoic logos doctrine.

## Iconography
None proper. Symbolic associations:
- **Fire** — the substance of the cosmic pneuma-logos
- **The cosmos itself** as the body of the divine
- **Seed** (*sperma*) — the seminal logos in each rational being
- **Web / network** — for the Stoic notion of cosmic sympathy and interconnection

## Disputes
- **Theism or pantheism?** Stoics call the cosmos God and call God material (active *pneuma*). Whether this is genuine theism (Frede, Long) or pantheism (Lapidge, Inwood) or pantheism-with-theistic-syntax is contested. The Stoics themselves did not feel a need to choose.
- **Providence and the problem of evil.** Stoic providence governs all events, including apparent evils; the Stoic response (Epictetus *Discourses* 1.6, Marcus Aurelius *Meditations*) is that apparent evils are good when viewed in cosmic perspective. Critics ancient (Carneades, Plotinus) and modern have found this unsatisfying.
- **Christian appropriation.** **Justin Martyr** (~+150) develops *logos spermatikos* into a Christian doctrine: the Stoic seeds of logos in all rational beings are *partial participations in Christ-the-Logos*; thus Heraclitus and Socrates were "Christians before Christ" (*1 Apol.* 46). This Christian-Stoic synthesis is one of the most consequential moves in the history of philosophy.
