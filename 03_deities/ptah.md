# Ptah

Ptah is the creator god of Memphite Theology, patron of craftsmen and artisans, and (in the Memphite Theology articulated on the Shabaka Stone) the supreme creator whose thought and word bring all things into being. Memphis was Egypt's Old Kingdom administrative capital and remained one of its most important religious centers; the Greek name "Egypt" itself derives from *Ḥwt-kꜢ-Ptḥ* ("House of Ptah's Ka"), the Memphite temple-complex name. Ptah's iconography — mummified, with tight-fitting cap, holding the composite *was-djed-ankh* scepter — is distinctive and stable across two millennia. He is paired with the lion-goddess [[sekhmet]] and the child-god Nefertem in the Memphite divine triad.

## Mythological role
Ptah's central theological role is set out in the **Memphite Theology**, a text preserved on the *Shabaka Stone* (in the British Museum, EA 498) — a basalt slab inscribed under Pharaoh Shabaka (25th Dynasty, ~700 BCE) but explicitly stating that the text was copied from a worm-eaten papyrus already much older. Most scholars (Allen 1988, Lichtheim 1973) accept the Memphite Theology as preserving Old or Middle Kingdom material. In this text, Ptah creates by **thought (in the heart) and word (on the tongue)**: "Ptah's teeth and lips form the seed and hands of Atum… The Ennead is the seed and hands of Atum. The Ennead came into being through the seed and the hands of Atum." The text subordinates the Heliopolitan ennead to Ptah, presenting Atum and the other gods as expressions of Ptah's creative thought-and-word. This is one of the world's earliest articulations of [[creation-by-word]] theology — directly comparable to (and significantly older than) the Logos doctrines of Hellenistic Judaism (Philo) and early Christianity (the Gospel of John's prologue), and to Genesis 1's "God said, let there be..."
