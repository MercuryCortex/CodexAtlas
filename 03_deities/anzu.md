# Anzu

## Identity

Anzu (earlier Sumerian **Imdugud**) is a monstrous divine being of Mesopotamian myth, depicted as a lion-headed eagle and associated with the storm, the thundercloud, and the chaotic powers that the ordered cosmos must subdue. In the third-millennium Sumerian record Imdugud is a great bird with outstretched wings — a powerful, ambivalent storm-creature — and by the second-millennium Akkadian tradition Anzu has become the antagonist of a major combat epic.

## Mythological role

The Standard Babylonian *Epic of Anzu* tells how Anzu, entrusted to guard the entrance to [[enlil]]'s chamber, seizes his chance and steals the **Tablet of Destinies** — the object that confers the authority to decree the fates of all things — while Enlil bathes. With the Tablet, Anzu becomes nearly invincible, and the gods despair until [[ninurta]] (in the older Sumerian version Ningirsu, or the hero Lugalbanda) takes up the challenge. After an initial failure — Anzu uses the Tablet's power to unmake Ninurta's arrows in flight — Ninurta, advised by Ea, overcomes Anzu, slays him, and restores the Tablet of Destinies, securing the rule of the gods. The victory is one of the foundations of Ninurta's warrior-kingship.

## Cross-tradition significance

Anzu is one of the clearest Mesopotamian examples of the **chaoskampf** mytheme: the cosmic monster whose defeat by the storm-warrior establishes divine order and legitimate kingship. It stands structurally alongside Marduk's defeat of [[tiamat]] in the *Enuma Elish* and Ninurta's defeat of [[asag]] in *Lugal-e*. The lion-headed-eagle iconography and the storm-bird-who-steals-power motif have invited comparison with later thunderbird and griffin figures, and the Tablet-of-Destinies theft resonates with the wider ancient Near Eastern motif of the contested object that confers cosmic sovereignty.

## Iconography

The Sumerian Imdugud is shown as a lion-headed eagle, often with wings spread and grasping two lions or stags in its talons (as on the famous Imdugud relief from Tell al-‘Ubaid). Later Anzu appears as a great bird of prey. The image was protective as well as fearsome — Imdugud could function as an emblem of the storm-god's might.
