# Nut

## Identity
Nut is the Egyptian sky goddess: in the Heliopolitan cosmogony, daughter of Shu (Air) and Tefnut (Moisture), sister-consort of Geb (Earth), mother of Osiris, Isis, Set, and Nephthys. Egyptian cosmology is notable in the wider Mediterranean / Near Eastern context for assigning the sky to a *goddess* (and the earth to a *god*) — the inverse of the more common pattern (compare Greek Gaia-Uranus or Sumerian Ki-An).

## Earliest attestation
- [[phase-1-002-pyramid-texts]] — already fully attested in Old Kingdom royal funerary literature.
- Painted continually inside coffin lids, sarcophagi, and tomb ceilings throughout pharaonic history — Nut arches over the deceased as the protective sky, often the most prominent figure in burial art.

## Mythological role
- **Daily solar cycle.** Nut swallows the sun (Ra) at evening, the sun travels through her body during the night, and she gives birth to him at dawn. The act of swallowing-and-giving-birth is repeated daily and is the model for the dead's hoped-for daily rebirth.
- **Separation of sky and earth.** Shu separates Nut from Geb by stepping between them and lifting her into her arched-sky position. The iconography (Nut arched, Shu raising her, Geb beneath) is one of the most reproduced Egyptian cosmogonic images.
- **Mother of the funerary gods.** As mother of Osiris she is the grandmother of Horus and the great-grandmother (so to speak) of every pharaoh.
- **Protectress of the dead.** Coffins and burial chambers are decorated with Nut's body to identify burial with the journey through her body toward rebirth at dawn.

## Iconography
- Arched naked feminine body forming the sky-vault
- Star-spangled
- Sometimes shown as a cow (the "celestial cow" — overlapping with Hathor)
- Sometimes as a sycamore-tree from which she nurses the dead

## Nut and the daily solar cycle

The most theologically active role Nut plays is her relationship with [[ra]]: **every evening the sun enters Nut's mouth (she swallows him); every morning the sun is born from Nut's body (she gives birth to him)**. This daily cycle is not merely metaphor — it is a precise cosmological mechanism that Egyptian theology uses to explain the sun's disappearance at sunset and reappearance at dawn.

The Egyptian term for sunset (*ḥtp*, "the sun rests / is received") and sunrise (*wbn*, "the sun shines forth / rises") are both implicated in this Nut-theology. The sun's nightly journey *through* Nut's body is the same journey described in the [[phase-1-036-amduat]] — the twelve-hour traversal of the underworld, the meeting of [[ra]] and [[osiris]] at midnight in the deepest chamber (the "Ra-Osiris midnight union," the most theologically potent moment in the Egyptian afterlife system). Nut's body is the outer container of this journey: the sun enters at her mouth (west), traverses the twelve nocturnal hours, and is reborn at her birth-canal (east).

This makes Nut simultaneously **death** and **birth** — the same body is both the moment of disappearance and the mechanism of resurrection. The theological structure is: **to be swallowed by Nut is to be set on the path of rebirth**. No other figure in Egyptian religion so completely embodies the identity of death-and-resurrection in a single continuous physical act (Allen 2005, Pyramid Texts §§ 785–790 on Nut's birthing language for the dead king).

## MASSIVE WIN: Nut on coffin lids — the dead inside the cosmic mother

The most consequential artistic decision in Egyptian funerary practice is the **painted image of Nut arched across the interior surface of virtually every Egyptian coffin lid**. When the mummy was placed inside the coffin, the deceased was positioned *inside Nut's body* — nestled against the inner surface on which her image was painted, enclosed within the cosmic womb that swallows and rebirths the sun each day.

This is one of the most powerful **resurrection-theology images in world religion**, and it operates on multiple simultaneous registers:

1. **Solar register**: the deceased enacts the sun's nightly journey — entering Nut at sundown, traversing the underworld, being reborn at dawn. Every morning the sun rises; the deceased's hoped-for resurrection is the same event.
2. **Maternal register**: to die is to return to the mother's body. Resurrection is not escape from the body but *re-emergence from it*. This is a theological inversion of the common anxiety about death: the coffin is not a box of confinement but a womb of transformation.
3. **Cosmic register**: the individual deceased is placed in the same position as [[osiris]] — the dying-god whose body is held within the cosmic structure, awaiting resurrection through the intercession of [[isis]] and the mechanism of [[ra]]'s daily cycle.

The [[phase-1-002-pyramid-texts]] (the oldest religious texts in the world, c. 2350–2175 BCE) contain explicit address to Nut as the mother of the dead king: *"O my mother Nut, spread yourself over me, so that I may be placed among the imperishable stars and never die"* (Utterance 443, Allen 2005 trans.). This formula is the theological origin of every coffin-lid painting of Nut — the image enacts the prayer.

The transmission of this theology into later traditions is a `parallel-motif` rather than `ancestor-of` without documented transmission, but the structural resonance with later resurrection frameworks (the cosmic womb in Gnostic cosmologies; the "return to the father" in Neoplatonic emanation; the tomb as threshold of rebirth in early Christian theology) makes Nut's coffin-iconography one of the most important **visual theological arguments** in antiquity (Hornung 1982).

## Nut as the Milky Way

Several Egyptologists, most influentially **Erik Hornung** (*Conceptions of God in Ancient Egypt*, 1982) and **Joanne Scarlata** in more recent archaeoastronomical work, have proposed that Nut's star-spangled arched body was specifically identified with the **Milky Way** — the galactic band that arches visibly across the dark Egyptian desert sky. If correct, this makes Egyptian cosmology **one of the oldest documented astronomical-theological interpretations of the Milky Way as a divine body**.

The evidence is visual and textual:
- The standard Nut image — a naked woman arched with stars densely distributed across her body — precisely mirrors the Milky Way's appearance as a bright stellar band arching across the sky.
- The Milky Way was visible in Egyptian skies with extraordinary clarity (no light pollution; ultra-dry desert air) and would have dominated nighttime cosmological thinking.
- Some Pyramid Text passages describe Nut as containing all the stars in her body, not merely as decorated with them — suggesting an identification of Nut's body with the stellar band rather than the general sky.

The identification remains **scholarly hypothesis rather than proven claim** (the Egyptians did not leave a single explicit text saying "Nut IS the Milky Way"). But it is a well-supported `parallel-motif` and an active field of Egyptological and archaeoastronomical research. If confirmed, it would make the Egyptian tradition the earliest known cosmic mythology centered on the Milky Way as a divine feminine figure — predating Greek (the milk of Hera), Norse (the road of the gods), and Hindu (the celestial river Ganga) Milky Way mythologies by two millennia (Hornung 1982).
