# Vāyu

## Identity
Vāyu (Sanskrit वायु, from *vā-*, "to blow") is the Vedic god of wind and breath — the lord of the invisible, the first god to drink Soma after Indra, and the divine embodiment of *prāṇa* (breath as life-force). In the Vedic cosmological scheme, Vāyu and Prāṇa are the same principle at different scales: what wind is to the cosmos, breath is to the body. The Sanskrit root *vā-* underlies both *vāyu* (wind) and *vāta* (air/wind in its more demonic or disease-causing aspect in Āyurveda) — the same force experienced benevolently (life-giving breath) or malevolently (storm, pestilence-carrying air).

Vāyu's structural primacy in Vedic sacrifice is encoded in the soma-offering order: Indra and Vāyu receive the first soma pressings before all other gods — Vāyu because he is fastest, because wind arrives before any other divine presence at the sacrifice. He is *pavana* (the Purifier) and *anila* (the Ever-Moving). He is the psychopomp who carries the breath of the dying out of the body and, in some Vedic formulations, returns it to the wind of the cosmos.

In the epics Vāyu fathers two of the most celebrated heroes: **Hanuman** (the divine monkey-warrior of the Rāmāyaṇa) and **Bhīma** (the strongest Pandava of the Mahābhārata). The wind-deity's son is the culture hero defined by invincible physical power and the ability to move with supernatural speed — the structural inheritance of the wind-father.

**The single most significant fact about Vāyu for cross-tradition investigation** is his Zoroastrian doubling: in the Avestan reform, Vāyu/Vāta becomes **split between the two cosmic armies** — good Vayu (functioning on Ahura Mazda's side, associated with protective wind and breath of life) and evil Vāta (functioning with Angra Mainyu, associated with death, storm-wind, disease). A single Vedic deity bifurcated into cosmic good and cosmic evil — preserved in the same tradition that demonized Indra and the Devas wholesale. This is one of the clearest windows into the Vedic/Zoroastrian schism as a *revaluation* rather than a clean break.

## Earliest attestation
- **Rig Veda** (~-1500 to -1200 oral) — Vāyu hymns are present across multiple books; RV 1.134 is the canonical Vāyu hymn; he is frequently paired with Indra as *Indra-Vāyu*. ([[phase-2-001-rig-veda-family-books]])
- **Atharva Veda** (~-1200 to -900) — wind/breath magical hymns; Prāṇa-Vāyu connection explicit. ([[phase-2-003-atharva-veda]])
- **Avesta** (~-1000 to -600) — *Hōm Yašt* and the *Vayu Yašt* (Yt. 15) preserve the split Vayu: dual-natured, serving both sides. (Boyce 1975 — the *Vayu Yašt* is the primary Zoroastrian evidence.)
- **Mahābhārata and Rāmāyaṇa** (~-400 to 400 CE) — epic mythology of Vāyu as father of Hanuman and Bhīma; the wind-hero-father pattern fully developed.

## Mythological role
1. **First soma recipient and sacrificial breath.** At the great soma sacrifice, Vāyu and Indra receive the first pressing before any other deity. Vāyu is fastest — wind moves before fire can be lit, before the earth trembles, before any other divine arrival. His structural position in the sacrifice encodes a cosmological priority.

2. **Psychopomp.** In Vedic death theology, the dying person's breath (*prāṇa*) returns to the wind (*vāyu*) — the individual life-force is absorbed back into the cosmic life-force. Vāyu carries the soul-breath out of the dying body. This is the Vedic version of the psychopomp function.

3. **Father of the wind-heroes.** Hanuman is the paradigmatic wind-born hero: can fly, move invisibly, cross the ocean in a single leap, carry mountains. Bhīma is invincible in physical strength. Both inherit the wind's defining qualities: speed, invisible force, the power of breath and storm.

4. **Zoroastrian split.** Good Vayu is protective, breath-giving, aligned with the divine order; evil Vata brings storms, death-wind, pestilence. The same Indo-Iranian deity preserving two aspects that Vedic tradition held together in tension, which Zoroastrian theology separated into distinct moral agents.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[enlil]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: The Sumerian and Akkadian Enlil is the wind-air deity who becomes the supreme deity of the Sumerian pantheon (AN-ENLIL-EA triad). Like Vāyu, Enlil's wind is the breath of the cosmos; his decree (*me* = cosmic law transmitted through wind) parallels Vāyu's sacrificial primacy. No documented transmission; independent convergence on the wind-as-cosmic-breath pattern. (Macdonell 1897)
- **[[fujin]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Japanese wind-god Fujin; wind as a distinct divine force, carrying a bag of winds (parallel to wind as contained/released). Structural parallel; no transmission.
- **[[stribog]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Slavic wind-deity; possible distant Indo-European inheritance from the same proto-IE wind-deity. The connection is underdetermined but plausible given the Indo-European distribution.
- **[[ahura-mazda]]** — *ancient-identification* (Zoroastrian): Good Vayu is aligned with Ahura Mazda's cosmic order in the Avesta; documented in the *Vayu Yašt*. This is the key cross-tradition edge — the Vedic-Zoroastrian schism bifurcated the deity.

**The breath-wind-life-force convergence** is one of the vault's deepest cross-linguistic patterns:
- Sanskrit *prāṇa/vāyu* = Greek *pneuma* (breath, spirit, wind) = Hebrew *ruach* (breath, wind, spirit of God moving on the waters) = Latin *spiritus* (breath → Holy Spirit) = Old Norse *önd* (breath of life given by Odin to the first humans). The linguistic independence of these equations makes their convergence structurally significant: across unconnected traditions, "wind" and "breath" and "life" and "spirit" are the same thing, and that thing generates the divine.

## Iconography
- Predominantly invisible — wind has no fixed iconographic form in early Vedic tradition.
- In later Puranic and temple iconography: a young male figure, white or dark blue, riding in a chariot drawn by forty-nine horses (the number encoding the 49 Maruts, the wind-storm troops who are his companions).
- The *Maruts* (storm-gods, the wind-troop) are closely associated with Vāyu and in some traditions are his sons.

## Disputes
- **Vāyu vs. Vāta**: The two names denote closely related but distinguishable aspects — Vāyu more often the benevolent, sacrificial, breath-giving wind; Vāta more often the pathological, disease-causing wind. In Āyurvedic medicine *vāta* is a dosha (bodily humor) that can become unbalanced. Whether these represent a single deity or two from the start is debated.
- **The Zoroastrian split**: Boyce (1975) documents the split Vayu in the *Vayu Yašt*, but the question of whether the split was a Zoroastrian innovation or whether the dual character existed already in proto-Indo-Iranian religion is unresolved.
- **Vāyu and the Maruts**: The relationship between Vāyu (singular wind-god) and the Maruts (the storm-troop, 7 or 49 in number) is ambiguous in the Rigveda; in some passages the Maruts are Vāyu's warriors, in others they are independent.
