# Āpaḥ (the Waters)

## Identity

The **Āpaḥ** (आपः, "the Waters") are the deified waters of the Rigveda — at once a class of beneficent goddesses and the undifferentiated primordial flood out of which the ordered cosmos arises. In their everyday cultic aspect the Waters are invoked as purifying, healing, life-giving **Mothers**; in the cosmogonic hymns of the late tenth book they are the **salila**, the dark indistinct flood that is "all this" before creation begins. In the Atlas the Āpaḥ are placed among the primordial entities beside [[tehom]], [[tiamat]], [[nun]], and [[ginnungagap]] because they fill the Vedic position in the cross-tradition primordial-waters slot — the formless water before the world.

## In the Rigveda

The Waters appear in two registers. As **deities**, they are hymned in the family books and beyond — most fully in the Āpaḥ hymn (RV 7.49) and RV 10.9, whose opening "*āpo hi ṣṭhā mayobhuvaḥ*" ("O Waters, you are indeed the bringers of comfort") is still recited in daily ritual: they purify, heal, carry away sin, and are addressed as mothers and goddesses.

As **cosmogonic principle**, the Waters are the substrate of creation. The Nāsadīya Sūkta (RV 10.129.3) describes the beginning as "*tama āsīt tamasā gūḷham agre 'praketaṃ salilaṃ sarvam ā idam*" — "there was darkness hidden by darkness in the beginning; an undistinguished **salila** (flood) was all this." The Hiraṇyagarbha Sūkta (RV 10.121.7) makes the Waters the bearer of the cosmic germ: "when the mighty Waters came, bearing the universal embryo (garbha) and giving birth to Agni, then arose the one breath of the gods." The primordial waters are thus both the formless precondition and the maternal medium that holds the first spark of ordered being.

## Cross-tradition significance (the Vedic deep)

The Āpaḥ are the **Vedic member of the cross-tradition primordial-waters cluster**: the undifferentiated water before creation, named *salila* in the Veda, *tĕhôm* in Israel, *Tiāmat* in Babylon, *Nun* in Egypt, and answered by *Ginnungagap* in the Norse world. Two qualifications matter. First, the Vedic case is **not a borrowing** from the Near East: it is an independent — and, with the Norse, an inherited **Indo-European** — development of the formless-precondition cosmogony (West 2007 derives the Vedic and Germanic pre-creation states from a common IE inheritance). Second, the Rigveda preserves the watery **Chaoskampf** in its own key: the serpent **[[vritra|Vṛtra]]** dams the cosmic waters, and **Indra** slays him to release them (RV 1.32). Where Marduk *defeats* the water-chaos Tiāmat, Indra *frees* the captive Waters — the same storm-god-versus-water-dragon structure, with the moral charge reversed: the Waters are the good that is liberated, not the chaos that is slain. The sovereign of these waters and of cosmic order (ṛta) is **[[varuna|Varuṇa]]**; in the later Purāṇic tradition the same primordial ocean becomes the **Kṣīra Sāgara**, the Milky Ocean on which Viṣṇu sleeps between world-ages.

## Disputes

The translation of *salila* in RV 10.129.3 is itself contested — "flood," "water," "surge," or more abstractly "indistinct flux" — and with it the question of how literally "watery" the Nāsadīya's primordial state is meant to be; the hymn's whole rhetorical strategy is to leave the precondition deliberately under-described (Brereton 1999). The relationship of the cultic Waters (the healing Mothers) to the cosmogonic salila is one of resonance rather than explicit identity in the texts. And while the structural parallel to the Near-Eastern deeps is real, scholars stress that it reflects either common Indo-European inheritance (with the Norse void) or independent invention, **not** transmission — the Vedic Waters carry no equivalent of the *tĕhôm*–*Tiāmat* loan-cognate.
