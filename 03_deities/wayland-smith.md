# Wayland the Smith

## Identity

Wayland (Old Norse **Völundr**, Old English **Weland**, German **Wieland**) is the legendary master-smith of Germanic tradition — a figure of superhuman craft whose name was a byword for the finest workmanship across the medieval Germanic world. In the Norse account he is of elvish kin ("lord of elves"); in the wider legend he is the maker of peerless swords and armor, and the hero of one of the darkest and most powerful revenge-tales in Northern myth.

## Mythological role

Wayland's story is told most fully in the Eddic poem **Völundarkviða**. King **Niðhad** captures the smith, **hamstrings him** to keep him from fleeing, and confines him on an island to forge treasures. Wayland's revenge is terrible: he lures the king's two young sons to his smithy and kills them, fashioning **goblets from their skulls, jewels from their eyes, and a brooch from their teeth**, which he sends to the unsuspecting royal family; he then rapes (or seduces) the king's daughter Böðvildr. Finally, having forged himself **wings** (or a feather-coat), Wayland rises into the air, proclaims his vengeance to the horrified king, and **flies away**. The tale was immensely popular: Wayland appears on the Anglo-Saxon Franks Casket, in *Beowulf* (which names a mail-coat as "Weland's work"), and across Germanic heroic legend.

## Cross-tradition significance

Wayland is the Germanic embodiment of the **supernatural master-smith** — and, more specifically, of the recurrent figure of the **lamed smith**. The parallel with the Greek [[hephaestus]] is striking: both are the supreme craftsmen of their worlds, and both are **crippled in the legs**, their lameness inseparable from their art (a pattern some scholars connect to the real-world association of smiths with injury, or to the smith as a liminal, set-apart figure). With the Vedic [[tvashtar]], the Canaanite [[kothar-wa-khasis]], and the Celtic [[goibniu]], Wayland completes the cross-tradition company of the divine and legendary smiths who forge the great weapons of gods and heroes.
