# Aengus Óg

## Identity
Aengus Óg ("Young Aengus" or "Aengus the Young Son," also *Mac Óc*, "Young Son") is the Irish god of love, youth, and poetic inspiration — son of the [[dagda]] (the Good God, chief of the Tuatha Dé Danann) and [[boann]] (the divine source of the River Boyne). His home is **Brú na Bóinne** — the Newgrange megalithic passage tomb in the Boyne valley, which he obtained from his father by a mythological legal trick: he asked for the Brú "for a day and a night," and argued that *all* time consists of days and nights, and thus he should never have to leave. The story, preserved in the *Aislinge Óenguso*, neatly encodes his association with timelessness and the Otherworld.

Four birds fly from his head — they are his kisses, taking visible form. Wherever he goes, love and poetry follow.

## The Swan-Maiden
The most famous Aengus myth (*Aislinge Óenguso*, "The Dream of Aengus"): Aengus falls in love with a woman he sees only in dreams — Caer Ibormeith, who alternates between human form and swan form each year at Samhain. He searches for a year, finally finds her as a swan on Loch Bél Dracon, transforms himself into a swan to be with her, and they fly together to Brú na Bóinne, singing music that puts all who hear it to sleep for three days.

The story is one of the most beautiful in Irish mythology — a love that requires the lover to transform himself to match the beloved's liminal nature.

## Association with Newgrange
Aengus's home is **Newgrange** (Brú na Bóinne) — the 5,200-year-old megalithic monument on the Boyne. The Irish mythological tradition thus assigns one of the most spectacular Neolithic monuments in the world to a deity of love and youth. The monument's winter-solstice solar alignment (the rising sun illuminates the inner chamber only at the winter solstice) and Aengus's solar/light associations (golden hair, birds as kisses of light) suggest a deep continuity between the Neolithic monument and the later mythological figure.
