# Chaos

## Identity
Chaos (Greek *Χάος*, from *χαίνω*, "to yawn / gape") is **the first being in Hesiodic cosmogony** — the primordial yawning gap or void from which the universe emerges. [[hesiod]] *Theogony* 116–122 opens: **"First of all there came into being Chaos; then broad-breasted Gaia... and Tartarus... and Eros... From Chaos came Erebus and dark Nyx."** Chaos is not "disorder" in the modern English sense — that meaning is a Roman-Stoic-Latin development — but the **primordial yawning gap of pre-cosmic being**, structurally parallel to Egyptian [[nun]], Mesopotamian Apsu/Tiamat, and other Near Eastern primordial-waters cosmogonies.

## Earliest attestation
- **[[hesiod]]** *Theogony* 116 (c. -700) — the foundational Greek attestation.
- Orphic theogonies (multiple recensions, see [[phase-2-009-hesiod-theogony-works-and-days]] and Orphic-cosmogonic theme) variously place Chaos before or after other primordials.
- **Pre-Socratic reception**: Pherecydes of Syros, Anaximander, and Anaximenes all rework the Hesiodic Chaos in different philosophical directions.
- **Aristotle** *Metaphysics* 1.4 (984b27–985a10) — reads Hesiod's Chaos as a primordial material substrate; this Aristotelian reading is load-bearing for the medieval reception.
- **Ovid** *Metamorphoses* 1.5–9 — Latin reworking: "Before the sea and the lands and the sky that covers all things, the face of nature in the whole world was one — they called it Chaos: a rough and unordered mass." Ovid's reading colors all later Latin and vernacular European cosmogonies.

## Mythological role
In Hesiod, Chaos is the **first thing that comes into being** (Hesiod uses *γένετο*, "came to be" — Chaos itself has a birth, suggesting Hesiod is *not* using it as a strict primordial uncreated principle). From Chaos emerge [[nyx-primordial]] (Night) and Erebus (Darkness); from their union come Aether and Hemera (Day). Gaia (Earth), Tartarus, and Eros also come into being immediately after Chaos in Hesiod's sequence.

Chaos has **no narrative mythology** in the standard sense — no actions, no descendants beyond the immediate cosmogonic sequence, no cult-presence. He/she/it is purely a **theogonic principle**.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[nun]]** (Egyptian) — *scholarly-parallel*: the Egyptian primordial waters from which Atum/Ra/the world emerges; structurally parallel to Chaos. Whether the parallel reflects direct transmission (West 1997 favors substantial Near Eastern influence on Hesiodic cosmogony) or independent invention is debated.
- **[[apsu]] / [[tiamat]]** (Mesopotamian, *Enuma Elish*) — *scholarly-parallel*: primordial waters whose division generates the cosmos. The *Enuma Elish* structural pattern is widely held to influence Hesiodic cosmogony (West 1966, 1997).
- **[[primordial-waters]]** (cross-cultural theme) — *parallel-motif*.
- **Genesis 1:2 *tohu va-bohu*** — *scholarly-parallel*: "the earth was void and without form" (KJV) / "wild and waste" — formally parallel pre-cosmic state. Direct genealogy from Mesopotamian sources is well documented for Genesis 1 (the Atrahasis / Enuma Elish lineage); Chaos sits in the same cross-cultural family.
- **Christian-Platonist *prime matter*** — *appropriated-by*: medieval Christian readings of Hesiodic Chaos, mediated through Aristotle's *Metaphysics* and Ovid's *Metamorphoses*, contribute to Christian-Platonist conceptions of prime matter / *materia prima*; Augustine, Aquinas, and Renaissance Platonists all engage Chaos as theological-philosophical principle (not merely classical-literary reference).

## Iconography
- **No standard anthropomorphic iconography in classical Greek art.**
- Late-antique and Renaissance art represents Chaos as a swirling formless cloud or a confused mass of elements (especially in Ovid-illustration cycles).

## Disputes
- **Etymology and meaning of "Chaos."** The Hesiodic Chaos is **NOT "disorder"** in the modern English sense — it is the yawning gap (*χαίνω*, "to gape"). The "disorder" meaning is a Roman-Stoic-Latin development (Ovid *Metamorphoses* 1.5–9 mediates it). Confusion of these two senses is one of the most persistent errors in popular treatments of Greek cosmogony.
- **Created or uncreated?** Hesiod's verb *γένετο* ("came to be") implies Chaos itself has a birth — a strikingly different theological structure from later monotheist creation accounts where the primordial is uncreated. Aristotle and Plato read Hesiod variously on this question.
- **Near Eastern transmission.** Whether Hesiodic Chaos reflects direct Mesopotamian *Enuma Elish* influence, indirect Phoenician transmission, or independent Indo-European cosmogonic tradition is debated. The dominant current view (West 1997; López-Ruiz 2010) is substantial Near Eastern influence on Greek cosmogony broadly, with Hesiodic Chaos a likely site of such influence.
