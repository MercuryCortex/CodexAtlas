# Tāwhirimātea

## Identity
Tāwhirimātea is the Māori atua of winds and storms — the most dramatically antagonistic of the sons of Ranginui and Papatūānuku. Unlike his brothers, Tāwhirimātea opposed the separation of the sky father and earth mother, wishing them to remain pressed together in their primordial embrace. When his brothers forced the separation despite his protests, Tāwhirimātea rose up in fury, attacking each brother in turn. He is the divine explanation for storms, the churning sea, toppled trees, and the constant struggle between the elements — the cosmic wrath that punishes those who broke the sky.

## Earliest attestation
Pre-literate Māori oral tradition; recorded systematically by Elsdon Best (1924) and earlier by George Grey (1855, *Polynesian Mythology*). The Tāwhirimātea episode is one of the most elaborated sections of the sons-of-Rangi narrative.

## Mythological role
**Opposition to the separation**: When the sons of Ranginui and Papatūānuku debated pushing their parents apart to let light into the world, Tāwhirimātea alone objected. He did not wish the cosmic intimacy of sky and earth to be broken. Overruled, he nursed his anger.

**The primordial storm**: After Tāne pushed Ranginui upward and light entered the world, Tāwhirimātea unleashed his children — the winds — upon the cosmos. He attacked Tāne first, destroying the great trees of the forest. He turned on Tangaroa (the sea), churning the ocean and causing the sea to rise. He drove Rongo and Haumia into the earth where they hid in Papatūānuku's body. Only Tū (the war-god, ancestor of human warriors) stood his ground and fought back — a confrontation that encodes why humans must struggle against storms, why trees are felled by wind, and why the sea is dangerous.

**Ongoing enmity**: Tāwhirimātea's wrath is not resolved — he continues to attack the world, which is why storms are perpetual. Unlike the other brothers who are accommodated within the new world order, Tāwhirimātea remains in a state of war with his siblings. This gives the Māori cosmogony a structural unresolvedness: the separation of sky and earth was a necessary act but one that generated perpetual cosmic conflict.

**Children of Tāwhirimātea**: Tāwhirimātea's numerous children are the named winds of the Māori tradition — different winds from different directions, violent squalls, destructive hurricanes. Each wind is a son or daughter of the storm-god.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **Tāwhiri** (wider Polynesian wind-deity concepts) — *cognate*: variants appear across Polynesia though without the same mythological prominence.
- **Storm-gods (general)** — *structural-parallel*: Tāwhirimātea's role as a storm-deity who opposes cosmogonic order parallels Typhon (Greek), Vritra (Vedic), and other storm-adversaries in the comparative-religion record — but no transmission is implied. The structural function (storm that resists the ordering of the world) recurs cross-culturally.

## Iconography
No specific iconographic tradition is recorded for Tāwhirimātea in pre-European carving. He is primarily present as the wind itself.

## Disputes
The degree to which Tāwhirimātea represents a primordial force outside the ordered cosmos (a true adversary) vs. a necessary part of the world's balance is debated in Māori studies. Some accounts suggest his storms are a form of justice — punishment for the rupture of the primal unity.
