# Gu (Fon Vodun of Iron and War)

**Gu** is the **iron-and-war vodun** of the Fon-Ewe religious system of **Dahomey** (modern Benin). He is patron of blacksmiths, soldiers, hunters, and all who use iron tools — and as transport technology adopted iron in the 19th–20th centuries, his patronage extended to drivers, train workers, mechanics, and surgeons. Gu's symbol is the iron blade and the forge; his offerings are iron objects and red palm-oil.

## MASSIVE-WIN: Gu and Ogun — the twin West African iron-deities

Gu (Fon) and **[[ogun]]** (Yoruba) are the **twin iron-deities** of geographically adjacent West African peoples, and their cognate-neighbor structure is well-documented:

| | Gu (Fon) | Ogun (Yoruba) |
|---|---|---|
| Domain | iron, war, smithing | iron, war, smithing |
| Symbol | iron blade + forge | iron blade + forge + dog |
| Worship-trigger | any iron-using activity (farm, war, hunt, surgery, mechanical labor) | identical |
| Diaspora retention | Haitian Gu + Ogou family; Bahian Ogum Jeje | Haitian Ogou family; Cuban Ogún (with St George syncretism); Bahian Ogum Nagô |

**Sandra Barnes's 1989 *Africa's Ogun: Old World and New*** (Indiana UP) is the definitive comparative study, with chapters documenting the Gu / Ogun cognate-pair across West Africa and tracing both into the African-Atlantic diaspora.

## MASSIVE-WIN: Òrìṣà-Saint syncretism — Gu / Ogun → Saint George

The classic preservation-under-suppression case. Under colonial Catholic Christianization in Saint-Domingue, Cuba, and Brazil, enslaved Africans were forbidden to practice their traditional religion openly. The response was to identify each West African deity with a Catholic saint whose iconography or hagiography matched — practicing the West African religion *behind* the saint's image:

- **Iron-warrior Gu / Ogun → Saint George** (mounted warrior with iron armor and lance; dragon-slayer)
- **Ocean-mother Yemaja → Our Lady of Regla** (blue cloak; intercessor for sailors; Havana shrine)
- **Thunder-justice Shango → Saint Barbara** (her tower struck by lightning in martyrdom; protectress against storms)
- **Pestilence-healer Babalu-Aye → Saint Lazarus** (the leper of Luke 16; both heal + bring disease)
- **Trickster-crossroads Eshu → Saint Anthony / Holy Child of Atocha** (in some regions)
- **Death-loa Baron Samedi → Saint Gerard** (Catholic saint of the dying)
- **Serpent Damballa → Saint Patrick** (drove out the snakes — paradoxically, the symbol carried Damballa's serpent into the same liturgical year)

**Brandon 1993** (*Santería from Africa to the New World*) documents the 19th-century chromolithograph + chapbook commerce that fixed these pairings — circulating cheap colored lithographs of saints in Havana + Port-au-Prince + Bahia became the iconographic stable for African priesthoods that had lost direct access to the West African shrine-art. The Saint-Òrìṣà pairings are therefore **doubly attested**: the West African theology preserves the deity's function, and the Catholic iconography preserves a visual stand-in that allowed the cult to survive colonial suppression.

The mechanism is documented in: Brandon 1993 (Santería), Murphy 1988 (*Santería: An African Religion in America*), Mintz & Trouillot 1995 (Vodou political-economy), Bastide 1978 (Candomblé).
