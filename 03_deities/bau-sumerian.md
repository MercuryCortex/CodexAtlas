# Bau

## Identity
Bau (also written Baba; the older transliteration Ba'u survives in older scholarship) is the chief goddess of Girsu and consort of [[nin-girsu]] in the Lagashite pantheon. Her domains are healing/medicine, midwifery, and fertility — but in the Lagashite context she is foremost the city-goddess, the queen of Girsu, whose temple Etarsirsir within the larger Eninnu sacred complex is the female counterpart to Ningirsu's. She is the divine recipient of major royal dedications from Ur-Nanshe (~-2500) through Gudea (~-2125); the second of the Gudea Cylinders ([[phase-1-018-gudea-cylinders]] Cylinder B) is largely concerned with her installation in the rebuilt Eninnu. From the Old Babylonian period her healing function is absorbed by the Babylonian goddess [[gula-akkadian]], who is iconographically identified by a dog — Bau's sacred animal.

## Earliest attestation
- Early Dynastic god lists from Fara/Shuruppak.
- The earliest Lagashite royal inscriptions (Ur-Nanshe, En-anatum, Entemena, Urukagina) name Bau as Girsu's principal goddess and dedicate temples and statues to her.
- The Reforms of Urukagina (~-2350) explicitly state that the reforms restore "what Bau and Ningirsu" require.
- The Gudea Cylinders (~-2125) contain the most extensive surviving celebration of her — Cylinder B describes her installation feast in the new temple.

## Mythological role
- Bau does not have a major independent mythological narrative; her role is cultic and civic. She is the addressee of votive prayer, the recipient of statues, and the divine guarantor of Lagashite social order in partnership with Ningirsu.
- She is associated with healing through her sacred animal, the dog. In later Babylonian iconography (transferred to Gula), votive dog figurines are deposited at her sanctuary as appeals for healing.
- The "seven daughters of Bau" are named in some Lagashite inscriptions and represent minor goddesses subordinate to her.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[gula-akkadian]]** — *ancient-identification*: by the Old Babylonian period Bau's healing function is identified with that of Gula (also called Ninkarrak, Ninisina at different cult sites — the Mesopotamian healing-goddess complex is itself layered). Gula's iconography of dog and seated goddess perpetuates the Bau type.
- Distinguish carefully: in older literature the spelling "Bahu" is sometimes encountered, and was occasionally compared with Hebrew *bohu* in Genesis 1:2 (*tohu wa-bohu*) — this is a phonological coincidence with no philological basis and is now universally rejected. Do not propagate the comparison.

## Iconography
Seated goddess in horned crown, often with a dog at her side or below the throne. Votive dog figurines (terracotta and bronze) are characteristic of her cult, and especially of Gula's later cult that perpetuates her medical function.

## Disputes
- **Reading Bau vs. Baba**. The Sumerian sign sequence permits both readings; current scholarship varies. Edzard 1993 prefers Bau; some recent Assyriologists prefer Baba. Either spelling refers to the same goddess.
- **The Bahu/bohu comparison**. Once popular in 19th-century comparative religion; now universally rejected by Assyriology and Hebrew Bible scholarship.
