# Ohyah (Nephilim, son of Shemihazah)

## Identity
**Ohyah** is one of the named Nephilim — the offspring of the Watcher-human unions — in the **Book of Giants** ([[phase-3-033-book-of-giants-qumran-manichaean]]). He is identified as the **son of [[semyaza]]** (the chief Watcher), making him among the most prominent Nephilim by lineage. He is paired with his brother [[hahyah-nephilim]] throughout the Book of Giants narrative — the two brothers receive ominous prophetic dreams about the impending Flood and send the giant [[mahaway-nephilim]] to consult their grandfather [[enoch]] for interpretation.

The Book of Giants thus elaborates the Watchers-tradition (1 Enoch 6–11) by giving the Nephilim *individual identities, dreams, and narrative agency* — where 1 Enoch presents the Nephilim collectively as devouring monsters, the Book of Giants names them and shows them as conscious beings aware of their impending doom.

## Earliest attestation
- **Qumran 4Q530 (Book of Giants fragment)** — Ohyah and Hahyah receive dreams.
- **Qumran 4Q531** — Ohyah-named fragment.
- **Manichaean Book of Giants** — Ohyah preserved across Middle Persian, Sogdian, and other Manichaean witnesses (Reeves 1992).

## Mythological role
Ohyah's narrative role in the Book of Giants:

1. **He receives a prophetic dream** of the impending Flood — a vision of celestial trees being uprooted and consumed by water.
2. **He and Hahyah consult their fellow giants**, who cannot interpret the dream.
3. **They commission Mahaway** to fly to Enoch (the antediluvian sage in heavenly seclusion) for interpretation.
4. **Enoch interprets the dream** — the Flood is coming as judgment on the Watchers' rebellion and their offspring; the Nephilim will be destroyed.
5. **Ohyah and Hahyah eventually fight a final battle** against the divine forces (in some Manichaean recensions, against the archangel Raphael).

The figure illuminates the Watcher-tradition's *internal narrative perspective* — the Nephilim know their doom and react to it. This is theologically interesting: the Watchers-tradition's evil is not purely external; the Nephilim have *interiority* and *foreknowledge*.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- The Mesopotamian flood-hero parallels (Utnapishtim in the Gilgamesh epic; Atrahasis) share the *flood-foreknowledge* narrative structure with the Book of Giants Nephilim — though here the foreknowledge does not lead to salvation; the Nephilim are *destroyed* by the Flood.
- Manichaean cosmology absorbs the Watchers-Nephilim tradition substantially (Reeves 1992).

## Iconography
Not standardized.

## Disputes
- **Ohyah / Ahyah etymology.** Aramaic / Sogdian variants. The name may relate to a divine-name root (*hyh* = "to be") but the exact etymology is uncertain.
