# Romulus

## Identity
Romulus is the legendary founder and first king of Rome (traditional date: 753 BCE), son of [[mars-roman]] and the Vestal Virgin Rhea Silvia, twin brother of Remus. He is one of the most politically significant mythological figures in world history — the story of his founding of Rome, the killing of Remus, and his eventual apotheosis as the god Quirinus was central to Roman self-understanding for over a millennium.

## The founding myth
**Birth and exposure**: Born to the Vestal Virgin Rhea Silvia (who was kept celibate to prevent dynastic rivals to her great-uncle Amulius), Romulus and Remus were exposed on the Tiber but suckled by a she-wolf at the Lupercal cave, then raised by the shepherd Faustulus. The she-wolf (*lupa*) nursing the twins became the defining symbol of Rome.

**Fratricide**: After overthrowing Amulius and restoring their grandfather Numitor, the twins disputed which hill to found the new city on and which had the auspicious augury (Romulus saw 12 vultures; Remus 6). When Remus mockingly leaped over Romulus's new wall, Romulus killed him with the words attributed by some sources: *"So perish whoever else shall leap over my walls."* Rome was founded on the murder of its co-founder.

**Institution of the city**: Romulus organized the Roman state — the Senate of 100 elders, the three original tribes, the asylum on the Capitoline to attract settlers, the Rape of the Sabine Women to provide wives for the new population. The city is built, in Roman mythology, through a sequence of violence: fratricide → kidnapping → war (with the Sabines) → eventual incorporation. This violent founding logic is the mythological substrate of Roman imperial theology.

## The divine-twins pattern — inverted
Romulus and Remus fit the Indo-European **[[divine-twins]]** structural template but with a crucial inversion: rather than alternating immortality (as in [[the-dioskouroi]]), or dual survival (as in [[the-ashvins]]), one twin kills the other. This is the **fratricidal inversion** of the divine-twins motif — the most politically charged version, because it makes the founding act of the city simultaneously a murder.

T.P. Wiseman (1995) argues that multiple competing versions of the myth existed in early Rome, not all involving Romulus killing Remus (some have Remus killed by Faustulus's fellow shepherds, others are ambiguous). The fratricide version won because it served the political theology of singular sovereignty — one city, one king, one law — most efficiently.

## Apotheosis as Quirinus
Romulus disappeared in a storm during a review of his troops; he was declared to have been taken up to heaven by Mars. He was thereafter worshipped as **Quirinus** — one of the three original Capitoline deities (Jupiter, Mars, Quirinus), later partly displaced by Juno. The apotheosis narrative parallels the Dioskouroi's alternation between Olympus and Hades, converting mortality into a form of divinity through death rather than through ongoing alternation.

The deification of a founder-king is a common ancient Near Eastern pattern (Egyptian pharaoh-as-Horus, Mesopotamian divine-king). Romulus's apotheosis is Rome's version — distinguishing the Roman case is its **retrospective** quality (Rome did not immediately treat Romulus as divine but developed the apotheosis narrative over centuries) and its **republican displacement** (the Roman Republic suppressed the cult of Romulus precisely because he was a *king*; Augustus later revived the Romulus mythology for the new monarchy).

## Cross-tradition significance
- **[[divine-twins]]** theme: anchor case for the fratricidal inversion pattern
- **Scapegoat mechanism** (Girard): Remus's death as founding violence — the sacrifice that creates the social order
- **Cain and Abel** structural parallel: the first murder that produces a city (Genesis 4:17 — Cain builds a city after killing Abel). No direct transmission; parallel structural logic in the ancient Mediterranean imagination about how cities begin.
- **Augustus's use of Romulus**: in the Augustan period, both Romulus (military founder) and Julius Caesar (deified dictator) were consciously invoked to legitimate the new monarchy. The founding mythology of fratricide was simultaneously the founding mythology of empire.
