# Corn Mother

## Identity

**Corn Mother** is the pan-North American agricultural goddess — a figure present under different names in Cherokee, Keresan Pueblo, Zuni, Iroquois, Ojibwe, and dozens of other traditions — who is the original source of corn and/or who sacrifices herself so that corn grows from her body. She is the most widely distributed agricultural deity in the Americas, and the structural parallel to Demeter (Greek) is one of the strongest cases for a genuinely universal grain-goddess archetype in comparative mythology. The primary named instantiations are **Selu** (Cherokee: the original corn woman whose sons kill her and from whose blood and body corn springs), **Iyatiku** (Keresan Pueblo: the mother of all the Pueblo people who lives inside the earth and sends corn up through the ground), and **Yellow Woman** (various Pueblo traditions: a figure who enters sacred relation with powerful forces and returns transformed, often with corn).

## Earliest attestation

- Corn domestication in Mesoamerica dates to approximately 9,000 years ago (8,700 BCE); its spread northward into the Southwest (~1 CE) and Eastern Woodlands (~800-1200 CE) was accompanied by the development of corn-ceremony complexes.
- The Corn Mother as a sacred figure is embedded in the ceremonial calendars of these traditions — her presence is attested through Green Corn Ceremony archaeology and pottery iconography reaching back centuries.
- First extensive Western documentation: James Mooney's *Myths of the Cherokee* (Smithsonian BAE, 1900) records the Selu narrative; early Pueblo ethnographies (Matilda Coxe Stevenson, *The Zuñi Indians*, 1904) document Iyatiku and related figures.

## Mythological role

**Selu (Cherokee)**: Selu is the original corn woman who lives with her husband Kanati (the hunter) and two sons. The sons discover that their mother produces corn by rubbing her body — specifically her sides and abdomen — into a basket. Disturbed by what they see as a supernatural and transgressive act, they kill her. Before dying, she gives them instructions: drag her body across a clearing and corn will grow where her blood falls. The clearing becomes the first corn field. Selu's death is not a punishment — it is a transformation: she *becomes* the corn, so that through her death her children and all future generations are fed. The logic is identical to Osiris: death is not the end of the sacred body but its transformation into the food of the living.

**Iyatiku (Keresan Pueblo)**: Iyatiku is the mother of all the Pueblo people; she lives inside the earth and is the literal substance of corn. In Keresan theology, every corn plant is Iyatiku's body; the harvest is receiving her; ritual care for corn is care for the mother. The annual corn ceremony cycle — from planting through Green Corn ceremonies to harvest — is the ritual relationship with Iyatiku. She does not die but is always present underground, continuously generating.

**The Green Corn Ceremony**: The most widespread pan-North American ceremony involving the Corn Mother figure — attested from the Creek to the Iroquois to the Cherokee — is a multi-day first-fruits ceremony in which the new corn must be ritually received before any community member may eat it. The ceremony involves purification, sacred fire renewal, and acknowledgment of the Corn Mother as the source of life. James Mooney and later Mary Haas documented these ceremonies as among the most stable elements of Eastern Woodlands ceremonial life.

## Cross-tradition identifications

- **[[demeter]]** — *structural-parallel* (grain-mother archetype): the most direct structural equivalent in world mythology. Both are mothers whose relationship to the earth produces grain; both encode the logic that the goddess's body / grief / sacrifice IS the grain cycle. Frazer's analysis in *The Golden Bough* identifies the corn-mother and Demeter as instantiations of the same universal pattern (the grain-spirit as dying-and-rising goddess). The parallel has no transmission — it reflects the universal human experience of grain agriculture organized through a maternal divine figure.
- **[[ceres-roman]]** — *structural-parallel*: Roman grain goddess governing the agricultural cycle; equivalent to Demeter in the Roman context.
- **[[nisaba]]** — *structural-parallel* (earliest grain goddess): Nisaba is the Sumerian/Mesopotamian goddess of grain and scribal arts — the earliest textually documented grain goddess, appearing in Sumerian literature from ~2500 BCE. The Corn Mother of the Americas and Nisaba of Mesopotamia represent independent development of the feminine grain-deity across civilizations built on grain agriculture.
- **[[osiris]]** — *structural-parallel* (death-generates-grain): Selu's body generates corn after her death; Osiris's dismembered body scattered across Egypt fertilizes the Nile plain. Both mythologies encode: the death of a sacred body = the grain that sustains the living. The logic of sacrifice-as-food-source appears independently in both traditions.
- **[[pachamama]]** — *structural-parallel*: Andean Earth Mother governing agricultural production; the most geographically proximate parallel in the Americas. Both Pachamama and the Corn Mother represent the Americas' indigenous theology of reciprocal relationship between humans and the feminine earth that sustains them.
- **[[changing-woman]]** — *contextual*: Changing Woman (Diné/Navajo) and the Corn Mother are the two principal feminine figures of agricultural and ceremonial life in the North American Southwest and adjacent traditions; corn pollen (Changing Woman's primary ritual substance) links them.
- **[[spider-woman-navajo]]** — *contextual*: Spider Woman as the creator-teacher of weaving in the Navajo tradition is a parallel type to the Corn Mother as the origin-figure of women's agricultural knowledge and ceremony.
- **[[white-buffalo-calf-woman]]** — *contextual*: White Buffalo Calf Woman (Lakota) brings sacred ceremony to the people as the Corn Mother brings corn — both are female figures who cross the boundary between the sacred and human world to give their communities the practice that defines their way of life.
- **[[wakan-tanka]]** — *contextual*: the sacred power recognized in the Corn Mother's self-sacrifice resonates with the broader Plains/Woodland concept of Wakan Tanka as the sacred force permeating all life.

## Iconography

- Corn cobs and ears as primary attribute; the Corn Mother figure frequently depicted holding or wearing corn
- Green and yellow garments (colors of corn at different stages)
- Ritual corn pollen — used in Cherokee, Navajo, and Pueblo ceremonies as the purest material substance of the Corn Mother's body
- In contemporary Native art, the Corn Mother is one of the most frequently depicted figures — especially in Pueblo and Cherokee contexts

## Disputes

- **One figure or many?** Selu (Cherokee) and Iyatiku (Keresan) have distinct mythological personalities and theological functions — whether they should be treated as the "same" figure or as independent traditions connected only by structural similarity is debated. The entry treats them as distinct instantiations of a pan-American pattern.
- **Frazer's comparative framework**: Frazer's identification of the Corn Mother as a "corn-spirit" equivalent to Demeter and Persephone has been critiqued for over-generalizing, stripping specific cultural meaning from each tradition, and imposing a Frazerian dying-and-rising framework that may not fit all traditions equally well. Contemporary Indigenous scholars emphasize the specific theological claims of each tradition.
