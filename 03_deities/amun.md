# Amun

## Identity
Amun (Egyptian *Jmn*, "the hidden one") is the principal god of the Theban region from the Middle Kingdom (~-2000) onward and the **state-religious high-god of Egypt's New Kingdom** (~-1550 to -1077) and most subsequent periods. The semantic core of his name — **hiddenness** — is theologically load-bearing: Amun is the god whose true nature is invisible and inaccessible even to other gods, the principle of concealed being that manifests through and is identified with the visible solar god [[ra]] (in the composite form [[amun-ra]]) and through the Pharaoh as his earthly incarnation. From the New Kingdom onward, Amun is also a major **oracular** deity, with the cult-images consulted by procession-oracle; the most famous of his oracles is at the Siwa Oasis in the Libyan desert, consulted by Alexander the Great in -332 and pronouncing him son of Zeus-Ammon.

## Earliest attestation
- Mentioned in passing in the [[phase-1-002-pyramid-texts]] (Old Kingdom, mid-3rd millennium BCE) as a relatively minor figure among the gods.
- Rises to prominence in the Middle Kingdom (11th–12th Dynasties, c. -2050 to -1700) with the political ascendancy of Thebes.
- Becomes the dominant national deity in the **New Kingdom** under the 18th Dynasty (Ahmose, Thutmose I–III, Amenhotep III), with massive temple construction at Karnak and Luxor.
- The brief Amarna interruption ([[akhenaten]], ~-1353–1336) attempts to suppress Amun in favor of [[aten]]; after Akhenaten's death the Amun cult is restored with vengeance under Tutankhamun and the 19th Dynasty.
- Spreads to Nubia (cult at Gebel Barkal / Napata becomes the religious center of Kushite kingship; 25th Dynasty Kushite kings rule Egypt c. -747 to -656 as Amun-priests).
- Oracle at Siwa Oasis established by Iron Age; visited by Croesus (per Herodotus 1.46), Cimon, Alexander (332 BCE), and emperor Hadrian.

## Mythological role
Amun is the **hidden creator** in Theban theology, articulated most fully in the Leiden Hymn to Amun (Papyrus Leiden I 350, 19th Dynasty) and the Cairo Hymn to Amun-Re (Papyrus Boulaq 17): Amun is the unseen breath / wind that animates all things, the creator who made himself and from whom all gods derive, the one whose name no creature truly knows. The Leiden Hymn famously identifies Amun, [[ra]], and [[ptah]] as the three aspects of a single divine being: **"All gods are three — Amun, Re, and Ptah, without their seconds. His identity is hidden as Amun, his face is Re, his body is Ptah."** This is the most sophisticated articulation of late-second-millennium Egyptian theology and has been described (Assmann 1995) as **"cosmological monotheism"** or **"differentiated monotheism"** — one god behind the multiplicity of cult-figures, anticipating later Hellenistic and Hermetic theological moves.

As king of the gods (the Egyptian *nsw n nṯrw*, "King of the Gods"), Amun authorizes Pharaonic rule: every legitimate Pharaoh is the son of Amun by physical descent (the "divine birth" reliefs at Luxor and Deir el-Bahari show Amun fathering the king on the queen).

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[ra]]** — *ancient-identification* (Egyptian-internal): the Middle / New Kingdom fusion produces [[amun-ra]], the composite solar-creator. The fusion is theologically explicit, not folk-syncretic.
- **[[zeus]]** — *ancient-identification*: [[herodotus]] *Histories* 2.42, 2.55 — "Zeus is called Ammon by the Egyptians." The **Zeus-Ammon** identification becomes one of the central Greek-Egyptian theological equations, deepened by the visit of Alexander the Great to the Siwa oracle (-332) which proclaims him son of Zeus-Ammon. From this point Zeus-Ammon iconography (Zeus with ram's horns) is widespread in Hellenistic and Roman coinage and statuary.
- **[[ptah]]** — *ancient-identification* (Egyptian-internal): the Leiden Hymn triad — Amun, Re, Ptah as the hidden, visible-solar, and material-creative aspects of one god.
- **Jupiter-Ammon (Roman)** — *folk-syncretism* + *ancient-identification*: the Roman extension of Zeus-Ammon; the cult is well-attested in North Africa and the Western Mediterranean.
- **[[serapis]]** — *parallel-motif*: both are Ptolemaic-Hellenistic engineered Greek-Egyptian high-gods, though Serapis derives from Osiris-Apis and Zeus-Ammon from Amun-Ra.

## Iconography
- Tall **double-plumed crown** — two ostrich feathers rising vertically (Amun of Karnak)
- **Ram-headed** form or full ram form (Amun of Siwa) — the ram with downward-curving horns is the principal Amun-animal
- **Blue or lapis-lazuli skin** in painted reliefs — signifying invisibility and the heavens
- *Ankh* (life) and *was*-scepter (dominion)
- In Zeus-Ammon syncretism: a Zeus-style mature bearded male with **ram's horns** curling out from the temples (Hellenistic-Roman convention; familiar on tetradrachms of Alexander the Great as deified Zeus-Ammon)

## Disputes
- **Origin of Amun.** Hornung 1982 treats Amun as a genuinely ancient local Theban god given national prominence by Middle / New Kingdom Theban dynasties. Some older scholarship treated Amun as a Theban creation imported from a different region (Libyan? Nubian?). Current consensus is local Theban origin with secondary expansion.
- **Theological status of Amun-Ra.** Whether the New Kingdom Amun-Ra theology counts as **monotheism**, **henotheism**, **summodeism**, or just polytheism with a single dominant god is a perennial argument. Assmann 1995 favors "cosmological monotheism"; Hornung 1982 prefers "one and many" / *complementary polytheism*. The Leiden Hymn explicitly says all gods are three aspects of one — but other contemporary texts treat the gods straightforwardly as separate persons.
- **Continuity with Hellenistic monotheism.** Whether the New Kingdom Amun-Ra theology directly influences later philosophical monotheisms (Stoic logos, Middle Platonism, Hermetic Corpus, Christian) is debated; Frankfurter (1998) documents direct cult-continuity for Amun in Roman Egypt; the philosophical line is less easy to trace.
