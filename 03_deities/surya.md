# Sūrya

## Identity
Sūrya (Sanskrit सूर्य) is the Vedic solar deity, the **eye of the cosmos**, the lord of the day, and in classical Hindu and Puranic tradition the supreme deity of the **Saura sect** (one of the historically attested major sectarian traditions of Hinduism, alongside Vaishnavism, Shaivism, Shaktism, and Ganapatya). His most distinctive iconographic attribute is his **chariot drawn by seven horses**, with Aruna (the dawn) as charioteer — a motif canonical from the Rig Veda forward and visually elaborated in the great Surya temples (Konark, 13th c. CE; Modhera, 11th c. CE).

Surya in the Vedic corpus is part of a tight cluster of solar-luminary deities — **Savitr** (the dawn / impeller aspect, addressed in the famous *Gāyatrī Mantra* at RV 3.62.10), **Mitra** (the contract-and-witnessing aspect, more abstract), **Pushan** (the nourishing-pastoral solar aspect), and the **Adityas** (the class of solar sons of Aditi). These overlap and partially merge across the Rig Vedic corpus; Surya proper is the most concretely-the-sun figure of the cluster. In Puranic and sectarian tradition Surya absorbs the Aditya complex and becomes the Saura supreme deity (Macdonell 1897; Doniger 1981).

## Earliest attestation
- **Rig Veda** (~-1500 to -1200 oral; later written) — RV 1.50 and 1.115 are the canonical Surya hymns; RV 3.62.10 is the *Gāyatrī Mantra* to Savitr-aspect-of-Surya; RV 10.10 the *Yama-Yamī* dialogue invokes Vivasvat (Surya). ([[phase-2-001-rig-veda-family-books]])
- **Atharva Veda** (~-1200 to -900) — extensive solar-magical-medical hymns. ([[phase-2-003-atharva-veda]])
- **Brahmanas / Upanishads** (~-800 onward) — solar theology incorporated into Brahman / Atman speculations (e.g., *Chandogya Upanishad* 3.19 on the solar-cosmic-egg).
- **Bhagavad Gita** (~-200 to 200 CE) — Krishna identifies himself with Surya among the Adityas (Gita 10.21: "of the Adityas I am Vishnu"; Gita 11 includes solar imagery). ([[phase-2-027-bhagavad-gita]])
- **Konark Sun Temple** (Odisha, c. 1250 CE, Eastern Ganga king Narasingha Deva I) — the most ambitious surviving Surya temple; the entire structure built as Surya's chariot with twelve pairs of stone wheels and seven horses (Donaldson 2003).
- **Modhera Sun Temple** (Gujarat, c. 1026 CE, Solanki dynasty) — comparable major Saura attestation.
- **Continuing Saura sect** — the Saura tradition continues in attenuated form into the modern period; the Saurapurana and Bhavishyapurana sections on Surya are the principal Puranic Saura textual corpus.

## Mythological role
Surya operates in three registers:

1. **Cosmic eye and witness.** Surya is the eye of the cosmos, the daily witness of all human action, and the source of *ṛta* (cosmic order) in its observable diurnal form. The *Gāyatrī Mantra* (to Savitr-aspect) is among the most-recited Hindu prayers from the Vedic period to the present.
2. **Cosmic chariot and the seven horses.** The seven-horse single-wheeled chariot is the canonical iconographic and cosmological vehicle — the seven horses variously interpreted as the seven days of the week, the seven meters of Vedic recitation, the seven colors of the spectrum, or the seven rays (in later astronomy-influenced interpretations).
3. **Ancestor and lawgiver.** Surya as Vivasvat is father of Yama (god of death) and of Manu (the first human / lawgiver); the *Manusmriti* claims this Vivasvat-Manu descent for its legal authority. The royal *Sūryavaṃśa* (solar dynasty) — to which Rama belongs in the *Ramayana* — claims descent from Surya through Ikshvaku.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[helios]]** — *scholarly-parallel* (Indo-European cognate): Sanskrit *sūrya*, Greek *hēlios*, Latin *sol*, Old English *sunne* all derive from PIE *seh₂uel-* (Macdonell 1897; Watkins 1995). The cognacy is etymological-linguistic; specific iconographic transmissions are not at issue at this level.
- **[[utu-shamash]]** — *parallel-form*: Mesopotamian solar deity. No documented Bronze Age Mesopotamia → India transmission; the parallel is at the level of the general cross-cultural solar-deity pattern (see [[sun-disk]] symbol node).
- **[[ra]]** — *parallel-form*: Egyptian solar deity. No documented Bronze Age Egypt → India transmission.
- **Iranian / Sasanian Mithra-Hvar-aspect** — *folk-syncretism*: documented Hellenistic-Sasanian-Indian iconographic exchange via the **Maga Brahmins** (a Brahmin community of Iranian / Magi origin in northwestern India), responsible for the distinctively non-Indic features of some Surya iconography (boots, Iranian dress). Pingree 1976 documents.

## Iconography
- **Seven-horse chariot with single wheel**, Aruna as charioteer — canonical iconographic load-bearing attribute.
- Solar disk / blazing halo behind the head.
- Lotus(es) in hand — two arms in older iconography, four arms in Puranic iconography.
- **Boots** — a distinctively non-Indic iconographic feature in some Surya images, reflecting the **Iranian / Magi-Brahmin (Maga) iconographic influence** documented by Pingree 1976.
- The Konark and Modhera Sun Temples (13th c., 11th c.) are the architecturally elaborated iconographic statements.

See [[sun-disk]] symbol node; Surya is canonical instance (with `parallel-form` to other solar-disk traditions).

## Disputes
- **Distinction Surya / Savitr / Aditya / Vivasvat.** The Rig Vedic solar cluster overlaps significantly; how cleanly to distinguish Surya from Savitr from the Adityas from Vivasvat is partly contested. Mainstream Vedic-philology (Macdonell 1897 onward) treats them as related-but-distinct figures within a single solar cluster, with later sectarian Saura tradition absorbing the cluster into Surya proper.
- **Iranian iconographic influence on Surya.** The Maga-Brahmin contribution (Iranian dress, boots, the Bhavishyapurana's distinctive Magha-priest passages) is well-documented (Pingree 1976) but the question of how deep and how early the Iranian influence on Surya iconography goes — pre-Hellenistic vs. Hellenistic/Sasanian — remains a live scholarly question.
- **The seven horses' interpretation.** The seven horses' symbolic content (days of the week, Vedic meters, spectrum-colors) is debated; the canonical Puranic readings differ.
