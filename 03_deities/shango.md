# Ṣàngó

## Identity
Orisha of thunder, lightning, drumming-and-dance, kingship, fire, war, and royal justice. Ṣàngó is by Yoruba tradition the fourth king (*Alaafin*) of Oyo, deified after his death; the historical-mythic line between Ṣàngó-as-king and Ṣàngó-as-cosmic-storm-power is permeable and theologically meaningful — Oyo political power and Ṣàngó's cosmic power were continuous. Ṣàngó is one of the most popular orishas in diasporic Yoruba religion (especially the Cuban Changó cult, which had powerful 20th-c. visibility through Celia Cruz's song "Babalú Ayé" and broader Cuban music).

## Earliest attestation
Oyo Empire (~14th–19th c.) records Ṣàngó's centrality to Oyo royal religion; the Alaafin's enthronement involved Ṣàngó-priesthood validation. Ifá corpus references throughout.

## Mythological role
The historical-narrative tradition makes Ṣàngó the fourth Alaafin of Oyo, a powerful sorcerer-king whose lightning-magic eventually killed many of his own followers; in grief, he hanged himself ("Ọba kò so" — "the king did not hang," in liturgical denial). After death, Ṣàngó's lightning continued to strike, and his cult was instituted. He is a god of cosmic *and* historical justice: he strikes the perjurer, the thief, the violator.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **Saint Barbara** — *folk-syncretism* (Cuban Lucumí): St. Barbara's iconography (tower / lightning protection legend) pairs with Ṣàngó. The cross-gender pairing (male orisha ↔ female saint) is theologically unproblematic in Lucumí where the *camino* (path) and *iconographic operational equivalent* matter more than gender match.
- **Baal-Hadad / Indra / Thor** — *scholarly-parallel*: Indo-European and Near-Eastern storm/thunder gods invite typological comparison; no historical-genealogical claim is intended.

## Iconography
Double-bladed axe (*oṣẹ Ṣàngó*) — the most distinctive attribute; red-and-white colors; thunder-stones (Neolithic stone celts found in the ground are identified as Ṣàngó's thunderbolts and become cult-objects); horse imagery in art; six as his sacred number.
