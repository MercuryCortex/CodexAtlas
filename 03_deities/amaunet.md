# Amaunet

## Identity

Amaunet ("the hidden one" — feminine form of Amun) is one of the eight primordial deities of the Hermopolitan Ogdoad (*Khmunu* = "city of the eight"), the theological system developed at Hermopolis Magna in Middle Egypt that describes the state of the world before creation. In this system, existence before the first sunrise was governed by four pairs of male-female cosmic principles, each personifying a fundamental quality of the primordial abyss: Nun and Naunet (the primordial waters), Heh and Hauhet (infinity and formlessness), Kek and Kauket (darkness — see [[kek-egyptian]]), and Amun and Amaunet (hiddenness / invisible wind). Amaunet is the female half of this last pair, personifying the invisible, ungraspable quality of pre-creation — that which exists but cannot be seen or located. Her name shares its root with [[amun]] — both derive from *imn* ("to be hidden, to be invisible") — making them a conceptual pair as much as a mythological couple.

## Earliest attestation

- **[[phase-1-002-pyramid-texts]]** (~2400 BCE): Amaunet appears by name in the Pyramid Texts alongside Amun, among the earliest theological documentation of the Ogdoad system.
- **[[phase-1-009-coffin-texts]]** (Middle Kingdom): the Ogdoad is developed more fully; Amaunet's role as primordial hidden goddess is elaborated.
- **Karnak relief** (Thebes, New Kingdom): Amaunet is depicted at Karnak receiving offerings from pharaohs, retaining her status as an ancient goddess even after Mut took the primary consort role.

## Mythological role

In Hermopolitan theology, the Ogdoad did not create the world by personal action but by their collective presence: their eight primordial forces constituted the conditions that made creation possible. The first act of creation — the primordial sunrise, the emergence of the Benben mound from the waters, the first cry of the primordial goose or the first sound of Thoth's voice — was the moment when these pre-conditions gave way to differentiated existence. The Ogdoad are thus the substrate of creation, not its agent.

Amaunet's specific attribute — hiddenness — was the quality most central to her partner [[amun]]'s theological evolution. As Amun became the supreme deity of the New Kingdom (Amun-Ra, "King of the Gods"), his identity retained the Amaunet-quality of invisibility: Amun is supremely powerful because he cannot be seen, cannot be fully grasped, cannot be localized. This is the paradox of Egyptian solar theology — the hidden god is the most powerful god. Amaunet contributed the feminine articulation of this principle to the tradition.

She was also associated with the north wind — the breath of the divine before any material wind — and in this capacity appears in some mythological contexts as a sustaining, life-giving force (the north wind, blowing from the Mediterranean, was the cooling, beneficial wind in Egypt).

## Cross-tradition identifications

- **[[amun]]** — *ancient-identification*: Amaunet and Amun are the direct masculine/feminine pair of the same principle; the Pyramid Texts treat them as a coupled force. Amaunet is the feminine mode of the hidden god — his shadow-form, his primordial spouse before Mut replaced her in official cult.
- **[[neith]]** — *folk-syncretism*: Neith, the self-created primordial goddess of Sais (also associated with weaving, war, and the north), shares Amaunet's profile of primordial female power and northern wind association. Later theology partially merged them. Neith's famous epithet — "that which is and which shall be; no one has ever removed my veil" — resonates with Amaunet's hiddenness.
- **[[thoth]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Thoth, the deity of Hermopolis Magna (where the Ogdoad theology originated), presided over the cosmological system in which Amaunet operated. Thoth's role as the one who spoke creation into existence from the Hermopolitan primordial hillock makes him the agent who emerged *from* the Ogdoad — Amaunet's theology is the precondition of Thoth's cosmological action.
- **[[atum]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: at Heliopolis, Atum performs self-creation from the primordial waters (Nun). The Hermopolitan Ogdoad and the Heliopolitan Ennead are competing cosmological accounts of the same primordial moment; Amaunet's "hidden principle" is the Hermopolitan counterpart to Atum's self-directed creative act.

## Iconography

Amaunet is depicted in two primary forms: (1) as a woman wearing the red crown of Lower Egypt, standing or seated, with a scepter — this is her most common anthropomorphic form; (2) as a serpent-headed woman, reflecting the Ogdoad convention where the female members of the four pairs were represented with serpent heads while the males had frog heads. Her association with the invisible wind is not typically rendered iconographically (wind being by definition invisible), but she appears in protective goddess postures associated with breath and life-force.

## Disputes

- The relationship between Amaunet and [[mut]] in Theban theology is complex: Mut eventually became the "official" consort of Amun-Ra in the imperial Theban cult (New Kingdom), but Amaunet was retained as an "archaic" figure — the original, ancient consort who preceded Mut. Whether Amaunet and Mut are separate goddesses with distinct cults or overlapping theological figures is debated.
- The identification of Amaunet with "Imentet" (the West, the land of the dead) is found in some sources but is likely a separate theological figure who happens to share a phonetically similar name.
