# Yamāntaka

## Identity
"Ender of Yama" — the wrathful Vajrayana wisdom-deity who destroys death itself. **The most iconographically violent figure in Tibetan Buddhist art**: nine heads (the central one a snarling buffalo-skull, eight others in tiered terror), thirty-four arms wielding tantric weapons, sixteen legs trampling male and female corpses. He is the **wrathful emanation of [[manjushri]]**, the bodhisattva of wisdom: wisdom in its fierce face, terrifying not because it wishes harm but because the ignorance it destroys produces self-defense in the practitioner.

## Mythological role
**Vajrabhairava narrative** (root tantra): a Hindu ascetic seeks to become Yama, the god of death. In doing so he becomes a monstrous tyrant, killing villagers and threatening the cosmos. Mañjuśrī, seeing this, takes on Yamāntaka form — out-monstering the monster — and subdues him. **The Geluk school**, founded by Tsongkhapa (1357–1419), takes Yamāntaka as its principal *yidam* (meditation deity); the Dalai Lamas have maintained an esoteric Yamāntaka practice lineage as core of their meditative training.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[manjushri]]** — peaceful form; wisdom-essence.
- **[[yama-vedic]]** — *polemic-inversion*: defeated antagonist; the Hindu death-god repurposed as the deity-to-be-conquered.
- **[[mahakala]] / [[bhairava]]** — *parallel-motif*: wrathful protector / wrathful Śaiva tantric deity (Bhairava is the Shaiva cognate, and Vajrabhairava is etymologically a Buddhist appropriation of Hindu Bhairava).
