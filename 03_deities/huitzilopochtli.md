# Huitzilopochtli

## Identity
The tutelary deity of the Mexica (Aztec) people specifically — not pan-Mesoamerican, not even pan-Nahua, but identifiably the patron god who led the Mexica from their mythic homeland Aztlán to the Valley of Mexico and who instructed them to found Tenochtitlan where an eagle perches on a cactus eating a serpent (the foundational vision of 1325). Huitzilopochtli is god of war and of the Fifth Sun — the present cosmic age — and the principal divine recipient of human sacrifice at Tenochtitlan's Templo Mayor, where his shrine sat alongside Tlaloc's. The two-shrine top of Templo Mayor (Huitzilopochtli on the south, Tlaloc on the north) is the central architectural-religious fact of late Mexica state religion.

## Earliest attestation
Mexica migration narratives place Huitzilopochtli's emergence as tribal patron during the Aztlán-to-Tenochtitlan migration (~12th–13th c.). Archaeological-iconographic attestation at Templo Mayor (excavated 1978 onward by Matos Moctezuma); the *coyolxauhqui* stone (1978 discovery) at the foot of the Templo Mayor depicts Huitzilopochtli's mythic birth and dismemberment of his half-sister Coyolxauhqui.

## Mythological role
The Coyolxauhqui-Coatlicue myth (Sahagún Book III): Coatlicue (Serpent-Skirt), already mother of Coyolxauhqui and the four-hundred Centzon Huitznahua, becomes mysteriously pregnant from a ball of feathers; her children (suspecting infidelity) plot to kill her; Huitzilopochtli is born fully armed from her womb, slays Coyolxauhqui, dismembers her, and casts her down the hill — she becomes the moon. The 400 Centzon Huitznahua become the stars. The myth ritually replays Templo Mayor's architecture: sacrificial victims roll down the temple steps reenacting Coyolxauhqui's dismemberment.

## Cross-tradition identifications
None significant. Huitzilopochtli is Mexica-specific; Mesoamerican religion otherwise lacks an equivalent figure.

## Iconography
Hummingbird headdress (his name means "Hummingbird of the South" or "Hummingbird on the Left" — left being the south in Mesoamerican direction-naming); fire-serpent weapon (Xiuhcoatl); black-painted face with yellow horizontal stripes; left foot or leg decorated; shield with five feather-tufts.

## MASSIVE WIN — Aztec Sacrifice as Cosmic Engineering

**The structural argument**: Aztec sacrifice is not gift-economy theology, not appeasement theology, and not scapegoat theology. It is **engineering** — the technical maintenance of a cosmic machine that requires human fuel to keep running. This is structurally distinct from every other major sacrificial theology in world religion.

### The Five-Suns Cosmology as Frame

In Mexica cosmology (Florentine Codex, Book III; León-Portilla 1963), there have been four previous suns (cosmic ages), each destroyed:
- First Sun (Nahui-Ocelotl): destroyed by jaguars
- Second Sun (Nahui-Ehecatl): destroyed by wind
- Third Sun (Nahui-Quiahuitl): destroyed by fire-rain (rain of lava)
- Fourth Sun (Nahui-Atl): destroyed by flood

The present **Fifth Sun** (Nahui-Ollin — "4-Movement") was created at **Teotihuacan** in the mythic past: the gods gathered in darkness and sacrificed themselves — leaping into a bonfire — so that the sun could be born. But the sun would not move. The universe was motionless. The gods discovered that only blood — *chalchiuatl* (the "precious liquid") — could make the sun move across the sky. They sacrificed themselves fully; their blood set the sun in motion. The human sacrificial obligation flows from this: *humans must continue what the gods began*. The sun is not self-sustaining; it requires the daily and periodic offering of human blood to keep moving.

### The "Cosmic Engineering" Distinction

| Theology | Sacrifice Functions As | Mechanism |
|----------|----------------------|-----------|
| Vedic *yajña* | Re-enactment of cosmogonic dismembering; sustaining cosmic order (*ṛta*) | Gift-and-repetition |
| Mesopotamian *akītu* | Ritual re-actualization of Marduk's cosmic victory; renewal | Liturgical performance |
| Hebrew/Temple | Gift (*korban*) to YHWH; atonement; covenant reaffirmation | Gift and covenant |
| Christian Eucharist | Memorial/re-actualization of the once-for-all sacrifice | Anamnesis |
| Greek sacrifice | Gift to the gods; communication with the divine | Gift-economy |
| **Aztec sacrifice** | **Literal fuel for the cosmic machine; the sun physically requires blood to move** | **Engineering — maintenance of a physical process** |

**Carrasco (1999)** is the most rigorous scholarly treatment of this distinction. The Mexica understanding was not metaphorical: the sun's motion was physically powered by *chalchiuatl*. Sacrifice was not optional piety but structural necessity. A day without sacrifice was a day the sun might fail to rise. The 52-year *xiuhmolpilli* ("New Fire ceremony") — where all fires in the empire were extinguished and a new fire drilled on the chest of a sacrificial victim on the hill of the star (*Huixachtlan*) when the Pleiades crossed the meridian at midnight — was the most explicit expression of this theology: the entire cosmos was at risk of extinction; only the successful drilling of the new fire and the lighting of the fires by blood-offering renewed the universe for another 52-year cycle.

### Cross-Tradition Significance

No other major religious tradition treats sacrifice as cosmic physical maintenance in this direct engineering sense. This makes the Aztec sacrificial system one of the most philosophically distinctive in world religion — not more or less "advanced" than alternatives, but genuinely different in its cosmological logic. The theological question the Mexica system answers is: *what keeps the physical processes of the universe running?* — and the answer is: *human blood, offered in precise ritual timing*. This is a version of the general theological question "what maintains cosmic order?" that also produces: Vedic *ṛta*, Zoroastrian *asha*, Egyptian *ma'at*, Confucian *lǐ*, Greek *logos* — but the Aztec answer is uniquely mechanistic.

**Investigative finding**: The Aztec case is important for the vault because it prevents any flattening of "sacrifice" across traditions. When [[sacrifice-as-cosmic-renewal]] notes that Aztec and Christian and Vedic sacrifice share a "cosmic maintenance" structure, the Aztec version is actually the most literal and least metaphorical expression of that logic — which makes the structural parallel more striking, not less. The Aztec sacrificial system is the purest expression of the idea that *the universe requires ongoing human action to continue existing*; this is the most radical form of human-cosmic interdependence in world religion.
