# Huitzilopochtli

## Identity
The tutelary deity of the Mexica (Aztec) people specifically — not pan-Mesoamerican, not even pan-Nahua, but identifiably the patron god who led the Mexica from their mythic homeland Aztlán to the Valley of Mexico and who instructed them to found Tenochtitlan where an eagle perches on a cactus eating a serpent (the foundational vision of 1325). Huitzilopochtli is god of war and of the Fifth Sun — the present cosmic age — and the principal divine recipient of human sacrifice at Tenochtitlan's Templo Mayor, where his shrine sat alongside Tlaloc's. The two-shrine top of Templo Mayor (Huitzilopochtli on the south, Tlaloc on the north) is the central architectural-religious fact of late Mexica state religion.

## Earliest attestation
Mexica migration narratives place Huitzilopochtli's emergence as tribal patron during the Aztlán-to-Tenochtitlan migration (~12th–13th c.). Archaeological-iconographic attestation at Templo Mayor (excavated 1978 onward by Matos Moctezuma); the *coyolxauhqui* stone (1978 discovery) at the foot of the Templo Mayor depicts Huitzilopochtli's mythic birth and dismemberment of his half-sister Coyolxauhqui.

## Mythological role
The Coyolxauhqui-Coatlicue myth (Sahagún Book III): Coatlicue (Serpent-Skirt), already mother of Coyolxauhqui and the four-hundred Centzon Huitznahua, becomes mysteriously pregnant from a ball of feathers; her children (suspecting infidelity) plot to kill her; Huitzilopochtli is born fully armed from her womb, slays Coyolxauhqui, dismembers her, and casts her down the hill — she becomes the moon. The 400 Centzon Huitznahua become the stars. The myth ritually replays Templo Mayor's architecture: sacrificial victims roll down the temple steps reenacting Coyolxauhqui's dismemberment.

## Cross-tradition identifications
None significant. Huitzilopochtli is Mexica-specific; Mesoamerican religion otherwise lacks an equivalent figure.

## Iconography
Hummingbird headdress (his name means "Hummingbird of the South" or "Hummingbird on the Left" — left being the south in Mesoamerican direction-naming); fire-serpent weapon (Xiuhcoatl); black-painted face with yellow horizontal stripes; left foot or leg decorated; shield with five feather-tufts.
