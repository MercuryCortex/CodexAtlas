# Nefertem

## Identity
Nefertem (Egyptian *Nfr-tm*, "the Beautiful One Who Is Complete" or "Perfect/Beautiful Completion") is the Egyptian lotus-god of the primordial dawn — the deity of the **blue water-lily** (*seshen*, *Nymphaea caerulea*) that opens at sunrise and from which, in the canonical Memphite cosmogony, the young Sun-god (Atum / Re) emerges at the first dawn. Nefertem is the son of the **Memphite triad** — [[ptah]] (father) and [[sekhmet]] (mother; sometimes Bastet substitutes) — and completes that triad as the divine youth. He is also the god of **perfume and fragrance**, the lotus-scent being his canonical attribute; the *Pyramid Texts* call him "the lotus at the nose of Re." His iconography is consistent across Egyptian history: a youth with the lotus-blossom on his head, often crowned with two tall plumes rising from the lotus, sometimes accompanied by sistra or menat-necklaces. (Pinch 2002; Hornung 1982.)

## Earliest attestation
- **Pyramid Texts** (Old Kingdom, c. -2400) — Spell 249 and related passages invoke the lotus-rising-from-the-primal-waters cosmogony; Nefertem is named as the lotus at Re's nose. ([[phase-1-002-pyramid-texts]])
- **Coffin Texts** (First Intermediate Period through Middle Kingdom, c. -2100 to -1700) — extend Nefertem's lotus-cosmogony role. ([[phase-1-009-coffin-texts]])
- **Memphite Theology / Shabaka Stone** (textual layer Old Kingdom, surviving inscription c. -700 redaction) — Nefertem within the Memphite triad with Ptah and Sekhmet. ([[phase-1-027-memphite-theology-shabaka-stone]])
- **Book of the Dead Spell 174** (New Kingdom onward) — the spell by which the deceased identifies with Nefertem as the lotus-of-Re for daily solar rebirth. ([[phase-1-010-book-of-the-dead]])
- **Tutankhamun's tomb** (c. -1325): the famous painted-wood head of the young king emerging from a lotus is widely read as a Nefertem-identification — Tutankhamun as the young god born from the cosmogonic lotus.

## Mythological role
Nefertem's mythological function is **cosmogonic-iconographic** rather than narrative — there is no major Nefertem narrative myth on the scale of Osiris or Isis. His role is to BE the lotus from which the sun emerges, the perfume of the dawn, the divine youth completing the Memphite triad. He concentrates the iconographic-theological pattern of the cosmogonic-lotus into a single named deity:

- The primal waters (Nun) recede; the primordial mound rises; the lotus blooms on the mound; from the lotus emerges the young Sun. Nefertem IS the lotus at this cosmogonic moment.
- In Memphite theology Ptah is the creator-by-word; Sekhmet is the destructive solar-feminine power; Nefertem is their son and represents the completed, beautiful, fragrant outcome of creation.
- In funerary contexts the deceased identifies with Nefertem (BD 174) to share in the daily solar rebirth.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[atum]]** — *ancient-identification*: in the Memphite cosmogony Nefertem's lotus is the seat from which Atum-as-young-sun emerges at the first dawn; some readings make Nefertem and Atum near-identical in the cosmogonic moment (Pinch 2002).
- **[[ra]]** — *ancient-identification*: Re-as-dawning-sun is identified with the figure emerging from Nefertem's lotus (Hornung 1982).

## Iconography
- **The blue water-lily / lotus on the head** — Nefertem's canonical attribute. The *Nymphaea caerulea* opens at dawn, closes at dusk — making it the natural iconographic vehicle for solar-rebirth theology. See [[lotus]] for the broader iconographic context.
- Two tall plumes rising from the lotus crown.
- Menat-necklaces, sistra (Late Period associations with Sekhmet-Bastet maternal cult).
- Sometimes depicted as a youth seated on a lotus.
- Tutankhamun-as-lotus-head (c. -1325) is the most famous royal Nefertem-identification.

## Disputes
- **Mother identity (Sekhmet vs. Bastet vs. Wadjet).** In Memphite theology Nefertem's mother is canonically Sekhmet, but in regional traditions Bastet (Bubastite) or Wadjet (Buto) substitutes (Pinch 2002).
- **Relation to Atum.** Whether Nefertem is a distinct deity who carries Atum or whether Nefertem and the young Atum are two aspects of the same cosmogonic figure is partly contested; mainstream Egyptology treats them as paired-but-distinct (Hornung 1982).
