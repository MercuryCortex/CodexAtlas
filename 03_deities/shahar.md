# Shahar

## Identity

Shahar (Ugaritic **Šaḥar**, "Dawn") is the Canaanite god of the dawn — the personified breaking of morning light, and by association the morning star (Venus seen at dawn). He is a son of the high god [[el-canaanite]] and the twin of [[shalim]], the god of dusk; together the pair personify the two thresholds of the day. He is best known today not from his own cult but from the long shadow he casts over the biblical and Western tradition through a single, momentous verse.

## Mythological role

Shahar appears in the Ugaritic poem usually called *The Birth of the Gracious (or Beautiful) Gods* (KTU 1.23), in which El fathers the twin astral deities Shahar and Shalim — Dawn and Dusk. The myth of the **morning star who rises toward the summit of heaven** is part of the wider Canaanite astral mythology in which the bright planet Venus ascends each dawn toward the zenith but is always overtaken and quenched by the rising sun — a natural image for a brilliant ascent that ends in a fall.

## Cross-tradition significance (the "Lucifer" wire)

Shahar is the named source of one of the most consequential figures in the Western imagination. **Isaiah 14:12** addresses the doomed king of Babylon as *"Helel ben Shahar"* — "**Shining One** (Day Star), **son of Dawn**" — and describes how he said in his heart, "I will ascend to heaven, above the stars of God I will set my throne," only to be "cast down to Sheol." This is the Canaanite morning-star myth — the dawn-light that climbs toward heaven and falls — redeployed as a taunt against royal pride. When Jerome's Latin Vulgate translated *hêlēl* as **"Lucifer"** ("light-bringer"), the Canaanite Shahar's son/aspect became, in later Christian reading, the proud angel cast out of heaven: the [[lucifer]] of tradition. The closely related god [[athtar]] — the masculine Venus who tries and fails to take Baal's throne — supplies the other half of the same "morning star who overreaches" complex.
