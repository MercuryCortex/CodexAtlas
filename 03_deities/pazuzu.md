# Pazuzu

## Identity

Pazuzu is the Mesopotamian demon-god of the southwest wind — a terrifying composite figure who brings pestilence, locusts, and drought on the winds blowing from the deserts of the southwest. He is the son of Hanbi (king of all evil spirits) and is identified in ancient sources as "the king of the evil wind demons." Yet Pazuzu occupies a paradoxical theological position: despite being a demon who brings disease and destruction, he is among the most widely invoked apotropaic (protective) figures in all of Mesopotamian religion. His image — placed in homes, worn as amulets, installed in doorways — was specifically used to repel Lamashtu, the most feared demon in the Mesopotamian world.

This paradox is the theological key to Pazuzu: he is a demon who repels demons. His grotesque, terrifying appearance — lion head, eagle talons, scorpion tail, four wings — is precisely what gives him power. The divine terrifying drives away the merely terrifying. In ancient Mesopotamia, Pazuzu was the divine face placed in doorways to say to other evil: *there is something worse than you already here.* His right hand raised in what art historians read as a "blessing" or warning gesture makes him simultaneously threatening and protective.

## Earliest attestation

- The majority of Pazuzu material dates to the Neo-Assyrian and Neo-Babylonian periods (ca. 1000–600 BCE), when his cult was most active.
- Bronze Pazuzu head figurines were mounted on sticks and placed in bedrooms and at doorways — among the most recognizable pieces of Mesopotamian material culture. The British Museum holds multiple examples.
- **Lamashtu incantation tablets**: the primary textual context for Pazuzu is the series of incantations against Lamashtu (the demon who attacked pregnant women, caused miscarriage, and killed infants). Pazuzu's name and image appear on amulets designed to be worn during pregnancy and placed near newborns — invoking his power against Lamashtu specifically.
- Pazuzu figurines have been found across the ancient Near East: Iraq, Syria, the Levant, Iran, and Egypt — indicating his reputation traveled widely.

## Mythological role

Pazuzu's primary mythological function is apotropaic — he is invoked not as an object of worship but as a weapon against worse evil. The logic of his invocation:

1. Lamashtu (daughter of [[enlil]] / Anu) is the demon who kills children and attacks pregnant women — the most feared demonic threat to human reproduction.
2. Pazuzu, as king of the evil wind demons, is more powerful than Lamashtu and can drive her away.
3. Therefore, invoking Pazuzu's presence repels Lamashtu.

This is the ancient logic of counter-demonology: you invoke the demon whose domain includes the demon attacking you. The world of evil spirits is hierarchical; invoking a higher demon drives away lower ones.

Pazuzu is thus a figure who belongs to the liminal space between the demonic and the divine — too terrifying to be truly benevolent, too useful as a guardian to be simply evil. This ambivalence is one of the most interesting theological structures in Mesopotamian religion: the apotropaic demon is simultaneously a manifestation of the hostile cosmic forces and the primary defense against them.

## Cross-tradition identifications

- **[[bes]]** (Egyptian) — *scholarly-parallel*: Bes is the Egyptian dwarf god with leonine features, grotesque face, and protective function over childbirth, sleep, and household thresholds. The structural parallel with Pazuzu is precise: both are frightening divine figures whose grotesque appearance is deployed prophylactically to repel evil. Both are particularly associated with the protection of pregnant women and newborns. Both appear as amulets worn on the body. This is the same theological logic — the apotropaic monster — in two traditions.
- **[[garuda]]** (Hindu) — *scholarly-parallel*: Garuda, the divine eagle-vehicle of [[vishnu]], is a massive, terrifying bird-being who destroys serpents and evil forces; his image is used apotropaically. The structural parallel: a composite winged creature with power over malevolent forces, invoked as protection.
- **[[asmodeus]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: The Hebrew/Jewish demon Asmodeus (from Zoroastrian *Aeshma daeva*) is a powerful demon who can be controlled and deployed against other demons by those with the right knowledge — structurally parallel to Pazuzu's paradoxical role as demon-against-demons.
- **[[beelzebub]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: The "lord of the flies" (a likely corruption of Baal Zebul, "lord of the high place") — an ambivalent Semitic divine figure who was demonized in Hebrew tradition. Like Pazuzu, Beelzebub straddles the divine/demonic divide.
- **[[ereshkigal]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Queen of the dead, ruler of the underworld; Pazuzu operates in the disease-carrying winds that come from the direction of the dead — both figures govern the dangerous boundary between the living world and the domain of death.

### MASSIVE WIN — The Apotropaic Monster Pattern

Pazuzu represents a cross-cultural theological pattern of the highest importance: the *apotropaic monster*, a divine figure whose power to frighten evil away is derived from their own terrifying, monstrous, or demonic nature.

- **Egypt**: [[bes]] (leonine dwarf god protecting childbirth and sleep)
- **Mesopotamia**: Pazuzu (disease demon protecting against worse demons)
- **Hindu**: [[garuda]] (divine eagle protecting against serpents and evil)
- **Greek**: Medusa (her severed head — the *gorgoneion* — placed on shields, buildings, and amulets to repel evil)
- **Chinese**: Door Gods (*Menshen* — ferocious warrior deities whose frightening faces protect household entrances)

The underlying logic is consistent: terror can only be repelled by something more terrifying. The divine protective function works not through beauty or benevolence but through the presence of a controlled, bounded terror that declares the space already claimed by something worse.

## Iconography

Pazuzu's iconography is one of the most distinctive and fully realized composite monster forms in the ancient world: human body with lion's head (or dog face in variant depictions), four wings spread wide (two up, two down), eagle's talons for feet, a scorpion's tail, an erect penis. He often stands on a bull or coiled serpent. His right hand is raised in a gesture that combines warning and blessing — a stance that says *I am here* as both a threat to evil and a protection for the household. Bronze Pazuzu heads, mounted on sticks (staffs?), were the primary cultic objects — the head alone, detached and placed on surfaces, invoked his protective gaze.

The 1973 film *The Exorcist* used an image of an authentic Pazuzu figurine as the inspiration for its demon — the archaeologically accurate image of a Neo-Assyrian bronze head — bringing the oldest apotropaic demon in the world into Western pop culture consciousness.

## Disputes

- The exact nature of Pazuzu's "worship" (if it can be called that) is debated: was he propitiated, invoked, or simply deployed? The amulet evidence suggests deployment — you use his image as a tool — rather than the cultic devotion given to gods like [[marduk]] or [[enlil]].
- Pazuzu's relation to the canonical Babylonian demon hierarchies (Anzu, Asag, Asakku) is not fully systematized in the textual record.
