# Vajrayoginī

## Identity
Vajrayoginī ("Diamond Yogini" or "Adamantine Female Practitioner") is the primary female yidam (meditational deity) of Tibetan Vajrayāna Buddhism and the most widely practiced wrathful female deity in the Kagyu, Gelug, and Sakya schools. She is a semi-wrathful red dakini: naked, standing in archer's pose or dancing, holding a skull-cup (kapāla) filled with blood in her left hand and a hooked knife (kartrikā) in her right, adorned with a crown of five dried skulls, a garland of fifty fresh heads, and a khatvanga staff (representing her consort Cakrasaṃvara). She stands on or tramples a human corpse representing the death of ego-clinging and ignorance.

Doctrinally, Vajrayoginī embodies the union of bliss (*ānanda*) and emptiness (*śūnyatā*) — the Tibetan Buddhist ideal of *mahāmudrā* (great seal) realization. Her blood in the skull-cup is not literal blood but represents the transmutation of the *five poisons* (ignorance, attachment, aversion, pride, jealousy) into the *five wisdoms*. Her nakedness represents freedom from conceptual overlay; her fierceness represents the sword-precision that cuts through delusion without hesitation or gentleness. The primary cycle associated with her practice is the Chakrasamvara Tantra, where she appears as consort-and-complement to [[chakrasamvara]].

Miranda Shaw's *Passionate Enlightenment* (1994) documents Vajrayoginī's role as a fully realized, self-sufficient wisdom deity — not reducible to consort-role — who transmits direct instruction to practitioners in visualization and inner-heat (tummo) yoga.

## Earliest attestation
The Vajrayoginī/Cakrasaṃvara cycle emerges in late Indian tantric Buddhism (~8th–10th c. CE), with the root tantra (*Śrī Cakrasaṃvara Tantra*, also called the *Laghuśambara*) composed approximately 8th–9th c. CE. Key Indian commentators include Kāṇha (Kṛṣṇācārya, ~9th c.) and Abhayākaragupta (~11th–12th c.). The tradition transmitted to Tibet from ~11th c., especially through the Kagyu lineage (Marpa Lotsawa, ~1012–1097 CE, who brought the Cakrasaṃvara teachings from Nāropā). Nāropā is traditionally said to have received the Vajrayoginī transmission directly from a *ḍākinī*.

## Mythological role
Vajrayoginī's role in the tantric system:
1. **Yidam**: She is visualized by the practitioner as the embodiment of their own enlightened mind during sādhana (practice). The practitioner identifies with Vajrayoginī — the wrathful red body, skull-cup, knife — as a method of transforming ordinary perception into pure perception.
2. **Tumo (inner heat)**: Vajrayoginī's fire (the red triangle of inner heat at the navel) is central to the Six Yogas of Nāropā — the practitioner generates heat through her visualization.
3. **Phowa (consciousness transference)**: Vajrayoginī is the destination-deity in phowa practice, in which the consciousness is ejected at death into her heart.
4. **Pure land (Khecara)**: Vajrayoginī rules the *ḍākinī* realm of Khecara ("sky-space") — the pure field practitioners aspire to be reborn in through her practice.
5. **Consort axis**: In the Cakrasaṃvara mandala (62 deities), Vajrayoginī is in yab-yum (wisdom-compassion union) with Heruka/Cakrasaṃvara — the central icon of inseparable bliss-emptiness.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[kali]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: The fierce naked skull-garlanded goddess is a shared iconographic type across Śāktism and Vajrayāna. Sanderson (2009) documents that Buddhist tantric traditions systematically appropriated Śaiva-Śākta iconographic vocabulary; Vajrayoginī inherits and transforms the Kālī-Bhairavī archetype.
- **[[durga]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Durgā as the transcendent warrior goddess who destroys cosmic darkness parallels Vajrayoginī's role as destroyer of the poisons/ignorance. Both wield weapons, both are wrathful-compassionate.
- **[[mahakala]]** — *direct-influence*: Mahākāla is the principal male dharmapāla; Vajrayoginī is the primary female yidam. The skull-cup iconography and wrathful-compassion doctrine are shared; both belong to the Cakrasaṃvara system.
- **[[sekhmet]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Sekhmet, the Egyptian lioness-goddess of destructive solar power who must be appeased to avoid total destruction, structurally parallels Vajrayoginī's wrathful energy as fierce-compassion that must be channelled precisely.
- **[[coatlicue]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Coatlicue (Aztec earth-mother wearing skull and severed-hand necklace) and Vajrayoginī (skull garland, skull-cup) represent the same archetypal pattern: the terrifying feminine power of cosmic dissolution and rebirth.
- **[[prajnaparamita-goddess]]** — *direct-influence*: The Prajñāpāramitā is the "mother of all Buddhas" — the wisdom-perfection principle. Vajrayoginī is her fierce, embodied instantiation in the Anuttarayoga tantric context.
- **[[tara]]** — *direct-influence*: In the same tantric Buddhist feminine spectrum, Tārā is the swift-protector (peaceful-semi-wrathful), Vajrayoginī is the fierce-transformative. Both are yidam in their respective lineages.

## Iconography
Standard iconography: naked, red-bodied; standing in archer's stance (one leg bent, one extended) or dancing; right hand holds the hooked knife (kartrikā/kartari) at shoulder or raised; left hand holds the skull-cup (kapāla) filled with blood/nectar; the khatvāṅga staff (topped with three skulls, skull-bowl, vajra) rests against her left shoulder and represents her union with Cakrasaṃvara; crown of five dried skulls; garland of fifty fresh severed heads (representing the fifty letters of the Sanskrit alphabet — transformation of language/concept); three eyes (third in the forehead); fanged smile; three hair-locks flowing free; she tramples a prostrate human corpse.

The *Vajravārāhī* (Diamond Sow) form adds a small sow-head emerging from her right ear — the sow symbolizes the transmutation of ignorance (pig = ignorance in Buddhist iconography) into wisdom.
