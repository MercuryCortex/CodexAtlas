# Almaqah (Almouqah)

## Identity
**Almaqah** (Sabaean *ʾlmqh*; Greek *Almouqah*; Arabic *Almuqah*) was the national high-god of the **Sabaean Kingdom** of South Arabia (modern Yemen, ~9th c. BCE – 6th c. CE), and the principal pre-Christian deity of the **pre-Aksumite Damʾat (Daʿamat) state** (~700–400 BCE) and the early **Aksumite Kingdom** (~100 BCE – 330 CE) before the conversion of [[ezana-of-aksum]] under [[frumentius]]. He is the **principal deity that Aksumite Christianity displaced** at the [[event-aksumite-christianization-c330]] — his crescent-and-disk symbol on pre-Christian Aksumite coinage is replaced by the Christian cross during Ezana's reign. This makes Almaqah one of the most important deities for the Christianity-from-prior-religion tracing edge of the Ethiopian wedge: he is what Aksum converted *from*.

Almaqah was structurally the **moon-god** of the Sabaean pantheon (paired with the sun-goddess **Shams** and the Venus-deity **Athtar / Astar** — see [[astar-aksumite]]). He is the source of the Sabaean royal-legitimation formula (Sabaean kings styled themselves *muqarribs* — "unifiers" — under Almaqah's patronage), the patron of the famous **Marib dam** (the largest hydraulic-engineering project of pre-Islamic Arabia, repeatedly attested in Almaqah's name in dedicatory inscriptions), and the principal recipient of South Arabian state-religious devotion at the **Awwam temple (Maḥram Bilqīs)** at Marib — one of the most extensively-excavated pre-Islamic Arabian sanctuaries.

## Earliest attestation
- **Sabaean royal inscriptions from Saba** (Yemen) from at least the 8th c. BCE.
- **Awwam temple (Maḥram Bilqīs)** at Marib — the principal Almaqah sanctuary; excavations by the American Foundation for the Study of Man (Wendell Phillips, 1951–52) and continuing German Archaeological Institute work since the 1980s.
- **Pre-Aksumite Damʾat-era inscriptions** at sites like Yeha and Hawelti (northern Ethiopia, ~700 BCE onward) attest Almaqah-worship migrating across the Red Sea with Sabaean colonist-settlement of the highlands. Yeha's Almaqah temple is one of the most-preserved pre-Christian monumental structures in Ethiopia.
- **Pre-Christian Aksumite royal inscriptions** invoke Almaqah alongside [[mahrem]] (the war-god), [[astar-aksumite]] (the astral deity), [[beher]] (a regional deity), and [[meder]] (the earth deity).
- **Pre-Christian Aksumite coinage** carries the *crescent-and-disk* symbol on the reverse — the Almaqah symbol. The shift to the *cross* on the reverse during Ezana's mid-reign is the principal material-culture marker of the [[event-aksumite-christianization-c330]] (Munro-Hay 1991, ch. 6).

## Mythological role
Almaqah was the *national patron-deity* of Saba — the cosmic-political guarantor of Sabaean kingship and the irrigated agricultural prosperity that underpinned the Sabaean state. His characteristic animal is the **bull** (whose horns echo the lunar crescent); his weapon-attribute is the *ʾrn* — a composite weapon of thunderbolt-and-arrow type. Sabaean royal inscriptions credit Almaqah with military victories, dam-construction successes, and dynastic continuity. Bronze and stone bull-iconography from Sabaean and pre-Aksumite contexts is extensive.

In the *pre-Aksumite* Damʾat period (c. 700–400 BCE), Sabaean colonists settled the highland Tigray region (the Yeha and Mahram Bilqis temples are the surviving witnesses) and brought Almaqah-worship with them. The Aksumite state that emerged later (from c. 100 BCE) inherited this pantheon, joined to it by political and linguistic continuity. By Ezana's pre-conversion period (early 4th c. CE), Almaqah remained the leading Aksumite deity, with [[mahrem]] as the war-god of the king's military identity.

The transition to Christianity in c. 330 *displaced* Almaqah from official state-religion status. His crescent-and-disk symbol disappears from royal coinage and inscriptions; his temples (where archaeologically traced) cease to be maintained as cultic centers and many are repurposed or fall into disuse. The transition is *material-culturally* the most thoroughly documented religious transition of any ancient African Christianization.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **Sin** (Mesopotamian moon-god) — *parallel-form*: structurally cognate moon-deity-supreme pattern. Direct dependence not established (Sabaean Almaqah is a distinct cultic-political development), but the broader Semitic moon-god-as-high-god pattern (also attested in some North Arabian contexts) links them.
- **Hubal** (Pre-Islamic Meccan / Quraysh) — *parallel-form*: another Arabian-Peninsular moon-and-divinatory high-god, structurally cognate.
- **The Christian cross** that replaced Almaqah's crescent-and-disk — *displacement-relationship*: the Aksumite Christianization is materially the most-documented religious-symbolic transition where one cosmic-symbolic system explicitly replaces another.

## Iconography
- **Crescent-and-disk** — the principal symbolic representation; appears on pre-Christian Aksumite coinage and stelae.
- **Bull** — sacred animal; bronze bull statuettes from Sabaean and pre-Aksumite contexts.
- **Ibex** — secondary sacred animal.
- **Vine** — agricultural-fertility attribute.
- **Throne-of-stone** — Sabaean temple iconography.

## Disputes
- **Whether Almaqah was originally a moon-god.** Modern Sabaean studies (Robin 2012) generally affirms the moon-god identification based on iconography (crescent-and-disk) and association with the bull. Some 20th-c. scholarship (Müller, Wissmann) proposed a sun-god identity; this is now largely rejected.
- **Pre-Aksumite "Damʾat / Daʿamat" continuity.** Whether the Sabaean colonist-settlement of pre-Aksumite Tigray was demographic (substantial population transfer) or political-cultic (royal-religious elite only, with the population remaining Cushitic-speaking). Phillipson 2012 favors the latter.
- **Date of Almaqah's decline.** Whether Almaqah continues in private/local devotion after the official Christianization c. 330. Some evidence of crescent-and-disk persistence in lower-quality pre-1500 Ethiopian material culture suggests informal continuation; the official-state cult ends with Ezana's conversion.
