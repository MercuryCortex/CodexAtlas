# Changing Woman

## Identity
*Asdzą́ą́ Nádleehé* ("the Woman who Changes" — literally "the Woman who is always renewing herself") is the most important, beloved, and active of the Diyin Diné'é (Holy People) in Navajo religion. She embodies *hózhó* — the ideal state of beauty, harmony, and right relationship — in living form. Changing Woman changes with the seasons: she is a young woman in spring, a mature woman in summer, an old woman in autumn, and renews as a young woman again in winter. She is not a goddess to be worshiped but the paradigmatic embodiment of the cosmological principle that everything in proper relationship undergoes cycles of renewal.

Changing Woman created the first Navajo clans by rubbing skin from her own body — from her breast and from her back — and molding it into human beings. She is therefore the literal ancestress of the Navajo people. She gave humanity the *Blessingway* ceremony (*Hózhóójí*) — the central ceremony that restores people to hózhó — and the *kinaaldá*, the female puberty ceremony modeled on her own coming-of-age.

## Mythological role
- **Cosmogonic creator**: creates the first Navajo from her own body (skin-rubbing); the clans trace their origin to her
- **Blessingway giver**: the Blessingway ceremony recreates the narrative of Changing Woman's life; the ceremonial patient is identified with her in the ceremony
- **Mother of the Hero Twins**: through her union with the Sun Bearer, she is mother of Monster Slayer and Born-for-Water, who rid the world of the monsters that threatened the People
- **Embodied hózhó**: her seasonal cycle of renewal is the primary cosmological demonstration that hózhó is achievable — the world renews, death is followed by life, age is followed by youth

## Cross-tradition identifications
No ancient-identification cross-tradition syncretism is documented. As a parallel-form comparison only:
- The seasonal renewal motif parallels Persephone (Greek: descent / return = winter / spring), Inanna/Ishtar (Mesopotamian: descent and return), and Isis (Egyptian: mourning / renewal). All are feminine figures embodying the seasonal cycle. The Navajo version is distinctive in that Changing Woman does not descend — she cycles *within* the world rather than leaving and returning.
