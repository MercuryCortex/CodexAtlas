# Erra

## Identity
Erra (also Irra) is the Akkadian god of plague, sudden violent destruction, and civic catastrophe — the figure invoked when entire cities are devastated by epidemic, war, or sudden ruin. He is functionally identified with [[nergal]] from the Old Babylonian period onward: the two share the Emeslam temple at Kutha, share the netherworld-and-plague portfolio, and are largely indistinguishable in cultic practice — but they retain distinct names and distinct emphases, with Erra concentrating the specifically catastrophic and Nergal the general netherworld-warrior role. Erra's central textual monument is the **Erra Epic** (also called the *Poem of Erra* or *Erra and Ishum*), a five-tablet Akkadian composition of approximately the late 2nd / early 1st millennium BCE that meditates on civic destruction and theological reckoning. The composition names its own author, **Kabti-ilani-Marduk**, who claims the poem was revealed to him in a dream — a rare autobiographical attribution in cuneiform literature.

## Earliest attestation
- Old Babylonian god lists name Erra alongside Nergal as functionally identified deities at Kutha.
- The **Erra Epic** (Cagni 1969/1977) survives in numerous Neo-Assyrian and Neo-Babylonian copies from Nineveh, Assur, Sultantepe, Babylon, and elsewhere; its composition is dated by most scholars to ~-800 BCE (8th century), though some place it as late as the early 7th century. Machinist 1983 reads it as a response to the Aramean incursions of the late 2nd millennium. The epic was so widely copied that fragments survive on amulets — apparently worn as plague-prophylactics, treating the text itself as apotropaic.
- Royal inscriptions of the Neo-Assyrian and Neo-Babylonian periods invoke Erra in military and curse contexts.
- The *Sibitti* (the Seven) — Erra's divine weapon-companions — are attested independently in Mesopotamian astral and apotropaic contexts; their incorporation into the Erra Epic dramatizes the poem's account of how Erra's violence is unleashed.

## Mythological role
The **Erra Epic** is Erra's principal mythological vehicle. Plot:
1. Erra is roused from indolence by his **Sibitti** (Seven divine weapons), who urge him to violence; he is restless and seeks pretext for slaughter.
2. He persuades [[marduk]] to temporarily leave his throne in Babylon (Marduk says his cult-statue is dirty and needs cleansing — Erra offers to govern in his absence).
3. With Marduk absent, Erra unleashes catastrophic destruction across Babylonia: Babylon, Sippar, Uruk, Der, and other cities are devastated; the populace turn on each other; the social order collapses.
4. Erra's vizier **Ishum** finally calms him; Erra repents (partially) and proclaims his own praise, predicting that the city that honors the poem will be spared future plague.

The epic is read variously as: (a) a theodicy of civic catastrophe (Frahm 2010); (b) a counter-text to the [[phase-1-008-enuma-elish]], inverting the Enuma Elish's cosmic order narrative; (c) a literary response to a specific Babylonian disaster (Machinist 1983 favors Aramean attacks; others propose Sutean or specific dynastic ruptures); (d) an apotropaic text whose recitation protects against future plague (the poem itself claims this function).

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[nergal]]** — *ancient-identification*: the Erra/Nergal merger is one of the cleaner cases of Akkadian theological consolidation. They share the Emeslam temple, the lion-headed mace, the plague portfolio, and (often) the same hymns and rituals. The persistence of both names through the Neo-Assyrian period suggests they were experienced as overlapping aspects of one divine identity rather than as fully identical.
- *Scholarly parallel*: Greek Ares (war) and Apollo (plague-arrows of *Iliad* 1) have been compared cross-culturally; the *Iliad* opening plague-narrative is sometimes compared structurally to the Erra Epic. These are *scholarly-parallel*, not ancient identifications.

## Iconography
Erra has limited independent iconography; he is typically depicted in shared Nergal-form: a warrior god with lion-headed mace, sickle-sword, and burning brand. The **Sibitti** (Seven) sometimes appear as seven star-symbols or as seven daggers/maces, especially on Neo-Assyrian apotropaic plaques.

## Disputes
- **Date of the Erra Epic**. Cagni 1969/1977 favors the 8th century. Machinist 1983 reads it as memory of late-2nd-millennium Aramean catastrophe. Frahm 2010 emphasizes its dialogical relationship with the Enuma Elish, suggesting a Babylonian intellectual milieu of theological self-questioning. The range -1100 to -700 is the scholarly window; precise dating remains open.
- **Erra and Nergal as one or two**. Per the vault's splitting rule, Erra is treated as a distinct node from Nergal because (a) the Erra name has its own major textual monument, (b) the Erra-specific "catastrophic-plague" emphasis differs from Nergal's general netherworld-warrior role, and (c) the historical record preserves both names rather than collapsing one. The syncretic edge captures the ancient identification.
- **Kabti-ilani-Marduk's authorship claim**. The Erra Epic is one of the few cuneiform compositions that names its own author; whether this is a real biographical attribution or a literary device (a divinely-revealed text "remembering" its scribe in pseudo-autobiography) is debated.
