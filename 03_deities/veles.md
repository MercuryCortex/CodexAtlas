# Veles

## Identity
Chthonic god of pre-Christian Slavic religion; patron of cattle ("Volos *skotij bog*" — "Volos the cattle-god" in the *Primary Chronicle*), wealth, trade, oaths, and the underworld. In Ivanov-Toporov's reconstructed "fundamental myth," Veles is [[perun]]'s adversary — the serpent-form god who steals heavenly waters / cattle / Perun's wife and is struck by lightning until he releases them. Topographically Veles inhabits the low-and-wet (valleys, marshes, the under-earth), opposite Perun's sky-mountains — a binary preserved in regional Slavic toponymy. Notably, in the canonical Vladimirian pantheon erected at Kiev in 980, Veles is **absent from the prince's hilltop**; the *Primary Chronicle* records his idol stood at the Podol (the lower-town merchant district) — the same Perun-high / Veles-low cosmological pattern. Under Christianization Veles was demonized into the folk-Slavic devil-figure, but the cattle-blessing role was simultaneously assimilated to **Saint Blaise (St Vlasiy)**, whose 11 February feast inherits Veles's livestock-protection rites in Russian, Ukrainian, and Belarusian peasant practice through the 19th c. and beyond.

## Earliest attestation
- **907 CE** — Treaty of Prince Oleg with Byzantium (*Primary Chronicle* sub anno 6415): the Rus' swear "by Perun, our god, and by Volos, the god of cattle." This is the earliest dated mention; pairs Volos with Perun in the formal oath-formula.
- **971 CE** — Treaty of Prince Svyatoslav: identical oath-formula. The pairing of the storm-god and the cattle-god as joint oath-witnesses is structurally significant — Perun guarantees the warrior's word, Veles the wealth-and-trade word.
- **980 CE** — Vladimir's hilltop pantheon at Kiev includes Perun, Khors, Dazhbog, Stribog, Simargl, and Mokosh but **not Volos/Veles**. Chronicle and later hagiographic notice: Volos's idol stood at the Podol (lower-town), not on the prince's hill. This is consistent with the high/low cosmological placement.
- **12th c. *Tale of Igor's Campaign* (*Tale of Igor's Campaign*)** — the poet Boyan is described as *Velesov vnuk*, "grandson of Veles" — Veles is patron of bards / poetry (this passage is the principal evidence for Veles's poetry-patron role; some scholars treat it as a learned bookish reference rather than living cult).
- **11th-c. Old Russian hagiography** — the *Life of St Avraamy of Rostov* records the destruction of Volos's stone idol at Rostov; similar notices for Yaroslavl. Cult-survival evidence outside Kievan elite, into the conversion-resistant Russian North.

## Mythological role
The reconstructed **Perun–Veles combat** (Ivanov & Toporov 1965/1974) is the centerpiece of Slavic comparative mythology. The pattern: Veles in serpent form steals Perun's wife (sometimes [[mokosh]]) / cattle / the rain-waters; Perun pursues, hurling lightning; Veles hides in trees (lightning splits trees), then in stones (lightning shatters stones), then in cattle (lightning strikes cattle), then under a man (lightning strikes men); finally Perun strikes Veles, who releases what he had taken; rain falls, the world is renewed. The structure exactly matches the IE storm-god-vs-serpent dragon-slaying formula reconstructed by Watkins 1995 (Indra-Vṛtra, Thor-Jǫrmungandr, Zeus-Typhon, Marduk-Tiāmat). The narrative is **reconstructed from folk-songs, charms, place-name evidence, and the Baltic parallel** — there is no surviving primary text. Veles is also lord of the dead: the *Veles-pastures* in Slavic folk-cosmology are where the souls of the deceased graze as cattle.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **Saint Blaise / St Vlasiy** — *folk-syncretism* (the textbook *dvoeverie* case). The phonetic alignment Volos ~ Vlas(iy) ~ Blasius was almost certainly opportunistic for the missionary Church: Blaise was a 4th-c. Armenian-Cappadocian martyr-bishop, but in Eastern Slavic peasant practice his feast (11 February) became the day when cattle were brought out and blessed, a function inherited directly from Veles. Russian and Ukrainian peasant practice preserved Volos-prayers under a thin Christian veneer through the 19th c.; Tokarev 1957 documents the practice still alive in 1920s ethnographic fieldwork.
- **Velinas / Velnias (Baltic, Lithuanian)** — *scholarly-parallel* / etymological cognate. The Baltic data is the strongest external control on Slavic Veles reconstruction: Velinas governs the realm of the dead in Lithuanian folk tradition, was demonized into "devil" (*velnias*) under Christianization, and appears in folk-tales as the trickster-adversary of Perkūnas — the same role-pair as Slavic Veles vs Perun. Gimbutas 1963; Greimas 1992.
- **[[hermes-greek]] / Hermes / Mercury** — *scholarly-parallel*: Ivanov-Toporov 1974 note the functional overlap (boundary-crossing, psychopomp, patron of merchants and thieves and oaths) without claiming any direct transmission. The parallel is typological — a generic chthonic-mediator god-type.
- **[[odin]] (wealth-magic-poetry-dead-god aspect)** — *scholarly-parallel*: also Ivanov-Toporov. They explicitly distinguish: Perun is the IE storm-god cognate of Thor (not Odin); Veles overlaps with Odin's *non-Thor* functions (sorcery, poetry, the dead, wealth). The pairing suggests the IE high-pantheon may have had a binary structure (storm-warrior + sovereign-magician) that the Norse split between Thor and Odin and the Slavs between Perun and Veles.

## Iconography
No surviving idol or unambiguous archaeological depiction. The textual cluster (cattle, serpent-form, lowlands, wool/fleece — Old Russian *volos* "hair / fleece" is etymologically connected) supplies the conventional iconography: a horned man-figure or serpent. Modern Rodnovery iconography depicts Veles as a horned, often bearded figure with cattle, or as a great serpent — these are 19th–21st-c. reconstructions, not ancient evidence. The Bulgarian / Serbian folk-image of the *zmey* (dragon) in the seasonal rain-bringing combat with Saint Elijah is the closest folk-survival of Veles iconography under Christian overlay (Elijah → Perun; *zmey* → Veles).

## Disputes
- **The Veles-Volos identity question.** Two names, two attestation patterns: *Veles* appears in the *Tale of Igor's Campaign* and Czech, Slovak, and South Slavic toponymy; *Volos* in the Rus' treaty oaths and East Slavic hagiography. Most scholars (Ivanov & Toporov, Jakobson) treat them as the same god under regional / phonetic variation. A minority (Łowmiański 1979) treats them as distinct, with *Veles* a learned Slavic-archaism and *Volos* an East-Slavic chthonic-cattle god that may originally have been a different deity altogether. The treaty oaths use Volos, not Veles, which is suggestive but not decisive.
- **The "fundamental myth" reconstruction.** As with [[perun]], the Perun-Veles combat narrative is widely repeated in modern Slavic-mythology handbooks but rests on Ivanov & Toporov's 1965/1974 reconstructive work and not on any surviving primary text. The strongest evidence is the toponymic-pairing pattern (Veles-valleys directly below Perun-mountains in Slovenian, Croatian, and Czech place-names — Katičić 2008–2014 *Božanski boj* expands the reconstruction further) and the Baltic parallel.
- **Veles as poetry-patron.** The *Tale of Igor's Campaign* reference to Boyan as "Velesov vnuk" is the sole textual basis; some scholars (Slavic studies dissenters) read it as a learned Old Slavonic literary trope (analogous to Greek poets calling themselves Muse-grandsons) rather than evidence of a living cult. The Boyan reference does **not** appear in the more reliable parts of the Old Russian chronicle tradition.
- **The Book of Veles (*Vles[k]niga*) is a 20th-c. forgery.** Important to flag: a "Slavic sacred text" circulated since the 1950s under this title and presented as Veles-tradition primary source is **not authentic**; it was composed by Yury Mirolyubov (Russian émigré) in the 1950s and is rejected by every serious Slavicist (Tvorogov 1990; Zaliznyak 2008). Modern Rodnovery and Russian-nationalist circles continue to cite it; the vault treats it as **Tier 4 / pseudoscholarship** and does not source Veles material from it.
