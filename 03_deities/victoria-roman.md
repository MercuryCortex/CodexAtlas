# Victoria (Roman)

## Identity

Victoria is the Roman personification of Victory — the divine ratification that the gods favor Rome and its armies. She is identified with Greek **[[nike-greek]]** through *interpretatio Graeca*. Unlike many Roman deities, Victoria is not a complex mythological figure but a theological concept given divine status: the idea that military victory is not merely human achievement but the gods' confirmation that the victorious side possesses *pietas* (right relationship with the divine).

In the Roman conception, Victoria's presence in battle was not automatic — it could be won or lost by the quality of Rome's religious observance. The *Pax deorum* ("peace with the gods") was the condition that ensured Victoria. This made her a perpetually live theological question: whose victory — and whose god — had divine backing?

## The Altar of Victoria: One of Late Antiquity's Defining Controversies

The **Altar of Victoria** in the Roman Senate house (*Curia Julia*) is the center of the most theologically significant debate Victoria generates — a controversy that crystallizes the entire conflict between Roman polytheism and Christianity.

**History of the Altar**:
- The golden statue of Victoria, wings spread, holding a laurel wreath, stood in the *Curia* from at least 29 BCE (Octavian/Augustus placed it there after the Battle of Actium).
- Every senator was expected to offer incense to Victoria before sessions — a ritual act of civic piety.
- **Constantius II** removed the Altar in 357 CE.
- **Julian the Apostate** restored it (~361–363 CE).
- **Gratian** removed it again in 382 CE and refused the title *Pontifex Maximus*.

**The Altar of Victory controversy (382–394 CE)** is a landmark moment in the history of Western religion:

1. **Quintus Aurelius Symmachus** (pagan senator, prefect of Rome) appealed to Emperor Valentinian II in 384 CE (*Relatio III*) for the Altar's restoration. His argument is one of antiquity's most eloquent defenses of religious pluralism: "We cannot reach so great a mystery by one route alone" (*uno itinere non potest perveniri ad tam grande secretum*). He argued from tradition, Roman success, and religious tolerance.

2. **Ambrose of Milan** (bishop) intervened directly, writing to Valentinian II (*Epistulae* 17 and 18) opposing restoration. His argument: the Emperor, as a Christian, cannot authorize pagan worship; Roman victories now come from the Christian God, not Victoria.

3. **Augustine of Hippo**, writing *The City of God* (413–426 CE) in part as a response to the sack of Rome by Alaric (410 CE) — which pagans blamed on the removal of the old gods — extensively addresses Victoria. His central argument: Rome's victories were never caused by pagan gods; God permitted Rome's power for providential purposes. Victoria is an invented concept, not a real deity.

The controversy ended with **Theodosius I** definitively removing the Altar in 394 CE after defeating the pagan usurper Eugenius (who had briefly restored it). Theodosius's victory at the Battle of the Frigidus (394 CE) — fought in a miraculous wind (*bora*) that supposedly aided his Christian forces — was interpreted as the definitive proof that the Christian God had replaced Victoria.

This sequence is the **hinge-point of Western religious history**: the last serious public defense of Roman polytheism, the first systematic Christian arguments for the incompatibility of polytheist civic religion with Christian empire, and the theological template for all subsequent Christian arguments about sacred history.

## Victoria in Imperial Ideology

Beyond the Senate-house controversy, Victoria was central to Roman imperial self-presentation:
- Emperors minted coins with Victoria on the reverse — her image = divine confirmation of the emperor's legitimacy.
- The title *Victoria Germanica*, *Victoria Britannica*, etc. = specific victories dedicated to the goddess.
- Military standards (*signa*) often incorporated Victoria imagery.
- The **Arch of Constantine** (315 CE) — a nominally Christian monument — still depicts Victoria in the spandrels, showing how deeply the iconography was embedded even after Christianization.

## Iconographic Legacy

Victoria's winged-woman-with-laurel form became the template for the Christian concept of the **angel** — the winged divine messenger. Art historians trace the visual grammar of Christian angels to Nike/Victoria iconography, with the wreath replaced by a halo and the palm branch retained as a martyr's attribute. This is one of the most archaeologically documented cases of pagan iconography being absorbed into Christian visual theology.

## Cross-tradition connections

- **[[nike-greek]]** — *ancient-identification*: Victoria is the Latin translation of Nike; iconography and theology fully shared.
- **[[jupiter]]** — *family-connection*: in Roman theology, Victoria is Jupiter's instrument; she executes the Father of Gods' decision about who deserves victory.
- **[[god-the-father-christian]]** — *polemic-against* / *theological-replacement*: the Ambrose-Augustine position: the Christian God has assumed Victoria's function; Roman victories now come from God, not from Victoria.
