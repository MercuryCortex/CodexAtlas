# Helios

## Identity
Helios (Greek *Ἥλιος*) is the **personified sun of Greek religion**, originally one of the second-generation Titans (son of Hyperion and Theia) and brother of Selene (moon) and Eos (dawn). He drives the sun-chariot across the sky daily and sees all things — the **"all-seeing"** epithet appears already in Homer (the sun witnesses Aphrodite's adultery with Ares in *Odyssey* 8). Originally a minor deity in mainland Greek cult (his major sanctuary was on Rhodes, with the Colossus of Rhodes one of his cult-statues), Helios becomes **cosmically central in late antiquity**: from the late Hellenistic period he is identified with [[apollo]]; from the Roman period with [[sol-invictus]] (Aurelian's institution of the imperial solar cult in 274 CE); with [[mithras-roman]] in Roman Mithraic religion; and is elevated to **supreme cosmic god** in [[julian-the-apostate]]'s solar monotheism (*Hymn to King Helios*, 362 CE). For the Codex Atlas's Christianity-to-older-traditions mission, Helios is one of the central transmission nodes — solar monotheism, Sol-Invictus cult, Mithraism, and the Christian appropriation of sun-iconography (the date of Christmas, the haloed Christ-Pantokrator) all run through him.

## Earliest attestation
- **Mycenaean** Linear B tablets — likely attestations of a sun-deity, though identification with later Helios is not certain.
- **Homer** *Iliad* and *Odyssey*: Helios is the all-seeing sun-god, witness in oaths; cattle of Helios on Thrinacia (*Od.* 12); witnesses Aphrodite-Ares (*Od.* 8).
- **Hesiod** *Theogony* 371–374: son of Hyperion and Theia, brother of Selene and Eos.
- **Homeric Hymn 31 (*To Helios*)** — earliest extended hymnody.
- Major Rhodian cult from the Archaic period; the **Colossus of Rhodes** (-282) is one of the Seven Wonders, a 33m bronze statue of Helios at the harbor entrance.
- Hellenistic Apollo-Helios identification standardizes (already in Euripides; Stoic philosophy treats them as the same god).
- **Roman imperial cult**: Sol Indiges and Sol Invictus traditions; **Aurelian's** institution of Sol Invictus as state god (~+274) makes Helios-Sol the official imperial deity.
- **Mithraism**: the iconographic *dexiosis* (handshake) between Mithras and Sol Invictus is the central scene of every Mithraeum; sometimes the two are merged ("Sol Invictus Mithras"), sometimes separate.
- **Julian** (*r.* 361–363) institutes a full philosophical-theological solar monotheism with Helios as the supreme god; his *Hymn to King Helios* is the principal text.

## Mythological role
Helios's narrative role is comparatively limited: he drives the sun-chariot daily; his son **Phaethon** wrecks the chariot in a famous catastrophic tale (Ovid *Metamorphoses* 2); his cattle on Thrinacia are killed by Odysseus's crew (causing their destruction); he reports infidelities he witnesses. The richness of Helios is **not in story but in cult-theology and iconography**, especially in late antiquity, where his role as cosmic witness and visible-cosmic-power makes him a natural focus for monotheist-aspiring philosophical theologies.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[sol-invictus]]** — *ancient-identification* (institutional): Aurelian's official cult-merger from 274 CE.
- **[[apollo]]** — *ancient-identification*: late-Hellenistic conflation, fully standardized by Roman imperial period.
- **[[ra]]** — *ancient-identification* (Greco-Egyptian): standard *interpretatio graeca* in Greek-Egyptian religious literature; Plutarch and Greek-Egyptian magical papyri use Helios for the Egyptian solar deity.
- **[[mithras-roman]]** — *ancient-identification* (Mithraic): the cult-merger "Sol Invictus Mithras" is well-attested epigraphically (CIMRM corpus); but Mithraic iconography also shows them as separate deities in handshake.
- **Christian Sol Iustitiae** — *appropriated-by*: the Christian "Sun of Righteousness" (Malachi 4:2 *sol iustitiae*) absorbs solar imagery; **December 25** as Christ's birthday is institutionally fixed in the 4th c. CE (Chronograph of 354) as the Christianization of the **Sol Invictus festival of December 25** ("Dies Natalis Solis Invicti," institutionalized by Aurelian). This is the **single most consequential pagan-to-Christian solar appropriation** in church history, well-documented in liturgical history (Talley 1991; Roll 1995).
- **Christ-Pantokrator iconography** — *appropriated-by*: the radiate-haloed cosmic-ruler Christ image of Byzantine art draws on Helios / Sol Invictus iconography (the radiate crown becomes the cruciform halo; the cosmic globe becomes the *kosmokrator* orb).

## Iconography
- **Radiate crown** — sun-rays emanating from the head, the principal Helios attribute (standard on Hellenistic and Roman coinage including the Colossus of Rhodes type)
- **Four-horse chariot (quadriga)** — Helios's chariot crossing the sky
- **Globe** — cosmic orb, especially in late-antique iconography (Sol Invictus / Mithras *dexiosis* scene)
- **Whip** (driving the chariot)
- The **Antikythera mechanism** (~-100) is calibrated for Helios's solar year — a striking material witness to the astronomical-theological complex

## Disputes
- **Christmas-from-Sol-Invictus thesis.** The 4th-c. Christianization of December 25 from the Sol Invictus festival is well-documented but the *theological* significance is contested. Some patristic scholars argue the date was chosen apologetically as a counter-festival; others argue for liturgical considerations (Annunciation March 25 + 9 months = December 25, the "computational hypothesis," Talley 1991). The two positions are not mutually exclusive.
- **Helios-Apollo identification's date.** When the Helios-Apollo identification fully consolidated — Euripides 5th c. BCE? Stoic 3rd c. BCE? Roman imperial? — is debated. The current consensus is gradual conflation through the Hellenistic period, fully standardized by Roman imperial times.
- **Julian's solar monotheism: Christian-influenced or independent?** Whether Julian's *Hymn to King Helios* is a Neoplatonist independent development or a counter-construction against Christian monotheism is debated. Most scholars treat it as Neoplatonist (Iamblichean) theology that *opportunistically* competes with Christianity rather than being primarily defined by it (Smith 1995; Athanassiadi 1981).
