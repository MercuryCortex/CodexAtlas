# Rama

## Identity

Rāma — *Rāmachandra* ("Rama-Moon"), seventh *avatāra* of [[vishnu]] in the classical *daśāvatāra* scheme — is the hero of Valmiki's Sanskrit *Rāmāyaṇa* (~3rd–2nd c. BCE in core form) and of Tulsidas's Awadhi vernacular *Ramcharitmanas* (1574 CE). In the bhakti devotional tradition centered on Rama — *Rāma-bhakti* — Rama is not merely a hero or an avatar but the supreme personal deity, simultaneously God-as-king and God-as-ideal-human. Where [[krishna]] embodies *līlā* (divine play, transcending limits), Rama embodies *maryādā* (the binding norm, the socially and cosmically given limit) — the two are theologically complementary poles of the Vaishnava personal-deity tradition.

## Maryādā Puruṣottama

Rama's distinctive theological title is *maryādā puruṣottama* — "the highest person of the limit / norm." Rama keeps every promise and obligation even at devastating personal cost:

- Keeps his father's boon — goes into 14-year exile rather than become king as is rightfully his.
- Keeps his warrior dharma — kills Ravana despite Ravana's Brahmin status.
- Keeps his king's duty — banishes Sita on a citizen's rumor, despite his love for her.

This makes Rama the paradigm of *dharmic* heroism: supremely virtuous precisely *because* he subordinates personal desire to cosmic-social order.

## Textual history

The Rama story has many forms across South and Southeast Asia:

- **Valmiki's Rāmāyaṇa** (Sanskrit, ~3rd–2nd c. BCE core): the critical text; Goldman et al.'s 7-volume Princeton translation (1984–2017) is the scholarly standard.
- **Kamban's Iramavataram** (Tamil, ~12th c. CE): deeply different theological emphasis; one of Tamil literature's masterpieces.
- **Tulsidas's Ramcharitmanas** (Awadhi, 1574 CE): the most-recited Hindu text after the [[phase-2-027-bhagavad-gita|Bhagavad Gītā]] in North India; Rama is explicitly identified as the supreme deity (not just Vishnu's avatar); deeply emotional bhakti framework. Lutgendorf 1991 is the standard study.
- **Buddhist *Dasaratha Jātaka***: Rama as a previous birth of the Bodhisattva; the moral of the jataka differs significantly from the Hindu versions.
- **Thai *Ramakien*, Indonesian *Kakawin Ramayana***: Southeast Asian receptions showing the pan-regional reach across Hindu and post-Hindu Buddhist contexts.

## MASSIVE WIN — Rama-avatar ↔ Christ incarnation

The Vaishnava avatar theology asks: why would the infinite, self-sufficient God *voluntarily descend* (*avatāra* = "descent") into a finite, mortal, suffering human form? The theological answers developed in the Puranas parallel the Christian Incarnation debate almost point-for-point:

- **Why descend at all?** Answer: to restore cosmic order (*dharma-saṃsthāpanārthāya* — BG 4.8); the Christian Incarnation also restores the disrupted order between humanity and God.
- **Does the avatar truly suffer?** This parallels the Docetist controversy — did Christ truly suffer on the cross, or was it appearance? The Ramaite tradition affirms Rama *genuinely* suffers (he weeps for Sita, mourns Lakshman's injury), which the bhakti tradition reads as God's capacity for compassion — exactly the anti-Docetist argument of Irenaeus and Athanasius.
- **The avatar's relationship to the supreme deity:** In Ramaite bhakti, Rama *is* Vishnu in full descent — not a mere manifestation but the complete divine person. This parallels the Chalcedonian "fully God, fully human" formula.

These structural parallels are noted in Hindu-Christian dialogue scholarship (Panikkar 1981; Clooney 2010) but are not claimed as historical transmission. They represent independent theological trajectories arriving at formally similar answers to the same problems about divine immanence.

## Modern reception

Rama's image has been politically mobilized in modern India: the Ram Janmabhoomi movement (claiming Ayodhya as Rama's birthplace), the demolition of the Babri Masjid (1992), and the 2024 Ram Mandir consecration make Rama simultaneously a devotional figure and a political symbol of Hindu majoritarianism.
