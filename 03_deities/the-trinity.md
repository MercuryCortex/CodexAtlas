# The Trinity

The doctrine — and as a node, treated as the unified divine reality — that God is *one being* (*ousia*) in *three persons* (*hypostases*): [[god-the-father-christian|Father]], [[jesus-christ-deity|Son]], and [[holy-spirit|Holy Spirit]]. The doctrine emerges gradually in the 2nd–4th centuries, fixed dogmatically at [[event-council-of-nicaea-325|Nicaea, +325]] (Father and Son *homoousios*) and the Council of Constantinople (+381) which secured the full divinity of the Spirit and produced the Nicene-Constantinopolitan Creed.

Trinitarian theology was the central doctrinal innovation of patristic Christianity. It distinguished Christianity from:
- **Strict Jewish monotheism** (Rabbinic Judaism's *yichud Hashem* — the absolute unity of God)
- **Subordinationist Christologies** (Arianism: the Son was created, not co-eternal — condemned at Nicaea)
- **Modalism / Sabellianism** (the three are masks/modes of one person, not really distinct)
- **Tritheism** (three gods — the constant accusation by opponents)

The Latin and Greek traditions developed distinct trinitarian vocabularies and emphases. Augustine's *De Trinitate* (+400/+419) gave the Western tradition its psychological-analogical method (Trinity as memory, understanding, will). The Cappadocian Fathers ([[basil-of-caesarea|Basil]], [[gregory-of-nyssa|Gregory of Nyssa]], [[gregory-of-nazianzus|Gregory of Nazianzus]]) gave the Eastern tradition the strict *ousia*/*hypostasis* distinction.

The *filioque* — whether the Spirit proceeds from the Father AND the Son, or from the Father alone — became the doctrinal symbol of the [[event-great-schism-1054|1054 schism]].

Comparative-religion note: structural triadic patterns appear in many traditions (Hindu Trimurti, Zoroastrian Amesha Spenta heptad, Neoplatonic three hypostases) — these are *parallels*, not transmissions, per current scholarly consensus.
