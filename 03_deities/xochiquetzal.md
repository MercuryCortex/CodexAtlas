# Xochiquetzal

## Identity
"Flower-Feather" (Nahuatl: *xōchitl* "flower" + *quetzalli* "quetzal feather / precious thing") — Aztec goddess of beauty, flowers, love, female sexuality, creative arts, and weaving. She is the Aztec ideal of feminine beauty and youthful desire. Where [[tlaloc]] governs rain and agricultural water, Xochiquetzal governs the blossoming of the earth — flowers, sensual pleasure, the erotic — and she is the patron of all craftswomen: weavers, embroiderers, painters. She is one of the few Aztec deities who inhabits the uppermost of the 13 heavens (Topan), the most exalted celestial region, underlining her status as a supreme being in her domain rather than a subordinate goddess. In some creation accounts she is the **first woman** and the original consort of the world, abducted by [[tezcatlipoca]] in a mythic event that divides primordial unity.

## Earliest attestation
- The name and associated iconography appear in Aztec codices from the Late Postclassic period (c. 1300–1521 CE).
- **Codex Borgia** (pre-conquest): Xochiquetzal's distinctive two-flower headdress and flower-weaving imagery appear in the tonalpohualli (260-day ritual calendar) pages.
- **Florentine Codex** (Sahagún, c. 1569 compilation): detailed description of her festival (the month Huei Tecuilhuitl), her attributes, and her domains over weaving and the erotic arts.
- Precursor imagery — female beauty-goddess figures with flower attributes — appears in Teotihuacan murals (c. 200–650 CE), suggesting the cult-type is Pre-Aztec.

## Mythological role
Two major mythic roles:
1. **The abduction**: In some sources, Xochiquetzal was originally the wife of [[tlaloc]] (rain god), dwelling in paradise with him. [[tezcatlipoca]] abducted her, and she became associated with Piltzintecuhtli (a solar youth-figure). This abduction myth divides the primordial erotic paradise, introducing desire-and-loss into the cosmos — a structural parallel to abduction myths in other traditions (Persephone, the Sabine women, the stealing of Freyja in Norse myth).
2. **Patroness of craftswomen**: Xochiquetzal taught women weaving, embroidery, and all the arts of adornment. Her festival (Huei Tecuilhuitl) included ritual weaving contests and offering of woven cloths. Prostitutes also venerated her — the erotic arts and the textile arts were both under her domain, united by the Aztec concept of *ixtlamachiliztli* (face-making / beautification as a sacred act).

In some accounts she is associated with the planet **Venus** as Evening Star (where [[quetzalcoatl]]/Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli is Venus as Morning Star) — she and Quetzalcoatl together represent the dual aspects of Venus as both erotic and martial/civilizing.

## Cross-tradition identifications

- **[[ixchel]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: The K'iche' Maya Ixchel (moon goddess, weaving, medicine) shares Xochiquetzal's domain of weaving and female creative arts; both are associated with the moon and with erotic power. The parallel is domain-based; the deities are distinct.
- **Aphrodite / Venus** — *scholarly-parallel*: beauty, desire, and erotic power are among the most widely distributed goddess-domains cross-culturally; the Xochiquetzal / Aphrodite parallel is typological, not historical. No transmission is claimed across the Atlantic.
- **[[inanna-sumerian]] / [[ishtar-akkadian]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Inanna/Ishtar governs love, desire, and beauty alongside war; Xochiquetzal's erotic-arts domain parallels the Mesopotamian love-goddess typology. Again, typological, not genetic.

## Iconography
The double-flower headdress flanking the face is the most reliable identifying attribute in codex art. Quetzal feathers (long, iridescent, emerald-green tail feathers of the resplendent quetzal bird) adorn her dress and hair. She is almost always depicted as young and beautiful. Butterfly imagery appears, especially in depictions of her old-age aspect (*Ichpochtli* as "The Maiden" who paradoxically ages while retaining her beauty-domain); the butterfly is associated with sexual pleasure and the souls of dead warriors in Aztec cosmology. Weaving equipment — the spindle, the backstrap loom — appears in ritual-calendar depictions.

## Disputes
- **Xochiquetzal vs Xilonen / Cihuacoatl / Coatlicue — the goddess-complex problem.** Aztec feminine divinity tends toward overlapping patronages; Xochiquetzal, Cihuacoatl (Snake-Woman, earth goddess), [[coatlicue]] (Serpent Skirt, earth-death goddess), and Xilonen (young-corn goddess) share domains in ways that sometimes suggest a single underlying "Great Goddess" and sometimes suggest distinct cults. The standard scholarly position (Nicholson; Townsend) keeps them distinct while acknowledging the overlapping.
- **Xochiquetzal as Venus Evening Star.** The identification of Xochiquetzal with Venus as Evening Star is present in some colonial-period sources but not systematic across all primary materials. Milbrath (1999) marshals astronomical evidence; Taube (1992) is more cautious. The pairing Quetzalcoatl (Morning Star) / Xochiquetzal (Evening Star) is elegant but may be a colonial-period systematization.
