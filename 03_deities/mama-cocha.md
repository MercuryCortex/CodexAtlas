# Mama Cocha

## Identity
Mama Cocha ("Mother Sea" or "Mother Lake" in Quechua; *qocha* = body of water, lake, sea) is the Andean goddess of the sea, lakes, rivers, and all waters — a primary female deity in both coastal Andean cultures (where the Pacific Ocean was her domain) and highland cultures (where Lake Titicaca, the sacred origin-lake of Andean civilization, was central). She is the divine protector of fishermen and those who depend on water for subsistence; offerings were made to her before fishing expeditions and voyages. Paired with Pachamama (earth-mother), Mama Cocha together forms the feminine divine matrix of Andean cosmology: earth and water, soil and sea, the two ground conditions of Andean life.

## Earliest attestation
Pre-Inca coastal Andean cultures (Moche, Chimú, Nazca) show sea-deity veneration in their iconography and are likely antecedents of the Mama Cocha figure. Inca state incorporation of coastal deities, including Mama Cocha, is documented from the Inca expansion into coastal territories (~1400–1533 CE). Colonial chroniclers (Cobo, Cieza) record the Inca Mama Cocha cult.

## Mythological role
**Protector of fishermen**: Mama Cocha's most immediate social function was as protector of fishing communities along the Pacific coast of what is now Peru. Coastal peoples were culturally distinct from highland Andeans, and their religious practices centered on the sea. The Inca state incorporation of Mama Cocha was part of the broader Inca project of integrating coastal, highland, and jungle deities into a coherent imperial pantheon.

**Lake Titicaca dimension**: Lake Titicaca was the mythological origin-point of Inca civilization — Inti (the sun) and Mama Quilla (the moon) rose from its waters in Inca origin narratives; Viracocha created the present humanity at its shores. Mama Cocha's governance of lakes extended to Titicaca, connecting her to the most sacred water in the Andean world.

**Rain and Illapa**: Illapa (thunder, rain) and Mama Cocha (sea, lakes) form a complementary pair in Andean water-cosmology: Illapa brings water from the sky, Mama Cocha governs water on the earth's surface and below. Rain returns to sea; the celestial river (Milky Way) feeds the earthly waters. The Andean hydraulic cosmos is not mechanistic but animated — each water body is under a deity's governance.

**Paired with Pachamama**: Pachamama (earth-mother) and Mama Cocha (sea-mother) together cover the non-celestial world. Some scholars (Rostworowski) argue that the pre-Inca coastal Andean tradition placed Mama Cocha as equivalent to or above Pachamama for maritime communities. The Inca state integrated both.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[pachamama]]** — *structural-parallel / paired deity*: Paired earth-water mother figures; see above.
- **Amphitrite / Tethys (Greek)** — *structural-parallel*: Sea-mother deity figures with protective maritime function; no transmission.
- **Yemoja / Olokun (Yoruba)** — *structural-parallel*: African sea/water mother deities with protective maritime function; no transmission. The cross-cultural pattern of a "sea mother" who protects fishermen and receives offerings before voyages is remarkably widespread.
- **[[tangaroa]]** — *structural-parallel*: Pan-Polynesian sea deity; Mama Cocha performs an equivalent role in the Andean coastal tradition. Both are sea-sovereigns receiving offerings from maritime peoples; no transmission.

## Iconography
Mama Cocha is typically represented as a woman associated with fish and the sea. Colonial Andean art sometimes depicts her wearing a fish-scale garment or surrounded by marine imagery. She is among the less visually elaborated major deities in the surviving record — her worship was primarily oral and ritual rather than monumental.
