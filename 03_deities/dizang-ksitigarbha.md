# Kṣitigarbha (Dìzàng / Jizō)

## Identity
Kṣitigarbha ("Earth Womb" or "Earth Treasury" — from Sanskrit *kṣiti* "earth" + *garbha* "womb/treasury") is the bodhisattva whose vow is the most extreme expression of compassion in the entire Mahāyāna Buddhist tradition: he vowed to refuse his own complete Buddhahood — to remain active and present in the world — until every sentient being in every hell realm has been liberated. The formula is uncompromising: "Not until the hells are empty will I become a Buddha; not until all beings are saved will I certify to Bodhi." He is the patron of the dead, the dying, the living-in-extremis, and especially of children who have died young.

In China (as *Dìzàng Púsà*), he is one of the four great bodhisattvas of Chinese Buddhism alongside Guanyin (compassion), Mañjuśrī (wisdom), and Samantabhadra (practice). His famous cult mountain is Jiuhua Shan (Nine Glorious Mountains) in Anhui Province, one of the four sacred Buddhist mountains of China — associated with Kim Gyo-gak (Jin Qiaojue), a Silla Korean monk (~696–794 CE) identified as a living manifestation of Kṣitigarbha.

In Japan (as *Jizō Bosatsu*), he is perhaps the single most beloved and omnipresent religious figure — small stone Jizō statues wearing red bibs and caps line roadsides, mountain paths, cemeteries, and village intersections throughout Japan. He is especially associated with *mizuko* (water children — children lost to miscarriage, abortion, or infant death), whose spirits he guides and protects in the realm between this world and the next.

## Earliest attestation
The primary scriptural source is the *Kṣitigarbha Bodhisattva Pūrvapraṇidhāna Sūtra* (地藏菩薩本願經, *Dizang Pusa Benyuan Jing*), translated into Chinese attributed to Śikṣānanda (~695–700 CE), though the sutra's Indian origins are uncertain and it may be a Chinese composition. Earlier Sanskrit references to Kṣitigarbha appear in the *Daśacakra Kṣitigarbha Sūtra* (~5th c. CE or earlier), attesting an Indian origin. The Japanese Jizō cult develops from the Nara period (8th c.) onward and becomes dominant in the Heian and Kamakura periods.

## Mythological role
Key narrative-doctrinal elements:
1. **The radical vow**: In a past life as a Brahmin girl, she (past-life gender shifts are common in bodhisattva hagiography) prayed and practiced intensely to save her deceased mother from hell. Discovering that her mother had been reborn in a bad realm, she vowed to empty the hell realms through her compassion. This vow established the *praṇidhāna* (bodhisattva's resolving vow) that structures all Kṣitigarbha practice.
2. **Intercession for the dead**: In the Chinese tradition, the forty-nine days after death are a liminal period in which the deceased moves through the courts of the ten hell-kings (including Yán Wáng / [[yan-wang]]). Kṣitigarbha intercedes in these courts on behalf of the dead; memorial rites during the forty-nine days invoke his intervention.
3. **Jizō as protector of children**: In Japanese tradition, Jizō appears to dead children in the *sai no kawara* (the river-bank between this world and the next) where children stack stones — a task perpetually interrupted by demons. Jizō gathers the children into his robes, protecting them and guiding them toward rebirth. The red bibs and caps on Jizō statues are offerings from parents who have lost children.
4. **The six realms guardian**: Kṣitigarbha is the bodhisattva of all six realms of rebirth (gods, demi-gods, humans, animals, hungry ghosts, hells) — he is present in each, working for liberation. His staff has six rings, representing his care for beings in all six realms.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[avalokitesvara]]** — *direct-influence*: Both are great compassion-bodhisattvas of East Asian Buddhism. Avalokiteśvara is the universal refuge; Kṣitigarbha is the specific hell-realm/liminal-death specialist. They are often paired in temples, and their compassion-functions are complementary: Guanyin/Avalokiteśvara saves you from the dangers of life; Dìzàng saves you from the dangers of death.
- **[[guanyin]]** — *direct-influence*: Guanyin (Avalokiteśvara in feminine Chinese form) and Dìzàng are the two most worshipped bodhisattvas in Chinese popular Buddhism. Temple imagery often depicts them together at the gates of Yama's court.
- **[[inanna-sumerian]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Inanna's descent into the Great Below — passing through seven gates, stripped of her divine attributes, ultimately resurrected — is among the oldest underworld-descent mythemes. Kṣitigarbha's deliberate, permanent descent into the hell realms out of compassionate will is the Buddhist instantiation of the same mytheme: the divine figure who willingly enters the realm of death as a redemptive act.
- **[[persephone-greek]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Persephone in the underworld as queen of the dead, and Jizō/Dìzàng as the guardian of the dead in hell realms, share the archetype of the compassionate-one-who-inhabits-the-underworld. Persephone's return (spring) and Jizō's vow to liberate all beings both project a future liberation from the underworld's finality.
- **[[jesus-christ-deity]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: The Christian doctrine of the Harrowing of Hell — Christ, between crucifixion and resurrection, descending into Sheol/Hades to liberate the righteous dead (1 Peter 3:19; Apostles' Creed "descended into hell") — is the closest structural Christian parallel to Kṣitigarbha's mission. Both are salvific beings who willingly enter the realm of death/punishment to liberate its inhabitants. The doctrinal parallel was noted in early Jesuit missionary encounters with Chinese Buddhism.
- **[[yan-wang]]** — *direct-influence*: In Chinese Buddhist-folk religious synthesis, Dìzàng and Yán Wáng (the Chinese King of Hell, cognate with Sanskrit Yama/[[yama-vedic]]) are intimately associated — Dìzàng intercedes in Yán Wáng's court. The two figures represent the poles of the death-realm: judgment (Yán Wáng) and mercy (Dìzàng).
- **[[hariti]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Hāritī the reformed child-protecting goddess and Jizō the guardian of dead children are both Buddhist figures specially associated with children's welfare, particularly children in danger or dead. Both represent the transformation of threat (Hāritī was a demoness; the death-realm is dangerous) into protection.
- **[[maitreya]]** — *direct-influence*: Both Kṣitigarbha and Maitreya are "future-oriented" compassion figures in Mahāyāna: Maitreya will come to liberate all beings at the end of a great cycle; Kṣitigarbha refuses Buddhahood until all are liberated now. They represent two temporal orientations of the same infinite-compassion vow.

## Iconography
Chinese/East Asian standard: a Buddhist monk in simple gray robes, shaved head (or wearing a crown of five Buddhas in more formal contexts); right hand holds a khakkhara (monk's walking staff with six rings that jingle to warn insects away, representing care for all beings in the six realms); left hand holds a wish-fulfilling jewel (*cintāmaṇi*, often depicted as a flaming pearl). He is usually depicted seated or standing, with a gentle contemplative expression.

Japanese Jizō: small stone figures (often 30–60 cm tall), simple rounded form; typically wearing a red *yodarekake* (bib) and red *boushi* (cap) placed there by worshippers; flanked by offerings. Groups of six Jizō (Roku Jizō) at temple entrances represent his guardianship in all six realms. Some Jizō hold children or have children carved around their feet.
