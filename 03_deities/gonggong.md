# Gonggong (Chinese flood-causing water deity)

## Identity
**Gonggong** (Chinese *共工*, *Gōnggōng*) is the Chinese mythological **water-deity whose actions cause the cosmic flood** that produces the Great Flood of the Yu-Gun tradition (see [[yu-the-great]]). The canonical narrative (Huainanzi 3, ~-139 BCE, with *Liezi* and *Shanhaijing* parallels):

> Gonggong, defeated in a contest for cosmic supremacy with **Zhuanxu** (the high god / sage-emperor), in his rage **butts Mount Buzhou** — the northwestern cosmic pillar that holds up the sky. The pillar is broken; the sky tilts and falls in the northwest, the earth-corners tilt in the southeast. Rivers and seas pour out from the broken cosmic order. **[[nuwa]]** (the creator-goddess) repairs the broken sky by melting colored stones; **Yu the Great** subsequently controls the flood-waters through engineering.

This makes Gonggong structurally **the Chinese counterpart of [[enlil]]** — the deity whose decision (or action) *causes* the catastrophic flood, in distinction from:

- **[[enki-ea]]** — the deity who *warns* the flood-hero in the Mesopotamian tradition.
- **[[yahweh]]** — the deity who *both* causes the flood and rescues Noah in the Hebrew tradition.
- **Zeus** — the deity who causes the Greek flood in the Deucalion narrative.

Gonggong's distinctive role in the Chinese flood-tradition is that he is the *cosmic-rebellious* cause — the flood is not a *judgment* (Mesopotamian/Hebrew) but a *cosmic-disorder side-effect* of Gonggong's rebellion against the heavenly order. This connects to the broader East Asian structural-typological signature of flood-as-cosmic-disorder-to-be-engineered rather than flood-as-divine-judgment.

## Earliest attestation

- ***Huainanzi* 3** (~-139 BCE, Liu An's Han-era syncretic encyclopedia) — the canonical philosophical-mythological narrative.
- ***Liezi* 5** (~3rd–4th c. CE redaction of older material) — the Gonggong-vs-Zhuanxu cosmic-contest narrative.
- ***Shanhaijing*** (Classic of Mountains and Seas, ~Warring States to Han period) — Gonggong's geographic-mythological associations.
- ***Lüshi Chunqiu*** (~-239 BCE) — additional mythological-philosophical attestation.

## Mythological role

Gonggong has three integrated mythological functions:

1. **Cosmic flood-causer** — his butting of Mount Buzhou breaks the cosmic order and produces the flood that necessitates [[yu-the-great]]'s engineering response.
2. **Cosmic rebel against heavenly order** — Gonggong's defeat by Zhuanxu (or in some versions Zhu Rong, the fire-god, or Yao) and his subsequent rage-attack on the cosmic pillar makes him the type of the *rebel-against-cosmic-order*. This is theologically resonant with the broader Chinese-philosophical concern for *order-vs-disorder* (Confucian and Daoist alike).
3. **Sky-falling etiology** — Gonggong's destruction of Mount Buzhou is the canonical Chinese etiology for *why the heavens are tilted to the northwest* (and rivers flow southeastward to the sea) — a cosmographical-mythological explanation.

## Cross-tradition identifications

- **[[enlil]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: structurally cognate as the deity whose action causes the flood. Lewis 2006 surveys the typological parallel; not a direct transmissive cognate.
- **Greek Typhon / Norse Loki / various cosmic-rebel figures** — *parallel-form*: structurally cognate cosmic-rebellious figures whose disorder produces cataclysm.

## Iconography

Variable across the surviving textual tradition:
- Sometimes anthropomorphic with red hair / fiery aspect.
- Sometimes serpent-bodied (human upper / serpent lower) — connecting to the broader Chinese water-and-serpent mythological complex.
- Sometimes accompanied by his nine-headed minister Xiangliu.

## Disputes

- **Pre-Han antiquity of the Gonggong narrative.** The *Huainanzi* preserves the canonical form; how much earlier the underlying mythology extends is debated. *Shanhaijing* and Warring-States bronze inscriptions suggest pre-Qin antiquity.
- **Cosmic-rebellion vs. cosmic-contest vs. flood-cause.** Different sources emphasize different aspects; the integrated narrative (rebellion → defeat → rage → mountain-butting → flood) is the canonical synthesis but may be a Han-period (Huainanzi) literary integration of originally independent mythemes.
