# Ninisina

## Identity
Ninisina ("Lady of Isin") is the Sumerian healing-goddess and city-goddess of Isin, capital of the Isin Dynasty (~-2017 to -1794). She is divine patron of physicians (*azu*) and of medical practice; her temple at Isin, the **Egalmah**, was the principal healing-cult center of the early second millennium and the source of much of the *asû* (physician) corpus that becomes the technical Akkadian medical literature of Mesopotamia. Through her son **Damu** — a dying-and-restored young god — she is also a mother of healing and a goddess of childbirth and midwifery (Black & Green 1992; Wiggermann 2000).

## Earliest attestation
- Early Dynastic god lists from Fara and Abu Salabikh (~-2500) name Nin-Isina among the regional pantheon.
- Royal hymns of the Isin dynasty (Išbi-Erra, Iddin-Dagan, Lipit-Ishtar) provide the principal extended cultic-literary attestation — see ETCSL 4.22 series.
- Excavations at Isin (German campaigns 1973 onward) have uncovered the Egalmah temple and a large dog-cemetery within the temple precinct, confirming the iconographic centrality of the dog as her sacred animal.

## Mythological role
Ninisina has no major narrative myth on the scale of Inanna or Enki; her cultic literature is hymnic-laudatory ("Ninisina A," etc.) and prayer-of-petition. Her central function is therapeutic intercession: she receives invocations from the sick and dispatches healing through her son Damu and through the human physicians (*azu*) who act in her name. The healing-prayer formula *Šūma kāšu uzāzu* ("If your name [is], I will not forget [it]") in *azu*-corpus texts shows her name's centrality to medical-ritual practice.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[gula-akkadian]]** — *ancient-identification*: by the Old Babylonian period the Sumerian Ninisina and the Akkadian/Babylonian Gula are explicitly identified; Gula is the continuation of the Ninisina cult under Akkadian-Babylonian patronage, and the Ninisina-Egalmah of Isin transfers its theology to the Gula-temples of Babylon, Nippur, and Borsippa (Black & Green 1992; Wiggermann 2000).
- **[[bau-sumerian]]** — *ancient-identification*: Bau of Lagash is a parallel healing-goddess, identified with Ninisina in syncretic god-lists; the two cults converge by the Old Babylonian period.
- **Ninkarrak** — *ancient-identification*: closely identified with Ninisina; some scholars treat Ninkarrak as a separate goddess merged into the Ninisina-Gula cluster (Wiggermann 2000).

## Iconography
The **dog** is her most distinctive emblem — dog burials and votive dog-figurines are massively over-represented at her sanctuaries at Isin and at later Gula-temples. She is sometimes shown with a lance or staff, and rarely with surgical-instrument iconography.

## Disputes
- **Identity / distinction from Gula and Ninkarrak.** Whether Ninisina, Gula, and Ninkarrak were originally three distinct goddesses later syncretized (Wiggermann's view) or one goddess under multiple regional names is debated. The current default is "three originally distinct, syncretized by the Old Babylonian period" (Wiggermann *RlA*).
- **Antiquity of the dog-symbolism.** Whether the dog is original to Ninisina or transferred from Gula or another healing cult is unsettled; the Isin temple dog-cemetery (Isin-Larsa period) is the earliest secure archaeological evidence.

## MASSIVE WIN: The Dog-Healer Connection

Ninisina's dog — shared with [[gula-akkadian]] and [[ninkarrak]] — reappears at [[asklepios]]'s healing sanctuaries in Greece 1,500 years later. At Epidauros, sacred dogs licked the wounds of petitioners as healing acts (recorded in the *iamata* inscriptions). Dog saliva contains lysozyme, immunoglobulin A, and histatins — genuine antibacterial and wound-healing agents. The ancient observation was pharmacologically accurate. Two civilizations, independently, embedded the same animal in the same healing-sanctuary role because it worked. See [[destroyer-healer]] for the full pattern.
