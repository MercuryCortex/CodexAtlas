# Anat

Anat (Ugaritic *ʿAnatu*) is the fierce warrior-virgin goddess of the Canaanite pantheon, sister and consort of [[baal-hadad]], daughter of [[el-canaanite]]. She is one of the most viscerally violent figures in ancient Near Eastern religion: in a famous passage from the [[phase-1-013-baal-cycle]] (KTU 1.3 II) she wades through human blood up to her thighs in slaughter, attaches severed heads to her belt and severed hands to her waist, and exults — a poetic intensity that has few rivals. She is also Baal's principal advocate and avenger: when Baal is killed by Mot, Anat tears Mot apart, threshes him with a sieve, burns him with fire, grinds him with a millstone, and scatters him as grain in the field (KTU 1.6 II) — the cosmological mythologization of the threshing of grain. Despite her epithet *btlt* ("the virgin / the maiden"), she has sexual relations with Baal in the Ugaritic texts (the famous *brk* passage of KTU 1.3 II); "virgin" here means "young woman of marriageable age" rather than "sexually inexperienced."

## Mythological role
Anat's roles in the Baal Cycle: she petitions El for Baal's palace; she avenges Baal's death by destroying Mot; she is Baal's perpetual ally in his combats. She is also independently martial: she massacres "the warriors" without apparent provocation in 1.3 II. The Egyptian Ramesside period (especially Ramesses II) imported Anat-worship from Canaan; she was given a royal sanctuary at Tanis and was depicted in Egyptian style. The Hebrew Bible attests an Israelite town named *Beth-Anat* ("House of Anat," Josh 19:38) and a judge named *Shamgar son of Anat* (Judg 3:31, 5:6) — toponymic and prosopographic evidence of Anat-cult in pre-exilic Israel. After the Iron Age her figure declines in West Semitic religion but persists in some forms (the Jewish Aramaic papyri from Elephantine in Egypt, ~5th century BCE, attest a Yahwist Jewish community worshipping Yahweh alongside Anat-Bethel and Anat-Yahu — an extraordinary attestation of Yahweh-Anat pairing in a Jewish diaspora community).
