# Babalu-Aye

## Identity
Babalu-Aye ("Father, Lord of the Earth" or "Father whose country is Earth") is the Yoruba orisha of infectious disease — particularly smallpox, measles, and skin diseases — and of the healing of those diseases. He is simultaneously the deity who sends illness and the one who withdraws it, making him one of the most theologically complex orishas: disease is not merely misfortune but divine communication, and Babalu-Aye is the translator.

He is depicted as an old man covered in straw (to conceal the pustule-covered skin of a smallpox victim), walking on crutches, attended by dogs. His iconography encodes suffering, marginalization, and divine proximity simultaneously.

## The deity of smallpox
Babalu-Aye's original Fon name, **Ṣọpọna** (in some traditions Ṣanpọna), is the direct name of the smallpox deity in West African tradition. Smallpox was historically one of the most terrifying diseases in West Africa, and the theological response — assigning it a specific deity whose favor could be sought and whose anger could be propitiated — represents a sophisticated theological processing of endemic disease into a manageable religious framework.

The deity is ambivalent by nature: he can send smallpox as punishment (for breaking taboos, for disrespect) but can also withdraw it, and he is the special protector of the poor and outcast — those whom disease strikes hardest. This dual function (punisher/protector) is characteristic of West African disease deities who must be approached with both fear and reverence.

## Diaspora and syncretic identification
The most important cross-tradition edge in the Babalu-Aye complex is his syncretic identification in **Cuba** with **Saint Lazarus** (*San Lázaro*). Two biblical Lazaruses feed this:
1. **The Lazarus of John 11** — raised from the dead by Jesus (resurrection motif)
2. **The Lazarus of Luke 16:20–21** — the poor man covered in sores who lies at the rich man's gate and is licked by dogs (disease, marginalization, and dogs — matching Babalu-Aye's iconography exactly)

This syncretism in the Cuban Lucumí tradition is one of the most precise and theologically resonant orisha-saint identifications in Santería: the dog-associated disease-deity and the sore-covered biblical beggar-saint fuse seamlessly. The annual pilgrimage to the Sanctuary of San Lázaro in El Rincón, Cuba (December 17) is one of the largest religious gatherings in Cuba, demonstrating the living power of this syncretic identification.

**In Brazil (Candomblé)**: Babalu-Aye is known as **Omolu** or **Obaluaiyê** — the same deity under different names, reflecting the different African regional traditions that arrived in Brazil.
