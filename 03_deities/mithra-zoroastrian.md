# Mithra (Zoroastrian)

## Identity
Mithra is the Avestan yazata of covenant, oath, and (in his developed form) the sun and warrior virtue. He is conspicuously absent from the Gathas of Zarathustra — the Gathic reform appears to have downplayed inherited Indo-Iranian deities — but is restored, magnified, and central in the Younger Avesta. The Mihr Yašt (Yašt 10) is one of the longest and oldest Avestan hymns, praising Mithra as the all-seeing guarantor of treaties, the swift charioteer of the rising sun, who breaks the bones of oath-breakers.

The Achaemenid kings Artaxerxes II (404–358 BCE) and Artaxerxes III invoke Mithra by name in their later royal inscriptions alongside Ahura Mazda and Anāhitā — a trinity that suggests Mithra's increasing prominence in the late Achaemenid imperial cult. From this Iranian Mithra two distinct downstream developments occur: (a) the continuing Mithra veneration in the Zoroastrian liturgy and the Mihragān festival (Phase 3 onward), and (b) the Roman mystery-cult of Mithras (1st–4th c. CE), which takes the name and a few iconographic elements (Phrygian cap, bull) but is substantially a Roman-period invention with limited Iranian continuity (Beck 2006). Vedic Mitra, Avestan Mithra, and Roman Mithras are *three separate nodes* in this vault, linked by syncretic edges with caveats.
