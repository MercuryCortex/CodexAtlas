# Mithra (Zoroastrian)

## Identity

Mithra (Avestan *Miθra*, Middle Persian *Mihr*) is the great Zoroastrian yazata of covenant, oath, sunlight, and warrior justice. He stands at the intersection of three major inheritance streams: (1) the **Proto-Indo-Iranian** deity \**Mitra* — the personification of contract and sworn agreement, shared with the Vedic Mitra; (2) the **Gathic reform's shadow** — conspicuously absent from the Gathas themselves, apparently downplayed by Zarathustra's concentration on Ahura Mazda; and (3) the **Younger Avestan restoration** — in the Mihr Yašt (Yašt 10), one of the oldest and longest Avestan hymns, Mithra is restored, magnified, and given an elaborate cosmic role.

## The Gathic Gap

The most discussed fact about Zoroastrian Mithra is his **absence from the Gathas**. Zarathustra's own hymns — the oldest stratum of the Avesta — do not name Mithra. The term *mithra* appears in the Gathas only as a common noun meaning "covenant" (Yasna 46.5). This absence is almost certainly deliberate: the Gathic reform concentrated divine power in Ahura Mazda and the Amesha Spentas (holy immortals), apparently setting aside the rich inherited pantheon of yazatas. The demotion of the *daevas* (see [[theme-vedic-avestan-split]]) was the negative side of this reform; the suppression or de-personification of Mithra was the positive-side casualty.

In the Younger Avesta, Mithra is restored with dramatic emphasis. The Mihr Yašt praises him as *having ten-thousand eyes and a thousand ears*, *riding white horses drawing his golden chariot*, *protector of the wide pastures*, *who never sleeps*, and *who breaks the limbs of the oath-breaker*. The restoration is complete: Mithra becomes second only to Ahura Mazda in practical Zoroastrian devotion.

## The Mihr Yasht: Primary Evidence

Yašt 10 (the Mihr Yašt) is the fullest surviving Avestan text dedicated to Mithra. Key attributes established there:

- **All-seeing quality** (*hazaŋra.gaośa*, thousand-eared; *baēuuarə.čašmana*, ten-thousand-eyed) — the cosmic witness who sees every oath sworn and every contract made; no oath can be hidden from him.
- **Solar warrior** — he rides his chariot before the sun, his golden armor blazing; associated with dawn light and the celestial east.
- **Xwaniθa** (the "Radiant One") — a solar title.
- **Lord of wide pastures** (*vouru.gaoyaoitiš*) — the covenants he guards include land-right and pastoral boundaries.
- **Enforcer against oath-breakers** — he sends his mace (*gurz*) against treaty-violators; war, disease, and misfortune follow the oath-breaker.

The combination of solar, covenantal, and warrior functions in a single deity has the closest parallel in Vedic Mitra — though the emphasis in each differs: Vedic Mitra is more abstract (sovereign of cosmic order alongside Varuna) and less militarized than Avestan Mithra.

## The Artaxerxes II Triadic Inscription

A striking development in late Achaemenid religion: **Artaxerxes II** (r. 404–358 BCE) and **Artaxerxes III** (r. 358–338 BCE) invoke not just Ahura Mazda but a *trinity* of Ahura Mazda, Mithra, and Anāhitā in their royal inscriptions. Earlier Achaemenid kings (Darius I, Xerxes) invoke Ahura Mazda alone or with the *fravaši* (souls) of the dead. The Artaxerxes innovation suggests:

1. Mithra's formal restoration to official royal religion after a period of Gathic-reform suppression
2. A Persian royal interest in a full-figured, militarized, solar patron alongside the abstract supreme deity
3. Possibly Babylonian or Near Eastern influence — both Marduk/Shamash (solar) and Ishtar/Inanna (Anāhitā) are cognate Babylonian figures

The triadic inscription (Artaxerxes II, Susa palace: *"By the favour of Ahura Mazda, Mithra, and Anahita this palace was built"*) is one of the few hard-dated (4th c. BCE) epigraphic attestations of Mithra as a named deity in official Persian religion.

## The Seven-Step Transmission Chain

Mithra is the deity with the widest documented transmission across the ancient and classical world:

1. **PII \**Mitra*** (c. 2000–1500 BCE steppe culture) — the covenant deity
2. **Vedic Mitra** (c. 1500–500 BCE) — the abstract sovereign of cosmic order, paired with Varuna; attested in the Rigveda
3. **Mitanni Mitra** (c. 1380 BCE) — named in the Hittite-Mitanni treaty (KBo I.3) as an oath-witness; confirms PII inheritance in Syria
4. **Avestan Mithra** (c. 1200–400 BCE) — restored in the Younger Avesta; the militarized solar covenant deity
5. **Achaemenid Mithra** (6th–4th c. BCE) — integrated into official royal religion under Artaxerxes II
6. **Roman Mithras** (1st–4th c. CE) — mystery cult of the Roman Empire; takes the name and some imagery but reconstructs the deity almost entirely; tauroctony (bull-slaying) has no Iranian precedent
7. **Afterlife Mithra** (Pahlavi literature, 3rd–9th c. CE) — one of the three judges at the Chinvat Bridge; guardian of covenants in the eschatological drama

This chain spans approximately 3,500 years and three continents — one of the most documented deity-transmission histories in the ancient world.

## Mithra as Chinvat Judge

In the fully developed Pahlavi afterlife geography (see [[theme-zoroastrian-afterlife-geography]] and [[phase-4-086-arda-viraf-namag]]), Mithra serves as one of the three judges at the Chinvat Bridge:

- **Mithra** — sees every oath, every covenant, every act performed under a sworn truth
- **Sraosha** — the messenger deity, witness to obedient righteous deeds
- **Rashnu** — holds the scales of cosmic justice

Mithra's role as judge-at-the-boundary is consistent with his covenantal domain — the one who enforces contracts in life becomes the one who adjudicates the ultimate contract (the soul's record of deeds) at death. This judicial function has structural parallels with the Egyptian Osiris-Thoth-Anubis judgment complex and may have influenced the angel Michael's psychopomp role in late Second Temple Jewish tradition.
