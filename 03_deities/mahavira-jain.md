# Mahāvīra

## Identity
Mahāvīra ("Great Hero," Sanskrit) — birth name **Vardhamana** — is the **24th and final Tirthankara** (Ford-Maker; literally "one who makes a ford across the ocean of existence") of the current Jain cosmic cycle, and the historical founder of Jainism as presently constituted. A Jina ("conqueror") — one who has conquered the passions and the karmic bondage that bind souls to the cycle of rebirth. Born c. 599 BCE (by Jain reckoning) into a kshatriya family in **Vaishali** (modern Bihar, northeastern India), he renounced household life at age 30, practiced extreme asceticism for **12.5 years** — including long periods of complete nudity, fasting, and silent wandering — attained **kevala-jnana** (omniscience, unlimited knowledge) at age 42 under a sala tree near Jrimbhikagrama, and died (attained *moksha*, liberation from rebirth) at age 72 in Pavapuri. His essential teaching: **ahimsa** (non-violence toward all living beings) as the supreme ethical principle, combined with the metaphysical framework of *anekantavada* (many-sidedness of truth) and *syadvada* (conditional predication). The Jain Agamas (canonical scriptures) preserve his teachings.

In the Jain cosmological system, Mahavira is not a creator-god and is explicitly not worshipped for worldly boons — he has attained liberation and has no ongoing causal interaction with the world. The Jain devotional practice (*puja*) directed at Mahavira and the other Tirthankaras is contemplative and aspirational: one meditates on the perfected being to cultivate the qualities that lead to one's own liberation.

## Earliest attestation
- **Acaranga Sutra** (first Anga of the Shvetambara Jain Agamas, c. 4th–3rd c. BCE): the earliest account of Mahavira's ascetic career; describes his practices in vivid, archaic Ardhamagadhi Prakrit.
- **Kalpa Sutra** (attributed to Bhadrabahu, c. 3rd c. BCE): canonical biography of Mahavira; lists the 24 Tirthankaras.
- **Pali Buddhist texts** (c. 3rd c. BCE onward): refer to Mahavira as "Nirgantha Nataputta" (the Nirgrantha from the Nata clan) — confirming historical contemporaneity and contact with the early Buddhist movement. [[phase-2-016-early-buddhist-suttas]] preserve these cross-references.
- **Western scholarly dating** places Mahavira c. 540–468 BCE, slightly later than the traditional Jain reckoning.

## Mythological role
In Jain cosmology, each cosmic cycle (a vast time-spiral) produces 24 Tirthankaras who re-discover and re-teach the eternal Jain dharma. Mahavira is the 24th and last of the current descending half-cycle (*avasarpini*) — meaning the current cosmic age has been getting progressively worse, and Mahavira's appearance marks the final great teaching before a long period without a Tirthankara. The previous Tirthankara was Parshva (the 23rd Tirthankara), said to have lived c. 877–777 BCE, whose four-fold order of monks, nuns, laymen, and laywomen Mahavira inherited and expanded into the five-fold vow system.

Mahavira's attainment of **kevala-jnana** (omniscience) under the sala tree is the pivotal event: at that moment he directly perceived all things in all times simultaneously — past, present, and future; all beings in all worlds. This omniscience is the foundation of his authority to teach. The doctrine of ahimsa that he articulated with maximal rigor — extending non-violence to microscopic organisms (nigodas), to plants, to the inadvertent harm caused by breathing — became the most rigorous non-violence ethic in world religious history.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[siddhartha-gautama-buddha]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: near-contemporaries (same century, same region of Bihar, same kshatriya social class, same renunciation-from-household framework, same critique of Vedic sacrifice and Brahmin authority), arriving at structurally different liberation paths — Jain metaphysical realism vs. Buddhist *anatman*; Jain extreme asceticism vs. Buddhist middle way. The Pali Buddhist texts' references to "Nirgantha Nataputta" confirm historical overlap. This is one of the great parallel-emergence events in religious history.
- **[[shiva]]** — *folk-syncretism* (partial): Mahavira's radical asceticism (*tapas*) parallels Shiva's role as the supreme ascetic in Hindu mythology. Some scholars note a Jain-Shaivite entanglement in the iconography of the naked meditating ascetic.
- **[[indra]]** — *ancient-identification* (Jain context): in Jain texts, Indra (re-imagined as a cosmic being rather than a Vedic warrior-god) celebrates Mahavira's birth and enlightenment — a Jain appropriation and subordination of the Brahmanical storm-god to acknowledge Mahavira's cosmic significance.
- **[[mahakala]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: both are "great" figures (maha) associated with time and liberation; both preside over a cosmos understood as cyclical and ultimately transcendable.

## Iconography
Mahavira's identifying emblem among the 24 Tirthankaras is the **lion** (simha). His canonical iconography: seated or standing in meditation (*kayotsarga*: standing upright, arms slightly away from the body, eyes downcast — the posture of complete renunciation), naked (in the Digambara tradition; Shvetambara tradition clothes him in white), with the halo of kevala-jnana. His physical perfections (the 32 marks of a great being) are described in detail in Jain canonical texts. The lion symbol appears on his throne and in his iconographic context.

## Disputes
- **Digambara vs. Shvetambara split.** The two major Jain sects disagree on key biographical facts: whether Mahavira was married (Shvetambara says yes, to Yashoda; Digambara says he was celibate from birth); whether women can attain liberation in this body (Shvetambara yes; Digambara no); and whether the fully authenticated Jain canonical scripture was lost. These disagreements go back at least 2,000 years.
- **Historical dating.** Western scholarship places Mahavira c. 540–468 BCE; Jain tradition dates him 599–527 BCE. The discrepancy matters for reconstructing his relationship to the historical Buddha.
- **Relationship to Parshva.** Whether the historical Mahavira was a reformer of an existing Parsva tradition or effectively the founder of a new movement is debated; the canonical texts present him as the continuation of an eternal tradition.
