# The Elect One / Son of Man (Enochic)

## Identity
**The Elect One** (also called the **Chosen One**, the **Righteous One**, and most distinctively the **Son of Man**) is a pre-existent heavenly figure who appears as the central messianic-eschatological character in the **Book of Parables** (also called Similitudes — 1 Enoch 37–71), the second of the five composite books of 1 Enoch. He is pre-existent (created before the world), enthroned beside the [[head-of-days]] (the Ancient-of-Days figure), and the appointed final-judge of the wicked and vindicator of the righteous at the end-time. This is one of the most theologically consequential figures in Second-Temple-Jewish apocalyptic literature — the **load-bearing antecedent of, or parallel to, the Gospel use of "Son of Man" for Jesus**.

The Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo canon, by retaining 1 Enoch as full scripture, retains the Enochic Son-of-Man in continuous canonical use — a theological position no other Christian tradition has held continuously.

## Earliest attestation
- **1 Enoch 37–71** — the Book of Parables, composed in Aramaic ~1st c. BCE to 1st c. CE.
- **Ge'ez Mashafa Henok** — the only complete textual witness; the Parables are *not* present in the Qumran Aramaic Enochic fragments (4Q201–4Q212), which was for a time taken as evidence of post-Christian (Christian-era Jewish) composition; current consensus (Nickelsburg-VanderKam 2012; Boccaccini 2007) accepts pre-Christian Jewish composition with the Parables' absence from Qumran as accident-of-preservation rather than evidence of late dating.

Key passages:
- **1 Enoch 46** — Enoch's vision of the Head of Days and "another, whose face had the appearance of a man" who will judge the kings and the mighty.
- **1 Enoch 48** — the naming of the Son of Man before creation: *"At that hour, that Son of Man was named in the presence of the Lord of Spirits, and his name before the Head of Days. Even before the sun and the constellations were created, before the stars of heaven were made, his name was named before the Lord of Spirits."*
- **1 Enoch 62** — the Son of Man on the throne of glory, judging kings.
- **1 Enoch 69–71** — culminates with Enoch *himself* being identified with the Son of Man in 71:14 (one of the most debated passages in apocalyptic literature).

## Mythological role
The Enochic Son of Man is theologically distinctive in three ways:

1. **Pre-existent.** The Elect One is *named* before creation (48:3). This is striking against the broader Jewish-monotheistic frame where pre-existence is typically reserved for divine Wisdom, the Torah, the Name, or other hypostatized attributes. The Elect One's pre-existence is a developed Second-Temple-Jewish christology — *prior to* Christian use.

2. **Enthroned beside the Head of Days.** The Elect One sits on a throne of glory (61:8; 62:5; 69:27) — a co-enthronement that scholars have read as transgressive of strict monotheism or as developmentally significant for *bi-throne* theologies (the Two Powers in Heaven debate; Boyarin 2004).

3. **Judge of the kings and the mighty.** The Elect One judges *political* enemies — kings, the mighty, the wealthy who oppressed the righteous (62:9; 63:1; 67:8). The political-eschatological dimension is foregrounded.

The figure's name **Son of Man** translates Ge'ez *walda sab'*, *walda eg'zaw*, *walda beʾsi* (three variants, sometimes within the same recension) — all roughly "son of a human" / "son of the male." The same Daniel 7:13 *kebar ʾenash* ("one like a son of man") background is operative.

## Cross-tradition identifications

- **[[jesus-of-nazareth]] (the Gospel Son of Man)** — *scholarly-parallel*, *load-bearing*. The relationship between the Enochic Son of Man and the Gospel Son of Man is one of the most consequential and contested questions in NT scholarship.
  - **Boyarin 2012** argues the Gospel use *presupposes* the Enochic tradition — the synoptic Jesus speaks of the Son of Man as a known apocalyptic figure his audience would recognize, not as a novel coinage.
  - **Vermes 1973** argued more conservatively for a generic-Aramaic *bar nasha* ("a human being / I") usage with Daniel 7:13 echoes but minimal Enochic dependence.
  - **Conservative NT scholarship** (Hooker, Bauckham) often argues for independent development from Daniel 7 alone.
  - The Boccaccini 2007 Enoch Seminar volume gathers the modern state-of-debate.

- **Daniel 7:13's "one like a son of man"** — *common-source*: both the Enochic and the Gospel uses draw on Daniel 7.

- **[[melchizedek]] (11Q13)** — *parallel-form*: the Qumran Melchizedek scroll (11QMelchizedek) presents Melchizedek as a heavenly-judge figure with structural parallels to the Enochic Son of Man.

- **Wisdom personifications** (Prov 8, Sir 24, Wisd. of Sol. 7) — *parallel-form*: pre-existent hypostatized agent of God.

- **Mandaean Manda d'Hayye** — *parallel-form*: heavenly knower-figure.

## Iconography
The Enochic Son of Man is not iconographically distinct in Ethiopian Orthodox art from the Christ-of-Christian-iconography (which has absorbed all Son-of-Man imagery). In contrast, scholarly reception-history of the Parables (post-Bruce 1773) sometimes visualizes the Enochic Son of Man as a distinctive figure separate from Jesus.

## Disputes

- **Date of the Parables.** Pre-Christian (1st c. BCE) vs. early-Christian-era Jewish (1st c. CE). Current consensus pre-Christian (Nickelsburg-VanderKam 2012; Boccaccini 2007); some hold for late-1st-c. CE composition (Suter; Knibb earlier). Absence from Qumran is no longer treated as decisive against pre-Christian dating.
- **Identification with Enoch himself (1 Enoch 71:14).** The final chapter has the angel address Enoch: *"You are the Son of Man who is born to righteousness."* Whether this is (a) original to the Parables, (b) a later redactional addition asserting Enoch's status, or (c) a scribal interpretation is contested. Nickelsburg-VanderKam 2012 surveys the positions.
- **Influence on Gospel Christology.** Boyarin 2012 maximal; Vermes 1973 minimal; current Enoch-Seminar consensus moderate (the Enochic background was available and contributory but not univocally determinative).
