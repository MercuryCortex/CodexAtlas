# Geb

## Identity
Geb is the Egyptian god of the earth, member of the Heliopolitan Ennead, son of Shu (Air) and Tefnut (Moisture), brother and consort of Nut (Sky), and father of the second generation — Osiris, Isis, Set, Nephthys. He is one of the unusual ancient near-eastern earth deities who is masculine (contrast Greek Gaia, Sumerian Ki — both feminine).

## Earliest attestation
- [[phase-1-002-pyramid-texts]] (Old Kingdom, 5th–6th dynasties, ~-2350 onward) — Geb is regularly named in the Heliopolitan cosmogony.
- [[phase-1-009-coffin-texts]] (Middle Kingdom) and [[phase-1-010-book-of-the-dead]] (New Kingdom onward) continue this.

## Mythological role
- **Earthly half of the Sky/Earth division.** In Egyptian cosmogony Geb is forcibly separated from Nut (his sister-consort) by their father Shu, who steps between them and lifts Nut into her arched-sky position. Standard iconography: Geb supine, Nut arched above, Shu standing between them, sometimes assisted by other gods raising Nut at her hands and feet.
- **King of the earth before Osiris.** In the divine succession Geb is the earth-king before passing rule to Osiris. The pharaoh sat on "the throne of Geb."
- **Father of Osiris.** This makes Geb the grandfather (via Osiris) of Horus, i.e., grandfather of all reigning pharaohs in their Horus-identification.

## Iconography
- Often reclining on his back, in green or with vegetation
- Goose hieroglyph (his name *gb* is written with the white-fronted goose)
- Sometimes phallic (the Geb-Nut separation)
- Occasionally wearing the Lower Egyptian red crown

## Geb and the political legitimacy of kingship

The theological grounding of pharaonic power runs through Geb with particular precision. Pharaohs are explicitly titled **"heir of Geb"** (*iwaw Geb*) in royal inscriptions — the earthly domain is the inheritance passed down from the earth-god. The chain of divine inheritance is: Geb (earth-king) → [[osiris]] (divine king, Geb's son) → [[horus]] (restored king, Osiris's son) → Pharaoh (Horus incarnate). Every pharaoh's claim to rule Egypt is a claim to Geb's inheritance, mediated through Osiris and Horus.

This inheritance chain is what makes Geb's role in the **Contendings of Horus and Set** so central. In that narrative (preserved in Papyrus Chester Beatty I, c. -1163 BCE), the divine tribunal adjudicating the kingship dispute between [[osiris]]'s son [[horus]] and [[set]] includes Geb as the sovereign grandfather whose authority is decisive. Geb's ruling is that [[horus]] is the legitimate heir — an act of juridical legitimation that grounds all subsequent pharaonic succession in Geb's earthly authority. The earth does not merely support the king physically; it authorizes him legally (Allen 2005, Pyramid Texts §§ 1–80 on Geb's throne-granting language).

The political theology encoded here is architecturally important: **earthly sovereignty derives from earth itself**. Compare the Hebrew concept of the land (*eretz*) as covenantally given — the earth as theological premise of political legitimacy, not merely its setting. The Egyptian version is older and more personalized, but the structural move is the same.

## MASSIVE WIN: Geb–Nut and the oldest documented Earth–Sky cosmic couple

The image of **Geb supine beneath [[nut]] arched above him** — the earth beneath the sky, separated by [[shu]] (air) between them — is among the **oldest visually documented cosmological images in world religion**. It appears in the [[phase-1-002-pyramid-texts]] and is rendered in thousands of Egyptian coffin-lid paintings, sarcophagi, and tomb ceiling decorations across 2,500 years. No other cosmological tradition produces an equally early, equally well-documented image of the earth-sky cosmic couple.

The pattern itself — **Earth and Sky as a primordial couple, separated at the moment of creation** — is a cross-tradition archetype of the first order:

- **Greek**: Gaia (earth, feminine) and Ouranos (sky, masculine) — separated violently when their son Kronos castrates Ouranos with an adamantine sickle, severing sky from earth. The Greek polarity is gender-inverted (earth = feminine; sky = masculine) relative to Egypt but structurally identical: primordial couple → forcible separation → ordered cosmos.
- **Hindu**: Dyaus Pita (sky-father, *div-* cognate with Latin *deus*) and Prithvi Mata (earth-mother) — the oldest attested Sanskrit pair, in the Rigveda (~1500–1200 BCE, Tier 1). The gender polarity matches Greek (sky = masculine) but diverges from Egyptian (Geb = earth = masculine; Nut = sky = feminine). The Egyptian system is the **exception** among these traditions — a masculine earth-god is rare in world religion.
- **Norse**: Ymir, the primordial giant whose slaughtered body becomes earth, sky, sea, and mountains (Prose Edda, 13th c. CE) — a variant where the cosmic division is achieved through sacrificial dismemberment rather than sexual separation.
- **Sumerian**: An (sky, masculine) and Ki (earth, feminine) — the Mesopotamian version closely parallels the Greek polarity.

The Egyptian Geb/Nut pair is **the oldest visually documented representative of this universal archetype**, and it inverts the most common gender assignment (earth-mother / sky-father). This inversion is itself a theological statement: the Egyptian masculine earth-god grounds the patrilineal royal inheritance, while the feminine sky-goddess is the cosmic womb through which the solar cycle of death and rebirth passes each night (Wilkinson 2003; Hornung 1982).

## Geb as judge of the gods

In the **Contendings of Horus and Set** (Papyrus Chester Beatty I, c. 20th Dynasty, ~-1163 BCE), Geb sits on the tribunal of gods adjudicating the eighty-year struggle between [[horus]] and [[set]] for the kingship of Egypt. His role is decisive: as the earth-sovereign and grandfather of both claimants (Geb is father of both Osiris/Horus-line and Set), his ruling carries the authority of the original earthly sovereign.

Geb's ruling in favor of [[horus]] resolves the longest dispute in Egyptian mythology. The tribunal scene is one of the rare moments in Egyptian religious literature where a god functions explicitly as a **judge dispensing juridical authority** — a structural precursor to later divine-judge imagery in Mesopotamian, Greek, and Hebrew traditions. Geb's role as earth-judge (his physical domain is the legal theater of human life) anticipates figures like the Greek Rhadamanthus and Minos (judges of the dead) and the Hebrew image of God as judge of earthly affairs, though no direct transmission is documented — this is a `parallel-motif` rather than `ancestor-of`.
