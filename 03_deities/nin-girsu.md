# Ningirsu

## Identity
Ningirsu ("Lord of Girsu") is the city god of Girsu and the principal warrior-storm-agricultural deity of the Lagash state. He is the son of [[enlil]] and [[nin-lil-sumerian]], consort of [[bau-sumerian]], and from the Ur III period is identified with [[ninurta]], the equivalent Nippur-centered deity — Ningirsu is the local Lagashite form, Ninurta the standard form. His emblem is the **Imdugud / Anzu-bird** — a lion-headed eagle whose representation on the silver vase of Entemena and on numerous Lagashite reliefs makes Ningirsu's iconography among the most distinctive of any third-millennium Mesopotamian god. The Eninnu ("House of Fifty") temple at Girsu, rebuilt by Gudea (~-2125) and celebrated in the two Gudea Cylinders ([[phase-1-018-gudea-cylinders]]), is the longest extant Sumerian-language description of a temple-construction project.

## Earliest attestation
- The Stele of the Vultures (~-2450), commissioned by Eanatum of Lagash, depicts Ningirsu in giant scale holding the Anzu-bird mace and crushing Lagash's enemies in a great net — among the earliest narrative reliefs in Mesopotamian art.
- Early Dynastic Lagashite royal inscriptions from Ur-Nanshe (~-2500) name Ningirsu as their principal divine patron.
- The Reforms of Urukagina (~-2350) place Ningirsu (with Bau) as the divine authority behind social-justice reform.
- The Gudea Cylinders (~-2125) — the principal source — provide an extended theological description: his weapons, his epithets, his dream-appearance to Gudea, his temple's cosmic significance.

## Mythological role
- *The Building of Ningirsu's Temple* (Gudea Cylinders A and B): Ningirsu appears to Gudea in an enigmatic dream demanding a new temple; Gudea travels to [[nanshe-sumerian]] for dream-interpretation, then carries out an elaborate three-year construction project celebrated in ritual detail. The text is the central source for Ningirsu's theological profile.
- *Lugale* (Ninurta and the Asakku) and *Anzu*: in his Ninurta-identified form, the warrior-god defeats the Asakku-demon and recovers the Tablet of Destinies from the Anzu-bird who has stolen it. These two myths are the great Ninurta-warrior narratives; their setting is Nippur but the figure is functionally identical to Ningirsu.
- His agricultural role is celebrated in *The Farmer's Almanac* and in agricultural-festival hymns; he is the divine plowman whose seasonal return brings the floods of the Tigris.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[ninurta]]** — *ancient-identification*: the syncretism is complete by the Old Babylonian period. Ningirsu's exploits become Ninurta's; Eninnu is treated as a "Lagashite branch" of the Nippur Ekur. In the Sumerian King List ([[phase-1-015-sumerian-king-list]]) Lagash is conspicuously omitted from the standard recension despite being one of the great Sumerian city-states — possibly because the Nippur scribal tradition that produced the King List had subsumed Lagashite Ningirsu into Ninurta.

## Iconography
- The Anzu-bird (Imdugud) — a lion-headed eagle — is his unique emblem, appearing on Lagashite reliefs, votive plaques, the Silver Vase of Entemena, and numerous seals.
- Lion-headed mace.
- The plow, sometimes shown in agricultural-festival contexts.
- In statuary, the goddess Bau is shown beside him; in the Gudea statues, Gudea kneels before Ningirsu's emblem.

## Disputes
- **Ningirsu and Ninurta — same god or syncretized?**. Current consensus treats them as the same deity at two cultic sites by the Ur III period at the latest; earlier (Early Dynastic) the relationship is debated — Lagashite Ningirsu may have been an originally independent local storm-warrior who was identified with Nippur's Ninurta in the third-millennium pan-Sumerian unification.
- **The omission of Lagash from the Sumerian King List**. Whether deliberate (Nippur scribal bias) or accidental (Lagash absorbed under the Ninurta cult) is debated.
