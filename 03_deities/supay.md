# Supay

## Identity
Supay is the Inca and Andean deity of death, lord of *Ukhu Pacha* (the inner/lower world, one of the three Andean cosmic levels alongside *Kay Pacha*, the present world, and *Hanan Pacha*, the upper world). In pre-conquest Andean religion, Supay was the ruler of the realm of the dead — a necessary and feared power, not simply an evil figure. The encounter with Supay was inevitable: all the dead passed into his domain. However, the Spanish colonial mission project systematically identified Supay with the Christian Devil, transforming him from a neutral underworld-lord into the embodiment of evil. This conflation is one of the most consequential acts of religious colonization in Andean history: the Quechua word *supay* still means "devil" today, the pre-Christian figure nearly erased beneath the colonial overlay.

## Earliest attestation
Pre-Inca Andean tradition; Inca-period sources record him in the context of the three-world cosmology (Hanan Pacha / Kay Pacha / Ukhu Pacha). Colonial-era sources (Cobo, Molina, Polo de Ondegardo) both describe the original figure and show the early stages of the Devil-conflation. By the 18th century, the conflation was nearly complete in colonial Quechua usage.

## Mythological role
**Lord of Ukhu Pacha**: The Andean cosmos is structured vertically in three levels — the upper world (Hanan Pacha, realm of the celestial deities), the present world (Kay Pacha, the world of the living), and the inner/lower world (Ukhu Pacha, realm of the dead, the interior of the earth, the chthonic). Supay governs Ukhu Pacha. The dead descend to his realm; the living can encounter that realm at caves, springs, and other apertures in the earth.

**Death cult**: The Inca practice of ancestor veneration (*malquis* — mummified royal ancestors who were consulted and fed offerings) was conducted in relation to the realm Supay governed. The recently dead and the malquis occupied a liminal position between Kay Pacha and Ukhu Pacha.

**Supay-cona (demon-minions)**: Colonial sources describe a class of beings called *supay-cona* (plural of supay) — supernatural entities or spirits associated with death and harm. These were refracted through the colonial Devil framework into "demons."

## Cross-tradition MASSIVE WIN: the colonial Devil-substitution
The Spanish mission strategy of identifying Supay with the Christian Devil is a textbook case of **interpretatio christiana applied as colonial tool**:

| Pre-conquest | Post-conquest |
|---|---|
| Supay = necessary underworld-lord | Supay = the Devil / embodiment of evil |
| Andean religion = complex three-world system | Andean religion = devil-worship |
| *Supay* (Quechua word) = lord of Ukhu Pacha | *Supay* (Quechua word) = devil, demon |
| Offerings to Supay = proper death ritual | Offerings to Supay = diabolism |

This transformation parallels what happened to the Norse Hel, the Greek Hades, the Aztec Mictlantecuhtli — neutral underworld-deities reframed as evil by Christian missionaries. But the Andean case is unusually well-documented because Quechua remained a living language and the colonial sources are extensive.

**The tío (uncle) cult**: In Bolivian tin and silver mining communities, the figure of the *tío* ("uncle") — a clay or rock figure of a horned devil inside the mines — receives offerings (coca leaves, cigarettes, alcohol) before miners descend. The tío is simultaneously Supay, the Christian Devil, and the spirit of the mountain/mine. This living syncretic cult is the direct descendant of the colonial conflation and is documented by Taussig (1980) and Nash (1979).

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **Satan / the Christian Devil** — *folk-syncretism*: The dominant colonial overlay; see above.
- **[[mictlantecuhtli]]** — *structural-parallel*: Aztec lord of the underworld, parallel function in Mesoamerica.
- **Hades** — *structural-parallel*: Greek underworld-lord, same neutral death-ruler function before Christian reframing; parallel trajectory of demonization.

## Iconography
Pre-conquest iconography of Supay is poorly preserved. Post-conquest depictions follow the Christian Devil tradition (horns, darkness). The *tío* figures in Bolivian mines are the most vivid living iconographic tradition — rough clay figures with horned heads, receiving coca and alcohol offerings.
