# Tehom

## Identity

Tehom (Hebrew **תְּהוֹם**, *tĕhôm*, "the deep") is the dark, formless primordial water of the Hebrew creation account — the abyss over whose face darkness lies and over whose waters the divine wind moves before the first act of creation (Genesis 1:2). It is not a god in the personified sense and has no cult, no iconography, and no genealogy: in the Priestly account it is an inert cosmological substrate, the unformed deep that God orders into a habitable world by separation rather than by combat. The word occurs some thirty-six times in the Hebrew Bible. In the Atlas, Tehom is placed among the primordial entities alongside [[nun]], [[tiamat]], [[ginnungagap]], and [[chaos-primordial]] because it names the same pre-creation deep — the Hebrew occupant of a slot filled, under different names, across the cosmogonies of the ancient world.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Tehom's most famous appearance is the second verse of the Bible: "and darkness was upon the face of the deep (*tĕhôm*); and the Spirit of God moved upon the face of the waters" (Gen 1:2, KJV). Strikingly, the word carries **no definite article** here — Hebrew would normally write "*the* deep," but *tĕhôm* stands bare, almost as a proper name. This grammatical detail is the single most-discussed feature of the term (see Disputes).

Beyond creation, tehom is the engine of the **Flood**: "all the fountains of the great deep (*tĕhôm rabbâ*) were broken up, and the windows of heaven were opened" (Gen 7:11) — and when the waters recede, "the fountains also of the deep ... were stopped" (Gen 8:2). Here the deep is a subterranean reservoir of chaotic water held back by the order of creation; the Flood is, in effect, a momentary un-creation, the deep allowed to return. The word recurs in the poetry of blessing (Gen 49:25; Deut 33:13 — "the deep that coucheth beneath"), in the Psalms (Ps 104:6), in wisdom (Prov 8:24, where Wisdom is brought forth "when there were no depths"), and in the prophets and Jonah (Jonah 2:5, "the deep closed me round about"). This twin function — the cosmogonic deep of Genesis 1 links forward to the Flood deep of Genesis 7–8 — makes tehom a hinge between the creation and the flood clusters.

## Cross-tradition significance (the Hebrew "deep")

Tehom is the **Hebrew endpoint of the flagship "the deep" cluster**: the primordial deep over which creation begins, named *tĕhôm* in Israel, [[tiamat|Tiamat]] in Babylon, [[nun|Nun]] in Egypt, and [[ginnungagap|Ginnungagap]] in the Norse world. The connection to Mesopotamia is the most secure: Hebrew *tĕhôm* and Akkadian *Tiāmat* are **cognate** Semitic words, both descended from a common root *tihām-*. Since Hermann Gunkel's *Schöpfung und Chaos* (1895), this cognate has been the most-documented case for Mesopotamian influence on Hebrew cosmology — Gunkel read Genesis 1 as a deliberate **demythologisation** of the Enuma Elish, in which the living salt-sea goddess Tiamat is reduced to an inert, de-deified deep that God orders without a fight.

The same primordial-deep role is filled independently in cosmogony after cosmogony: Egyptian Nun (the infinite dark waters from which Atum rises), Sumerian [[nammu|Nammu]] (the sea-mother upstream of Tiamat), the Greek Khaos of Hesiod, and the Norse Ginnungagap. What is distinctive about the Hebrew handling is the **demythologising move**: where Babylon dramatises a combat and Egypt an emergence, the Priestly writer strips the deep of personality and divinity entirely. Yet the older combat-myth survives elsewhere in the canon — in the poetic and prophetic texts where YHWH splits the [[yam|Sea]] and crushes the dragon [[leviathan|Leviathan]]/Rahab (Ps 74:13–14; Isa 51:9–10; Job 26:12) — so that the Hebrew Bible preserves both the demythologised deep of Genesis 1 and the living [[chaoskampf|Chaoskampf]] it was meant to replace.

## Disputes

Whether tehom proves **literary dependence** on the Enuma Elish, or merely a **shared Semitic vocabulary**, has been debated since Gunkel. The linguistic cognate (*tĕhôm* ~ *Tiāmat*) is not itself in doubt; what is contested is what follows from it. The decisive philological objection is that *tĕhôm* cannot be a direct **loan** from *Tiāmat* — a borrowing would have produced Hebrew *tĕʾōmā* or similar, not *tĕhôm*; both words instead descend from the common Northwest-Semitic *tihām-* (compare Ugaritic *thm*, Arabic *tihāmah*). On this basis **David Toshio Tsumura** (*Creation and Destruction*, 2005) mounts the major dissent: tehom is a perfectly ordinary inherited Semitic word for the deep, Genesis 1 shows no trace of a combat (no *Chaoskampf*), and the article-less form reflects normal Hebrew usage for certain cosmographic terms rather than a fossilised proper name. The mainstream position (Day 1985; Wyatt 2005) holds that the older combat-cosmogony is genuinely present in the wider biblical corpus and that Genesis 1 deliberately suppresses it — leaving tehom as the demythologised trace. The honest state of the question: the **cognate is secure, the dependence is not**, and the Hebrew deep may owe as much to a shared ancient Near-Eastern inheritance as to any specific Babylonian text.
