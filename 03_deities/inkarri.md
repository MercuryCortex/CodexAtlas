# Inkarri

The **post-conquest Andean messianic figure** — the executed last Inca emperor whose **severed head** is buried/preserved in the earth and whose **body is regrowing** to reunite with the head, at which moment the Inca empire will be restored and Spanish colonial order undone.

The myth emerged from the **two historical Inca decapitations**:
1. **Atahualpa** (executed by garrotting by Pizarro at Cajamarca, 1533) — the conquest-ending execution
2. **Túpac Amaru I** (executed by beheading at Cuzco, 1572) — the resistance-ending execution of the last Sapa Inca holdout

Both decapitations fused in Andean folk memory into a single Inkarri figure whose head was preserved (variously buried at Cuzco, hidden in a mountain, kept by the Spanish king in Madrid) and whose body is slowly regrowing from the earth. When the regrowth completes, the Inca world will return.

**MASSIVE WIN: a 16th-century-emergent universal-eschatological-pattern.** The Inkarri myth is the Andean instance of the same returning-savior pattern documented elsewhere in the vault: Islamic [[muhammad-al-mahdi]], Christian Second Coming of [[jesus-christ-deity]], Buddhist [[maitreya]], Hindu [[kalki]], Zoroastrian [[saoshyant]]. **Inkarri is unique in being demonstrably post-conquest** — emerging within the 16th-17th centuries in direct response to the colonial trauma, not inherited from any pre-Columbian eschatology. This is the strongest available evidence that the **eschatological-returning-savior** pattern can emerge *de novo* from specific historical trauma — it is not necessarily an ancient inheritance — which has major implications for the cognitive-universal-versus-cultural-transmission debate in comparative religion.

The post-conquest emergence also encodes anti-colonial resistance directly into the mythological structure: the savior returns not at general world's end but at the **end of colonial subordination**. The Túpac Amaru II rebellion (1780-1783) explicitly invoked Inkarri eschatology; the modern Peruvian *Sendero Luminoso* uprising (1980s-2000s) also drew on Inkarri framing in its early recruitment in Ayacucho.
