# Heracles

## Identity
Heracles (Greek *Ἡρακλῆς*, "glory of Hera" — ironically, since the goddess persecutes him throughout his life) is the **single most famous hero of the Greek tradition** and an instructive **boundary-case in Greek divinity**: mortal by birth (son of [[zeus]] and the mortal Alcmene), he completes the **Twelve Labors** assigned by his cousin Eurystheus, is murdered by his wife Deianira's accidentally-poisoned shirt, burned alive on Mount Oeta, and **deified** on his funeral pyre, ascending to Olympus to marry Hebe (the personified Youth) and join the company of the gods. His cult is therefore peculiar: he received both **heroic worship** (chthonic sacrifices to the dead-hero) and **divine worship** (Olympian-type sacrifices to the immortal god). For the Codex Atlas's mission Heracles is doubly load-bearing: (1) [[herodotus]] *Histories* 2.43–44 identifies the Greek Heracles with an Egyptian god (most plausibly [[khonsu]]); (2) Heracles is syncretized with the Phoenician Melkart from earliest Greek-Phoenician contact, generating one of the longest-running Mediterranean theological identifications.

## Earliest attestation
- **Mycenaean Linear B**: no clear attestation, but proto-Heracles-narrative material is reconstructable.
- **Homer** *Iliad* 5.392, 11.690, 14.323–324, 15.640, 18.117, 19.95–133, *Odyssey* 8.224, 11.601–626 — Heracles is named as Zeus's mortal son who was deified.
- **Hesiod** *Theogony* 950 — Heracles married to Hebe on Olympus after apotheosis.
- **Stesichorus** *Geryoneis* (c. -570) — extended treatment of one labor.
- **Pisander of Camirus**, *Heracleia* (c. -600) — the earliest canonical list of the Twelve Labors (lost; reconstructable from Apollodorus).
- **Pindar** Olympian, Nemean, and Isthmian odes — extensive Heracles content.
- **Pan-Hellenic cult**: Olympic Games claim Heraclean founding (recorded by Pindar); cult-centers at Thebes (his birthplace), Argos, Marathon (the Heracleion).
- **Phoenician syncretism**: the Heracles temple at Tyre is in fact a Melkart temple; the Heracles temple at Gadir (Cadiz, founded ~-1100 by Phoenicians) is the westernmost — both are pre-Greek but receive Heracles identification.

## Mythological role
The Twelve Labors (canonical Apollodoran list): Nemean Lion, Lernaean Hydra, Ceryneian Hind, Erymanthian Boar, Augean Stables, Stymphalian Birds, Cretan Bull, Mares of Diomedes, Belt of Hippolyta, Cattle of Geryon, Apples of the Hesperides, Cerberus from Hades. These labors take him to the boundaries of the known world (Hesperides in the far west; Cerberus from Hades; Geryon's cattle at the world-edge), make him a **boundary-crossing hero par excellence**.

Beyond the labors: countless lateral exploits (sacking Troy a generation before Agamemnon; killing Antaeus the giant; supporting Olympus on his back while Atlas fetched the apples; founding cities; siring 50 sons on the daughters of Thespius in a single night), then his death (poisoned shirt of Deianira, sent in jealousy under false claim of love-philtre), apotheosis on the Oetan pyre, and Olympian marriage.

His **apotheosis** is the **paradigmatic Greek case of mortal-to-god transition**: it is invoked in the Hellenistic ruler-cult justifications, in late-antique pagan theological apologetics against Christian resurrection claims, and in modern history-of-religions discussions of the cross-cultural category of *dying-and-rising hero-god*.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **Melkart (Phoenician)** — *ancient-identification* + *syncretic-fusion*: the Tyrian Melkart (Baal-of-Tyre) is identified with Heracles from earliest Greek-Phoenician contact. The Heracles temple at Gadir / Cadiz (founded ~-1100) is a Melkart temple in Greek dress. Phoenician-Greek Mediterranean trade-network theology depends substantially on this identification (Bonnet 1988).
- **[[khonsu]] (Egyptian)** — *ancient-identification*: [[herodotus]] *Histories* 2.43–44 reports an Egyptian god dated 17,000 years before Amasis whom the Egyptians identify as Heracles; most modern scholars identify this as Khonsu of Thebes (Lloyd 1976).
- **[[hercules]]** — *ancient-identification*: Roman cult-twin, institutionally absorbed via Magna Graecia.
- **Sandan / Sandon (Cilician)** — *ancient-identification*: the Tarsus Sandan-cult is identified with Heracles in Hellenistic-Roman period; Sandan's annual cult-fire ceremony at Tarsus is the local *imitatio* of Heracles's Oetan pyre apotheosis.
- **Vedic Indra** — *scholarly-parallel* (cautious): both are dragon-slaying / monster-slaying strong-warrior gods with cattle-rustling labor-narratives; the Indo-European parallel is suggested by Watkins 1995 *How to Kill a Dragon* but is structural rather than genealogical.
- **Christian Christ as boundary-crossing mortal-to-divine figure** — *scholarly-parallel* (cautious; debated): the Heracles apotheosis was used in pagan polemics against Christian resurrection (Celsus, Porphyry) as evidence that the pattern is not novel; Christian apologists (Justin, Origen) responded both by rejecting and (selectively) appropriating Heraclean motifs.

## Iconography
- **Lion-skin** (the Nemean Lion's hide draped over head and back) — the signature attribute
- **Club** (olive-wood, traditionally self-made)
- **Bow and arrows**
- Mature bearded male, muscular athletic build
- In Hellenistic-Roman art, often shown at rest after labor — the famous "Farnese Hercules" type (Lysippus 4th c. BCE, surviving in Roman copies)
- The Twelve Labors appear in standard sequence on the metopes of the Temple of Zeus at Olympia (~-460) — a canonical visual program

## Disputes
- **Mortal-deified or always-divine?** The classical view treats Heracles as mortal-by-birth, deified-at-death (so hero-cult before apotheosis, Olympian-cult after). Some scholarship treats this as an artificial harmonization of two distinct cult-traditions (a chthonic Heracles-hero and an Olympian Heracles-god) that were partly merged.
- **Indo-European versus borrowed origin.** Whether Heracles is fundamentally an Indo-European hero (with Indra-style structural parallels) or a Greek absorption of Phoenician Melkart material is debated. Burkert 1985, Bonnet 1988, and West 1997 all give weight to the Phoenician-influence position; pure Indo-European reconstruction has lost favor.
- **Heracles-Christ polemical use.** The 2nd–3rd c. pagan-Christian polemical exchange over apotheosis vs. resurrection is a genuine and important historical phenomenon, but the *theological depth* of the parallel was overstated in 19th–early-20th-c. comparative religion (Frazer-tradition) and is treated more cautiously by current scholarship.
