# Tlaloc

## Identity
Pan-Mesoamerican rain-storm god, attested in central-Mexican religion under the name Tlaloc from at least the Teotihuacan period (~150–550 CE; Teotihuacan's so-called "Tlaloc" iconography is well-attested, though scholars caution that the name is reconstructed). Tlaloc governs rain, thunder, lightning, agricultural fertility, and mountains — clouds gather on the peaks, where Tlaloc's house *Tlalocan* is located, and from which rain descends. Tlalocan is also a paradise — the dead by drowning, lightning-strike, or certain waterborne diseases go to Tlalocan rather than Mictlan.

## Earliest attestation
Teotihuacan iconography (~150 CE onward); continuous attestation through Toltec, Aztec periods; pre-Aztec antecedents (the "Tláloc" complex) clearly span more than 1500 years.

## Mythological role
Tlaloc presides over the Third Sun in the Mexica five-suns cosmology (a sun destroyed by fire-rain); he is responsible for the proper rain-cycle that makes agriculture possible. His four-quadrant assistants (the *tlaloque*) carry pots of water that pour out as rain; lightning is the breaking of the pots. He shared the top of Tenochtitlan's Templo Mayor with Huitzilopochtli — the two shrines representing the agricultural-rain and warrior-state poles of Mexica state religion.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[chac]] (Maya)** — *scholarly-parallel*: structurally identical Maya rain-god; the two are clearly the same pan-Mesoamerican concept under different linguistic-cultural forms.
- **Cocijo (Zapotec)** — *scholarly-parallel*: Oaxacan rain-god in the same pan-Mesoamerican pattern.

## Iconography
The distinctive "Tlaloc mask": large goggle-eyes (often interpreted as snake-coils framing the eyes), prominent fangs / teeth, sometimes a curling upper-lip "moustache." Blue coloration; rain symbols (drops); lightning serpents.

## Temporal Depth — The Oldest Mesoamerican Deity

Tlaloc is the **most ancient major deity** in the Mesoamerican pantheon in terms of continuous attestation. The goggle-eyed mask-face ("Tlaloc face") appears in **Teotihuacan** iconography from at least 150 CE — predating the Aztec Mexica by more than 1,000 years. Teotihuacan's so-called "Storm God" or "Rain God" imagery (discussed with caution by Pasztory 1974 — she notes the Tlaloc name is retrospectively applied from Nahuatl sources) covers building facades, murals, and ceramic vessels throughout the Teotihuacan period (c. 150–550 CE). By the time the Mexica built their Templo Mayor, they were inheriting a rain-god theology approximately 1,500 years old.

This temporal depth makes Tlaloc theologically important as evidence that the Mesoamerican rain-god concept is not a product of Aztec state religion but of the deep agricultural-cosmological needs of sedentary maize-cultivating civilization in central Mexico. Tlaloc represents the earliest stratum of Mesoamerican religious thought that survived, with remarkably stable iconography (the goggle-eyes and fang-teeth persist through 1500 years), into the Mexica synthesis.

## Cosmic-Maintenance Role

At the Templo Mayor of Tenochtitlan, Tlaloc's shrine occupies the **north** face of the twin shrine at the pyramid's summit — directly opposite Huitzilopochtli's shrine on the south. The architectural pairing is the most precise expression of Aztec cosmological theology: the **Fifth Sun** (Huitzilopochtli's domain) requires blood-sacrifice to move, and the **earth's fertility** (Tlaloc's domain) requires water-sacrifice and child-sacrifice (*niños tlaloques*) to produce rain. Matos Moctezuma's excavation of the Tlaloc side of the Templo Mayor (1978–) revealed enormous quantities of offerings: aquatic animals (shells, coral, crocodiles), green stone (chalchihuites = water-stones = Tlaloc's precious material), and child sacrifices — children's tears being homologous to rain (Sahagún, Florentine Codex). The ritual logic: Tlaloc cries, therefore rain falls; the children's tears feed Tlaloc and make him cry.

This is another expression of the Aztec **cosmic engineering** theology (see [[huitzilopochtli]] for the full comparative analysis): sacrifice is not gift but mechanism — the rain will not fall unless the appropriate sacrificial technology is applied.

## Cross-Tradition Significance

Tlaloc's attributes and functions parallel the rain-storm god complex across world religion — but with important distinctions:

| Tradition | Rain God | Distinctive Feature |
|-----------|----------|-------------------|
| Aztec | Tlaloc | Goggle-eyes; mountain-caves as rain-source; Tlalocan paradise; children's tears as rain-mechanism |
| Maya | [[chac]] | Long nose; serpentine lightning; ax-god aspect |
| Zapotec | Cocijo | Combines Tlaloc and Cociyo features; clearly in same pan-Mesoamerican complex |
| Mesopotamian | Adad (Akkadian) / Ishkur (Sumerian) — no nodes yet | Thunder and rain; storm as divine warfare |
| Greek | [[zeus]] | Thunderbolt; rain as divine power |
| Vedic / Hindu | [[indra]] | Storm-warrior; rain-giver; slayer of Vritra (drought-demon) |
| Norse | [[thor]] | Thunder-hammer; storm; agriculture protector |

The pan-Mesoamerican Tlaloc/Chac/Cocijo complex is the clearest evidence for deep cultural continuity across pre-Columbian Mesoamerica — these different peoples shared a rain-god theology whose iconographic and functional features are too specific to be coincidental, representing either a common ancestor tradition or deep cultural contact.
