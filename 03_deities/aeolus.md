# Aeolus

## Identity

Aeolus (Greek **Αἴολος**, *Aiolos*), son of Hippotes, is the Greek master of the winds — and, in his oldest attestation, pointedly **not their god**. The Odyssey introduces him as "Aiolos Hippotades, dear to the deathless gods" (Od. 10.2): a mortal favorite to whom [[zeus]] has delegated the winds, making him their *tamiēs* — their "keeper," "steward," "dispenser" — "to still or to rouse whichever he will" (Od. 10.21-22). He lives on Aiolia, a **floating island** ringed by an unbroken wall of bronze beneath sheer cliff (Od. 10.3-4), feasting perpetually with his wife and his twelve children — six sons wedded to six daughters (Od. 10.5-12). His name is usually connected with the adjective *aiolos*, "nimble, quick-shifting, glancing" — apt for winds, though the connection may be folk-etymological (see Disputes).

Two figures must be kept distinct from him. First, the **Anemoi** — Boreas, Zephyros, Notos, Euros — who are the winds themselves as divine persons; Aeolus is not a wind but the winds' warden. Second, the **homonymous Aeolus son of Hellen**, eponymous ancestor of the Aeolian Greeks: ancient mythographers already distinguished (and laboriously re-connected) two or three Aeoluses (Diodorus Siculus 4.67; the sources are sorted in Gantz 1993). This node is the wind-keeper of Odyssey 10 and his later divinized reception.

## In the Odyssey (10.1-79)

The episode is a compact tragedy in miniature. Aeolus entertains [[odysseus]] and his crew for a full month, questioning him about Troy; at departure he gives Odysseus a bag made from the hide of a nine-year-old ox, in which he has **bound the ways of the blustering winds**, lashed shut in the hollow ship with a shining silver cord "so that not even a little breath might escape" — leaving only Zephyrus, the west wind, free to blow the ships home (Od. 10.19-26). For nine days and nights Odysseus holds the sheet himself; on the tenth, with Ithaca in sight — close enough to see men tending fires — he falls asleep. The crew, convinced the bag holds gold and silver from Troy, open it; the winds burst out and hurl the ships back across the sea to Aiolia (Od. 10.28-55). Aeolus's second reception is the theological hinge of the episode: he drives Odysseus from his door — "begone, most reproached of living men; it is not right for me to help or convey a man **hated by the blessed gods**" (Od. 10.72-75). The relapse itself is read as proof of divine enmity (the wrath of [[poseidon]]): no wind-magic, however perfect, can override the will of the gods — the delegated steward will not act against his principals.

The sealed, endogamous island family became tragic material in its own right: Euripides' lost *Aiolos* dramatized the incest of Aeolus's children Macareus and Canace (cf. Ovid, *Heroides* 11, which plays on the irony of a father who commands the winds but not his own anger).

## From steward to wind-king (later tradition)

From the classical period the mythic island was anchored in real geography: the volcanic **Aeolian (Lipari) Islands** north of Sicily were "the islands of Aiolos" already for Thucydides (3.88). Hellenistic rationalism made the keeper a wise king: Diodorus Siculus (5.7-8) presents a pious, just Aeolus who taught the use of sails and **forecast the winds from the fire-signs of the volcanoes** — the wind-lord euhemerized into a weather-wise ruler. The decisive transformation is Latin: in Vergil's *Aeneid* (1.50-86), **rex Aeolus** sits in a vast cavern where the struggling winds are imprisoned under a mountain, granted by Jupiter the power "to tighten and loosen their reins"; bribed by Juno with the nymph Deiopea, he drives his spear into the mountainside and looses the storm against Aeneas. This divinized cave-king — not Homer's genial island host — is the Aeolus that dominates later Western reception, from the "cave of the winds" of Renaissance art to the personified wind-god of opera and emblem books.

## Cross-tradition significance (the wind-master slot)

Aeolus is the **Greek member of the Atlas's wind-deity family** — [[stribog]], whose "grandsons" are the winds (Slavic); [[fujin]], the bag-carrying wind-kami (Japanese); [[vayu]], the Vedic wind-god and breath-lord — with the broader air-realm figures [[enlil]], [[shu]], and [[pazuzu]] adjacent. Within that family he fills a **distinct slot**: not the personified wind (in Greek that role belongs to the Anemoi) but the *controller* — the figure who binds, stills, and releases winds at will. The honest comparative statement is that **no transmission connects any of these figures to Aeolus**: there is no linguistic cognate (Aeolus's name is Greek, and the Greek share of the Indo-European wind-god inheritance runs through the Anemoi, not through him — West 2007 on the Vāyu/Vāta family), and the parallels are functional convergence on a role any seafaring or storm-visited culture needs to imagine.

The **bag of winds** deserves its own honesty. The image of winds bound in a container is a widespread folklore motif (Thompson, *Motif-Index*), alive in European maritime magic into the early modern period — Olaus Magnus (1555) describes northern wind-sellers who knotted winds into a rope for sailors, a bought-and-bound wind exactly parallel in logic to Aeolus's corded sack. The visual match with Fūjin's wind-bag, which the Fūjin node rightly calls an iconographic coincidence, should not be silently upgraded to contact: the best-**documented transmission argument** in this area — the Greek wind-god type with billowing wind-mantle travelling through Gandharan Greco-Buddhist art and the Kizil caves into East Asian wind-god iconography (Tanabe 2003) — concerns the *Anemoi/Boreas* mantle type, not Aeolus's ox-hide bag. Aeolus's own comparative value is precisely his **theological restraint**: Homer's Greece, unusually, imagines total wind-control as a *delegated, revocable stewardship* under Zeus rather than as the property of a great god — and the episode's point is that even perfect weather-magic bends to divine justice.

## Disputes

**God or mortal?** Homer never calls Aeolus *theos*: he is a man "dear to the deathless gods," holding the winds by Zeus's appointment, and his refusal scene turns on his deference to the gods' evident will (Heubeck & Hoekstra 1989 on Od. 10). The divine wind-king is a later development, canonized by Vergil. The Atlas types him **deity** for the comparative wind-family slot he completes (as with other divinized figures), while this node keeps the Homeric distinction explicit — he is the family's *keeper*, not its god.

**One Aeolus or three?** The wind-keeper son of Hippotes and the eponymous ancestor son of Hellen are distinct figures whom ancient genealogists (Diodorus 4.67 preserves one harmonization) welded into a single line; the scholiasts counted as many as three. Gantz (1993) treats the conflation as secondary — the Odyssey's wind-keeper is best taken as an independent figure of sailors' saga.

**Etymology.** The link with *aiolos* "quick-shifting" is ancient and attractive but not probative; the adjective may equally have generated the eponym-name independently, and no Indo-European wind-word stands behind the name. The absence of a cognate is itself the strongest argument that the cross-tradition parallels on this node are convergence, not inheritance.
