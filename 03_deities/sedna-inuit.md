# Sedna

## Identity

**Sedna** is the sea mother and mistress of all marine animals in Inuit tradition — the most important deity in the pan-Arctic Inuit religious complex. She lives at the bottom of the ocean, and all seals, walruses, whales, and fish either flow from her body (as her transformed finger joints) or are held in her matted hair. When humans violate taboos — mishandling game, mixing forbidden substances, failure of ritual respect — the animals are tangled in Sedna's hair and she withholds them, causing famine. The angakkuq (shaman) must then undertake a dangerous spirit journey to the sea floor, soothe Sedna, comb out the tangled animals from her hair, and release them back to the sea. She is simultaneously creation-source, punisher, and the pivot of the entire food economy.

Her name and attributes vary across Arctic regions: Arnakuagsak in Greenland; Nerrivik ("food dish") in the eastern Arctic; Sanna ("the one down there") on Baffin Island; Takannaaluk ("the terrible one down there") in some traditions. The Nunavut Inuit name Sedna has become the standard scholarly designator and the name of Sedna, the trans-Neptunian astronomical object, named in 2004 in her honor.

## Earliest attestation

- Sedna traditions belong to pan-Arctic Inuit oral culture extending back thousands of years; the spread of Inuit culture across the Arctic dates archaeologically to the Thule culture expansion (~1000 CE), though pre-Thule Dorset-culture antecedents suggest deeper roots.
- First extensive documentation in Western scholarship: Franz Boas, *The Central Eskimo* (Smithsonian BAE Annual Report 6, 1888) — the foundational ethnographic record, documenting the origin narrative and shamanic practices.
- The severed-fingers origin narrative is attested with consistent structural elements across Inuit groups from Alaska to Greenland — one of the most geographically stable indigenous American mythological complexes.

## Mythological role

**Origin narrative** (primary Baffin Island version): Sedna was a young woman who was either (a) abandoned on the ice by her father or a group of hunters, or (b) kidnapped and escaping, or (c) a woman who refused to marry and was thrown from a boat as punishment. As she grasped the side of the kayak, her fingers were chopped off, joint by joint. Each severed segment sank into the water and was transformed: the first joints became the smallest seals; the middle joints became the larger ringed and bearded seals; the final joints became walruses; in some versions further transformations produce whales, fish, and polar bears. Sedna herself sank to the ocean floor and became its ruler.

**Shamanic descent**: When marine animals are scarce (because Sedna is withholding them in her matted hair in punishment for human taboo violations), the angakkuq (shaman) enters trance and descends — spirit-traveling through the ice and down to the ocean floor. The descent is described as physically dangerous even in spirit form: the shaman must pass through obstacles (currents, barriers, the creatures guarding her). Reaching Sedna, the shaman must soothe her distress, comb her tangled hair (which she cannot do herself, having no fingers), and ask which taboos have been broken. The shaman then returns, reports the violations to the community, and the community confesses and undertakes purification. Only then do the animals return.

This is one of the most precisely documented shamanic descent-and-return complexes in world religion — structurally distinct from the purely initiatory underworld descent (Inanna, Persephone) in that it is **a repeated, necessary, socially integrated practice** rather than a one-time mythological event. The shaman functions as the intermediary between the community's food supply and the cosmic ledger of taboo violations Sedna enforces.

**Creation by dismemberment**: The origin of all Arctic marine species from Sedna's severed fingers is one of the clearest examples of the global creation-by-dismemberment motif — the body of a primordial being generating the diversity of living things.

## Cross-tradition identifications

- **[[mama-cocha]]** — *structural-parallel*: Andean sea-mother governing marine abundance; the two most prominent indigenous American sea-mother figures. Independent development in separated maritime traditions.
- **[[olokun]]** — *structural-parallel*: Yoruba deep-sea deity ruling the ocean depths, inaccessible to ordinary humans, requiring specialist intermediaries.
- **[[yemoja]]** — *structural-parallel*: Yoruba sea-mother governing marine life and the food of coastal communities.
- **[[tangaroa]]** — *structural-parallel*: Polynesian sea deity ruling ocean and marine life; sea-deity archetype in Pacific maritime tradition parallels Sedna in Arctic maritime tradition.
- **[[tiamat]]** — *structural-parallel* (creation-by-dismemberment): Tiamat's body becomes the cosmos; Sedna's fingers become the marine species. Female bodies dismembered as the source of created world.
- **[[osiris]]** — *structural-parallel* (dismemberment-as-generation): Osiris's scattered body generates agricultural abundance; Sedna's severed fingers generate marine abundance.
- **[[inanna-sumerian]]** — *structural-parallel* (shamanic descent structure): the angakkuq's descent to Sedna's ocean floor is structurally parallel to Inanna's descent to Ereshkigal's realm — both require a specialist to descend and return; both link the abundance of life on the surface to conditions below.
- **[[persephone-greek]]** — *structural-parallel* (descent-controls-abundance): as Persephone's presence below = winter (no crops) and return = spring (crops), the angakkuq's ability to reach Sedna and release animals = successful hunting season. Both mythologies encode the control of surface abundance through the condition of a figure below.
- **[[gitche-manitou]]** — *contextual*: the broader Algonquian-sphere concept of sacred pervasive power frames the Inuit Sedna complex; both traditions locate transformative power at non-anthropomorphic cosmic thresholds.

## Iconography

Sedna is depicted in Inuit art primarily through:
- A female figure without fingers or with truncated finger-stumps
- Surrounded by marine animals (seals, whales, fish) flowing from her hands or hair
- Tangled hair filled with animals — the visual emblem of withheld abundance
- Ocean-floor dwelling position
Contemporary Inuit artists including Kenojuak Ashevak and Pitseolak Ashoona have produced widely circulated prints of Sedna; she has become an iconic figure of Inuit cultural identity in contemporary art.

## Disputes

- **Origin narrative variants**: the narrative of how Sedna came to lose her fingers varies significantly across regional traditions — different perpetrators (father, husband, strangers), different motives, different moral registers. Some versions frame her as an innocent victim; others as a woman being punished for transgression; others as a being whose nature was always supernatural. Scholars debate whether to read the variation as regional divergence or as the normal variation of oral tradition.
- **One deity or many?** Some scholars treat the various regional names (Sedna, Arnakuagsak, Nerrivik, Sanna) as distinct deity figures united only by structural function; others argue they represent a single pan-Arctic figure with regional name variation. Most modern scholarship treats them as variants of a unified complex.
- **Gender and creation theory**: feminist scholars including Louise Profeit-LeBlanc have examined the creation-from-severed-female-body motif as encoding specific cultural logics about women's bodies as sources of both nourishment and danger — paralleling global analyses of the creation-from-feminine-dismemberment pattern.
