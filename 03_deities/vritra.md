# Vṛtra

## Identity
Vṛtra ("the obstructor," from the Sanskrit root *vṛ-*, "to cover, enclose") is the great dragon (*ahi*) of the Rig Veda, slain by Indra in the cosmogonic combat that releases the waters and establishes Indra's kingship over the gods. The principal Vedic narration is **Rig Veda 1.32** (the hymn *Indrasya nu vīryāṇi* — "The Manly Deeds of Indra"), which Calvert Watkins (1995) identified as the locus classicus for the inherited Indo-European poetic formula *gʷhen- ogʷhim* ("to slay the serpent"). The Vedic phrase *áhann áhim* ("he slew the dragon," 1.32.1) is the direct cognate of the Hittite *MUŠilluyankan kuenta* ("he slew the serpent") — the same formula, the same myth, in two Indo-European branches.

## Earliest attestation
- **Rig Veda 1.32** and additional Vṛtra-slaying hymns scattered across the family books (esp. 1.80, 1.85, 2.11, 4.18, 6.17, 8.6, 8.96; cf. Macdonell 1897:158–164 for the inventory).
- The Vedic compositions date to the second millennium BCE in oral form; the redacted Rig Veda corpus stabilizes ~-1200 to -1000.

## Mythological role
**The Vṛtra-slaying** (*Vṛtrahatya*). Vṛtra has enclosed the cosmic waters (or, in some readings, the cattle-light of the dawn — Macdonell 1897) within the mountains, leaving the world parched and lifeless. Indra, fortified by *soma*, smites Vṛtra with the *vajra* (the thunderbolt, fashioned for him by Tvaṣṭṛ from the bones of the sage Dadhīci), releasing the waters and inaugurating the cosmic order. Indra's epithet *Vṛtrahan* ("slayer of Vṛtra") becomes one of his principal titles — and the cognate Avestan epithet *Vərəθragna* (preserved as the name of an Iranian god) is the linguistic relic of the same myth in the Iranian branch (Macdonell 1897; Boyce 1975).

In the later **Mahābhārata** and **Purāṇic** traditions, Vṛtra is re-narrated as a Brahmin-born demon whose slaying causes Indra to incur Brahmin-killing sin; the myth is moralized and complicated.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[illuyanka]]** — *parallel-form / inherited PIE formula*: Vedic and Hittite cognates of the same Indo-European serpent-slaying formula (Watkins 1995).
- **[[typhon]]** — *parallel-form / inherited PIE formula*: Greek instance.
- **[[tiamat]]** — *parallel-form*: Mesopotamian chaos-serpent (not Indo-European; structural parallel within the wider Old World mythological pattern).
- **[[lotan]]** — *parallel-form*: Ugaritic-Northwest-Semitic instance.
- **[[apophis]]** — *parallel-form*: Egyptian (non-IE) instance.
- **Apaosha** — *ancient-identification* (Indo-Iranian close cognate): the Avestan drought-demon defeated by Tištrya is the closest Iranian cognate of Vṛtra (Macdonell 1897; Boyce 1975).

## Iconography
Vedic religion is largely aniconic; Vṛtra has no developed early iconography. Later Hindu illuminated manuscripts depict Indra-slaying-Vṛtra in conventional miniature-painting style.

## Disputes
- **Naturalistic vs. mythological interpretation.** 19th-century Vedic philology (Max Müller, Bergaigne) read the Vṛtra-Indra combat as a meteorological allegory (thunderstorm freeing the rain-waters from the cloud-mountain); the current consensus treats this as overly reductive — the myth is cosmogonic and political-theological, with Indra's victory establishing kingship over the gods. The naturalistic substrate may nonetheless be real (Jamison-Brereton 2014).
- **Vṛtra and Vala — one demon or two.** Some traditions (and some scholars) distinguish Vṛtra (the waters-obstructor) from Vala (the cave-obstructor of the dawn-cattle, defeated by Indra in cooperation with the Aṅgirases); others treat them as variants of the same chaos-figure. Macdonell 1897 treats them as related but distinct.
