# Ninkarrak

## Identity
Ninkarrak ("Lady of Karrak" — Sumerian *nin* + the toponym *karrak*) is a Sumerian-Akkadian **healing-goddess** of the city of Karrak (location uncertain, likely a settlement near Isin), member of the four-way Mesopotamian healing-goddess syncretic cluster comprising **[[ninisina]] (of Isin), [[gula-akkadian|Gula]] (the eventual Akkadian-Babylonian super-figure who absorbs the others), [[bau-sumerian|Bau]] (of Lagash), and Ninkarrak (of Karrak)**. By the Old Babylonian period (~-1800 onward) these four are explicitly identified in cultic compendia and god-lists; by the Neo-Babylonian period Ninkarrak is functionally Gula under a localized toponymic name. Her sacred animal is the **dog** — the canonical healing-goddess emblem shared with Ninisina and Gula, attested by votive dog-figurines and dog-burials at healing-cult sites. (Black & Green 1992; Krebernik 2001 *RlA*.)

Her most-cited single textual attestation is the **Code of Hammurabi epilogue**, where the curse-formula invokes Ninkarrak among the gods who may punish violators of the code — a Babylonian-imperial-period acknowledgment of her cultic importance (Roth 1995).

## Earliest attestation
- **Early Dynastic god-lists** (~-2500 onward) — Ninkarrak appears in regional god-lists alongside other healing-figures.
- **Code of Hammurabi epilogue** (~-1750) — curse-formula invoking Ninkarrak: "May Ninkarrak [...] inflict upon him a serious injury [...] without ever assuaging the pain." One of the principal first-millennium attestations.
- **Old Babylonian Isin-period cultic texts** — Ninkarrak attested as part of the Isin-Gula-Ninisina healing-cluster.
- **Neo-Babylonian period** (~-700 onward) — Ninkarrak progressively absorbed into Gula; the name persists in conservative cultic contexts.

## Mythological role
Like the other members of the Mesopotamian healing-goddess cluster, Ninkarrak has **no major narrative myth** on the scale of Inanna or Enki. Her role is **cultic-therapeutic**:

1. **Receiver of healing-prayers and -invocations** — the sick invoke Ninkarrak (alongside Ninisina, Gula, Bau) for cure.
2. **Patron of *azu* and *asû* (physicians)** — the human practitioners of Mesopotamian medicine act in her name.
3. **Invoker of disease as punishment** — the Hammurabi curse-formula shows the inverse: the healing-goddess can also inflict disease on those who deserve it. The Mesopotamian healing-goddesses are theologically capable of both cure and curse, consistent with the broader Mesopotamian understanding of illness as divinely-administered.

She is part of the four-way syncretic cluster:
- **[[ninisina]] of Isin** — Sumerian original; documented previously in this vault.
- **[[gula-akkadian|Gula]]** — Akkadian-Babylonian super-figure who eventually absorbs the others.
- **[[bau-sumerian|Bau]] of Lagash** — Sumerian parallel.
- **Ninkarrak of Karrak** — this entry.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[ninisina]]** — *ancient-identification*: by the Old Babylonian period the two are explicitly identified in cultic compendia. Some scholars (Krebernik 2001 *RlA*) argue Ninkarrak was originally distinct (a separate city-goddess of Karrak) merged into the Isin-Gula cluster; others (older view) treat Ninkarrak as a Ninisina-epithet from the start.
- **[[gula-akkadian|Gula]]** — *ancient-identification*: Gula absorbs the Ninkarrak cult in the first millennium; functionally identical by Neo-Babylonian period.
- **[[bau-sumerian|Bau]]** — *ancient-identification*: parallel healing-goddess; identified in cultic compendia.

## Iconography
- **Dog** — canonical healing-goddess sacred animal, shared with Ninisina and Gula; votive dog-figurines and dog-burials at Mesopotamian healing-cult sites (Isin Egalmah is the best-attested).
- Lance / staff in some seal attestations.
- Surgical / scalpel iconography (rare).

The iconography is largely indistinguishable from Ninisina's and Gula's — consistent with the syncretic cluster having converged on a shared visual vocabulary by the Old Babylonian period.

## Disputes
- **Original-distinct vs. always-an-epithet.** Krebernik 2001 *RlA* and Wiggermann 2000 *RlA* read Ninkarrak as originally a distinct city-goddess of Karrak (possibly near Isin) merged into the Isin-Gula cluster by the Old Babylonian period. Older scholarship sometimes treated Ninkarrak as a Ninisina-epithet from the start; mainstream now follows the originally-distinct view.
- **Location of Karrak.** The toponym *karrak* is not securely identified with any excavated site; mainstream guesses place it near Isin in the alluvial plain. The location-uncertainty is one reason for the historical interpretive difficulty.
- **Strength of the four-way syncretism.** The Old Babylonian-period syncretism of Ninisina-Gula-Bau-Ninkarrak is well-attested; whether all four were ever cleanly distinct deities or whether the syncretism reflects pre-existing functional overlap is debated.
