# Rahab

## Identity

Rahab (Hebrew **רַהַב**, *rahaḇ*, "boisterousness, arrogance, storm-surge") is the chaos-monster of the sea in the Hebrew Bible — the raging deep personified as a defeated cosmic enemy of YHWH. The name is built from a root meaning "to storm, to bluster, to behave arrogantly," and the creature is in effect *the Blusterer*: the surging, insolent sea that the creator-god subdued at the establishment of cosmic order. Rahab has no cult, no genealogy, and no anthropomorphic iconography; it survives only in the poetic and prophetic registers — the Psalms, Job, and Deutero-Isaiah — as a named trophy of divine victory. In the Atlas it is placed among the chaos-beings alongside [[leviathan]], [[lotan]], [[yam]], and [[tiamat]] (and the biblical sea-dragon *Tannin*), because it names the same primordial sea-chaos slot under a distinctively Israelite by-name.

Two cautions are built into the entry. First — **the homonym trap**: this Rahab is *not* Rahab of Jericho, the woman who shelters the spies in Joshua 2 and reappears in the genealogy of Matthew 1:5. The two are unrelated words spelled identically in unpointed Hebrew (the woman's name is conventionally vocalised *Rāḥāḇ*, רָחָב, with the guttural *ḥêṯ*; the monster is *Rahaḇ*, רַהַב, with *hê*) and belong to entirely different domains. Second — **the cognate caution**: unlike Leviathan, Rahab has no secure Ugaritic or Babylonian antecedent (see Disputes), so its place in the cross-tradition combat-grid rests on the shared *myth* and the convergent *type*, not on a name-for-name etymology.

## In the Hebrew Bible

Rahab is named in a small, tightly thematic cluster of texts, all poetic:

- **Psalm 89:10[11]** — *"You crushed Rahab like one who is slain (*ke-ḥālāl*); with your mighty arm you scattered your enemies."* Set within a hymn to YHWH's kingship over the raging sea (vv. 9–10), this is the clearest statement of Rahab as the chaos-power defeated at creation, "crushed like a carcass."
- **Isaiah 51:9** — *"Awake, awake, put on strength, O arm of YHWH … Was it not you who cut Rahab in pieces (*hammaḥṣeḇet rahaḇ*), who pierced the dragon (*məḥôleleṯ tannîn*)?"* Here Rahab stands in synonymous parallelism with **Tannin**, the sea-dragon, and the passage immediately invokes the drying of "the great deep" (*tĕhôm rabbâ*) — fusing the primeval combat with the Exodus sea-crossing (see below).
- **Job 26:12–13** — *"By his power he stilled the Sea (*yām*); by his understanding he struck down Rahab. By his wind the heavens were made fair; his hand pierced the fleeing serpent (*nāḥāš bāriaḥ*)."* Rahab is paralleled with Yam (Sea) and then with the "fleeing serpent" — the very epithet given to [[leviathan|Leviathan]] at Isaiah 27:1 — so the three names converge inside four cola.
- **Job 9:13** — *"God will not turn back his anger; beneath him bowed the helpers of Rahab (*ʿōzərê rāhaḇ*)."* The "helpers of Rahab" are the chaos-monster's allied forces, subdued under the creator — a fragment presupposing a fuller combat narrative.

A second, **figurative** usage applies the chaos-monster's name to **Egypt**, the great power whose insolence YHWH humbles:

- **Psalm 87:4** — *"I will record Rahab and Babylon among those who know me"* — Rahab here is a cipher for Egypt, paired with Babylon.
- **Isaiah 30:7** — *"Egypt's help is worthless and empty; therefore I have called her 'Rahab who sits still'"* (*rahaḇ hēm šāḇeṯ*) — a pointed pun: the blustering chaos-monster reduced to an impotent braggart that "sits still."

This second usage is theologically continuous with the first: Egypt-at-the-Exodus is YHWH's historical chaos-enemy, drowned in the sea, just as Rahab is YHWH's cosmogonic chaos-enemy, cut to pieces in the deep — the two collapse most explicitly at Isaiah 51:9–10, where the arm that "cut Rahab in pieces" is the same arm that "dried up the sea … so that the redeemed might pass over." The primeval combat and the Red Sea deliverance are narrated as one act.

## Cross-tradition significance

Rahab is one of the **named survivals of the ancient Near-Eastern combat-cosmogony** within the Hebrew Bible — a fragment of the [[chaoskampf|Chaoskampf]] in which a divine warrior defeats the primordial chaos-sea and establishes the ordered world. Since Hermann Gunkel's *Schöpfung und Chaos* (1895), the Rahab passages have been read — together with the Leviathan, Yam, and Tannin texts — as the poetic residue of a combat-myth that the Priestly Genesis 1 deliberately *demythologises*: where Genesis 1 has God order an inert [[tehom|deep]] by speech alone, the older poetry remembers a YHWH who "crushed Rahab," "stilled the Sea," and "pierced the dragon." John Day's *God's Conflict with the Dragon and the Sea* (1985) is the canonical modern treatment, and Mary Wakeman's *God's Battle with the Monster* (1973) is the monograph organised most directly around this chaos-figure.

The honest comparative claim must be carefully calibrated, because **Rahab sits differently in the grid than Leviathan does**. The Leviathan↔[[lotan|Lotan]] link is a *secure linguistic cognate* (Hebrew *lwytn* = Ugaritic *ltn*, same root *lwy*, with a verbatim epithet-calque at Isaiah 27:1). Rahab has **no such anchor**: there is no attested Ugaritic *rhb*-monster and no Babylonian *Rahab*, and the name appears to be a *native Israelite coinage* from the Hebrew root "to storm/bluster." Its kinship to [[tiamat|Tiamat]] (Babylon), Lotan (Ugarit), and the wider grid is therefore **structural and typological** — the convergent figure of "primordial sea-chaos defeated by the warrior-creator" — rather than a documented transmission of a particular name or text. Within Hebrew itself, by contrast, Rahab's identity with [[leviathan|Leviathan]], the dragon *Tannin*, and [[yam|Yam]] is secure: the poets name the one defeated chaos-power by several interchangeable by-names, and Job 26:12–13 assimilates Rahab to the "fleeing serpent" of Leviathan in a single passage. So the correct statement is: **Rahab is a fully native Hebrew chaos-monster that occupies the same comparative slot as Tiamat/Lotan by convergence on the shared Northwest-Semitic combat-pattern, and that is interchangeable with Leviathan/Tannin/Yam by direct poetic synonymy inside the canon.**

In later tradition the chaos-Rahab is re-personified as **"the Prince of the Sea" (*śar šel yām*)** in rabbinic legend (e.g. b. Bava Batra 74b and related aggadot), where God commands the sea's angelic prince — sometimes named Rahab — to swallow the waters at creation, kills him for refusing, and hides his carcass beneath the sea; the medieval *Midrash* and later kabbalistic material extend the figure. This is the same trajectory by which [[leviathan|Leviathan]] becomes an eschatological banquet-creature — the demythologised biblical chaos-monster re-mythologised in Second Temple and rabbinic imagination.

## Disputes

- **No Ugaritic/Babylonian cognate — convergence, not transmission.** The central caution. Rahab cannot be tied to a specific foreign name the way Leviathan is tied to Lotan or tehom to Tiamat. The name is best explained as a Hebrew formation from the root *rhb* ("to storm, behave insolently"); there is no *rhb*-dragon at Ugarit. Day (1985) accordingly treats Rahab as the Israelite tradition's *own* designation for the chaos-sea within the inherited Northwest-Semitic combat-pattern, not as a loan. The cross-tradition edges to Tiamat and Lotan in this node are therefore typed **parallel-form / structural**, deliberately weaker than the cognate edges on the [[tehom]] and [[leviathan]] nodes.
- **How "mythic" is the language? (Gunkel–Day vs. Tsumura).** Whether the Rahab passages genuinely preserve a combat-*myth*, or merely deploy stock poetic imagery of God's mastery over a stormy sea, is contested. David Tsumura (*Creation and Destruction*, 2005) mounts the major dissent against the Gunkel–Day mainstream, arguing that the "chaos" framework is overstated and that much of the supposed combat-vocabulary is ordinary figurative poetry. The mainstream (Gunkel 1895; Day 1985; Wakeman 1973) holds that the Rahab/Leviathan/Yam poetry is a real survival of an inherited combat-cosmogony. The honest state of the question: **the imagery of a defeated chaos-sea is undisputed; whether it encodes a once-told myth or only a poetic topos is the live debate.**
- **The Egypt-cipher relation.** Whether Rahab-as-Egypt (Ps 87:4; Isa 30:7) is a *secondary historicising* of the chaos-monster, or evidence that the name was always politically applicable, is discussed but not decisive; most read it as the mythic chaos-figure recruited to name Israel's archetypal historical oppressor, with the Exodus sea-drowning as the bridge.
- **The homonym.** Worth restating as a dispute only insofar as careless harmonisation sometimes conflates the two Rahabs: the woman of Jericho (*Rāḥāḇ*, root *rḥb*) and the sea-monster (*Rahaḇ*, root *rhb*) are etymologically distinct and should never be merged. Standard lexica (TDOT, s.v. רַהַב) keep them separate.
