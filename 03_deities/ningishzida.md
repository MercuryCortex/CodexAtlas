# Ningishzida

## Identity
Ningishzida ("Lord of the True Tree" / "Lord of the Steadfast Tree" — Sumerian *nin* + *ĝiš* "wood/tree" + *zi(d)* "true, steadfast") is a Mesopotamian chthonic, fertility, and protective deity whose most striking attribute is the **entwined twin serpents** rising from his shoulders or wrapped around his staff. The iconography is documented on the **libation vase of Gudea of Lagash (Louvre AO 190, c. -2100)** — one of the most-cited single objects in the iconographic prehistory of the later Hermetic [[caduceus]]. Ningishzida is the personal god of **Gudea of Lagash** (~-2144 to -2124), who erected the libation vase in his honor; he is also widely attested as a chthonic throne-bearer and doorkeeper in underworld contexts. (Wiggermann 1992, Black & Green 1992.)

## Earliest attestation
- Early Dynastic god-lists from Fara and Abu Salabikh (~-2500) include Ningishzida among the regional pantheon.
- **Gudea Cylinders** (~-2125) and the Gudea libation vase (Louvre AO 190) — principal Lagashite attestation of Ningishzida as Gudea's personal god, with the canonical entwined-serpent iconography. ([[phase-1-018-gudea-cylinders]])
- *Descent of Inanna* and related underworld literature — Ningishzida as underworld throne-bearer / vegetation-god alternating with Dumuzi. ([[phase-1-017-descent-of-inanna]])
- Continuous cult attestation through Old Babylonian and into Neo-Assyrian periods (Wiggermann *RlA* 2000).

## Mythological role
Ningishzida operates in three overlapping registers:

1. **Personal god / tutelary** — Gudea of Lagash names Ningishzida as his personal god in the Gudea Cylinders and the libation vase inscription. As personal god he intercedes for Gudea with the higher gods.
2. **Chthonic throne-bearer / doorkeeper** — in the *Descent of Inanna* tradition and related underworld literature, Ningishzida appears among the gods of the netherworld; in one Sumerian myth (the *Adapa* tradition's later layers) he is doorkeeper of An's heaven alongside Dumuzi.
3. **Vegetation / fertility** — the "tree" in his name and his pairing with Dumuzi mark him as a dying-and-returning vegetation figure; he and Dumuzi alternate periods in the underworld with the seasons.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[ninazu]]** — *ancient-identification* (father, shared iconography): Ningishzida is regularly identified as Ninazu's son; both share the entwined-serpent and horned-serpent iconographic complex (Wiggermann 1992).
- **[[dumuzi-tammuz]]** — *ancient-identification*: paired with Ningishzida in the *Descent of Inanna* sequence; both are young dying-and-returning vegetation gods. Black & Green 1992.

## Iconography
- **The entwined twin serpents** — Ningishzida's signature attribute, depicted either rising from his shoulders or wrapped around a staff he holds. The Gudea libation vase (Louvre AO 190) is the canonical visual attestation: two serpents in symmetrical entwined ascent up the vase. **This is the iconographic substrate of the later Hermetic [[caduceus]]** per Friedlander 1992 (case-for-Mesopotamian-origin). The transmission from Mesopotamian Ningishzida to Hellenistic Hermes-kerukeion is iconographic rather than narrative — the visual pattern survives the intervening cultural translation.
- The horned serpent (*bašmu* / *mušḫuššu*-adjacent dragon) — part of the broader Mesopotamian chthonic-protective-spirit vocabulary.
- Tree iconography — the *giš* in his name.

## Disputes
- **Strength of the Ningishzida → caduceus transmission.** Friedlander 1992 documents the iconographic continuity from Gudea's vase (c. -2100) through Levantine and Hellenistic intermediaries to the canonical Hermes-kerukeion (6th c. BCE onward). The transmission is **iconographic rather than document-specific** at every step — i.e., we can see the pattern persisting but cannot trace every transmission node. Some scholars treat it as `ancestor-of` (strong), others as `parallel-form` (weaker). The vault carries it as `ancestor-of` per Friedlander 1992 at the [[caduceus]] node.
- **Distinct from Ninazu vs. coalesced.** Whether Ningishzida and Ninazu were originally distinct deities later coalesced into a shared serpent-iconographic complex, or whether they were distinct father-son figures from the beginning, is a minor scholarly question (Wiggermann *RlA*).
