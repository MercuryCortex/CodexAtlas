# Kagutsuchi

## Identity
Kagutsuchi (Hi-no-Kagutsuchi, "Blazing Fire") is the god of fire in Shinto mythology, born as the last child of [[izanagi]] and [[izanami]] in the *Kojiki*'s creation sequence. His birth is simultaneously the completion of the creative act and the origin of death — his fire-body burns [[izanami]] so severely that she dies, descending to become the ruler of Yomi (the underworld).

## The Mythological Sequence
In the *Kojiki* creation narrative, Izanagi and Izanami create the Japanese islands and the major kami through sexual union. Kagutsuchi is the last to be born. His burning fire-body scorches Izanami's genitals during birth; she suffers, vomits, defecates, and urinates — and from her emissions spring further deities (of clay, sand, earth, water, and the mountain). She then dies from the burns.

Izanagi's grief turns to rage. He draws his **ten-span sword** (*totsuka-no-tsurugi*) and beheads and dismembers Kagutsuchi into eight pieces. From each piece of the fire deity's corpse spring new kami — mountain gods, storm gods, and deities associated with smithing and earth. This is the [[cosmic-body-cosmogony]] pattern applied within the Shinto corpus in miniature.

## Theological significance
Kagutsuchi encodes two of the deepest problems in Japanese mythological theology:

1. **The origin of death**: Izanami's death through childbirth is the first death in the Kojiki's cosmos. Before Kagutsuchi, Izanagi and Izanami had created without cost; Kagutsuchi is the deity whose existence makes that creation irreversible. Fire enables technological civilization but its cost is mortality.

2. **Creation through destruction**: Kagutsuchi is killed, and from his death more creation emerges (the eight kami from his corpse). Death is not an end but a transformation into further creative power — the same logic as the [[cosmic-body-cosmogony]] pattern at a smaller scale.

## Cult and ritual
Fire deities require ritual management. The main shrine dedicated to Kagutsuchi (Atago Shrine, Kyoto; Himure Hachimangu in Shiga) are associated with fire-prevention. In the annual Otaimatsu fire festival (Tōdai-ji, Nara), fire is ritually processed as part of the spring renewal cycle.
