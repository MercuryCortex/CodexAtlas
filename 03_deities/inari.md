# Inari Ōkami

## Identity
The most widely-worshipped kami in Japan after the imperial Amaterasu; an estimated **one-third of all Shinto shrines in Japan (~32,000)** are Inari shrines, with **Fushimi Inari Taisha** in southern Kyoto (founded 711 CE) as head shrine. Originally an agricultural-grain deity, Inari expanded through medieval and early-modern Japan into a deity of commerce, swordsmithing, and fortune. Famously androgynous / variable in gender across sub-traditions: the Fushimi tradition presents Inari as masculine; the Toyokawa Inari tradition (a Sōtō Zen Buddhist temple, not a Shinto shrine) presents the figure as the ḍākinī **Dakiniten**, feminine.

## Mythological role
Inari's messengers are **kitsune** (fox-spirits), depicted at the entry torii of every Inari shrine in paired statues holding key, jewel, or scroll. The famous Senbon Torii (thousand vermilion gates) at Fushimi are donations from supplicants whose prayers were answered — a votive-stack literally walkable. Inari's worship is the cleanest live example of **honji-suijaku** Shinto-Buddhist syncretism: in many shrines the same deity is approached as kami at the shrine gate and as Buddhist Dakiniten at the inner sanctum.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **Dakiniten** (Buddhist ḍākinī) — *folk-syncretism*; the most layered Shinto-Buddhist identification.
- **[[demeter]]** — *parallel-motif*: agricultural/grain deity.
- **[[oshun]] / [[lakshmi]]** — *parallel-motif*: female fortune-and-fertility deity venerated in commerce.
