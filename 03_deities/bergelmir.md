# Bergelmir

## Identity
**Bergelmir** (Old Norse *Bergelmir*, "mountain-roarer" or "bear-roarer") is the **Norse flood-survivor** — the giant who survives the **Ymir-blood-flood** in the foundational Norse cosmogonic-mythological complex. The narrative (Snorri's *Gylfaginning* 7 and *Vafþrúðnismál* 35):

> The first primordial being, **Ymir** (the giant whose body is dismembered to create the world), is killed by Odin and his brothers (Vili and Vé). When Ymir is slain, his blood floods so much that it drowns all the jötnar (giants) **except Bergelmir and his wife**, who escape in a hollowed-out tree-trunk (or a boat) and survive. From Bergelmir all subsequent giants descend.

The Norse narrative is **structurally cognate** with the Mesopotamian-Hebrew-Greek-Vedic flood-traditions: a primordial catastrophic flood, a single surviving male-female pair, an ark/vessel (in Norse, a hollowed-out tree-trunk), and post-flood-repopulation of the species. The distinctive Norse features:

1. **The flood is *blood*** (Ymir's blood) rather than water — connecting the flood-motif to the broader Norse cosmogonic-dismemberment pattern (Ymir's body parts become the world).
2. **The survivors are *giants* (jötnar)**, not humans — humanity in the Norse cosmogony is created *separately* by Odin from driftwood (Ask and Embla); the giants are a parallel and competing race.
3. **The flood is *not divine judgment*** (as in Mesopotamian/Hebrew) but a *side-effect of cosmogonic violence* (Ymir's killing).

These distinctive features make Bergelmir the **typological-cognate-but-narratively-distinct** Norse instantiation of the broader Indo-European flood-tradition pattern.

## Earliest attestation

- **Snorri's *Prose Edda*, *Gylfaginning* 7** (~1220 CE) — the canonical narrative form, drawing on older oral tradition.
- ***Vafþrúðnismál* 35** (Poetic Edda; older oral tradition ~9th–10th c. CE, surviving written 13th c.) — the older poetic attestation.

The Norse mythological corpus survives in writing only from the post-conversion (post-1000 CE) Christian-Iceland period (Snorri's Edda, the Poetic Edda); the underlying oral tradition is pre-Christian Scandinavian but cannot be directly dated.

## Mythological role

Bergelmir is the **progenitor of all subsequent giants** in the Norse cosmos. The narrative theological function: after the catastrophic primordial-creation event (Ymir's killing and dismemberment), the cosmos is *repopulated* through the survival of one giant-couple — making Bergelmir's role structurally similar to Noah's, Manu's, or Deucalion's, but at the *giant* rather than the *human* level.

The Norse mythological-cosmological structure parallels the Mesopotamian / Hebrew / Vedic / Greek flood-traditions enough that comparative-mythologists (Witzel 2012; Lincoln 1986) treat Bergelmir as part of the broader Indo-European or Laurasian flood-narrative complex.

## Cross-tradition identifications

- **[[ziusudra]] / [[utnapishtim]] / [[noah]] / [[deucalion]] / [[manu]]** — *parallel-form*: cognate flood-survivor figure across Indo-European and west-Asian traditions. Witzel 2012 treats these as instantiations of a deep Laurasian mythological substrate.
- **Norse mythological cognates of Mesopotamian flood-tradition.** Direct transmission from Mesopotamian to Scandinavian is not documented; the cognate-relationship is *structural*, not *transmissive*.

## Iconography

Not strongly standardized in surviving Norse pictorial tradition.

## Disputes

- **Etymology of *Bergelmir*.** *Berg-* possibly from "mountain"; *-gelmir* from "roarer" (cognate Old Norse *galmr*); alternative readings include "bear-roarer." Not securely settled.
- **Independent flood-tradition or Christian-Iceland Christian-influence?** Whether the Bergelmir flood-narrative in Snorri (writing in Christian Iceland post-1000 CE) reflects pre-Christian Norse oral tradition or is *influenced by* the Christian Noah-tradition. Most current scholarship (Simek 1993) accepts pre-Christian substrate, with Snorri's redaction possibly Christianizing certain narrative elements.
- **Hollowed tree-trunk or boat?** Snorri's vocabulary is ambiguous between *lúðr* (a hollow vessel — possibly tree-trunk or mill-bin) and a more conventional boat. The tree-trunk reading is the more common scholarly interpretation.
