# Loki

## Identity
The trickster of Norse mythology — both companion and adversary of the Æsir, neither fully god nor fully giant (his parents are both giants but he is sworn-brother to Óðinn). Loki's role is structurally indispensable: many of the gods' principal possessions (Thor's hammer, Sif's golden hair, Freyr's ship, Óðinn's spear) are obtained because of Loki's tricks, and many of the gods' troubles are caused by them too. He is the father, by the giantess Angrboða, of three of the principal monsters of Norse cosmogony — the wolf Fenrir, the Midgard Serpent Jörmungandr, and Hel. He himself bore the eight-legged horse Sleipnir while transformed into a mare. After bringing about the death of Baldr (which Loki orchestrated by tricking blind Hǫðr into casting the mistletoe-dart), he is bound by the Æsir under a venom-dripping serpent; he will break free at Ragnarǫk and fight against the gods, killing and being killed by Heimdallr.

## Earliest attestation
Old Norse literary attestation; *Lokasenna* (Poetic Edda) is the most extensive Loki-centered poem; Snorri's *Gylfaginning* gives the principal narrative summaries.

## Mythological role
Multi-faceted trickster: provides services to the gods (recovering Thor's hammer; cutting Sif's golden hair and obtaining replacements; obtaining the gods' magical objects from the dwarves); causes troubles (insulting the gods in *Lokasenna*; bringing about Baldr's death). His gender-fluidity is notable: he gives birth (to Sleipnir) and is mocked for it. At Ragnarǫk he leads the army of the dead against the gods.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **Christian Satan** — *contested-parallel*: Loki's bound-monster-tormented-by-venom punishment and Ragnarǫk-rebellion has structural parallels with Christian Satan; whether Christian-period reshaping of Loki traditions occurred is debated.
- **Èṣù / Coyote / Anansi** — *scholarly-parallel*: comparative-religion trickster category, deployed by Hyde (1998) among others; the comparison is typological, not historical.

## Iconography
Limited; small medieval depictions show him sometimes bearded. The Snaptun stone (~10th c., Denmark) showing a face with sewn lips is sometimes identified as Loki (in reference to a narrative in which dwarves sew his lips).
