# Turms

## Identity
Turms is the Etruscan god of commerce, travel, messages, and transitions — the Etruscan equivalent of Greek **[[hermes-greek]]** and Roman **[[mercury-roman]]**. He is distinguished by his psychopomp role: as escort of the souls of the dead from the world of the living to the Etruscan underworld, Turms appears repeatedly in Etruscan tomb painting as a winged guide accompanying the deceased on their final journey. His iconography is borrowed wholesale from the Greek Hermes: winged sandals (*talaria*), the caduceus (the double-serpent staff), and the broad-brimmed traveller's hat (*petasos*). This direct iconographic borrowing documents the depth of Greek-Etruscan religious contact mediated through Magna Graecia from the 6th century BCE.

## Earliest attestation
- Turms appears in Etruscan tomb paintings from approximately -550–500 BCE, already in full Hermes iconographic type.
- His name (*Turms*) is inscribed on Etruscan bronze mirrors that depict mythological scenes borrowed from Greek tradition — he appears in Etruscan-language versions of Greek myths.
- The Tomba del Triclinio at Tarquinia and related 5th–4th century BCE Etruscan funerary contexts show Turms as psychopomp.

## Mythological role
Turms's primary function in Etruscan religion was **psychopomp** — the divine guide who escorts souls of the newly dead from the mortal world to the underworld realm. Etruscan funerary belief required divine guidance for this transit: without Turms, the soul could not cross. This function made him indispensable to the Etruscan afterlife theology, which differed from Greek underworld theology in imagining a more actively populated and monitored passage. Alongside his role as soul-guide, Turms governed commerce (he was the divine patron of merchants, as Hermes was in Greece), travel (roads, boundaries, and the transition between territories), and messages between the divine and human worlds.

The **caduceus** — the staff of two intertwined serpents — that Turms carries is the instrument of boundary-crossing: it opens passages between worlds, reconciles opposites, and signals the herald's protected status. The caduceus appears in Etruscan, Greek, and Roman religious art and was later adopted as a medical symbol (confusion with Asclepius's single-serpent staff *notwithstanding*).

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[hermes-greek]]** — *ancient-identification*: Turms is the Etruscan Hermes, with iconographic borrowing complete and early. The psychopomp function, caduceus, winged sandals, and petasos are shared wholesale.
- **[[mercury-roman]]** — *ancient-identification*: Mercury absorbed both Hermes and Turms traditions; the Roman messenger-commerce-psychopomp deity.
- **[[thoth]]** (Egyptian) — *scholarly-parallel*: Thoth guides and records the dead in the Egyptian Duat (underworld), weighs hearts at judgment, and serves as divine scribe and herald — the same compound of guidance, communication, and underworld passage that defines Turms.
- **[[anubis]]** (Egyptian) — *scholarly-parallel*: Anubis guides the dead to judgment in the Duat; both Anubis and Turms function as the divine escort at the threshold of death. The Etruscan Turms and Egyptian Anubis occupy the same structural slot in their respective afterlife theologies.
- **[[nabu]]** (Babylonian) — *scholarly-parallel*: Nabu is the divine scribe, herald, and messenger of the Babylonian pantheon; the messenger-between-worlds function links him to Turms's communication domain.
- **[[ningishzida]]** (Sumerian) — *scholarly-parallel*: Ningishzida, whose symbol is intertwined serpents (paralleling the caduceus), is a lord of the underworld and a guide of the dead — the serpent-staff-underworld-guide cluster links both deities.

## Iconography
Turms is depicted identically to Hermes: a young, beardless or lightly bearded male wearing a broad-brimmed hat (*petasos*) and winged sandals. He carries the caduceus (kerykeion — double-serpent staff). In Etruscan tomb contexts he often wears a chlamys (short traveler's cloak). Unlike the demonic underworld figures of Etruscan art (such as **[[charun-etruscan]]**), Turms appears calm, youthful, and protective — a guide, not a threat.

## Disputes
- **Etymology of "Turms"**: uncertain; possibly derived from Greek *Hermes* through Etruscan phonological adaptation (*H-* → *T-* shift is not standard; alternative derivations proposed from Etruscan roots). The name may be independently Etruscan.
- **Turms vs. Charun**: Etruscan underworld theology distinguishes between Turms (the divine psychopomp who guides) and **[[charun-etruscan]]** (the demonic doorkeeper who threatens). This two-figure system has no direct Greek parallel — it represents a distinctively Etruscan elaboration of the underworld passage.
