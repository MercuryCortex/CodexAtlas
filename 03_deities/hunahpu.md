# Hunahpu

## Identity
Hunahpu (and his twin Xbalanque) are the Hero Twins of K'iche' Maya religion — the protagonists of the central narrative of the *Popol Vuh*, the Maya creation epic recorded in colonial K'iche' Guatemala (c. 1554–1558 CE). Hunahpu means "One Blowgunner" in K'iche', reflecting his weapon. Together Hunahpu and Xbalanque descend into **Xibalba** (the Maya underworld), defeat the lords of death through a combination of cunning, endurance, and magical trickery, die and are resurrected, and ascend to become the **sun and moon** — Hunahpu becoming the sun, Xbalanque the moon (or in some readings, both become sun-figures representing day and night). Their story is the most fully articulated narrative of underworld descent and resurrection in pre-Columbian literature, with direct parallels to dying-rising deity patterns documented across the ancient world.

## Earliest attestation
- The Hero Twin myth is attested far earlier than the colonial *Popol Vuh* text. **Classic Maya ceramics** (c. 200–900 CE) — especially the "rollout" painted vessels showing ballgame scenes and underworld journeys — depict the Hero Twin narrative in detail, with the twins identifiable by their distinctive black-and-white paint and ballgame equipment.
- Freidel, Schele & Parker (1993) identify Hero Twin imagery at major Classic sites including Copan and Palenque, pushing the narrative's attestation to at least 200 CE.
- The **Popol Vuh** (K'iche' Maya text, c. 1554–1558, in K'iche' with Spanish alphabetic script by Dominican friars) is the primary literary source; the colonial date of the written text does not reflect its pre-Columbian origin, which the ceramic evidence establishes.

## Mythological role
The *Popol Vuh* Hero Twin narrative unfolds in three main acts:

**Act 1 — The First Twins.** The Hero Twins' predecessors, Hun Hunahpu (the Maize God, father of Hunahpu and Xbalanque) and Vucub Hunahpu, descend to Xibalba to play ball with the lords of death. They fail the trials, are sacrificed, and Hun Hunahpu's head is placed in a calabash tree. The severed head spits into the hand of Xquic (Blood Moon), who conceives the Hero Twins.

**Act 2 — The Hero Twins in Xibalba.** Summoned to Xibalba for their own ballgame challenge, Hunahpu and Xbalanque navigate the underworld's houses of death (Dark House, Cold House, Fire House, Razor House, Bat House) through cunning and assistance from mosquito-spies. In Bat House, Hunahpu's head is cut off by the bat-demon Camazotz; Xbalanque fashions a substitute head from a pumpkin and replaces his brother's head in the subsequent ballgame using the actual head as the ball. After defeating the lords of Xibalba, the twins allow themselves to be sacrificed — they instruct the underworld lords how to grind their bones and cast them into the river. From the river, they reconstitute themselves, first as fish, then as vagabond-dancers. They disguise themselves as poor performers, impress the lords of Xibalba with their death-and-resurrection dance-trick (they kill each other and bring each other back), and the lords of death demand to experience the trick themselves. The twins oblige — and do not resurrect the lords. Xibalba is defeated.

**Act 3 — Solar apotheosis.** Hunahpu and Xbalanque ascend from the underworld and become the sun and the moon, their faces permanently marked with the ballgame black-and-white paint visible from earth.

## MASSIVE WIN — Hero Twin descent as Mesoamerican dying-rising pattern

The Hunahpu / Xbalanque narrative is **the closest Mesoamerican parallel to the dying-rising deity pattern** documented in Mesopotamian (Inanna's Descent to the Underworld), Egyptian (Osiris dismemberment and reconstitution), and Levantine ([[adonis]], [[attis]], [[dumuzi-tammuz]]) traditions. The structural parallels are striking:

| Feature | Hero Twins | [[inanna-sumerian]] | Osiris ([[osiris]]) |
|---------|-----------|---------------------|---------------------|
| Descent to underworld | Voluntary summons to Xibalba | Voluntary descent to Kur | Dismemberment by Set |
| Death in underworld | Ground into bone-powder | Killed, hung on a hook | Dismembered, scattered |
| Reconstitution | Fish → human forms | Revived by Enki's clay-figures | Reassembled by Isis |
| Victory over death | Xibalba lords defeated | Returns to upper world | Becomes king of the dead |
| Cosmic outcome | Become sun and moon | Inanna returns; Tammuz as substitute | Osiris rules the Duat |

These parallels are typological (no documented transmission between Mesoamerica and the Old World). Their independent emergence suggests that **underworld descent / death / resurrection is a near-universal narrative structure for solar theology**: the sun disappears (sets, descends underground) and returns (rises) daily — and solar deities mythologize this as underworld trial and resurrection.

## Cross-tradition identifications

- **[[kukulkan]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: both are Maya solar divine figures; the Hero Twins' apotheosis as sun and moon connects to Kukulkán's solar-morning-star symbolism. The twins represent the creation of the current sun and moon, which Kukulkán governs as reigning solar deity.
- **[[itzamna]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Itzamna is the elder creator-divinity of the Maya; the Hero Twins' victories in Xibalba create the conditions for the current cosmic order that Itzamna heads.
- **[[xipe-totec]]** — *scholarly-parallel* (Mesoamerican dying-rising comparison): Xipe Totec's skin-shedding = agricultural renewal parallels the Twins' death and resurrection as solar renewal. Both are Mesoamerican expressions of the death-and-rebirth cosmological axis.
- **[[tezcatlipoca]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: the lords of Xibalba function structurally as Aztec underworld / death deities do; Tezcatlipoca's realm of death and darkness parallels Xibalba.

## Iconography
Classic Maya painted ceramics (rollout vessels) are the primary iconographic source. The Hero Twins are distinguished by: **Hunahpu** — black spots or dots on his white-painted body, associated with the sun; **Xbalanque** — jaguar patches on his skin, associated with the night-sun (the sun as it travels through the underworld). Both carry blowguns and are shown in ballgame gear (padded hip-belt, kneeguards). Post-classic codex representations are rarer; the colonial *Popol Vuh* was not illustrated.

## Disputes
- **Hunahpu = Sun, Xbalanque = Moon, or both are solar?** Tedlock (1985) identifies Hunahpu with the sun and Xbalanque with the moon in the standard reading. Some scholars (Coe 2015) argue both twins represent solar figures — the day-sun and the jaguar-night-sun respectively — with the moon represented by Xquic (Blood Moon, their mother).
- **Classic vs. Postclassic narrative continuity.** The *Popol Vuh* is K'iche' Maya (Highland Guatemala); Classic Maya iconography of the Hero Twins is Lowland Maya (Peten, Yucatan). The degree to which the colonial text reflects continuous pre-Columbian narrative vs. Highland K'iche' elaboration of a more diffuse Classic Maya tradition is debated.
