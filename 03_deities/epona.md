# Epona

## Identity
Epona — her name derives from Gaulish *epos* ("horse") + the divine suffix *-ona* — is the Gaulish goddess of horses, mares, and foals, uniquely distinguished as the **only Celtic deity to receive a Roman state festival**: December 18 in the Roman calendar. She was worshipped from her Gaulish heartland throughout the entire Roman Empire wherever cavalry units were stationed — from Britain to North Africa, from Spain to the Danube frontier — carried by Roman auxiliary cavalry soldiers who adopted her as their patron. Over three hundred inscriptions and dedications to Epona survive, making her one of the best-epigraphically-documented Celtic deities, despite almost no surviving literary mythology.

## Earliest attestation
The earliest inscriptions date to the 1st–2nd century CE, concentrated in the Rhineland and Burgundy regions. The Roman festival of December 18 is attested in the *Menologia Rustica* and in Juvenal's *Satires* (Sat. 8.157, where Epona is mocked as a stable-goddess). The Greek writer Pausanias (2nd c. CE) does not mention her, suggesting her cult's spread was primarily through the Roman military mechanism. Iconographic evidence (reliefs, statuettes) is spread across the 1st–4th centuries CE.

## Mythological role
Almost no literary mythology of Epona survives — her ancient story has been largely lost. What survives is **iconography and inscription**. The standard iconographic type shows her riding sidesaddle (an unusual posture — most deities ride astride or are depicted standing), flanked by one or two mares, often with a foal nursing. She commonly carries a *patera* (libation dish), grain-basket, or cornucopia, marking her abundance-and-fertility dimension alongside the horse-patronage.

Her sovereignty function is inferred from parallels with Irish mythology — the Irish *rígan* (sovereignty-queen) figure who appears as a horse or rides one is structurally analogous. The Mabinogion's Rhiannon — a Welsh figure who appears on horseback and cannot be caught, is accused of eating her foal-son, and undergoes humiliation before restoration — is widely regarded as a mythological survival of the Epona-type goddess.

Her psychopomp function — the horse as conveyor between the worlds of living and dead — is suggested by key iconography and by the universal cross-cultural pattern of the horse-as-death-guide (compare Sleipnir, Pegasus, the white horse of apocalyptic traditions). Whether Epona explicitly performed this role is inferred rather than directly attested.

**Roman Adoption.** Epona's integration into the Roman world is remarkable for its depth: she was worshipped in Rome itself, given a Latin festival, and received the Roman epithets *Regina* ("Queen") and *Augusta*. The mechanism of her spread was the Roman auxiliary cavalry (*auxilia*) — Gaulish cavalry units brought their horse-goddess into Roman military religion, where she became universal to cavalry practice. This is one of the clearest documented cases of a provincial deity fully entering Roman state religion — a reversal of the usual Roman policy of assimilation.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[cernunnos]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Epona and Cernunnos are the two most iconographically attested Celtic deities of the pre-Roman Gaulish tradition; both preside over the animal-human boundary — Cernunnos over wild beasts, Epona over domesticated horses.
- **[[dagda]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: the Dagda's abundance (cauldron of plenty) parallels Epona's cornucopia/grain-basket; both are Celtic deities of nourishment and sovereignty.
- **[[the-morrigan]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: the Morrígan appears as a crow and a sovereignty-queen; Epona's mare-riding sovereignty is the Gaulish parallel of the Irish sovereignty-figure. Both embody the feminine divine power over the land.
- **[[brigid]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Celtic fertility-and-sovereignty divine feminine cluster.
- **[[venus-roman]]** — Roman festival context of December 18 places Epona within the Roman sacred calendar; no direct theological syncretism, but her December festival is the mechanism of Roman state integration.

## Iconography
The single most consistent iconographic trait: **sidesaddle posture** — Epona is the only Gaulish deity regularly depicted riding sidesaddle, giving her a distinctive, serene, queenly appearance. She is frequently flanked by two mares (sometimes with a foal), emphasizing her role as mother of horses. The patera/grain-basket in her hand marks the abundance-and-fertility dimension. Reliefs are found in stables, cavalry barracks, and private shrines across the former Roman Empire.

## Disputes
Whether Epona is a pan-Celtic goddess with deep pre-Roman roots or a late Gaulish deity whose cult was amplified by Roman cavalry adoption is disputed. The relative scarcity of pre-Roman-period inscriptions makes her pre-Roman depth hard to assess. The identification with Rhiannon (Welsh) is widely accepted in Celtic studies but not universally. Whether her sidesaddle posture is a sign of sovereignty/nobility or simply a practical convention in the iconographic type is debated.
