# Baldr

## Identity
Son of Óðinn and Frigg; the most beautiful, beloved, and innocent of the Æsir. Baldr's death is the central tragic event of Norse mythology and the first major sign of approaching Ragnarǫk. His story is one of the most extensively retold Norse narratives.

## Earliest attestation
Old Norse literary attestation; the Merseburg charms (Old High German, ~9th c.) may reference a related figure ("Phol ... Balder"). The Vǫluspá and *Gylfaginning* are the principal narrative sources.

## Mythological role
Baldr's prophetic dreams of his own death; Frigg's oath-collection from all beings; the missed mistletoe; Loki's manipulation of blind Hǫðr; the dart that kills Baldr; the gods' grief; Hermóðr's ride to Hel to ransom him; the giantess Þǫkk (Loki?) refusing to weep; Baldr remaining in Hel until after Ragnarǫk, when he will return to lead the renewed world. Baldr's funeral pyre on the ship *Hringhorni* is one of the great Norse mourning-scenes.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **Christ** — *scholarly-parallel* (contested): dying-and-resurrecting (well — *will-resurrect-after-Ragnarǫk*) good god. The structural parallel is real; whether Christian-period reshaping of the Baldr narrative occurred, or whether the parallel is independent Indo-European dying-god material, is debated. Lindow 1997 is balanced on this.
- **Dying-god comparative category (Adonis, Tammuz, Osiris)** — *scholarly-parallel*: Baldr is sometimes included in Frazer's broader dying-god category; J.Z. Smith's critique of the category applies here as elsewhere.

## Iconography
Radiant; sometimes shown on the funeral pyre on the ship Hringhorni.
