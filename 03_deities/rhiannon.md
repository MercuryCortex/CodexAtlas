# Rhiannon

## Identity
Rhiannon is the preeminent female divine figure of the Welsh *Mabinogi* — appearing in the First and Third Branches as a figure of otherworldly sovereignty, horse-power, and wrongful ordeal. Her name is regularly derived from Proto-Celtic *\*Rīgantonā* — "Great Queen" — the same root as Irish *rígan* (queen) and Gaulish **Epona** (whose name contains *epo-* "horse" + *-ona* — making Epona the "Great Horse" to Rhiannon's "Great Queen," with both carrying sovereignty and horse iconography). Rhiannon is the Welsh literary survival of a pan-Celtic horse-sovereignty goddess whose Gaulish form (Epona) was so widely venerated that the Roman army carried her cult across the entire empire.

## Earliest attestation
Rhiannon appears in the **First Branch of the Mabinogi** (Welsh prose, composed ~1100 CE from earlier oral tradition). The manuscripts survive from the 14th century (*Llyfr Gwyn Rhydderch* ~1350; *Llyfr Coch Hergest* ~1400), but the tales are universally accepted as encoding pre-Christian mythological material. There is no earlier named attestation, though Epona (Gaulish, 1st c. BCE – 3rd c. CE, extensively inscribed in Roman-period dedications) represents the broader Celtic horse-sovereign divine type that Rhiannon instantiates in the Welsh tradition.

## Mythological role
**The First Branch.** Pwyll, lord of Dyfed, sees Rhiannon riding at a supernatural pace on a white horse. No one can catch her, however fast they ride — until Pwyll calls out to her, at which point she stops and reveals she has been waiting for him, having chosen him as her husband. She is clearly the active agent of their union: she *chose* Pwyll, not the reverse. After a suitor named Gwawl attempts to claim her, Rhiannon engineers his defeat with a magic bag trick. She marries Pwyll.

After the birth of their son, Rhiannon is falsely accused of killing and eating him (the baby disappears; the frightened ladies-in-waiting smear blood on the sleeping Rhiannon and place animal bones beside her). Rhiannon cannot prove her innocence. Her penance is to stand at the gate of the court and offer to carry guests on her back like a horse — the sovereignty goddess humiliated, forced to embody the mare she once surpassed. The son (Pryderi) is eventually recovered; the calumny is exposed.

**The Birds of Rhiannon.** Rhiannon's birds sing over the sea in a way that causes the dead to sleep and the living to forget time — seven years pass like a moment in their presence. This psychopomp function (guiding between waking and sleeping, life and death) is her most otherworldly attribute and connects her to the Celtic *síde* (otherworld) tradition.

**The Third Branch.** In *Manawydan uab Llŷr*, Rhiannon marries [[manannan-mac-lir]] (here named Manawydan) after Pwyll's death. They face a supernatural enchantment that destroys Dyfed (the land falls silent and empty). Rhiannon is taken into the otherworld; Manawydan must negotiate her release.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[epona]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: the clearest Celtic-internal connection. Epona (Gaulish horse-goddess, dated to 1st c. BCE onward) and Rhiannon share the horse-sovereignty complex, the otherworld-queen function, and the probable common derivation from Proto-Celtic *\*Rīgantonā* / divine-queen-horse cluster. Epona is the goddess that Roman soldiers venerated — she is one of the only Celtic deities to receive a dedicated festival in the Roman calendar (*December 18*).
- **[[macha]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: the Irish sovereignty-goddess forced into humiliating horse-labor (Macha races the king's horses while pregnant) is the nearest mythological parallel to Rhiannon's horse-bearing ordeal. Both are wrongfully punished sovereignty goddesses whose violation of dignity is the narrative's moral and theological center.
- **[[the-morrigan]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: the Irish triple-sovereignty-goddess as structural equivalent; both embody the land's sovereign power and its transmission through divine feminine choice.
- **[[manannan-mac-lir]]** — *mythological partner*: Rhiannon's second husband is the Welsh form of the Irish lord of the otherworld — the most explicit indication of Rhiannon's own otherworldly nature.
