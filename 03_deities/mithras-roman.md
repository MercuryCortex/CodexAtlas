# Mithras (Roman mystery cult)

## Identity
**Mithras of the Roman mystery cult is a distinct religious phenomenon from both the Vedic Mitra and the Zoroastrian/Iranian Mithra**, despite the shared name. The Roman cult emerges in the 1st century CE in the Mediterranean and spreads through the Roman Empire — especially via the army on the Rhine and Danube frontiers — until its suppression with other pagan cults at the end of the 4th century. Worshippers met in small underground or cave-imitating temples (**mithraea**), assembled in male-only initiate brotherhoods of seven graded ranks (Corax, Nymphus, Miles, Leo, Perses, Heliodromus, Pater), and centered their worship on the great cult-relief of **Mithras Tauroctonous** — Mithras kneeling on the back of a bull, drawing back its head and plunging a dagger into its neck.

We split this node from any Iranian or Vedic Mithra node because the cult content is overwhelmingly Roman; the Iranian connection (long dominant in 20th-c. scholarship via Cumont) is now seen by most specialists (Clauss, Beck, Gordon, Martin) as **substantially overstated**.

## Earliest attestation
- Earliest secure datable Mithraic monument: Statius *Thebaid* 1.719–720 (~+90s CE) describes Mithras "twisting the unruly horns under the rocks of a Persian cave" — first literary reference.
- Earliest dated mithraeum dedications: ~+80/+100 CE (Rome, Capua, Pannonia).
- Cumont's 19th-c. discovery and cataloguing of mithraea launched modern study; the cult is known almost entirely from iconography and inscriptions, not surviving sacred texts.

## Mythological role
Mithras's central iconographic narrative (the **tauroctony**) shows him slaying a cosmic bull in a cave; from the bull's blood and seed wheat and grapes spring; a dog and snake lick the blood, a scorpion attacks the bull's genitals, a raven watches; torch-bearers Cautes (upraised torch — rising) and Cautopates (lowered torch — setting) flank the scene; the zodiac frames it overhead. **Beck (2006)** argues the tauroctony encodes a stellar/cosmological map: each figure corresponds to a constellation; the slaying represents Mithras's cosmic power over the precession of equinoxes and the soul's transit through the spheres. **Ulansey (1989)** independently developed an astronomical reading.

Initiates ascended through the seven grades, each associated with a planet (Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, Moon, Sun, Saturn), and a ritual death-and-rebirth ceremony.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[mithra-zoroastrian]]** — *scholarly-parallel* / *contested*: shared name; Cumont (1899, 1903) argued the Roman cult is descended Zoroastrian Mithra mediated via Anatolian Magians. Mainstream now (Clauss, Beck, Gordon, Martin since 1971 Manchester conference): the Roman cult is *Roman in origin*, using *Persian* exotic-foreign branding (Phrygian cap, "Persian cave" claims) but with cult content native to Hellenistic-Roman astrology and mystery-religion. The name and the foreign-mystery branding are real Iranian traces; the substance of the cult is not.
- **[[sol-invictus]]** — *ancient-identification*: dedicatory inscriptions repeatedly couple *Sol Invictus* and *Mithras*: "to the unconquered Sun-god Mithras". Whether this is true identification or paired cultic relationship varies by monument.

## Iconography
- Phrygian cap (signaling "Persian" exoticism)
- Knife or dagger in the bull-slaying
- Bull, dog, snake, scorpion, raven (tauroctony scene)
- Torch-bearers Cautes (right) and Cautopates (left)
- Zodiac arch overhead
- Sun and Moon at upper corners
- Sometimes shown emerging from a rock (the "rock-birth" — *Mithras Petrogenitus*)

## Disputes
- **Iranian vs. Roman origin.** **The defining scholarly question.** Cumont (1899): Iranian, transmitted via Anatolian Magi. Manchester 1971 conference and subsequent (Beck, Clauss, Gordon, Martin): Roman invention with Persian branding. The mainstream now strongly favors Roman invention; some scholars (Ulansey, Beck) propose Tarsus / Cilicia as the specific birthplace ~-67/+50, where pirate-Magian and Hellenistic-astrology contact occurred.
- **Christian parallels.** Older popular literature (and some 19th-c. scholarship) claimed extensive Mithraic→Christian borrowing (December 25 birth, savior figure, baptism, communion meal). Most of these claims are **overstated or false**: December 25 as Mithras's birth is post-Christian (Sol Invictus official dedication 274 CE), the communion meal is misread, etc. Genuine parallels — male initiate brotherhoods, soul-ascent through spheres, mystery-secrecy — reflect shared Hellenistic-Roman religious vocabulary, not direct dependence (Bremmer, Gordon).
- **Sacred texts.** The cult was famously *non-literary*: we have iconography and inscriptions but virtually no surviving sacred narrative. What we know is largely reconstructed from cult-imagery.
