# Aži Dahāka

## Identity

**Aži Dahāka** (Avestan *Aži Dahāka*, "the serpent Dahāka"; *aži* = "serpent, dragon") is the great three-headed dragon of Zoroastrian myth — in the older texts a monstrous serpent-creature fashioned by **Angra Mainyu** (Ahriman, the Hostile Spirit) to destroy the world of Ahura Mazda's good order. The *Hōm Yašt* describes him as **"three-mouthed, three-headed, six-eyed, of a thousand skills, the most powerful druj-monster"** that the Lie (*druj*) brought against the material world (Yasna 9.8). He is the Iranian occupant of the cross-tradition **chaos-serpent** slot and the Avestan member of the inherited Indo-European dragon-slaying mytheme; in the Atlas he is placed among the chaos-beings alongside [[vritra]], [[tiamat]], [[typhon]], [[illuyanka]], and [[jormungandr]] because he fills the same role — the serpent of disorder overcome by a champion of cosmic order.

Across the long Iranian tradition the figure undergoes a striking transformation. In the **Avesta** he is a pure dragon. In the **Pahlavi** books he is **Dahāg** (also *Bēvar-Asp*, "he of ten thousand horses"), bound beneath a mountain until the end of time. In Ferdowsi's **Shāhnāmeh** (c. 1010 CE) he is fully **humanized** as **Zahhāk** (Ḍaḥḥāk), the Arab-descended tyrant from whose shoulders two black serpents grow, demanding human brains to feed them — the usurper who rules a thousand years until the smith Kāva and the hero Fereydun (the Persian reflex of Thraētaona) depose and chain him. Dragon, bound demon, and serpent-shouldered tyrant are three stages of one figure.

## In the Avesta and the Pahlavi tradition

The defining event is the combat with **Thraētaona** (Avestan Θraētaona, son of Āθwiya). It is told or invoked in at least four Yashts and the Hōm Yašt:

- **Yasht 5 (Ābān Yašt) 5.33–35** — Thraētaona sacrifices to the waters-goddess Arədvī Sūrā Anāhitā for the strength to overcome Aži Dahāka and free the two women Saŋhavāci and Arənavāci whom the dragon held.
- **Yasht 9 (Gōš / Drvāsp Yašt) 9.13–15**, **Yasht 15 (Rām Yašt) 15.23–25**, and **Yasht 17 (Aši Yašt) 17.33–35** — repeat the boon-request against "the three-mouthed, three-headed, six-eyed" dragon, naming him explicitly as a creation of Angra Mainyu against the material world.
- **Yasna 9.7–8 (Hōm Yašt)** — Thraētaona, "of the heroic clan of Āθwiya," is named as the one who **smote Aži Dahāka** after Haoma was first pressed; this is the passage that fixes the dragon's three-headed description.

Crucially, the Avestan hero **binds** the dragon rather than annihilating him. The Pahlavi sources (the **Bundahishn**, the **Dēnkard**) make the sequel explicit: Dahāg is chained beneath **Mount Damāvand** in the Alburz range, where he remains until the end of the world. In the apocalyptic **Zand ī Wahman Yasn**, at the close of the age the dragon **breaks his bonds** and devours a third of all living things, until the ancient hero **Keresāspa** (Pahlavi Kirsāsp, Persian Garshāsp), roused from death for the purpose, finally **slays** him — clearing the last great evil before the **Frašōkereti**, the Renovation of the world. The bound-then-loosed structure makes Aži Dahāka both a cosmogonic and an eschatological figure.

## Cross-tradition significance (the Iranian dragon and the Indo-European serpent-slaying)

Aži Dahāka is the **Iranian endpoint of the Indo-European dragon-slaying mytheme**, and the parallel to the **Vedic** material is the securest in the comparative dossier. The Avestan hero **Thraētaona son of Āθwiya** is the etymological and functional twin of the Vedic **Trita Āptya**, and both overcome a **three-headed serpent** — Aži Dahāka "the three-headed" answering the Vedic **Viśvarūpa Triśiras** ("having all forms, three-headed") whom Trita and Indra slay in Rigveda 10.8.8 and 10.99.6. This is a genuine **genealogical cognate**, an Indo-Iranian inheritance preserved independently in both daughter traditions rather than a borrowing of one from the other (Benveniste & Renou 1934; Watkins 1995; West 2007). The diagnostic shared epithet "three-headed" is what raises this above mere structural resemblance.

The wider Indo-European reach is carried by a **poetic formula**. Calvert Watkins (*How to Kill a Dragon*, 1995) reconstructed the Proto-Indo-European phrase *\*gʷhen- h₃gʷhim-* ("slew the serpent"), and **Yasht 5.39** preserves the Avestan reflex (*jan-* "smite" + *aži-* "serpent") as the direct cognate of Vedic *áhann áhim* ("[Indra] slew the serpent," Rigveda 1.32.1, of the Vṛtra combat). The same formula surfaces in Greek (Apollo and Python), Hittite (Illuyanka), and Norse (Thor and the world-serpent). Within this family the Iranian dragon stands beside [[vritra]] (Vedic), [[typhon]] (Greek), and [[illuyanka]] (Hittite) as a **true cognate reflex** — the persistence of the wording across separated branches over millennia being some of the strongest evidence in comparative mythology for an inherited mytheme (see the [[chaoskampf]] MASSIVE-WIN section).

The parallels to **non-Indo-European** chaos-serpents are of a different and weaker kind. The resemblance of Aži Dahāka to Mesopotamian [[tiamat]], Ugaritic-Hebrew Yam/[[leviathan]], or Egyptian Apophis is **structural / typological**, not genetic: Iranian is Indo-European and these traditions are not, so the shared "champion defeats chaos-serpent" pattern reflects either independent convergence on a natural mythic schema or the broad Bronze-Age cultural networks of the ancient Near East — not a demonstrable line of transmission. The Atlas marks this distinction on the edges themselves: the Vṛtra and Typhon links are typed *parallel-form* (cognate within IE), while the Tiamat and Leviathan links are typed *structural-parallel*.

A separate and more contested question is whether **Iranian apocalyptic** — the bound dragon loosed at the end of time and slain before a cosmic renewal — helped shape the **Second-Temple Jewish and Christian** imagery of the chained dragon (the binding of the dragon for an age, then his loosing and final destruction, in Revelation 20). Some scholars of the combat myth (Forsyth 1987) and of Zoroastrian–Jewish exchange treat the Iranian end-time dragon as part of the comparative background to apocalyptic; others caution that the dragon-binding motif is independently rooted in the older Near-Eastern Chaoskampf and that direct Iranian influence on this specific image is not securely demonstrated. The honest state of the question: the **Indo-Iranian cognate (Aži Dahāka ~ Viśvarūpa) is secure**; the **structural parallels to the non-IE chaos-serpents are real but typological**; and the **apocalyptic-transmission claim is plausible but not proven**.

## Disputes

The chief uncertainties concern **etymology and origin**, not the existence of the combat. The first element *aži* ("serpent") is uncontroversial and cognate with Vedic *áhi* and Greek *óphis*. The second element, **dahāka**, is debated: it has been connected to a root meaning "to sting / bite / harm," and separately to an ethnonym (the **Dahae**, an Iranian people), which would make "Dahāka" a designation of foreignness later literalized in the Shāhnāmeh's portrayal of Zahhāk as a non-Iranian usurper; Skjærvø (EIr 2011) reviews the proposals without a settled verdict. Second, scholars debate how much of the **humanized Zahhāk** of Ferdowsi is an old Iranian development and how much is a medieval Islamic-era reshaping (the serpent-shouldered tyrant, the Arab genealogy, the cannibal demand) layered onto the inherited dragon. What is **not** in dispute is the core Avestan datum — a three-headed serpent created by the Hostile Spirit and overcome by the hero Thraētaona — and its standing as the Iranian reflex of the Indo-European serpent-slaying myth.
