# Scylla and Charybdis

## Identity

Scylla (Σκύλλα) and Charybdis (Χάρυβδις) are the paired hazards of the narrow strait in Book 12 of the Odyssey — and they are a *pair* by design, which is why the Atlas holds them on one node. Homer sets their two rocks a bowshot apart (Od. 12.101-102): in the higher, sheer cliff lairs Scylla, a monster with twelve dangling feet and six long necks, each mouth carrying three rows of close-set teeth (12.89-91), whose voice is no louder than a newborn puppy's — an untranslatable play on her name and *skylax*, "puppy" (12.86). Beneath the low rock opposite, crowned by a great fig tree, Charybdis sucks down and belches forth the dark water three times a day (12.103-106). Neither can be defeated and both cannot be avoided: the strait forces a choice of which evil to sail nearer, and that forced choice is the pair's entire mythological function. Circe calls Scylla "not mortal, but an immortal evil" (12.118) — the textual basis, together with the vault's chaos-monster convention ([[typhon]], [[leviathan]]), for typing these uncultic beings as deities. Neither received worship; they are hazards of the mythic seascape, not objects of cult.

## In the Odyssey

The pair appears twice, in forewarning and in fact. **The briefing (Od. 12.55-126):** Circe — not Hermes, whose aid to Odysseus was the moly against Circe herself (10.277-306) — lays out the route options after the Sirens. One way leads through the Planctae, the drifting rocks that only one ship has ever passed, "the Argo, known to all" (12.69-72); the other through the strait of the two rocks. She describes both monsters, and when Odysseus asks whether he might ward off Scylla and fight clear of Charybdis (12.112-114), she rebukes him: there is no fighting this; flight is best, and the prayer that prevents a second strike is to Krataiis, Scylla's mother (12.116-126). Best to hug Scylla's side and lose six men than lose the ship whole to the whirlpool. **The passage (12.234-259):** while every eye watches Charybdis gulping and spewing, Scylla snatches six of the crew from the deck — Odysseus calls it the most pitiful thing his eyes saw in all his sea-wanderings (12.258-259). **The return (12.426-446):** after the crew's slaughter of the Sun's cattle on Thrinacia, Zeus's storm destroys the ship, and Odysseus alone is carried back to Charybdis; he clings to the fig tree above the vortex until his mast and keel are vomited up again, then drops onto them and paddles clear.

## Localization and reception

Already by the late fifth century BCE the strait was fixed in real geography: Thucydides (4.24.5) identifies the narrows between Rhegium and Messina as "the Charybdis where Odysseus is said to have sailed through," rationalizing the danger as the rush of converging seas. Strabo (1.2.15-16) preserves both the rationalizing tradition — including Polybius's account of the swordfish-hunters off the Scyllaean rock — and Eratosthenes' famous mockery of chart-minded readers: you will trace Odysseus's wanderings when you find the cobbler who sewed the bag of the winds. The Hellenistic and Roman poets elaborate the figures: Apollonius Rhodius has Thetis and the Nereids hand the Argo past the pair like a passed ball (Argonautica 4.789-846, 920-967), and names Scylla's mother as Hecate-Krataiis (4.828-829); Ovid supplies the nymph-backstory (Metamorphoses 13.730-14.74) in which Circe, jealous over Glaucus, poisons Scylla's bathing pool and transforms her waist into a ring of raging dog-heads. The mythographers file her in the monster-brood of Typhon and Echidna (Hyginus, Fabulae 151). Medieval Latin gives the proverb its canonical form — Walter of Châtillon's hexameter "incidis in Scyllam cupiens vitare Charybdim" (Alexandreis 5.301) — though proverbial two-evils use of the pair already circulates in the Greek paroemiographic tradition; Erasmus collects it in the Adagia, and it survives in English as "between Scylla and Charybdis": the canonical two-evils navigation image.

## Cross-tradition significance

The pair's cross-tradition story is mostly a story of **documented transmission, not convergence**, and it runs in two directions. *Within Greek epic*, the paired-hazard passage is shared repertoire: Homer himself cites the Argonautic tradition at the Planctae (Od. 12.69-72), and since Karl Meuli (1921) a strong line of scholarship — renewed by M. L. West (2005) — has argued that the Odyssey's fabulous wanderings rework a pre-Homeric Argonautica, making Scylla-Charybdis and the Clashing Rocks two drawings from one older stock of impossible-passage hazards. What is secure is Homer's own citation of the Argonautic tradition (Od. 12.69-72); the further step — that the Scylla-Charybdis episode itself reworks a pre-Homeric Argonautica — is a strong and much-renewed scholarly argument (Meuli 1921; West 2005), not a settled fact. *Downstream*, the reception chain — Greek epic to Latin school-text to medieval proverb to modern idiom — is fully documented, which is precisely why this pair, and not any other mythic hazard, became, through the Latin school tradition and Erasmus's Adagia, the proverbial Western image of the choice between two evils. By contrast, the *upstream* Near-Eastern comparanda are typological only: West (1997) sets the Odyssey's wonder-voyage beside Gilgamesh's crossing of the waters of death and the twin-peaked gate of Mashu, but no cognate, borrowing, or transmission is claimed for Scylla or Charybdis specifically, and the vault does not wire one. Devouring straits and monstrous whirlpools recur in seafaring lore worldwide as convergent responses to real tidal races; the honest statement is that the Greek pair is distinctive not for the hazard but for the *structure* — two evils, one forced choice.

Within the Atlas, the pair completes the [[theme-guide-through-underworld]] hub from the terrain side: the guide tradition's divine-advisor pattern (Hermes' moly, Circe's briefing) exists because terrain like this exists — hostile, supernatural, and unnavigable without forewarned intelligence. And against the [[chaoskampf]] pattern the pair is the instructive inversion: the monster the hero is explicitly forbidden to fight (Od. 12.116-120), where endurance, sacrifice of the six, and mētis replace the slaying.

## Disputes

**Genealogy.** The tradition never settled Scylla's parentage: Homer names only the mother Krataiis (Od. 12.124-125); Apollonius identifies Krataiis with Hecate (Arg. 4.828-829); the scholiastic tradition also offers Phorcys as father; Hyginus (Fab. 151) reassigns her wholesale to Typhon and Echidna. Charybdis has no Homeric genealogy at all — the Poseidon-and-Gaia parentage appears only in the late commentary tradition (Servius ad Aen. 3.420; the Homeric scholia offer Pontos-and-Gaia variants). The instability is itself evidence that the pair entered the tradition as narrative hazards first and were genealogized afterward. **Geography.** Whether Homer "meant" Messina was already an ancient quarrel: the localization is early (assumed by Thucydides) and the strait's real tidal races made it plausible, but Eratosthenes' skepticism has become the modern consensus — the wanderings are wonder-geography, and Messina is a secondary anchoring, not a decoding. **Priority.** The Meuli-West thesis that the episode reworks Argonautic material is strong but not universal; the alternative view treats the shared hazards as common oral-epic stock with no recoverable priority. Both positions are represented in the commentary tradition (Heubeck and Hoekstra 1989 ad Od. 12).
