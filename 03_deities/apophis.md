# Apophis (Ꜥꜣpp / Apep)

## Identity
Apophis (Egyptian *Ꜥꜣpp*, conventionally read *Apep*; Greek *Apōphis*) is the great chaos-serpent of Egyptian religion — the cosmic enemy of Ra and of *maʿat* (cosmic order), who attacks the solar barque each night as Ra passes through the *Duat* (underworld). He has no temple, no cult of worship (only of apotropaic-execration), no birth and no genealogy: he is the primordial non-being against which the ordered cosmos is constantly maintained (Hornung 1982). The Egyptian conception of Apophis is distinctive among the chaos-serpent type for being **resolutely non-anthropomorphic and ritually un-narrated**: there is no myth of Apophis's *origin* and no permanent defeat of him; he is the *daily* and *eternal* threat that the ritual life of Egypt is structured to repel.

## Earliest attestation
- **Coffin Texts** (~-2000 to -1700, Middle Kingdom) — the earliest extensive Apophis references in the underworld-journey corpus.
- **Book of the Dead** (~-1550 onward, New Kingdom) — the developed Apophis-slaying spells (chs. 7, 39, 108).
- **Book of Gates** and **Book of Amduat** (New Kingdom royal-tomb compositions) — the most pictorially developed underworld-Apophis scenes (the great serpent attacking the solar barque).
- **The Book of Overthrowing Apep** (P. Bremner-Rhind, ca. -310, Ptolemaic) — the principal apotropaic-execration text, with extensive Apophis-slaying spells.

## Mythological role
**The nightly combat.** As Ra's solar barque traverses the twelve hours of the night through the *Duat*, Apophis lies in wait in the seventh hour, attempting to swallow the sun. The defenders of the barque — Set (in his pre-demonized phase, often as a positive figure here), Mehen the protective serpent-coil, Sia, Hu, the Eye-of-Ra — repel the attack each night; the sun rises. This is the **cosmic-ritual structure** of Egyptian religion: the daily renewal of order against the eternally-recurring threat of dissolution.

**Apophis is never killed permanently.** This is the theological difference from the Indo-European and Semitic chaoskampf: in Egypt, the chaos-serpent is *contained* and *repelled*, but the threat is permanent. The ritual life of the temple — including the destruction of wax Apophis-effigies daily at sunrise and sunset, and the recitation of the *Overthrowing of Apep* spells — is what keeps the cosmos ordered.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[set]]** — *ancient-identification* (partial, late): post-Third Intermediate Period demonization of Set assimilates him to Apophis as a chaos-figure (Hornung 1982; te Velde 1967).
- **[[typhon]]** — *parallel-form / Greek interpretatio*: Plutarch identifies the Egyptian Set-Typhon-Apophis complex with Greek Typhon (*De Iside et Osiride*).
- **[[tiamat]]**, **[[lotan]]**, **[[illuyanka]]**, **[[vritra]]** — *parallel-form*: chaos-serpent type across Old World traditions; Egyptian is the non-Indo-European, non-Semitic instance.

## Iconography
The most distinctive Egyptian chaos-serpent iconography: Apophis depicted as an enormous, multiply-knotted serpent in New-Kingdom royal-tomb wall paintings (Valley of the Kings — the *Book of Gates* tableaux), attacked by Set or Mehen with spears, knives, and divine fire. The *Eye of Ra* is sometimes shown spitting fire at Apophis. Wax Apophis-effigies — known from Greco-Roman period temples — were ritually pierced, spat upon, dismembered, and burned.

## Disputes
- **Pre-Coffin-Texts existence.** Whether Apophis pre-dates the Middle Kingdom textual attestation is debated; some scholars see Pyramid-Texts-era allusions to a chaos-serpent (PT 226 etc.), others treat Apophis as a Middle-Kingdom theological development.
- **Identity with Set.** Hornung 1982 treats the Set-Apophis assimilation as a Late-Period theological development reflecting the demonization of Set; te Velde 1967 sees deeper roots. Both agree Set and Apophis are originally distinct: Set is a god (with cult, with temples, with priesthood) who *becomes* chaos-associated; Apophis is never a god in the cultic sense — he is the un-deity, the non-being against which all gods stand.
