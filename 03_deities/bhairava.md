# Bhairava

## Identity
Bhairava (Sanskrit, "the Terrifying") is the wrathful, terrifying form of Shiva and the central deity of much medieval Hindu Tantric tradition. In the Kashmir Trika non-dualist tradition, Bhairava is the supreme consciousness in its fierce active-Śaktic register — the *paramaśiva* in his manifest cosmic-fierce form. In the Kāpālika (skull-bearing) ascetic tradition (~7th–13th c. CE), Bhairava is the cultic central deity of antinomian *vāmācāra* (left-handed) Tantric practice — including cremation-ground meditation, skull-cup ritual use, taboo-transgression as soteriological practice. In urban Hindu cultic life, Kāla-Bhairava is the *kṣetra-pāla* (territorial guardian) of cities — most famously of Banaras (Varanasi), where Kāla-Bhairava's temple is the principal civic-protective shrine and the deity is considered the actual administrative authority over the city (a famous local saying: "Kāla-Bhairava is the *kotwāl* — police-chief — of Banaras"). The fierce iconography (naked or tiger-skin-clad, ash-smeared, holding severed head, garlanded in skulls, accompanied by black dogs) is among the most striking in the Hindu iconographic repertoire.

## Earliest attestation
- **Late Brahmaṇa references** to Rudra's fierce aspects (~late Vedic).
- **Tantric scriptural emergence** ~6th–8th c. CE: the *Svacchanda Tantra*, the *Brahma-yāmala*, and other early Bhairava-stream Tantric texts.
- **Sangam-era Tamil references** to fierce Shiva-cults.
- **Continuous epigraphic and architectural attestation** from the 7th c. onward across pan-Indian temple traditions.

## Mythological role
- **Severing of Brahmā's fifth head**: the cardinal Bhairava-myth. When Brahmā arrogantly claimed supremacy over Shiva, the latter manifested as Bhairava and severed Brahmā's fifth head with a fingernail. The severed head adhered to Bhairava's palm (the *brahmahatyā* — Brahmin-killing — sin), and he wandered for twelve years as an outcaste ascetic until reaching Banaras, where the head fell from his hand. The site became Kapālamocana ("the dropping of the skull"), the sacred site at Banaras where Kāla-Bhairava's principal temple stands. The myth simultaneously: (1) establishes Bhairava's iconographic skull-bearing; (2) provides the etiology for the Kāpālika ascetic tradition; (3) anchors Bhairava's cult-supremacy at Banaras; (4) embeds the antinomian-Tantric pattern (the supreme deity himself transgresses caste-purity norms).
- **Cremation-ground association**: Bhairava and his Śakti Bhairavī meditate in cremation grounds; the antinomian-Tantric ritual practices the meditation-on-death in those settings.
- **Vijñāna-Bhairava**: in the Vijñāna-Bhairava Tantra (Kashmir Shaivism), Bhairava is "Bhairava of Knowledge / Consciousness" — supreme consciousness questioned by his Śakti Bhairavī about the nature of liberation, with 112 *dhāraṇās* (concentration-techniques) as the answer. The text is one of the foundational Tantric meditation manuals.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[mahakala]]** (Vajrayāna Buddhist): the Vajrayāna Mahākāla is iconographically and structurally Bhairava-derivative; the Vajrayāna Buddhist pantheon's wrathful protector deities (Mahākāla, Yamāntaka, Hayagrīva) are substantially Śaiva-derivative per Sanderson 2009. The transmission is one of the most documented cross-tradition deity-flows of medieval Tantric South Asia.
- **Tibetan Tantric Buddhist Heruka and the wrathful *yidam* deities**: structurally cognate.
- **Nepali Bhairava cult**: Kathmandu Valley's syncretic Hindu-Buddhist religious culture has multiple major Bhairava cults — the Aṣṭa-Bhairava (Eight Bhairavas) protecting Kathmandu, the Indra-Jātrā festival's Akāś-Bhairava and Hāthu-Dyo procession, the Pāśupati-Bhairava-related ritual complexes.
- **Kāpālika ascetic tradition** (~7th–13th c.): the antinomian ascetic order that took Bhairava as cultic-central deity; ancestors of the later Nāth and Aghorī ascetic traditions. The Kāpālika legacy persists in the Aghora ascetic tradition based principally at Banaras and Tarapith (Bengal), where transgressive cremation-ground practice continues.

## Iconography
- Naked or in tiger-skin, ash-smeared.
- Holding a severed head (Brahmā's fifth head), drinking from a *kapāla* (skull-cup).
- Garlanded with skulls and severed limbs.
- Accompanied by black dogs (Bhairava's *vāhana* / vehicle).
- Trident, drum, and Tantric implements.
- The Kāla-Bhairava at Banaras is depicted seated on a dog, naked, blue-skinned, holding the skull-cup.

## Disputes
- **Date of Bhairava-cult emergence**: the philological evidence places the developed Bhairava cult ~6th–7th c. CE; some scholars argue for substantial earlier oral-cultic continuity reaching back to late-Vedic Rudra-fierce-aspects.
- **Kāpālika tradition's historical-vs.-stereotyped reality**: how much the Kāpālika tradition was a major institutional renouncer order (per Lorenzen 1972) vs. how much it was partly a hostile literary stereotype (per Sanderson) is contested. The Sanderson position is that the Kāpālikas were a real but smaller institutional tradition than literary representation suggests; Lorenzen treats them as a major medieval tradition.
- **Sanderson's Śaiva → Buddhist directionality** (2009): contested by Buddhist-studies scholars regarding the Mahākāla / wrathful-deity transmission.
