# Murugan

## Identity

Murugan — known in Sanskrit tradition as Skanda, Karthikeya, or Subramanya — is the supreme deity of Tamil Shaivism and the most widely worshipped god in Tamil Nadu and among the global Tamil diaspora. He is the son of [[shiva]] and [[parvati]], brother of [[ganesha]], and the divine warrior-youth who commands the gods' armies and defeats the demon Surapadman. His primary weapon is the *vel* — a divine lance given to him by his mother Parvati — which functions not merely as a weapon of war but as a piercing symbol of spiritual wisdom cutting through ignorance. He rides a peacock (whose magnificent beauty and ability to kill serpents makes it a symbol of the soul's triumph over desire and ego) and carries a rooster banner.

Murugan is unique among major Hindu deities in that Tamil literary and devotional tradition may preserve an independent, pre-Brahminic layer of his cult: the Tamil Sangam-period literature (ca. 300 BCE–300 CE) describes a mountain god associated with love, warfare, and forest landscapes (*kurinci* landscape in Tamil poetics — the mountainous region), and this figure gradually merged with the Sanskrit Skanda while retaining distinct Tamil theological emphases. In Tamil culture, Murugan is identified as the god of the Tamil language itself — some traditions hold that he revealed or inspired the Tamil language to the poet Agastya.

## Earliest attestation

- **Tamil Sangam literature** (ca. 300 BCE–300 CE): Murugan appears in early Tamil poetry as *Seyon* ("the red one") and *Murukan* — a mountain god associated with the *kurinci* (mountainous) landscape, love unions, and martial valor. The *Tirumurukārruppatai* ("Guide to Murugan's Shrines," ca. 100–300 CE), attributed to the poet Nakkirar, is an early dedicated poem celebrating his six abodes.
- **Sanskrit Mahabharata** (epic strata ca. 200 BCE–200 CE): Skanda receives his fullest Sanskrit mythological biography in the Vana Parva (book 3) — his birth from Agni and the Pleiades (Krittika), his appointment as general of the divine army, his defeat of Taraka.
- **Iconographic attestation**: Skanda/Murugan images from the Kushan period (1st–3rd c. CE) in northwest India and early South Indian temples.

## Mythological role

The principal mythological narrative of Murugan centers on his birth and martial mission:

The universe is threatened by the demon Surapadman (in Tamil tradition) or Taraka/Tarakasura (in Sanskrit tradition), whose boon from Brahma has made him invincible to any being except the son of Shiva. Shiva, however, is in deep meditation and will not produce a son — the gods are desperate. The god of desire (*Kama*) attempts to disturb Shiva's meditation and is incinerated by Shiva's third-eye fire. Parvati undertakes severe austerities to win Shiva as her husband, finally succeeding. Their union produces a child of incomparable radiance — Murugan — whose birth from six sparks or flames is caught by the six Pleiades (Krittika/Karthika), giving him six heads (Shanmukha). He grows instantly to divine adulthood, is given the vel by Parvati, and defeats the demons in cosmic battle, freeing the gods and restoring order.

Beyond the narrative, Murugan's theological significance in Tamil Shaivism is enormous: he is the *arul* (grace) of Shiva made accessible — the compassionate form of divine power that descends to earth on behalf of devotees. His six faces simultaneously see all six directions of the universe; his twelve arms protect all beings. The Shaiva Siddhanta philosophical tradition (Tamil Nadu) understands Murugan as the lord of *jnana* (wisdom), who initiates souls into liberation.

## Cross-tradition identifications

- **[[skanda-karthikeya]]** — *ancient-identification*: the Sanskrit pan-Hindu counterpart of Murugan; same deity in different cultural register. The Tamil tradition preserves emphases on grace, beauty, and the vel as symbol of wisdom-piercing-ignorance that sometimes differ from the northern Sanskrit martial emphasis.
- **[[shiva]]** — *ancient-identification* (parentage): Murugan as the son of Shiva is foundational; he is in Tamil theology sometimes understood as Shiva's *sakti* (power) made accessible.
- **[[parvati]]** — *ancient-identification* (parentage): Parvati gives Murugan the vel and is the model of devoted power behind his martial mission.
- **[[ganesha]]** — *ancient-identification* (sibling): the famous rivalry and reconciliation of Murugan and Ganesha (who wins the prize of divine fruit by circling his parents — the universe itself — while Murugan circles the globe literally) is a major narrative teaching the value of wisdom over mere mobility.
- **[[apollo]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: both are divine young males combining martial power with aesthetic beauty, intellectual patronage, and solar imagery; both are associated with arts (music for Apollo; Tamil poetry/language for Murugan); both represent the divine in its most beautiful, graceful, and excellent masculine form.
- **[[horus]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: divine warrior-child who avenges and restores the cosmic order; both Murugan and Horus are the young son of great divine parents who takes up arms to defeat the enemy of the divine order (Surapadman / Set).

## Iconography

Murugan is always depicted as a beautiful young man — the eternal youth, eternally unmarked by time — with six faces and twelve arms in his full cosmic form (Shanmukha), or with two arms in his more intimate, gracious human-scale form. He rides the peacock *Paravani* (the peacock who represents the soul or spiritual aspiration, also associated with the rainbow and beauty). He carries the vel (lance), a sword, and sometimes a bow; his banner carries a rooster (*seval*). Red is his color — the red of dawn, of battle valor, of the *kurinci* mountain flowers. The six Pleiades (the Krittika stars) are his nursing mothers. His face radiates light.

## Disputes

- The question of whether Tamil Murugan predates or postdates Sanskrit Skanda is a major scholarly debate; Hart (1975) and Clothey (1978) argue for partial independence of the Tamil tradition; others argue the identification was ancient and the Tamil and Sanskrit streams developed simultaneously in mutual influence.
- The number and identity of Murugan's consorts varies by region and tradition: in Tamil tradition his two consorts are Valli (a hunter's daughter — representing grace without prior devotional qualification) and Devasena (daughter of Indra — representing those who follow scriptural discipline). The two consorts represent different paths to divine grace.
