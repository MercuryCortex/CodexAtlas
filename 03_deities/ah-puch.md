# Ah Puch

## Identity
Ah Puch (also **Kisin**, "The Flatulent/Stinking One"; **Yum Cimil**, "Lord of Death"; **Hunhau**, "One Lord") is the Maya **lord of Xibalba** — the underworld of fear and disease beneath the earth. He is the most feared deity in the Maya pantheon, one of the **twelve lords of Xibalba** who torment the dead with disease, filth, cold, pain, and horror. He is identified as **Hun Came** ("One Death") in the Popol Vuh, one of the two supreme lords of Xibalba, who are ultimately defeated and humiliated by the Hero Twins **[[hunahpu]]** and **[[ixbalanque]]**. His iconography is among the most distinctive in Mesoamerican art: skeletal body, decomposing flesh, owl feathers, and a body adorned with bells or dark spots signifying putrefaction.

## Earliest attestation
- Classic Maya period iconography (c. 300–900 CE): skeletal death-deity figures (labeled "God A" in Schellhas's 1904 classification system) appear extensively in Classic Maya painted ceramics, codices, and stone reliefs.
- **Dresden Codex** (pre-conquest Maya screenfold book, c. 11th–12th c. CE): the death deity appears prominently in almanacs governing auspicious/inauspicious times.
- **Popol Vuh** (K'iche' Maya creation epic, recorded c. 1550s CE): the Xibalba lords including One Death (Hun Came / Ah Puch) are named and described in the Hero Twins narrative.
- **Diego de Landa's *Relación de las cosas de Yucatán*** (1566): the Franciscan bishop describes *Kisin* as the deity of death, noting the screech-owl as his messenger of death.

## Mythological role
**The twelve lords of Xibalba**: Xibalba is a structured multi-level underworld with twelve named lords who govern specific forms of suffering: One Death (Hun Came) and Seven Death (Vucub Came) rule overall; below them are lords of disease, cold, filth, pain, bone-shattering, and other afflictions. The elaborate *named* underworld — far more structured than most mythological afterlife realms — constitutes a theology of death that maps the specific horrors of human mortality onto a divine bureaucracy.

**The Hero Twins' victory**: In the Popol Vuh, the lords of Xibalba repeatedly kill the father of the Hero Twins ([[hunahpu]]) by tricking him into the underworld. When the Twins descend, they are subjected to successive "houses of horror" (Dark House, Cold House, Fire House, Razor House, Bat House). Through wit and ingenuity, they survive, ultimately *dying and resurrecting themselves* as the climactic demonstration of their power, and then defeating the Xibalba lords not by strength but by trickery — convincing the lords to submit to sacrifice, then refusing to resurrect them. The Xibalba lords are humiliated; their cult is diminished; they are henceforth only permitted to receive the offerings of the wretched.

**Owl messengers**: The screech-owl (*Chol* or *Muluc*) is Ah Puch's herald: its cry at night announces that death approaches a household. This owl-as-death-herald motif is documented by de Landa (1566) and remains in contemporary Maya folk belief.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[mictlantecuhtli]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Aztec skeletal death-lord of Mictlan; the closest parallel within Mesoamerica — both are skeletal, owl-associated lords of a structured underworld; independent developments within the broader Mesoamerican religious sphere.
- **[[hades]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: lord of the structured Greek underworld, feared and propitiated; both rule the dead with juridical sovereignty and are rarely depicted positively.
- **[[osiris]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: king of the Egyptian dead; both are simultaneously the decomposed body and the continuing power that judges/rules the dead. Both incorporate the idea that the death-lord himself has died.
- **[[ereshkigal]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: queen of the Sumerian Great Below; both rule a multi-level underworld with named regions and specific lords.
- **[[yama-vedic]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Vedic judge of the dead; both preside over the structured afterlife with specific regional sufferings for the unworthy.
- **[[baron-samedi]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Haitian Vodou death Lwa who shares the skeletal iconography and the grotesque-comedy approach to mortality. Both are terrifying yet culturally familiar death figures.
- **[[anubis]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: animal-messenger-associated death deity; both feature the sacred animal as the herald/mediator between the living and dead worlds.

## MASSIVE WIN: the structured hierarchical underworld

Xibalba with its **twelve named lords and six houses of torment** represents a distinctive theological elaboration — the underworld as bureaucracy:

| Underworld | Tradition | Named regions/lords | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Xibalba** (12 lords, 6 houses) | K'iche' Maya | Yes — named individually | *Popol Vuh* |
| **The 10 Buddhist Hells** | Buddhist | Yes — named courts with named judges | *Ksitigarbha Sutra* |
| **Dante's Hell** (9 circles) | Medieval Christian | Yes — named sins and punishments | *Inferno* |
| **The Duat** (12 gates) | Egyptian | Yes — named gate-guardians | *Amduat* |
| **Mictlan** (9 levels) | Aztec | Yes — 9 levels with obstacles | Various |
| **Naraka** (multiple) | Hindu | Yes — named hells | *Puranas* |

The pattern: the afterlife is not simply "below the earth" but a structured administrative system with named zones, named officials, and differential punishments calibrated to the life lived. This theological move — organizing death — appears independently across traditions.

## MASSIVE WIN: the owl as death messenger

The owl-as-death-herald is one of the most widely distributed animal-omen patterns in human culture:
- **Maya**: Ah Puch's screech-owl announces death (de Landa, 1566; continues in Yucatec folk belief today)
- **Roman**: Cicero records the owl as a death omen; Augustus reportedly saw an owl before his death
- **Hindu**: the owl (*uluka*) as Lakshmi's vehicle carries ambivalent death/wealth associations; screech-owl cry is a death omen in rural India
- **European folk tradition**: "An owl seen in the daytime / hooting at night = death in the family" — documented from medieval Britain, France, Germany, Spain
- The owl's nocturnal nature, silent flight, haunting call, and ability to rotate its head create an uncanny quality that independently generates death-association across cultures

## Iconography
Ah Puch is one of the most iconographically distinctive deities in Mesoamerican art:
- **Skeletal body** with visible ribs and vertebrae
- **Skull head** or a head with decomposing flesh and exposed bones
- **Black spots** on skin signifying putrefaction (*God A* in codex form)
- **Owl feathers** incorporated into dress
- **Bells** (*cascabeles*) attached to the body — in some interpretations, bell-rings announce death's approach
- **Bloated abdomen** suggesting decomposition
