# Ganesha

## Identity
Gaṇeśa — "Lord of the *gaṇas*" (Śiva's host of attendants) — is the elephant-headed son of Śiva and Pārvatī, the remover and placer of obstacles, and the deity first invoked at the beginning of any Hindu ritual or new venture. Iconography: elephant head (with one tusk broken, the famous *Ekadanta* — myths variously explaining the breakage), pot-belly, four arms holding a noose, a goad, a sweet (*modaka*), and various gesture-mudras; mounted on a small mouse / rat (*mūṣaka*). In Hindu tradition, when Pārvatī created Gaṇeśa from the substance of her own body and posted him as guard, Śiva (not recognizing the newly created son) beheaded him; Pārvatī's grief required Śiva to revive him with the head of the first creature found — an elephant. As scribe, Gaṇeśa is the divine writer of the *Mahābhārata* under Vyāsa's dictation (a tradition that inserts him into the Bhārata transmission). The five-day festival *Gaṇeśa Caturthī* (especially central in Maharashtra) is a major modern public celebration.

## Earliest attestation
Gaṇeśa's emergence as a distinct deity is post-Vedic (~100 CE onward); earlier Vedic references to *Gaṇapati* and *Brahmaṇaspati* are linguistic ancestors but not iconographically Gaṇeśa proper. Iconographic sculpture: from ~4th–5th c. CE onward, widely spread by the Gupta period.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[hermes-greek]]** — *parallel-motif*: both threshold-figures, scribes, patrons of language and travelers (no historical link).
- Tibetan / East Asian Buddhism: Gaṇeśa transmitted as protective deity (*Mahā-Gaṇapati*) and as obstacle / hindrance to be tamed (*Vināyaka* in Japanese esoteric Buddhism, Shingon's *Kangiten*).
