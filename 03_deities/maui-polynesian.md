# Māui

## Identity
Māui is the great pan-Polynesian trickster and demigod — one of the most widely distributed mythological figures in Oceania. Found across Māori, Hawaiian, Tahitian, Samoan, Tongan, and other Polynesian traditions, Māui is a semi-divine culture-hero who performs impossible feats through cunning and magic rather than brute force: fishing up islands from the ocean floor, lassoing the sun to slow it, stealing fire for humanity, and ultimately dying in his failed attempt to win immortality by crawling through the body of the death-goddess Hine-nui-te-pō. His name and core narrative cluster are attested across the full breadth of Polynesia, making him a compelling datum for the reconstruction of ancestral Polynesian religion.

## Earliest attestation
Reconstructed to ancestral Polynesian tradition (~1000 BCE onward by comparative linguistics); the *Māui* name-cluster and core episodes (fishing up land, sun-snaring) are attested in every major Polynesian tradition. Māori tradition records elaborate genealogies and narrative cycles; Beckwith's *Hawaiian Mythology* (1940) provides the Hawaiian version. The pre-literate oral attestation is rich; written versions begin with European-era recording (~1820s onward).

## Mythological role
**Fishing up the islands**: Māui uses a magical fishhook (fashioned from his grandmother Murirangawhenua's jawbone in Māori tradition) to pull up islands from the ocean floor — the North Island of New Zealand in Māori tradition, the Hawaiian Islands in Hawaiian versions, various island-groups in other traditions. The land-fishing episode is the primary cosmogonic act attributed to him.

**Slowing the sun**: The sun moved too fast, leaving insufficient daylight for cooking and working. Māui braided ropes of his sister's hair or twisted flax, lassoed the sun at its rising, and beat it with his grandmother's jawbone until it agreed to move slowly. This episode is a *solar regulation myth* — the demigod imposes civilized time on a cosmos that initially runs too fast.

**Stealing fire**: Māui steals fire for humanity from Mahuika (fire-deity, his ancestor). The fire-theft narrative parallels Prometheus in function; Māui repeatedly re-extinguishes it to steal more, until Mahuika in fury throws all fire at the world — Tāne shelters humanity in the trees, and fire is preserved in the wood (the origin of fire-by-friction).

**Death of Māui**: Seeking immortality, Māui attempts to crawl into the sleeping body of Hine-nui-te-pō (the death-goddess) while his companions, the birds, are sworn to silence. A fantail laughs, waking the goddess, who crushes Māui — ending the demigod's life and sealing human mortality. The failed immortality quest is one of the most structurally resonant episodes: the hero is killed by death itself, and mortality is thereby confirmed as the human condition.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[tangaroa]]** — *structural-contrast*: Tangaroa is Māui's rival and (in some tellings) family member; Māui's fish-up-the-islands intrudes on Tangaroa's oceanic domain. The tension between Māui (land-maker, transformer) and Tangaroa (sea, conservation) runs through Polynesian cosmology.
- **[[tane]]** — *mythological-sibling*: In Māori tradition, Tāne and Māui share the cosmos of the children of Ranginui and Papatūānuku; Tāne shelters humanity during Māui's fire-theft disaster.
- **Prometheus (Greek)** — *scholarly-parallel*: Both are fire-thieves who acquire fire for humanity through trickery, suffer consequences, and embody the figure of the transgressive benefactor.

## Iconography
Traditionally depicted with his fishhook (hei matau) — a key taonga (treasure) in Māori material culture; the hei matau (fish-hook pendant) is still worn as a symbol of strength and luck. The jawbone weapon (from his grandmother Murirangawhenua) is a distinctive attribute. In contemporary and tourist art, Māui is often depicted as muscular, tattooed, bearing the jawbone hook.

## Disputes
The degree to which "Māui" is a single unified figure vs. a cluster of trickster-hero narratives accreted around a name is debated. The Hawaiian Māui and the Māori Māui share core episodes but differ in genealogy and social context. Whether the *death in Hine-nui-te-pō* episode is pan-Polynesian or specifically Māori is unresolved in the literature.
