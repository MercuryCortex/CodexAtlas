# Shu

## Identity
Shu is the Egyptian god of air and the atmospheric void between sky (Nut) and earth (Geb). With his sister-consort Tefnut (Moisture) he is the first-born of Atum, beginning the Heliopolitan Ennead's generational structure: Atum → Shu/Tefnut → Geb/Nut → Osiris/Isis/Set/Nephthys. His role is structurally cosmogonic — he is the *separation* itself, the principle that holds heaven and earth apart and so makes the world habitable.

## Earliest attestation
- [[phase-1-002-pyramid-texts]] — fully present in Old Kingdom Heliopolitan theology.
- [[phase-1-009-coffin-texts]] include a "Spells of the Shu-theology" cluster (Coffin Texts 75–83) in which Shu is the first articulator of cosmic difference.

## Mythological role
- **Separation of Nut and Geb.** The Ennead's central cosmogonic act: Shu steps between his children Nut and Geb and lifts Nut into the sky position. Iconography across Egyptian art: Shu stands with arms raised supporting Nut's arched body, sometimes with other gods raising her feet and hands.
- **Atumic emanation.** Atum produces Shu and Tefnut by self-generation (variously by masturbation, by sneezing, by spitting — different texts give different versions); they are the first pair of opposites whose union begets the cosmos.
- **Solar boat companion.** Shu travels with Ra in the day-boat across the sky.
- **Identified with sunlight.** As the air that holds light, Shu is also associated with the bright atmosphere of the day.

## Iconography
- Standing male figure with arms raised
- Single ostrich feather on his head (the *šw* hieroglyph)
- Sometimes lion-headed, particularly when paired with the leonine Tefnut
- Often shown supporting Nut
