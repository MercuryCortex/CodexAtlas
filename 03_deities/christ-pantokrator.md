# Christ Pantokrator

The iconographic Christ-form of Byzantine and Eastern Orthodox tradition: enthroned, bearded, in the apse of a cross-domed church, right hand raised in blessing, gospel book in left, cruciform halo. The earliest surviving full Pantokrator icon is the 6th-century example at the [Monastery of Saint Catherine, Sinai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christ_Pantokrator) — the oldest extant Christian portrait icon.

The Pantokrator emphasizes cosmic kingship and judgment, not the redemptive suffering of Western crucifixion-focused piety. Doctrinally identical to [[jesus-christ-deity|Christ]] but iconographically and devotionally distinct — it's the figure that the worshipper sees overhead in every dome of every Orthodox church.

Thomas F. Mathews (1993, *The Clash of Gods*) argued — controversially — that early Christian imperial Christology and its visual culture deliberately appropriated late-antique pagan visual conventions, including [[zeus|Zeus]] Pantokrator iconographic types, to claim cosmic sovereignty for Christ. His thesis is part of a broader reconsideration of early Christian art as polemic-by-image against Roman religious culture rather than merely passive cultural absorption.

The Pantokrator is the load-bearing Eastern Christian image; its absence in the Latin West (apart from import) corresponds to different liturgical and theological emphases.
