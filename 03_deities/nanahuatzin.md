# Nanahuatzin

## Identity

Nanahuatzin ("the Pimply One" or "Full of Sores") is the humble, disease-ridden Aztec god who becomes the **sun of the present age** by an act of self-sacrifice. His lowly, suffering condition is essential to the meaning of his myth: it is the poorest and sickest of the gods, not the proud and wealthy one, who has the courage to die and be reborn as the sun.

## Mythological role

Nanahuatzin's myth is the Aztec **creation of the Fifth Sun** at Teotihuacan, told most fully in Sahagún's *Florentine Codex* (Book 7). In the darkness after the destruction of the previous world, the gods gather and build a great sacrificial fire, asking who will throw himself in to become the new sun. The rich, proud god **[[tecciztecatl]]** volunteers, but four times he approaches the flames and flinches back, afraid. Then the humble, scab-covered Nanahuatzin, who has been doing penance with thorns and his own blood, simply **leaps into the fire** without hesitation and is consumed. Shamed, Tecciztecatl follows. Nanahuatzin rises in the east as the radiant sun — **[[tonatiuh]]** — while Tecciztecatl rises as a second sun, until a god hurls a rabbit into his face to dim him into the moon. But the new sun hangs motionless and demands blood; so the assembled gods sacrifice themselves, offering their hearts to set the sun in motion — establishing the cosmic debt that human sacrifice would forever repay.

## Cross-tradition significance

Nanahuatzin embodies the central Aztec theological conviction that **the cosmos is sustained by sacrifice** — the sun moves only because the gods (and, in imitation, humans) offer their blood. His myth is the great Mesoamerican instance of **creation through self-immolation** and of the **humble figure exalted** through willingness to die — a pattern that resonates with the wide cross-tradition theme of life and cosmic order won through a god's self-giving death.
