# Lugalbanda

## Identity
Lugalbanda ("Junior King" / "Young King" — Sumerian *lugal* "king" + *banda* "junior, young, fierce") is a Sumerian heroic king of Uruk, posthumously deified, who serves in Mesopotamian epic tradition as the **father of Gilgamesh**. The Sumerian King List names Lugalbanda as a king of the first dynasty of Uruk with an extraordinary reign-length (1200 years in the King List's heroic-age idiom — see Glassner 2004). Two surviving Sumerian narrative epics center on him: **Lugalbanda in the Mountain Cave** (Lugalbanda I) and **Lugalbanda and the Anzud Bird** (Lugalbanda II), both belonging to the broader "Matter of Aratta" cycle that also includes *Enmerkar and the Lord of Aratta* and *Enmerkar and Ensuhgirana* (Vanstiphout 2003). In the canonical Babylonian *Gilgamesh* tradition Lugalbanda is invoked as Gilgamesh's divine father; Gilgamesh's mother is the goddess **Ninsun**, and through Lugalbanda's posthumous deification Gilgamesh acquires his "two-thirds divine, one-third human" formula (George 2003).

## Earliest attestation
- **Sumerian King List** (~-2100 redaction, older traditions): Lugalbanda among the kings of the first dynasty of Uruk, reign-length given in the heroic-age idiom. ([[phase-1-015-sumerian-king-list]])
- **Lugalbanda in the Mountain Cave** (Sumerian, Old Babylonian copies c. -1800 of an older composition; Black et al. 2004; ETCSL 1.8.2.1).
- **Lugalbanda and the Anzud Bird** (Sumerian, Old Babylonian copies; Black et al. 2004; ETCSL 1.8.2.2).
- **Standard Babylonian *Gilgamesh*** (~-1300 to -1100 standard form; older Old Babylonian *Gilgamesh* layer; George 2003): Lugalbanda invoked as Gilgamesh's father. ([[phase-1-004-gilgamesh-old-babylonian]])

## Mythological role
The two Lugalbanda epics relate the young king's exploits during a military expedition against the eastern city of Aratta. In **Lugalbanda I** (*Lugalbanda in the Mountain Cave*), Lugalbanda falls sick during the march and is abandoned in a cave; he prays, recovers through divine intervention, and rejoins the army. In **Lugalbanda II** (*Lugalbanda and the Anzud Bird*), Lugalbanda encounters and befriends the **Anzud-bird** (the lion-headed eagle-like cosmic bird), feeds its young, and is rewarded with extraordinary running-speed — using which he serves as a messenger between the Uruk army and Inanna, securing divine favor for Uruk's victory over Aratta. The Anzud-bird episode is among the most-cited single passages in Sumerian heroic literature (Vanstiphout 2003).

His subsequent epic role is generative rather than narrative: he is the father of Gilgamesh, and Gilgamesh's divine-human status derives in part from Lugalbanda's posthumous deification (George 2003 documents this from the standard Babylonian text).

## Cross-tradition identifications
None directly attested; Lugalbanda is a tradition-internal Mesopotamian deified-king figure without ancient cross-cultural identifications. Comparative-religion claims linking him to Indo-European heroic-king types (e.g., Watkins 1995 on inherited PIE heroic-poetic vocabulary) operate at the level of *parallel-form* rather than ancient identification.

## Iconography
Limited direct iconographic attestation. Bull-related imagery is associated with him in the surviving epic (the *banda* epithet carries a wild-bull connotation in Sumerian heroic idiom — Vanstiphout 2003), but no canonical statue type or seal-iconography is securely identified as Lugalbanda. He is best known from the literary record rather than the visual record.

## Disputes
- **Historicity.** Whether the Sumerian King List's Lugalbanda corresponds to any historical king of Early Dynastic Uruk is unresolved — the King List's pre-flood and early Uruk reign-lengths are heroic-age idiom rather than historical chronology (Glassner 2004). The epic Lugalbanda may be a legendary elaboration of an obscure historical figure or a wholly literary construction.
- **Relation of the two Lugalbanda epics.** Whether Lugalbanda I and II form a continuous narrative (the cave-sickness setting up the Anzud-bird encounter) or are two independent compositions about the same hero is partly contested; mainstream scholarship treats them as a two-part cycle (Vanstiphout 2003).
