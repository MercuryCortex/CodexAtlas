# Bandua

## Identity
Bandua is the most widely and evenly distributed deity in the entire Lusitanian-Gallaecian religious corpus — with over 50 Latin votive inscriptions spread across the northwestern Iberian Peninsula from the Douro valley northward through modern Galicia. Unlike most Lusitanian deities, who are hyper-local (attested only in a single valley or at one cult site), Bandua appears almost everywhere in the Celtic northwest — but always with a local epithet specifying *which* Bandua: *Bandua Veigebreaego* (of Veigebraega), *Bandua Roudeaeco* (of the Roudeaecum territory), *Bandua Araugel(e)nsi* (of Araugela), and so on.

This pattern suggests that "Bandua" functioned as a **deity-type or generic title** — the protective divinity of a specific *castro* (hilltop settlement) or territory — rather than a single deity with a fixed mythology. Every hilltop community had its Bandua; the name is the function.

## Etymology: The Binding God
The etymology of *Bandua* is analyzed by Prósper (2002) as deriving from PIE *\*bʰendʰ-* ("to bind") — the same root that produces:
- Latin *offendimentum* (obligation, binding)
- Sanskrit *bandha* (bond, tie)
- Germanic *band* (cord, fetter) → English "band"

If correct, Bandua is literally "the Binder" — the deity who binds the community together, who creates and enforces the obligations between people within the territory. This is not martial in the aggressive Ares/Mars sense but in the *territorial-sovereign* sense: Bandua is the deity of the social-territorial bond, the force that holds the *castro* community together against external threat and internal dissolution.

The contrast with [[nabia]] (river, flowing water, the land's fertility) and [[ataecina]] (underworld, regeneration, the dead) is structurally clean:
- **Nabia** = the living earth, water, abundance — what sustains the community
- **Ataecina** = the underworld, death, and regenerative return — what follows death
- **Bandua** = the community's defense and binding force — what protects the living from external threat

Together these three, plus [[endovelicus]] (healing oracle, mediator with the divine), constitute the functional core of Lusitanian-Gallaecian religion as attested epigraphically.

## Cross-Tradition Parallels

**Gaulish Dunatis and the Atlantic fortress-god type**
The Gaulish deity *Dunatis* (from *dūno-* = fortified hill, hillfort) is a structural cognate: the deity of the *dun*/hillfort who protects the enclosed community. Both Bandua and Dunatis are Celtic protection-deities whose identity is inseparable from the defended elevated settlement. The *castro* (Galicia) / *dun* (Ireland, Scotland) / *dūnum* (Gaul) is the fundamental Celtic settlement form — and each tradition has a deity to match. See [[tradition-celtic]].

**Mars Ultor and the Roman protective-war type**
The Roman identification of Bandua with Mars (partial, in later inscriptions) shows how a Roman colonizer would read the function. But Bandua is not Mars Bellator (the aggressive war-god) — he is closer to Mars Ultor (the avenger of violation) or to the Lares (household/community protection deities). The difference matters: Mars is the god of Roman military expansion; Bandua is the god of Gallaecian territorial resistance. They are functional opposites who happened to share a martial vocabulary.

**Irish Lugh and the sovereign-protector archetype**
The Irish god [[lugh]] — *Lugh Samhildanach* ("the Many-Skilled") — combines solar, martial, and sovereign functions; he leads the Tuatha Dé Danann against the Fomorians (the hostile exterior force). Bandua's territorial protection function is structurally parallel to Lugh's community-defender role. Both are Celtic divinities whose function is "the deity that stands between the community and its destruction."

## The *Castro* Landscape
Bandua cannot be understood without the *castro* — the hilltop enclosed settlement that defines Gallaecian-Lusitanian material culture from ~800 BCE through the Roman conquest. The *castro* is a fortified hilltop village: stone walls, circular huts, commanding views in all directions. Over 4,000 *castros* are identified in the archaeology of Galicia and northern Portugal alone.

The *castro* hilltops are often the same hilltops where megalithic monuments stood 3,000 years earlier — the sacred high-points of the landscape are occupied, reoccupied, and re-sacralized across multiple cultural cycles. Bandua is the deity of the *castro* in its defensive social function; the [[tradition-megalithic-atlantic|megalithic tradition]] explains why those hilltops were already sacred when the Celts arrived.
