# Mullissu

## Identity
Mullissu (also Mulliltu, Mulissu) is the Akkadian/Assyrian rendering of Sumerian [[nin-lil-sumerian]] — chief consort of the senior high god and mother of the major deities (Sin, Nergal, Ninurta). The name is etymologically *NIN.LÍL* (the cuneiform writing is the same), with the Akkadian pronunciation Mullissu emerging in the Old Babylonian period and becoming standard by the Middle Babylonian and Neo-Assyrian periods. Her theological role tracks the migration of the high-god office across Mesopotamian political history: when Enlil is the imperial high god (Sumerian and Old Babylonian), she is consort of Enlil; when Ashur replaces Enlil at the head of the Assyrian state pantheon (Middle Assyrian and Neo-Assyrian), Mullissu is reassigned as consort of [[ashur]] — the same goddess in a new theological-political configuration. In the Neo-Assyrian period she is increasingly syncretized with Ishtar (especially Ishtar of Nineveh and Ishtar of Arbela), absorbing oracular and warrior functions originally separate from the older Ninlil-Mullissu profile.

The Greek reception of Mullissu via Herodotus (*Histories* 1.131, 1.199) gives the form **Mylitta** — Herodotus identifies Mylitta with Aphrodite Ourania and describes an alleged sacred-prostitution rite at her temple in Babylon. The Mylitta = Mullissu identification is the standard scholarly view but rests on this single Greek source, and the further Mylitta = Aphrodite chain is a Greek *interpretatio*, not a Mesopotamian theological claim. The Herodotean sacred-prostitution narrative itself is contested (most current scholarship reads it as Greek ethnographic stereotyping rather than reliable Babylonian ritual practice).

## Earliest attestation
- Old Babylonian sources begin using the Akkadian pronunciation Mullissu (alongside the Sumerian-writing NIN.LÍL).
- Middle Assyrian royal inscriptions name Mullissu as consort of Ashur from the 14th century BCE.
- Neo-Assyrian state-temple records: Mullissu has her own cella in the Ashur temple at Assur; she is invoked in royal-grant formulae alongside Ashur.
- The **Neo-Assyrian prophecies** (Parpola 1997) record oracular pronouncements attributed to Mullissu (in syncretism with Ishtar of Nineveh and Ishtar of Arbela) addressing Esarhaddon and Ashurbanipal — a striking attestation of female-divine prophetic patronage in the imperial Assyrian period.
- Herodotus, *Histories* 1.131, 1.199 (~-440 BCE) names "Mylitta" as the Assyrian-Babylonian name for Aphrodite Ourania.

## Mythological role
Mullissu inherits Ninlil's mythological profile in the Akkadian retellings:
- ***Mother of the major deities***: Sin (moon), Nergal (netherworld), and Ninurta (warrior-farmer) are her sons in the standard Akkadian theology, as they were Ninlil's in Sumerian.
- ***Consort of the imperial high god***: structurally she is the queen-of-the-pantheon, whose theological role tracks whoever sits on the high god's throne — Enlil in the older system, Ashur in the Neo-Assyrian.
- ***Neo-Assyrian oracular patron***: in the Assyrian prophetic corpus Mullissu (often in fused identification with Ishtar of Nineveh) speaks oracles of protection and victory to the king. This role is a Neo-Assyrian theological development; it is not strongly present in the older Ninlil-Mullissu profile.
- ***Greek reception as Mylitta***: Herodotus's account of the alleged sacred-prostitution rite at "Mylitta's" Babylon temple is the most famous Greek text on Mesopotamian religion. The text is read variously as ethnographic distortion, as reflecting an actual (but misunderstood) ritual practice, or as a stereotyping topos applied to Eastern femininity. Current consensus is closer to the first.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[nin-lil-sumerian]]** — *ancient-identification*: the Mullissu = Ninlil equation is foundational and uncontested. The two are the same goddess in Sumerian and Akkadian.
- **[[ashur]]** — *consort* (Neo-Assyrian): Mullissu is paired with Ashur as imperial queen-consort, replacing the older Enlil-Ninlil pairing as Ashur replaces Enlil at the head of the Assyrian state pantheon.
- **Ishtar of Nineveh / Ishtar of Arbela** — *Neo-Assyrian syncretism*: in the Neo-Assyrian prophetic and royal corpora Mullissu is increasingly fused with the warrior-oracular Ishtars; this is a late development, not original to the Mullissu profile.
- **Mylitta (Greek, Herodotus)** — *Greek reception*: the standard scholarly identification, but it rests on a single Greek source and the further Mylitta = Aphrodite chain is Greek *interpretatio*, not Mesopotamian theology.
- Distinguish from the Hurrian/Hittite Hebat, sometimes scholarly-paralleled but not an ancient identification.

## Iconography
No fixed independent iconography in the older stratum; Mullissu appears as a seated goddess in horned crown beside Enlil or Ashur. In the Neo-Assyrian period her syncretism with Ishtar of Nineveh brings lion-and-stars iconography, though this fully belongs to the Ishtar side of the identification.

## Disputes
- **Mylitta = Mullissu**. This is the standard scholarly identification but rests on a single source (Herodotus). Lambert 2004 treats it as well established. A minority view holds that "Mylitta" is a corrupt or misheard form of another theonym (occasionally Belili or even Ninlil-Belit-ili is proposed). The majority position remains Mylitta = Mullissu = Ninlil.
- **The Herodotean sacred-prostitution narrative**. Herodotus 1.199 describes every Babylonian woman as required to prostitute herself once at the temple of Mylitta. Whether this reflects (a) actual Babylonian ritual practice misunderstood, (b) a specific ritual class (the *qadishtu* or *nadiitu* priestesses) sensationalized, or (c) pure Greek ethnographic stereotyping is heavily debated. The recent consensus (since Beard & Henderson 1997, and Stephanie Budin's 2008 *The Myth of Sacred Prostitution in Antiquity*) leans strongly toward (c) — there is no clear Mesopotamian-internal evidence for the rite as Herodotus describes it.
- **Mullissu's syncretism with Ishtar of Nineveh**. The Neo-Assyrian fusion of Mullissu with Ishtar is a real theological development but uneven in the sources; Parpola 1997 reads it as systematic, others as more occasional. The Ishtar identification is not present in the older Mullissu profile.
