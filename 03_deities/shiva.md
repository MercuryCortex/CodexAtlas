# Shiva

## Identity
Shiva (Sanskrit: *Śiva*, "the Auspicious") is one of the two supreme deities of the developed Hindu pantheon (with Viṣṇu), the central deity of all Śaiva traditions, and the transcendent supreme consciousness in the Kashmir Trika non-dualist Shaivism developed by Vasugupta, Utpaladeva, and Abhinavagupta (~800–1100 CE). The development from the Vedic *Rudra* — the outsider storm-god of the Rig Veda — to the classical Shiva of medieval Hinduism is one of the principal long-arc deity-developments in any religion. Shiva is simultaneously: cosmic destroyer-recreator (in the classical Trimūrti scheme), supreme ascetic-yogi meditating in the Himalaya, householder-with-Pārvatī, terrifying-Bhairava of cremation grounds, dancing-Naṭarāja whose dance is the rhythm of cosmic creation/destruction, transcendent supreme consciousness of Kashmir non-dualism, and the immobile cosmic *liṅga* of temple-iconographic worship. The constitutive paradox — asceticism and eroticism, destruction and creation, ferocity and beneficence — is the central interpretive framework for the deity (Doniger 1973).

## Earliest attestation
- **Rig Veda** (~1500–1200 BCE): the deity Rudra, the outsider storm-god, the wild howler. Rudra hymns are ambivalent — Rudra is dangerous and propitiated rather than loved. See [[rudra-shiva-early]] for the Vedic-period figure.
- **Indus Valley seals** (~2500–1900 BCE): the "Paśupati seal" from Mohenjo-Daro depicts a horned, ithyphallic, yogic-posed figure surrounded by animals. The identification with proto-Shiva (Marshall 1931) has been periodically contested (Asko Parpola, Doris Srinivasan) but remains a plausible early proto-Shaiva visual attestation.
- **Śatarudriya** (Yajurveda): the "Hundred Names of Rudra" liturgy; one of the earliest expanded litanies and a foundational Shaiva ritual text.
- **Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad** (~5th–4th c. BCE; see [[phase-2-021-shvetashvatara-upanishad]]): the principal proto-theistic emergence of Rudra-Śiva as ultimate divine principle.

## Mythological role
The principal mythic complexes:
- **Burning of Kāma**: Śiva burns the god of love Kāma with his third eye when Kāma tries to disrupt Śiva's meditation — the foundational story of Śiva's ascetic ferocity.
- **Marriage to Pārvatī**: Śiva's relationship to Pārvatī (Satī's reincarnation), centered on the dynamic of ascetic-husband and erotic-wife — the deity-couple-with-tension that Doniger 1973 reads as constitutively paradoxical.
- **Cosmic dance (*tāṇḍava*)**: the Naṭarāja form depicting Śiva dancing the cosmos into and out of existence — the central iconographic image of Tamil Shaivism (Chola bronze sculpture is the supreme artistic expression).
- **Halāhala-poison swallowing**: at the cosmic ocean-churning, Śiva swallows the world-poison to save creation, holding it in his throat (which turns blue — *Nīlakaṇṭha*).
- **Liṅga**: the abstract aniconic form. The *Śiva-liṅga* — usually a cylindrical stone form set in a *yoni* base — is the principal Hindu temple-iconographic object. The liṅga is theologically read in the developed tradition not as primarily phallic (though that reading is also classical) but as the formless-form, the cosmic-axis, the unmoving point of supreme consciousness.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[rudra-shiva-early]]** — *continuous-development*: the Vedic Rudra → classical Shiva development is one of the principal long-arc deity-transformations in Hindu tradition. See [[rudra-shiva-early]].
- **Possible Indo-European parallels (cautious)**: comparative Indo-European studies have proposed structural-functional parallels to other Indo-European outsider/wild deities (Dionysus in Greek; Cernunnos in Celtic; Odin in some readings) — but the parallels are structural-typological, not historical-genealogical at the level of direct transmission (Doniger 1973, Mallory & Adams 2006). The deeper Indo-Iranian inheritance: Rudra is cognate in some respects with Avestan ahuric/daevic figures (the philological argument is more contested for Rudra than for Indra, Mitra, Varuṇa, Soma).
- **Buddhist Vajrayāna identifications**: in Tantric Buddhist iconography, several "wrathful" Buddhas (Mahākāla, Hayagrīva, Yamāntaka) are explicitly Shaiva-derivative or syncretically Shaiva-cognate. Sanderson 2009 argues for substantial Śaiva → Vajrayāna directional borrowing.
- **Southeast Asian Shiva worship**: the Khmer empire (8th–14th c.) was substantially Shaiva (Angkor's principal religious orientation); Bali maintains continuous Hindu-Shaiva worship into the present; medieval Java and Sumatra had major Shaiva institutional presence.

## Iconography
- **Naṭarāja**: cosmic dancer, ringed in flame, dancing on the demon of ignorance (Apasmāra), four arms holding drum, fire, abhaya-mudrā (fear-not gesture), and a hand pointing to his foot.
- **Yogi-Shiva**: meditative, ash-smeared, on Mount Kailash, trident planted beside him.
- **Liṅga**: cylindrical stone form in *yoni* base.
- **Ardhanārīśvara**: half-male (Shiva), half-female (Pārvatī) — the androgynous form representing the non-duality of cosmic male and female principles.
- **Bhairava**: terrifying form, naked, ash-smeared, garlanded with skulls, holding a severed head; lord of the cremation grounds. See [[bhairava]].

## Disputes
- **Indus Valley Paśupati identification**: contested; the Marshall 1931 reading remains influential but not universally accepted.
- **Rudra → Shiva continuity**: the historical-philological mainstream (Witzel, Sanderson, Doniger) treats the development as continuous with substantial substrate-absorption; alternative readings emphasize distinct origins.
- **Sanderson's Śaiva-Age thesis** (2009): the argument that medieval Śaivism was the dominant religious force across South and Southeast Asia (~700–1300 CE) and substantially shaped contemporary Buddhism and Vaishnavism via institutional pressure and ritual influence is contested by Buddhist-studies scholars (Davidson 2002).
