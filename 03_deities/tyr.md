# Týr

## Identity
Týr is etymologically the *original* Indo-European sky-god in Germanic guise — Proto-Germanic *Tīwaz cognate with Proto-Indo-European *Dyēus (also yielding Sanskrit *Dyaus*, Greek *Zeus*, Latin *Iuppiter* < *Dyēus pəter*). In Norse mythology of the surviving literary record, however, Týr has been substantially demoted — Óðinn has assumed most chief-god functions, and Týr is now principally a war-god and oath-god, the god by whom oaths are sworn. He is famously one-handed: he placed his hand in the wolf Fenrir's mouth as a guarantee while the gods bound him, knowing the binding would not be released; when Fenrir realized he was tricked, he bit off Týr's hand. The Tuesday / *Tīwesdæg* / *dies Martis* weekday calque identifies Týr with Mars.

## Earliest attestation
Tacitus *Germania* 9 identifies the Germans' "Mars" — this is Tīwaz/Týr. The *Tiwaz* rune (T) in the Elder Futhark bears his name. Old English *Tīw*; Old Norse *Týr*. The Indo-European *Dyēus etymology was demonstrated by 19th-c. comparative philology.

## Mythological role
The principal Týr narrative is the binding of Fenrir (*Gylfaginning*): the gods deceive the wolf Fenrir into accepting a magical fetter; Fenrir demands a god's hand in his mouth as security; only Týr is willing to provide it; Týr loses the hand when the fetter holds. At Ragnarǫk, Týr fights the hound Garmr; they kill each other.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **Mars** — *ancient-identification* (Tacitus; weekday): Roman interpretatio germanica.
- **Zeus / Jupiter / Dyaus** — *scholarly-parallel*: cognate Indo-European sky-god. This is one of the clearest demonstrable Indo-European cognate-god identifications.

## Iconography
One-handed (the principal distinguishing feature); spear or sword.
