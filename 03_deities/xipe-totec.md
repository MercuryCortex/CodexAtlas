# Xipe Totec

## Identity
"Our Lord the Flayed One" — one of the principal Aztec deities, god of agricultural seasons, spring renewal, and sacrifice. Xipe Totec is identified primarily through his central ritual and iconographic feature: wearing the flayed skin of a sacrificed human being. Priests donned the skins of sacrificial victims during the Tlacaxipehualiztli festival (the second month of the Aztec solar calendar), wearing them until they rotted off — an enactment of the earth shedding its dry outer layer to reveal new green growth beneath. This makes Xipe Totec one of the most transparent dying-rising agricultural deities in world religion: the flayed skin is the old, dry husk of the earth in winter; the living priest-body within is the new spring growth underneath.

## Earliest attestation
- Iconographic attestations of a flayed-skin deity appear in Late Preclassic contexts (c. 200 BCE–200 CE) at several sites, suggesting the cult is older than the Aztec imperial period.
- Major textual attestation: **Florentine Codex** (Sahagún, ~1569 compilation), Books 1–2 — detailed description of the Tlacaxipehualiztli ceremony and Xipe's attributes.
- **Codex Borgia** (pre-conquest, Central Mexican) — one of the fullest iconographic treatments of Xipe, with the characteristic flayed-skin suit and red body paint visible.
- Xipe Totec's cult was widespread beyond the Aztec Mexica: Zapotec, Mixtec, and Tlapanec traditions all have Xipe-like flayed-deity figures, suggesting a deep Mesoamerican agricultural complex predating Aztec hegemony.

## Mythological role
Xipe Totec presides over the Tlacaxipehualiztli festival (roughly February–March), during which gladiatorial sacrifice (*tlauauanilli*) and flaying were performed. Captive warriors were sacrificed; their skins were worn by priests for 20 days. The ritual logic is made explicit in the sources: just as the maize-seed must shed its dry outer hull before it germinates, the earth must shed its dry winter skin for spring growth to appear. Xipe is also god of goldsmiths — the smith works a metal from its rough outer ore-shell (a parallel to the skin-shedding). He is associated with eye diseases, whose sufferers prayed to him during the festival period.

## Cross-tradition identifications

- **[[quetzalcoatl]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: in some cosmological schemes Xipe Totec is identified as the East / Red aspect of a four-directional Tezcatlipoca complex; Quetzalcoatl is the White / West position. The identifications vary by source and are contested (Nicholson 1971; Klein 2000).
- **[[tezcatlipoca]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: the four-Tezcatlipoca directional schema in some sources assigns Xipe to the East as "Red Tezcatlipoca"; the schema is found in late colonial sources and its pre-conquest form is debated.
- **Dying-rising agricultural deity (Frazer's category)** — *scholarly-parallel*: Xipe fits the Frazerian category of dying-and-rising grain deity more explicitly than most other deities in that category — the agricultural symbolism of skin-shedding = earth renewing is stated in the indigenous sources themselves. The parallel with [[osiris]] (whose dismemberment and reconstitution parallels agricultural death-and-growth) and [[adonis-hellenistic]] (Greek grain-deity killed and mourned, linked to Adonis-gardens) is typological; no transmission is claimed.

## Iconography
Uniquely identifiable in Mesoamerican art: the god wears a complete flayed human skin as a suit, with the victim's face as a mask over the priest's face and the victim's hands hanging loose at the wrists. Body paint beneath the skin is red (the living color). The *chicahuaztli* (a rattling staff used in rain petitions) is his implement. Some depictions show a golden beak-mask. Stone sculptures of Xipe Totec survive in considerable numbers from Aztec and regional contexts, many showing the characteristic double-face (priest's face visible through the eye-holes of the victim's skin-mask).

## Disputes
- **Was Xipe Totec originally an Aztec deity or adopted from neighboring groups?** Sahagún's sources suggest Xipe was adopted by the Mexica from the Gulf Coast Yopis or Tlapanecs; the cult's geographic spread before Aztec hegemony supports an origin outside the Basin of Mexico. The dating of the earliest flayed-deity iconography matters for tracing the cult's origin.
- **The four-Tezcatlipoca schema.** Several colonial sources present a directional cosmological scheme in which four "Tezcatlipocas" correspond to the four world-quarters and four colors (Black / North / Tezcatlipoca; White / West / Quetzalcoatl; Blue / South / Huitzilopochtli; Red / East / Xipe). Whether this schema reflects pre-conquest Aztec theology or is a post-conquest systematization by indigenous informants trained in scholastic categorization is unresolved.
