# Nisaba

## Identity
Nisaba (older transliteration *Nidaba*; both forms refer to the same goddess) is the Sumerian patron of scribes — the divine guarantor of writing, accounting, measurement, and the scribal craft generally — and also the goddess of grain. Her cult center is **Eresh** (location debated; somewhere in southern Sumer, possibly near Umma); her principal temple is the Ezagin ("Lapis-Lazuli House"). The two domains, grain and writing, are not arbitrarily linked: the earliest cuneiform writing emerges from accounting practices for grain and other commodities, so the patron of grain becomes the patron of the tally — the patron of writing. The scribal colophon "Nisaba be praised" (*dnisaba zà-mí*) closes a substantial fraction of all third-millennium and early-second-millennium Sumerian literary tablets. Her consort is the lesser deity Haya, who also has scribal associations. From the Old Babylonian period onward, the Babylonian god [[nabu]] progressively assumes Nisaba's scribal patronage; by the Neo-Assyrian period Nabu has replaced her in colophons, though her grain function remains hers.

## Earliest attestation
- Among the very earliest divine names in cuneiform: the Uruk IV / III archaic god lists (~-3100 to -2900) include NÀGA / NISABA.
- The Fara/Shuruppak and Abu Salabikh god lists (~-2600 to -2500) list her prominently.
- Hymn 42 (the closing hymn) of [[phase-1-003-enheduanna-hymns]] is dedicated to Nisaba — closing the temple-hymn cycle with the patron of the scribal craft is itself a theological statement.
- The Kesh Temple Hymn ([[phase-1-001-kesh-temple-hymn]]) is attributed in its colophon to Nisaba ("Nisaba be praised").
- The Instructions of Shuruppak ([[phase-1-005-instructions-of-shuruppak]]) reference scribal-craft transmission under her aegis.

## Mythological role
- *Hymn to Nisaba (Nisaba A)*: extended praise of her scribal patronage, her temple at Eresh, her wisdom and her stylus.
- *The Song of the Hoe* — a literary composition in which the hoe is celebrated and Nisaba is invoked as the goddess who measures fields and records yields.
- In [[phase-1-018-gudea-cylinders]], when Gudea is shown the temple plan in a vision, it is Nisaba who holds the *tablet of the favorable stars* — the divine architectural plan derived from astronomy. Her involvement legitimates the construction.
- She is invoked at the close of countless scribal-school exercises, hymns, royal inscriptions, and laments — a near-universal closural blessing in Sumerian literature.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[nabu]]** — *ancient-identification*: the scribal-craft patronage transfers progressively from Nisaba to Nabu through the Old Babylonian into the Neo-Assyrian period. The transition is gradual rather than sudden: many OB texts invoke both, with Nisaba dominant; many Neo-Assyrian texts invoke Nabu alone. The grain function is not transferred.
- *Scholarly parallel*: Nisaba has been compared (Bottéro, Glassner) to Egyptian Seshat (goddess of writing and measurement), Greek Demeter (grain), and broader "patron of writing" figures cross-culturally; these are *scholarly-parallel* claims, not ancient identifications.

## Iconography
Nisaba is depicted as a seated goddess in horned crown holding a stylus or measuring rod, often with a sheaf of grain growing from her shoulders. The combination of grain-and-writing iconography is distinctive to her.

## Disputes
- **Reading Nisaba vs. Nidaba**. The cuneiform sign NÀGA / NISABA permits either reading; current scholarship varies, with Michalowski 2002 preferring Nisaba and older scholarship (Kramer, Sjöberg) often using Nidaba. The forms refer to the same goddess.
- **Location of Eresh**. Disputed; no excavated cult site has yielded direct identification.
