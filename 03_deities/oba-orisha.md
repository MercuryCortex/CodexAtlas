# Ọ̀bà

## Identity
Ọ̀bà is the Yorùbá òrìṣà of the **Ọ̀bà River** (a tributary of the Niger system) and the patron of **marriage and wifely devotion** — and, by the bitter turn of her central myth, of the betrayed and humiliated wife. She is the senior of the three wives of [[shango]], the storm-king; the others are [[oya]] and [[oshun]].

## Earliest attestation
Preserved in Yorùbá oral tradition and the Ifá corpus, and localised to the Ọ̀bà River where it joins the Ọ̀ṣun. Recorded ethnographically by Awolalu, Bascom, and Verger, and carried into Cuban Lucumí practice through the Atlantic trade.

## Mythological role — the severed ear and the river's confluence
The defining narrative: anxious to secure [[shango]]'s love over her rival, Ọ̀bà asks [[oshun]] — the favoured junior wife — the secret of her cooking. Ọ̀ṣun deceives her, claiming she flavours Ṣàngó's soup with a slice of her own ear. Trusting, Ọ̀bà cuts off her ear and cooks it into Ṣàngó's meal; he recoils in horror and rejects her. Shamed, Ọ̀bà flees and becomes the Ọ̀bà River — and where her waters meet [[oshun]]'s, they churn in violent rapids, an *aetiology* of the rivers' turbulent confluence and of the two orishas' lasting enmity. Ọ̀bà is therefore depicted covering the side of her head where the ear is gone. She completes the storm-king's river-wife triad with [[oya]] (his battle-companion) and [[oshun]] (his favourite).

## Cross-tradition
Ọ̀bà belongs to the Yorùbá **feminine-river cluster** with [[oshun]], [[oya]], and [[yemoja]] — divinised rivers personified as women whose temperaments are the rivers' moods. Her myth is a Yorùbá instance of the widespread *co-wife rivalry* and *self-mutilating devotion* motif, where a wife's excessive sacrifice destroys the love it meant to win.

## Diaspora syncretism
In Cuba ([[tradition-santeria-lucumi]]) Ọ̀bà is syncretised with **Santa Catalina de Siena** (St Catherine of Siena), and in some lineages with St Rita — both saints of suffering devotion (per [[theme-orisha-saint-syncretism]]). She remains a reserved, little-fed orisha, said to dwell "at the bottom of the river."

## Iconography
A covered or missing ear; copper and coral/pink beads; the married woman's head-tie; offerings set at the riverbank. She is invoked for marital fidelity — and warned against as the goddess of devotion turned self-destructive.
