# Ògún

## Identity
Orisha of iron, metalwork, war, hunting, roads, labor, and (in modern extension) all technology that involves metal — automobiles, railroads, surgical instruments, firearms. Ògún is the orisha most directly engaged with material work and the tools of human labor. He is fierce, hot, demanding — but indispensable to civilization, since all metal tools (and therefore agriculture, building, transportation) depend on him.

## Earliest attestation
Cult well-attested across West Africa (Yoruba, Edo, Fon traditions); the Yoruba Ogun-iron association is sometimes traced to the Iron Age in West Africa (~500 BCE attested in the Nok culture).

## Mythological role
In a major Yoruba narrative, when the orishas first descended to earth, the bush was so thick they could not pass; Ògún alone had a cutlass and cleared the way — making him the orisha of the road and of all paths. His warrior-aspect is celebrated in *Ijala* — the Yoruba hunter's chant-poetry — and in his association with Oyo military traditions.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **Saint Peter** (Cuban Lucumí, common pairing) — *folk-syncretism*: Peter holds the keys (iron); pairing is operational rather than narrative.
- **Saint James (Santiago)** — *folk-syncretism* (some Cuban traditions, also Brazilian): the warrior-saint; Iberian *Santiago Matamoros* iconography pairs with Ògún's warrior aspect.
- **Ogou Feray (Haitian Vodou)** — *manuscript-transmission*: direct West-African-to-Haitian transmission of the orisha; in Vodou, Ogou is a *family* of related *lwa* (Ogou Feray, Ogou Badagri, Ogou Balindjo) rather than a single figure.

## Iconography
Iron implements — knife, machete, anvil, chains, miniature tools; green-and-black colors in Yoruba and Cuban Lucumí (red-and-black in some Vodou *nasyon*); dog as sacrificial animal.
