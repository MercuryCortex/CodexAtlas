# Guanyin

## Identity
Guanyin — short for *Guanshiyin* (觀世音, "Observing the Sounds of the World") — is the female form of the Bodhisattva of compassion in Chinese, Korean, Japanese (as Kannon, 観音), and Vietnamese (as Quan Âm) Buddhism. She is the most widely worshipped deity in Chinese popular religion across the past millennium, and one of the most theologically and iconographically richly developed female saviour-figures in any religion. In her standard popular iconography she stands or sits on a lotus, wears white robes, carries a water-vessel (*kalaśa*) and willow-branch (sometimes a lotus instead), and embodies maternal-saving compassion.

Guanyin is the Chinese transformation of the Indian Bodhisattva [[avalokitesvara]]: in early Chinese texts (Kumārajīva's 406 CE translation of the Lotus Sūtra, etc.) and iconography Guanyin is masculine, following the Indic original. The feminization is a long Chinese-cultural process from ~7th–10th c. CE, given decisive popular form by the *Miaoshan* legend (~10th–12th c.), which provides Guanyin with a fully female biography: she is Princess Miaoshan, third daughter of King Miaozhuangwang, who refuses marriage, becomes a nun, is martyred by her father, attains enlightenment, returns to heal him by giving him her own eyes and arms (an *Imitatio* of the thousand-armed-thousand-eyed iconography). The Miaoshan-narrative resolves the iconographic-theological tension between masculine Sanskrit Avalokiteśvara and feminine Chinese Guanyin by giving the latter a self-standing Chinese biography. Yü 2001 is the definitive scholarly treatment of the transformation.

## Earliest attestation
Kumārajīva's 406 CE Chinese Lotus Sūtra is the foundational text; the masculine-Indic phase runs to ~7th c. Pilgrimage to Mount Putuo (Zhejiang) as Guanyin's earthly seat is documented from ~10th c.; the Miaoshan-biography from ~10th–12th c.

## Manifestations
- **Lotus Sūtra Chapter 25** ("Universal Gateway") — the foundational text in Chinese Buddhism: invoking the name of Guanyin / Avalokiteśvara saves from fire, water, robbery, prison, demons, and the perils of childbirth.
- **Pure Land devotion** — Guanyin attends Amitābha in the Pure Land; she is invoked alongside Amitābha in mortuary practice.
- **Popular religion.** Guanyin in popular Chinese practice transcends sectarian Buddhism: she is invoked for fertility (Songzi Guanyin, "Child-giving Guanyin"), safe sea-passage, recovery from illness, and general protection. Statues are present in nearly every Chinese Buddhist temple, in many Daoist temples, and on household altars.
