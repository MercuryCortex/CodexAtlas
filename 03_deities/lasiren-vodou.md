# Lasirèn

## Identity
Mermaid lwa of the sea — Lasirèn is the feminine ocean-spirit of Haitian Vodou, the singer beneath the waves who calls humans into the water and gives them gifts of music, dreams, and (sometimes) extended sojourns in her undersea palace. She is partly Kongo-derived (the Simbi water-spirit class) and partly Atlantic-mermaid iconography absorbed during the Middle-Passage centuries. She is sometimes paired with the male sea-lwa Agwé as consort or sister; sometimes paired-aspected with La Baleine (the whale).

## Earliest attestation
Haitian Vodou liturgical tradition; documented in Métraux (1959) as the standard mid-20th-c reference; Brown's *Mama Lola* (1991) is the definitive contemporary ethnography of an individual Vodou priestess and includes extensive Lasirèn material.

## Mythological role
Lasirèn lives in the sea. Initiates and the dead can dwell with her under the water — a recurring theme in Vodou narratives is the human who disappears into the sea, dwells with Lasirèn for what feels like a brief time, and returns months or years later transformed and bearing gifts. The "stay with Lasirèn" is a metaphor for initiation, for trauma-survival, and (per Brown 1991) for the collective memory of the Middle Passage — those who went into the Atlantic and never returned are imagined as having joined Lasirèn's undersea community.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[simbi-kongo]]** — direct-borrowing from Kongo water-spirits via the Petwo Vodou branch.
- **[[yemoja]]** (Yoruba) — structural-parallel; both are divine-feminine sea-figures of West-African origin, but Vodou and Òrìṣà religion did not merge.
- **Mami Wata** (West-African coastal) — parallel-form sharing the mermaid iconography.
- **Stella Maris / Our Lady of Regla** — folk-syncretism with maritime Catholic Marian devotion.

## MASSIVE WIN — Atlantic Middle-Passage memory figure
Brown (1991) develops the argument that Lasirèn is not merely a generic sea-goddess but specifically an Atlantic-trauma memory-figure — the divine reception of the Middle Passage. The enslaved who died in the Atlantic crossing become Lasirèn's people; the song heard from the water is their continued song. This frames Lasirèn alongside Erzulie Dantor and Baron Samedi as members of the Vodou pantheon-subset that carries explicit historical-trauma memory rather than abstract cosmological function. Joseph Murphy and the Murphy-Sanford *Osun Across the Waters* (2001) collection make a related argument for the Atlantic-orisha-women as memory-figures of slavery and resistance.

## Iconography
Mermaid; comb and mirror (the universal mermaid attributes); long flowing hair; conch-shell or sea-horn; white and blue cloth; offerings of fish, champagne, perfume placed at the water's edge or on a Vodou altar.
