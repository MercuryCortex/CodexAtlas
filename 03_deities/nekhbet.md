# Nekhbet

Vulture-goddess of Upper Egypt — one of the very oldest tutelary deities of the Nile Valley, attested in the predynastic period at Nekheb (modern El-Kab), the original royal city of Upper Egypt opposite Hierakonpolis/Nekhen across the Nile. Her counterpart Wadjet is the cobra-goddess of Lower Egypt. Together they are the **Two Ladies** (*Nebti*).

## The Two Ladies

The pairing of Nekhbet and Wadjet is one of the canonical pan-Egyptian theological compounds, embedded in the royal titulature from the 1st Dynasty (~ -3000) onward. The pharaoh's official titulary has five names — Horus name, *Nebti* name (Two Ladies), Golden Horus name, throne name (*nesu-bity*), and birth name — and the *Nebti* name is the second of these, marking the pharaoh's sovereignty over both Egypts in theological-iconographic language. The vulture and the cobra appear together on the royal brow regalia (the *uraeus* + vulture-head crest) for the entire 3,000-year history of the pharaonic state.

Sovereignty itself is articulated as the *combination* of the two — the pharaonic state's deepest theological commitment to dyadic-unified rulership. There is no Egyptian pharaoh who is *only* king of Upper Egypt or *only* king of Lower Egypt; the title is always *nesu-bity* ('he of the sedge and the bee', symbolising the two regions), and the regalia is always the vulture + cobra pair.

## Vulture iconography

Nekhbet appears either as a vulture in flight (often shown with wings outstretched protectively over the king, holding a *shen*-ring of eternity in her talons) or as a vulture-headdress on the head of a queen or goddess. The vulture-headdress is one of the principal feminine regalia in Egyptian iconography — worn by queens (most famously by [[nefertiti]] and the Amarna royal women, by [[hatshepsut]] in her crowning portraits, by Cleopatra in late portrait reliefs), by the consorts of Amun in the Theban imperial religion, and by goddesses who acquire Nekhbet's protective function.

The vulture's protective role is theologically grounded in observed bird-behaviour: the Egyptian griffon vulture (*Gyps fulvus*) was observed sheltering its young under outspread wings, and the iconography of Nekhbet's outspread wings over the king is a direct projection of this maternal-protective image. The royal *Pyramid Texts* (Old Kingdom, ~ -2400) already describe Nekhbet as suckling the king, which is the central scene of her ritual relation to royalty.

## Mut absorbs Nekhbet's iconography

In the New Kingdom (~ -1550 onward), the rise of the Theban priesthood and the imperial expansion of Egypt drive a theological reorganisation in which [[mut]] (the Theban consort of [[amun]]) absorbs many of Nekhbet's iconographic functions, especially the vulture-headdress. By the Ramesside period, Mut is regularly depicted with the vulture-headdress, and the New-Kingdom imperial-religion absorbs the Upper-Egyptian Nekhbet into the Theban supreme-mother Mut. This is one of the cleanest examples of regional-deity to imperial-deity absorption in Egyptian religion — the older tutelary remains in her local cult at Nekheb, but her iconographic signature migrates to the imperial centre at Thebes.

## Eileithyia

Herodotus 2.83 (~ -440) records the *interpretatio Graeca* identifying the goddess of Nekheb with Eileithyia, the Greek goddess of childbirth and midwifery. The match is functional — both protect mothers and the newborn, both are vulture/eagle-associated, both are tutelary. In Ptolemaic and Roman Egypt the identification is operational at the El-Kab cult centre, and the city receives the Greek name Eileithyiapolis.
