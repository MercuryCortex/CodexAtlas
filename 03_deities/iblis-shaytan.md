# Iblīs / Shayṭān

Quranic figure who refused God's command to prostrate before Adam (Surah 2:34, 7:11–18, 15:28–44, 17:61–65, 18:50, 20:116–123, 38:71–85). The Quran identifies Iblīs simultaneously as a *jinn* (Surah 18:50) and as one of the angels, creating a productive theological tension in Islamic exegesis. His refusal is framed in terms of pride (*kibr*) and his claim of ontological superiority (created from fire vs. Adam from clay).

## Quranic profile

The name **Iblīs** appears in nine Quranic surahs. **Shayṭān** (the generic Arabic term for adversary-spirit, cognate with Hebrew *śāṭān*) appears throughout as the agent of human temptation. The two overlap but are not identical in Quranic usage: Iblīs is the individual who fell; Shayṭān is the type.

Key Quranic passages:
- **Surah 2:34** — refusal to prostrate before Adam; divine expulsion
- **Surah 7:11–18** — dialogue with God; Iblīs requests a reprieve until Judgment Day to mislead humanity
- **Surah 15:28–44** — elaborated account; Iblīs claims the ability to seduce all but the sincerely devout
- **Surah 38:71–85** — most detailed narrative; Iblīs's explicit accusation against God's wisdom in creating humans from clay

## Distinction from Christian Satan

The Quranic Iblīs shares the basic narrative function of the adversary-tempter with the Christian Satan and the Jewish *śāṭān*, but the Islamic tradition explicitly rejects the cosmological dualism that the Christian tradition sometimes imports. Iblīs is a creature of God, operating under divine permission—he is not a counter-divinity. Islamic theology consistently treats Iblīs as a test-agent within God's sovereign plan rather than a genuinely autonomous force of evil.

The Second Temple pseudepigraphic substrate (Adam's fall, angelic-disobedience motifs in 1 Enoch and Vita Adae et Evae) underlies all three traditions' adversary-figures. Reynolds 2018 documents the specific Quranic reworking of these materials as independent theological re-interpretation rather than direct Christian borrowing.

## Cross-tradition significance

The Iblīs narrative is the Quran's most extensive engagement with the creation-of-Adam complex (Gen 1–2 in the Hebrew Bible; the Watchers in 1 Enoch). The Quran's version is striking in that Iblīs is given a *dialogue* with God — a theological-philosophical confrontation about the value of human beings — rather than the silent rebellion of later Christian demonology.

The parallel-form relationship to Ahriman (Zoroastrian adversary) is structural: both are cosmic adversaries operating under the sovereignty of the supreme deity, but neither tradition directly borrowed from the other at the level of mythological narrative.
