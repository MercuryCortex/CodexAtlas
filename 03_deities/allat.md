# Allāt

## Identity
Allāt (Arabic *al-Lāt*, اللات — "the Goddess," with the definite article *al-* + *lāt*; cognate with Ugaritic / Northwest Semitic ʾlt "goddess") is a principal pre-Islamic Arabian goddess with extensive Nabataean, Palmyrene, Safaitic, and Hejazi attestation from the mid-first millennium BCE through the Islamic conquest. She is named in **Quran 53:19-22** (Sūrat al-Najm) as one of the **three "daughters of Allāh"** alongside al-ʿUzzā and Manāt — the so-called "Gharaniq verses" / "Satanic Verses" context in which the early Islamic tradition rejects pre-Islamic goddess-worship. Her principal cult-center in the immediate pre-Islamic Hejaz was at **Taif** (near Mecca), where her sanctuary held a great aniconic cult-stone (*baetyl*) destroyed in the year 9 AH (~630 CE) following Muhammad's conquest of the region. (Hoyland 2001; Hawting 1999.)

**Important disambiguation:** Pre-Islamic Arabian Allāt (al-Lāt) is **distinct from** the Akkadian / Mesopotamian *Allatu* (= Ereshkigal, queen of the underworld), despite the orthographic similarity. The Akkadian Allatu is a Mesopotamian-Akkadian identification of Ereshkigal and is documented under [[allatu-akkadian]]. The Quranic / Arabian Allāt is a separate Northwest-Arabian / North-Arabian deity with her own attestation history. The two should not be conflated. (See Disputes below.)

## Earliest attestation
- **Herodotus *Histories* 1.131 and 3.8** (c. -440 BCE) — earliest external attestation; Herodotus reports that the Arabians worship two deities, *Orotalt* (Dushara) and *Alilat*, whom he identifies with Dionysus and Aphrodite Urania respectively.
- **Nabataean inscriptions** (c. -200 onward) — extensive epigraphic attestation across Petra, Hegra, and the broader Nabataean realm; Allat is one of the principal Nabataean goddesses.
- **Safaitic and Lihyanite inscriptions** (c. -200 onward) — North Arabian epigraphic attestation.
- **Palmyrene Allat-temple** (1st-3rd c. CE) — the Palmyra Allat-sanctuary with its monumental lion-statue and bilingual Aramaic-Greek inscriptions identifying Allat with Athena. Destroyed in part by ISIS in 2015; partly reconstructed.
- **Quran 53:19-22** (~610-632 CE) — the principal Islamic-period textual attestation; Allāt named alongside al-ʿUzzā and Manāt and her worship rejected. ([[phase-4-034-quran]])
- **Ibn al-Kalbi *Kitāb al-Aṣnām* (Book of Idols, c. 9th c. CE)** — later Islamic-period source reconstructing pre-Islamic Arabian cult; describes the Taif cult-stone and Muhammad's destruction of it. (Treated source-critically per Hawting 1999 — the *Kitāb al-Aṣnām* is later Islamic-period polemical reconstruction, not contemporary attestation.)

## Mythological role
Allāt's mythological role in pre-Islamic Arabian tradition is **cultic rather than narrative** — no extended Allāt-myth survives. What survives is her cultic-functional role:

1. **High goddess of the North Arabian / Nabataean / Hejazi pantheon.** Allāt is consistently one of the top deities in the regional pantheon, often paired with the male high god (Dushara in Nabataean contexts; sometimes Ba'al-Shamin).
2. **One of the three "daughters of Allāh."** The Quranic tradition (Sura 53:19-22) preserves the pre-Islamic conception of al-Lāt, al-ʿUzzā, and Manāt as three goddesses associated with Allāh (the high god) — a configuration the Quran emphatically rejects in favor of Allāh's unique sovereignty without consort or daughters.
3. **Warrior / Athena-type** (Palmyrene-specific). In Palmyra under Roman period the *interpretatio graeca* identifies Allāt with Athena and her iconography becomes helmet-spear-shield Athena-type with the lion as her sacred animal.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **Athena** — *ancient-identification* (Palmyrene): explicit bilingual Aramaic-Greek inscriptions at Palmyra identify Allat with Athena; her cult-image takes Athena's iconographic vocabulary (helmet, spear, shield, lion).
- **Aphrodite Urania** — *ancient-identification* (Herodotean): Herodotus 1.131 identifies Arabian *Alilat* with Aphrodite Urania (the "heavenly" Aphrodite). This is the Greek-period externally-attributed identification.
- **al-ʿUzzā** and **Manāt** — close cultic associates; the "three daughters of Allāh" triad.
- **NOT identical with [[allatu-akkadian]]** — see Disputes.

## Iconography
- **Aniconic *baetyl*** (the Taif cult-stone) — canonical pre-Islamic Arabian form; Allāt's principal cult-image was an aniconic standing stone at Taif (Hoyland 2001).
- **Throne / enthroned cult-image** — Herodotus 1.131 reports an enthroned cult-image.
- **Lion** — Palmyrene Allat-Athena's sacred animal; the monumental Palmyra Allat-lion (destroyed 2015, partly reconstructed) is the most famous iconographic survival.
- **Helmet / spear / shield** — Palmyrene Allat-Athena iconography.
- **Crescent moon** — in some pre-Islamic North Arabian iconographic attestations; the lunar association is partial and contested.

See [[crescent-moon-star]] symbol node — Allāt is one canonical pre-Islamic Arabian instance.

## Disputes
- **Allāt vs. Allatu-Akkadian.** These are TWO DIFFERENT figures with confusingly similar names:
  - **Pre-Islamic Arabian Allāt / al-Lāt** = "the goddess," Northwest Semitic feminine form with the *al-* article; principal pre-Islamic Arabian goddess. This entry.
  - **Akkadian Allatu** = Ereshkigal-alias, queen of the Mesopotamian underworld. See [[allatu-akkadian]].
  Mainstream scholarship treats them as separate (Hoyland 2001; Hawting 1999); the orthographic similarity is coincidental Northwest-Semitic-vs-Akkadian.
- **Solar vs. lunar Allāt.** Some scholars (older comparative-religion literature) read Allāt as a solar deity (sun-disk on some Nabataean attestations); others read her as lunar (crescent on some attestations); the iconography is mixed and the consensus is that she is *neither cleanly solar nor cleanly lunar* — a high-goddess figure whose iconographic vocabulary varied regionally. (Hoyland 2001.)
- **Historicity of the [[satanic-verses-incident|"Satanic Verses" incident]].** The Islamic-tradition narrative of Muhammad temporarily accepting al-Lāt, al-ʿUzzā, Manāt as legitimate intercessors before retracting (the *gharānīq* / "high-flying cranes" verses) is preserved in early Islamic sources (Ibn Ishaq, Tabari) but is contested in later Islamic scholarship; Hawting 1999 + Ahmed 2017 *Before Orthodoxy* survey the source-critical issues. This is mainly a matter of Islamic-tradition reception-history; the [[satanic-verses-incident]] node treats the issue in detail. The Taif sanctuary was destroyed at [[event-conquest-of-mecca-630|the conquest of Mecca]] / Taif period in 8 AH.
- **Sourcing for pre-Islamic Arabian cult.** Per Hawting 1999, the principal Islamic-tradition sources for pre-Islamic Arabian cult (*Kitāb al-Aṣnām*, etc.) are later Islamic-period polemical-reconstructive texts and must be used source-critically. The epigraphic record (Nabataean, Palmyrene, Safaitic) provides the contemporary attestation.
