# Sirens

## Identity

The Sirens (Greek **Σειρῆνες**, *Seirēnes*) are the death-singers of the Greek sea-passage: divine or daimonic female singers whose song no mortal can resist and whose meadow is heaped with the bones of the men who listened. In Homer they are **two** (the poet uses dual grammatical forms, Od. 12.52), **unnamed**, and — contrary to the whole later tradition — given **no physical description whatever**: no wings, no bird body, no fish tail. The bird-bodied woman of archaic and classical art, the canonical later triad of names, the genealogy from a river-god and a Muse, and finally the medieval fish-tailed mermaid are all accretions, each datable, which makes the Sirens an unusually clean stratigraphy in Greek myth: a voice first, a body later, and a different body later still. They are not sea-spirits in the early sources — they sit in a flowery meadow (*leimōn*) on an island, and the wind drops to a dead calm when they sing (Od. 12.168-169). What they offer is not beauty but **knowledge**: their song claims that whoever listens sails away knowing more, for "we know all things that come to pass on the fruitful earth" (Od. 12.191). In the Atlas they anchor the deadly-singer slot of the music lens — the disordering counter-voice to [[orpheus]] — and the Greek head of the long chain that ends in the Atlantic mermaid.

## In the Odyssey

The episode is told twice: Circe's warning (Od. 12.39-54) and the passage itself (12.158-200). Circe's protocol is precise — knead beeswax into the crew's ears; if Odysseus himself wishes to listen, let him be bound upright to the mast, and if he begs release, bind him tighter. The Sirens sit in their meadow "and about them is a great heap of bones of rotting men" (12.45-46). Their song (12.184-191) is a masterpiece of targeted flattery: they hail Odysseus by name and epithet — "great glory of the Achaeans" — and promise the complete, true account of everything the Argives and Trojans suffered at Troy by the will of the gods, and of all that happens on the nourishing earth. The lure, in other words, is **total knowledge delivered as song** — functionally, the Sirens claim the omniscience of the Muses (compare the invocation at Iliad 2.484-492) with none of the Muses' sanction. Odysseus hears, is bound tighter by Eurylochus and Perimedes, and the ship passes; nothing pursues. The Sirens' power is purely acoustic: the crew with waxed ears passes untouched (Od. 12.192-200).

## In the wider Greek corpus: genealogy, cult, cosmos

**Genealogy and names.** The Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women* places them on the island **Anthemoessa**, "flowery" (fr. 27 M-W). Apollodorus makes them daughters of the river-god Achelous and the Muse Melpomene (*Library* 1.3.4); Apollonius of Rhodes says Terpsichore (*Argonautica* 4.895-896); Euripides makes them "winged maidens, daughters of Earth" (*Helen* 167-178). The names crystallize as triads: Peisinoe, Aglaope, Thelxiepeia — one lyre, one voice, one aulos (Apollodorus, *Epitome* 7.18) — or, in the Campanian localization, Parthenope, Leucosia, Ligeia.

**The Argo and the death-prophecy.** In Apollonius (*Argonautica* 4.891-919) Orpheus drowns out the Sirens with his lyre; only Boutes swims for shore and is rescued by [[aphrodite-greek|Aphrodite]]. A fate-clause attaches to them: they live only until a ship hears and passes unharmed (Hyginus, *Fabulae* 141; Apollod. *Epit.* 7.19); after Odysseus's escape they hurl themselves into the sea. Lycophron (*Alexandra* 712-737) has the three bodies wash ashore in Italy — **Parthenope at Naples**, giving the city its archaic name.

**Actual cult.** This is what tips the Sirens from literary monsters into the deity folder: Strabo records the Sirenussae headland near Surrentum with a *hieron* of the Sirens (1.2.12; 5.4.8) and, at Neapolis, the **monument of Parthenope** (1.2.13; 5.4.7), where a **gymnastic contest was celebrated by command of an oracle** (5.4.7). A defeated myth-figure with an oracularly mandated tomb-cult is a cult-heroine, not a metaphor.

**Funerary and cosmic Sirens.** From the fifth century, sirens appear on Attic grave monuments as mourners — bird-bodied women tearing their hair, singing the lament (the marble Siren of the Kerameikos is the famous example); Euripides can invoke them as ideal mourning-accompanists (*Helen* 167-178), and Plato locates Sirens in Hades, so charmed by Hades's discourse that they will not leave (*Cratylus* 403d). Then comes the great inversion: in the myth of Er, **eight Sirens stand on the whorls of the Spindle of Necessity, each singing one tone, the eight together one harmony** (*Republic* 10.617b) — the earliest explicit image of the music of the spheres makes the death-singers the cosmos-singers. Pausanias (9.34.3) preserves the balancing tale: the Sirens, put up to it by Hera, challenged the Muses in song, lost, and had their feathers plucked for the Muses' crowns — sanctioned song defeating unsanctioned song, the Orpheus episode's logic in divine key.

## Cross-tradition significance

**Upstream — the body is borrowed, the voice is not.** The bird-bodied woman of siren iconography appears in the orientalizing seventh century, when Greek art was absorbing Near Eastern composite beings wholesale; iconographic transmission of the **form** from Near Eastern models is the standard view (Hofstetter 1990). But Homer's Sirens, older than every surviving image, have no described body at all — so the fusion of borrowed bird-woman image with native death-singer is a Greek development, and comparisons of the siren to the Egyptian *ba*-bird (the human-headed soul-bird) are **typological, not genealogical**: Vermeule (1979) sets them side by side precisely as parallel imaginations of the dead and their voices, without claiming descent.

**Downstream — a genuinely documented transmission chain.** The Sirens are one of the vault's clearest cases of long-range transmission of a *name and image* rather than a being. The Septuagint used σειρῆνες for the wailing desert creatures of the prophets (LXX Isa 34:13; 43:20; Mic 1:8; Job 30:29 "I have become a brother of sirens"), and Jerome kept *sirenes* at Vulgate Isa 13:22 — planting the Greek singers inside the biblical text. The *Physiologus* moralized them (still half-bird) as the lure of pleasures and heresies; patristic writers made Odysseus bound to the mast a type of the Christian at the Cross (Clement of Alexandria, *Protrepticus* 12; the classic study is Rahner 1963). The **fish tail arrives only c. 700 CE**, in *Liber Monstrorum* 1.6 — from there the Romance words (*sirène*, *sirena*) simply mean "mermaid," and that mermaid, carried on Atlantic ships and prints, is the European image absorbed by West African and Caribbean water-spirit religion: [[mami-wata]] (Drewal 2008) and the Haitian lwa [[lasiren-vodou]], whose very name is the Greek word after twenty-five centuries of transit. The distinction must be kept sharp: the *name and iconography* transmitted; the water-spirits themselves are African, and the resemblance of role (dangerous, gift-giving feminine water-power) is convergence — the mami-wata node's own verdict on the global mermaid category ("deep antiquity, convergent imagination, or periodic transmission") applies here with the one difference that for the *image* the transmission chain is actually documented.

**The music lens.** Within the vault's music cluster the Sirens are the indispensable antagonist: [[music-orpheus-tradition]] and [[music-orphic-lyre-cosmic]] both stake their central claim (order wins by being the stronger attractor, not by silencing) on the Argo episode, and [[music-music-of-spheres]] inherits Plato's transposition of the same singers into the harmony of the cosmos. The Sirens are what the ordered-music theology needs disorder to sound like — and, in Plato, what the ordered cosmos sounds like once the singers change employers.

## Disputes

**How many, and what did they look like?** Two in Homer (dual forms), three almost everywhere later, with unstable names; Homer gives no physical form, so whether the archaic audience already pictured bird-women or the image was fused onto the episode later is not decidable from the text (Heubeck & Hoekstra 1989 ad loc.). The bird form and the fish form then coexist for centuries in medieval art — the shift is a drift, not an event, with *Liber Monstrorum* 1.6 as the first fish-tailed text.

**Etymology.** Unknown. The ancient derivation from *seira* ("rope" — the Sirens as "binders") is speculative, and proposed Semitic derivations (e.g., from *šîr*, "song") have not won acceptance; the standard etymological dictionaries leave the word unexplained. Any claim that the name itself proves an Eastern origin should be treated as unproven.

**What is the song?** The live interpretive quarrel. Gresseth (1970) reads the Sirens as otherworld figures whose lure is deadly knowledge — Muses of death. Pucci (1979/1998) showed the song's diction is markedly **Iliadic**: the Sirens sing the poetics of the Iliad at the hero of the Odyssey, so the episode is the Odyssey dramatizing the fatal attraction of the rival epic's frozen heroic glory. Doherty (1995) reads the episode through its gendered frame — female singers, male listener bound and mastered — as part of the Odyssey's anxiety about female narrators. These readings are not exclusive, but they cannot all be the poem's primary point, and the vault takes no side.
