# Khepri

## Identity

Khepri — "the one who comes into being," "the Becoming One" — is the Egyptian god of the rising sun, self-creation, and transformation. His name derives from the Egyptian root *kheper* ("to come into being," "to transform," "to become"), which is also the root of the word for the scarab beetle (*kheprer*). Khepri is the sun at the moment of dawn: the newly born, self-created solar disc ascending from the eastern horizon after its nightly passage through the underworld. He is depicted as a man with a scarab-beetle head, or as a full scarab, and sometimes as a scarab beetle with the solar disc and the goddesses [[isis-egyptian]] and [[nephthys]] at his sides. The scarab rolling its dung ball across the ground was the Egyptian metaphor for the sun being pushed across the sky — the beetle was observed to emerge spontaneously from the earth, giving rise to the belief in self-generation, making Khepri the theological emblem of uncaused, self-caused becoming.

## Earliest attestation

- **[[phase-1-002-pyramid-texts]]** (~2400 BCE): Khepri appears in the Pyramid Texts as one of the forms of the solar deity traversing the sky; the formula "Khepri is in his bark" places him explicitly as the dawn form of the sun god.
- **[[phase-1-009-coffin-texts]]** (Middle Kingdom, ~2100–1650 BCE): elaborate solar theology develops the three-fold Ra formula — Khepri (dawn), Ra (noon), Atum (dusk).
- **Amduat** (New Kingdom, ~1550 BCE onward): the royal funerary text dedicated to the sun's nightly journey through the twelve hours of the underworld; the climactic moment of the twelfth hour is the emergence of Khepri — the reborn solar deity — from the body of the serpent Apophis and the womb of [[nut]].
- **Iconographic**: the scarab amulet is the single most widely distributed Egyptian religious object found across the ancient world, from Egypt to Mesopotamia, the Levant, Anatolia, Greece, and Carthage — testament to the theological and apotropaic power of the Khepri symbol.

## Mythological role

Khepri is not merely a sun god but the theological answer to the question of uncaused causation: what caused the first moment of creation? The scarab beetle was believed by Egyptians to spontaneously generate itself from dung — to appear without parents, without prior cause — and so became the perfect emblem of self-creation (*kheper djesef* — "he who creates himself"). This makes Khepri the divine personification of the cosmological principle of self-caused being, the ground of existence that requires no prior cause to explain itself.

In the Amduat, the sun's nightly journey is one of the great Egyptian cosmological narratives: Ra in his bark traverses the twelve hours of the underworld (the Duat), passing through gates, overcoming serpentine opposition (including [[apophis]]), absorbing the energy of the dead, and emerging at dawn reborn as Khepri. The nightly death and morning rebirth of the sun was the cosmic template for human death and resurrection: as Khepri rises each dawn, so the dead may rise in the Field of Reeds. The scarab image placed over the heart of a mummy expressed this hope directly.

The three solar aspects — Khepri (dawn / becoming), Ra (noon / being), [[atum]] (dusk / completing) — were not three separate gods but three facets of the same divine solar power, a theology of dynamic unity. This is one of the earliest examples of what Hornung calls Egyptian "one-and-many" theology: the capacity to be simultaneously one god and many manifestations.

## Cross-tradition identifications

- **[[atum]]** — *ancient-identification*: Atum is Khepri's night-face and dusk-complement. Coffin Texts spell 75 has Atum-Khepri addressing himself — the dawn and the dusk aspects of the same deity in dialogue. Both are self-created: Atum (*tem* = "to complete" / "the complete one") is self-generated by spitting or masturbating the first gods from himself; Khepri is self-generated as the scarab emerges from earth. They are the alpha and omega of the solar journey — the point of departure and the point of renewal.
- **[[ra]]** — *ancient-identification*: Ra-Khepri is a frequent New Kingdom combination name; the two are not distinct deities but the noon and dawn modes of the solar principle.
- **[[amun]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: The Theban theological program synthesized Amun (the hidden, invisible god — *amun* = "the hidden one") with Khepri-Ra's self-creating solar power. Amun-Ra as "the great hidden one who makes himself manifest" is structurally homologous with Khepri: both embody the divine that is its own cause.
- **[[thoth]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: as the deity who assists Ra's solar bark and records divine order, Thoth accompanies Khepri's dawn journey; Thoth and Khepri share cosmological roles in the Amduat's bookkeeping of the underworld.

## Iconography

The primary iconic form: a man with the head of a scarab beetle (*Scarabaeus sacer*, the sacred dung beetle of Egypt). Alternatively depicted as a complete beetle, or as a beetle-headed god within the solar bark. A scarab beetle with outstretched wings bearing the solar disc is a common decorative motif on pectorals, amulets, and funerary equipment. The scarab was the most replicated Egyptian religious symbol: millions of small faience scarab amulets were produced across 2,000 years, worn by the living and placed on mummies of the dead.

## Disputes

- The precise relationship between Khepri, Ra, and Atum as one god vs. three gods is a matter of Egyptian theological nuance. Hornung (1982) argues Egyptian religion was comfortable with an "inclusive" logic where one god could be three aspects and three aspects could be one god — not a contradiction but a different relationship to identity than Greek logic allows.
- Whether Khepri should be understood as an independent deity with his own cult center or purely as a solar epithet/manifestation of Ra is debated; the evidence suggests the latter is more accurate, with Khepri functioning primarily as a theological concept rather than a separately worshipped deity.
