# Lahmu and Lahamu

## Identity
Lahmu and Lahamu are the twin-pair primordial offspring of [[apsu]] and [[tiamat]] in the Babylonian cosmogony — the first divine generation born from the commingling of fresh and salt primordial waters. In [[phase-1-008-enuma-elish]] I.10–11 they appear immediately after the opening cosmic situation: "Lahmu and Lahamu emerged, names being established; they grew and matured; thereafter Anshar and Kishar were born, surpassing them." They serve as the genealogical-link between the primordial water-pair (Apsu-Tiamat) and the cosmic-frame pair (Anshar = "Whole Sky" and Kishar = "Whole Earth"), who in turn beget [[an-sumerian]]/Anu and the rest of the pantheon. Their name probably derives from Akkadian *laḫāmu* "to be hairy"; in iconography "lahmu-figures" are bearded, shaggy nude male attendant-protectors carrying jars of streaming water — a stock motif in Mesopotamian visual art associated with apotropaic threshold-protection. Whether the divine pair Lahmu/Lahamu of *Enuma Elish* is identical with these iconographic lahmu-figures, or whether the same name labels related but distinct entities, is debated; Lambert (2013) treats them as the same complex.

## Cross-tradition identifications
No clear external syncretism. The Lahmu/Lahamu pair is theologically primordial within Babylonian cosmogony but had little cult or comparative-religious afterlife.

## Disputes
- **Lahmu the god vs. lahmu the iconographic-type.** Iconographic "lahmu-figures" (hairy nude male water-bearers) are pervasive in Mesopotamian apotropaic art and architectural protection from at least the Akkadian period. Whether these visual lahmus represent the same theological entity as the *Enuma Elish* Lahmu, or a separate but related class of *apkallu* / protective beings, is debated. Lambert (2013) sees a unified complex; others (Wiggermann) argue for an older separate iconographic tradition that was secondarily absorbed into the Enuma Elish theogony.
- **Etymology.** The "hairy" etymology is the consensus but not certain; some older suggestions derived the name from *liḫmu* ("strife") with a martial-protective sense.
