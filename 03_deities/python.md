# Python

## Identity

Python (Greek **Πύθων**, *Pȳthōn*) is the great serpent-dragon of Delphi — the chthonic, earth-born guardian of the oracular site at Pytho on the slope of Mt. Parnassus, slain by [[apollo|Apollo]], who then takes the oracle for himself and founds the Pythian cult. Python is not a worshipped god but a mythological antagonist: a personification of the older, earth-bound prophetic power that the Olympian Apollo displaces. The figure is variably gendered across the sources — an unnamed **she-dragon** (*drakaina*) in the earliest narrative, the *Homeric Hymn to Apollo*, and a **male serpent named Python** in the later, dominant tradition (Apollodorus, Ovid). In the Atlas, Python is placed among the chaos-serpents alongside [[typhon]], [[vritra]], [[illuyanka]], [[tiamat]], [[apophis]], and [[leviathan]] because it fills the same comparative slot — the serpent whose defeat by the ordering god founds an order — though here the prize is the **oracle** rather than cosmic kingship.

## In the Delphic myth

Python's central narrative is the *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* (Hymn 3, 7th–6th c. BCE). After his birth on Delos, Apollo travels the Greek mainland seeking a site for his oracle and comes to the spring beneath Parnassus, where a monstrous she-dragon (*drakaina*) ravages flocks and men. Apollo shoots her with his bow; as she dies he taunts her: "There rot (*pythein*) now upon the man-feeding earth" — and from that rotting the place takes the name **Pytho** and the god the title **Pythian** (and his priestess the **Pythia**) (Hymn Ap. 3.300–374). The Hymn supplies this *pythein* ("to rot") folk-etymology itself; it also, strikingly, makes the Delphic dragoness the **foster-mother of [[typhon]]** (3.305–355), knitting the two great Greek serpents together.

In the later and more familiar tradition the serpent is **male and named Python**, and is explicitly the guardian of an oracle that belonged to **[[gaia]]** (Earth) — sometimes Themis — before Apollo (Aesch. *Eumenides* 1–8; Apollod. *Bibl.* 1.4.1; Ovid *Met.* 1.438–451; Hyginus *Fab.* 140). Apollo's slaying of Python therefore becomes a transfer of the oracle from the **chthonic, earth-born order to the Olympian sky-god** — and, because the killing incurs blood-guilt, the aetiology of Apollo's purification, of the **Septerion** ritual at Delphi, and of the **Pythian Games** founded in the serpent's commemoration (Plut. *Quaest. Graec.* 12; *De defectu oraculorum*; Ovid). Apollo's own epithet *Pythian* and the whole institution of the Pythia thus descend, in Greek aetiology, from the dead body of Python.

## Cross-tradition significance (the Greek dragon-of-the-oracle)

Python is the **Greek chaoskampf serpent in its oracle-seizure variant**: the champion-god kills the chthonic serpent and takes over its site. The single most secure cross-tradition connection is **linguistic and genealogical**. Calvert Watkins (*How to Kill a Dragon*, 1995) reconstructed the Proto-Indo-European serpent-slaying formula \**gʷhen- h₃gʷhim-* ("slew the serpent") and cited the Greek phrase **ἔπεφνε … ὄφιν** (*épephne … óphin*, "he slew the serpent"), of Apollo and Python, as a true cognate of the Vedic **áhann áhim** ("[Indra] slew the serpent"), of [[indra|Indra]] and [[vritra|Vritra]]. On this leg Python is **genealogically related** to Vritra and to the other Indo-European dragons (Hittite [[illuyanka|Illuyanka]], Norse [[jormungandr|Jörmungandr]]) — inherited from a common Indo-European poetic and mythic stock, not merely resembling them.

The other leg of Python's reach is **structural and areal**. Joseph Fontenrose's *Python: A Study of Delphic Myth and Its Origins* (1959) — the standard monograph on this very figure — ranges the Delphic combat alongside the non-Indo-European Near-Eastern dragons: Mesopotamian [[tiamat|Tiamat]], Egyptian [[apophis|Apophis]], and the biblical [[leviathan|Leviathan]]/Rahab. These parallels are **typological** (the ordering, solar/oracular god against the chthonic or marine serpent) rather than linguistic, since Akkadian, Egyptian, and Hebrew are not Indo-European; they belong to the areal-convergence side of the chaoskampf field, plausibly reinforced by Bronze-Age cultural contact (West 1997, 2007), not to the inherited-cognate side. The honest distinction is the one the hub itself draws: **the Apollo–Python formula is a secure Indo-European cognate; the parallels to the Semitic and Egyptian serpents are structural convergence.**

## Disputes

**Was there one serpent or two — and what sex?** The sources disagree: the *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* knows an **unnamed female** dragoness, while the classical mythographers know a **male Python**. Fontenrose (1959) argued that the female *drakaina* of the Hymn and the male *Python* of the later tradition are variants of a single combat that has been doubled and re-gendered in transmission; some later authors (and the scholiasts) even split them into two distinct monsters (the she-dragon Delphyne and the serpent Python). The Atlas treats them as **one figure under two faces**, with the male, named **Python** as the dominant form.

**Inherited mytheme vs. local cult-aetiology.** A second question is how much of the Python story is **inherited Indo-European combat-myth** and how much is **local Delphic aetiology** built backward from the cult (to explain the names Pytho/Pythia, the Septerion ritual, and the Pythian Games). The two are not exclusive: the *gʷhen- h₃gʷhim-* formula (Watkins 1995) shows the combat is genuinely old and inherited, while the *pythein* folk-etymology and the games-aetiology are transparently Greek and secondary. The serpent is therefore best read as an **inherited dragon-combat localized onto, and harnessed to explain, the Delphic oracle** — the Greek member of the chaoskampf family whose distinctive prize is prophecy.
