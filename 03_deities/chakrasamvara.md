# Cakrasaṃvara (Heruka)

## Identity
Cakrasaṃvara ("Wheel of Supreme Bliss" or "Binding of the Wheel") — also called Heruka (referring to his wrathful form) — is the principal wrathful male yidam (meditational deity) of one of the four major Anuttarayoga tantric systems in Tibetan Buddhism. He is dark blue, four-faced (dark blue, green, red, yellow), twelve-armed, standing in dancing warrior posture, and is locked in yab-yum (father-mother embrace, sexual union icon) with his consort [[vajrayogini]]. Most distinctively, he stands on the prostrate bodies of Bhairava and Kālarātrī — wrathful forms of Śiva and his consort — an explicit iconographic statement of Buddhist supersession of Śaiva tantric tradition.

Doctrinally, Cakrasaṃvara embodies *upāya* (skillful means / compassionate method), the masculine principle of tantric non-dual enlightenment. His union with Vajrayoginī (*prajñā*, wisdom, the feminine principle) is the visual representation of the *advaya* (non-dual) philosophical ideal: the inseparability of compassion and wisdom, bliss and emptiness. The 62-deity mandala of Cakrasaṃvara is one of the most complex iconographic systems in Tibetan art.

Alexis Sanderson's landmark 2009 essay "The Śaiva Age" documents that the entire pīṭha (sacred site) system of the Cakrasaṃvara mandala — its 64 sacred geography locations — is derived point-for-point from Śaiva tantric sacred geography, with Buddhist deities systematically replacing Śaiva ones. This is among the most precisely documented cases of one religious tradition appropriating another's cosmological architecture in world religion scholarship.

## Earliest attestation
The root tantra (*Śrī Cakrasaṃvara Tantra*, also *Laghuśambara* or *Herukābhidhāna*) is dated by scholars to approximately 8th–9th c. CE (late Indian tantric period). Key early commentators include Kāṇha/Kṛṣṇācārya (~9th c.), Lūyipāda, Ghantāpāda, and Nāropā (956–1040 CE). The tradition reached Tibet primarily through Marpa Lotsawa (~1012–1097 CE), who transmitted it to Milarepa — making this one of the foundational lineages of the Kagyu school. The Cakrasaṃvara system is also central to Sakya and Gelug practice.

## Mythological role
Within the tantric system:
1. **Yidam visualization**: The practitioner generates Cakrasaṃvara's form (blue, twelve-armed, four-faced) in the course of sādhana practice; identifies themselves as Cakrasaṃvara and their partner/consort as Vajrayoginī; this visualization transmutes ordinary desire-experience into the direct perception of bliss-emptiness.
2. **Mandala of 62 deities**: The Cakrasaṃvara mandala contains 62 deities arranged in four circles at four directional sacred pīṭhas — a cosmological map of the transformed body as enlightened geography.
3. **Trampling of Bhairava**: The most theologically charged iconographic element — Cakrasaṃvara stands on Bhairava (wrathful Śiva) and Kālarātrī (his consort). This enacts two levels of meaning: (a) the historical: Buddhist tantric appropriation and supersession of Śaiva tradition; (b) the doctrinal: the trampling of ego-attachment and fierce delusion (represented by Bhairava's wrathful energy) beneath the higher compassion of awakened mind.
4. **Six Yogas of Nāropā**: The Cakrasaṃvara transmission is the primary vehicle for the Six Yogas system (inner heat/tummo, illusory body, dream yoga, clear light, consciousness transference/phowa, bardo/intermediate-state yoga) — among the most complete meditation systems in any tradition.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[bhairava]]** — *direct-influence*: Cakrasaṃvara literally stands on Bhairava. Sanderson's documentation establishes this as not merely symbolic but as a historical-political act: Buddhist tantric teachers systematically occupied Śaiva sacred sites and placed their own deities where Bhairava had previously been worshipped. The most explicit cross-tradition power-relationship in Tibetan iconography.
- **[[shiva]]** — *direct-influence*: Bhairava is a form of Śiva; Cakrasaṃvara trampling Bhairava = Buddhist cosmology placing itself above the Śaiva system from which it drew heavily. This is simultaneously a borrowing and a supersession.
- **[[mahakala]]** — *direct-influence*: Mahākāla is the Cakrasaṃvara system's guardian and protector (dharmapāla). Snellgrove documents their close iconographic and ritual relationship within the same tantric cycle.
- **[[yamantaka]]** — *direct-influence*: Yamāntaka (Vajrabhairava) is another major Anuttarayoga yidam — the "killer of death," associated with the Guhyasamāja/Yamāntaka cycle. Together with Cakrasaṃvara and Kālacakra, these three form the trio of principal Anuttarayoga systems in Tibetan Gelug practice.
- **[[kalachakra]]** — *direct-influence*: The Kālacakra cycle is the third major Anuttarayoga system alongside Cakrasaṃvara and Guhyasamāja. All three share the yab-yum iconographic structure and bliss-emptiness doctrine.
- **[[shekhinah]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: The Kabbalistic *hieros gamos* — the union of the Holy One (masculine *tiferet*) with the Shekhinah (feminine presence, dwelling of God) — is the closest structural parallel in Western mysticism to the Cakrasaṃvara-Vajrayoginī yab-yum. Both represent the cosmos-sustaining union of masculine and feminine divine principles as the ground of all being.
- **[[avalokitesvara]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Both Avalokiteśvara and Cakrasaṃvara personify karuṇā in Tibetan Buddhism; Avalokiteśvara as the peaceful form (maṇi mantra, thousand arms of gentle compassion), Cakrasaṃvara as the wrathful form (ferocious compassion that destroys delusion without hesitation).
- **[[tara]]** — *direct-influence*: Tārā is the female counterpart/emanation of Avalokiteśvara in the peaceful-compassion spectrum; Vajrayoginī is Cakrasaṃvara's consort in the wrathful-wisdom spectrum. The two pairs (Avalokiteśvara-Tārā; Cakrasaṃvara-Vajrayoginī) map the duality of Buddhist compassion-expression.

## Iconography
Standard Tibetan iconography: dark blue body; four faces — the principal dark blue with white, red, and yellow additional faces; twelve arms (holding an assortment of ritual objects: skull-cup, vajra, trident/khatvāṅga, hook, noose, bell, damaru drum, flayed elephant-skin stretched overhead); tiger-skin skirt; crown of five dried skulls; garland of fifty human heads; crescent moon ornament. His right leg is extended (warrior pose), trampling Bhairava beneath; his left leg is bent, with Kālarātrī beneath. He is in yab-yum embrace with [[vajrayogini]], her legs wrapped around his waist, the two in inseparable union.
