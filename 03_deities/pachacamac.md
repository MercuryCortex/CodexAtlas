# Pachacamac

## Identity
Pachacamac — from Quechua *pacha* (world, earth, time) and *kamaq* (animator, he who gives soul/life, from *kamay* = to animate, to bring into being) — is "He Who Animates the World," the great oracle deity of the Peruvian coastal tradition, worshipped continuously at the Pachacamac temple complex in the Lurín Valley (near present-day Lima) for approximately 1,800 years, from roughly 200 BCE through the Spanish conquest of 1532. The site itself is one of the largest pre-Columbian ceremonial complexes in South America, with successive building phases spanning Wari, Lima, Ychsma, and Inca periods. Pachacamac's cult was already ancient when the Inca absorbed it — the Inca built their own temple to Inti (the Sun) at the site while leaving Pachacamac's oracle undisturbed, a pragmatic recognition of the oracle's pan-Andean prestige.

Pachacamac represents the *coastal* theological tradition, in contrast to the highland creator [[viracocha]] — their relationship and possible harmony is the central question of Andean comparative theology (Salomon 1991). Where Viracocha is associated with the high altiplano, Lake Titicaca, and stone-and-clay creation, Pachacamac is associated with the coast, the animate earth, and oracle speech.

## Earliest attestation
- Archaeological evidence: the Pachacamac temple site dates to approximately 200 BCE (Lima culture levels), making it one of the longest-continuously-occupied oracle sites in the Americas.
- Documentary: the *Huarochirí Manuscript* ([[phase-8-006-huarochiri-manuscript]], c. 1608) records Pachacamac in the context of Andean local theology. Early Spanish colonial sources — Cieza de León (1553), Miguel Cabello Balboa (1586) — describe the Pachacamac oracle and the creation narrative. Hernando Pizarro visited the oracle in 1533 (just after the conquest) and left a description.

## Mythological role
The primary Pachacamac creation narrative (preserved in colonial sources): the previous creator Con (or Kon) made the first humans but gave them a barren world. Pachacamac displaced Con, killed his humans, and created new humans from the ground. But Pachacamac also neglected to feed his new creation. The humans starved. The Sun (Inti / [[inti]]) intervened, giving the woman of the first human pair the capacity to produce food rapidly — she gave birth to food plants. Pachacamac was angered and killed the child; from the child's body grew the food plants of the coastal Andes: from his teeth came maize, from his ribs came yucca, from his flesh came various fruits and vegetables. This myth of the deity who creates but fails to provide, with a subsequent food-provision from a solar or sacrificial source, is characteristic of Pachacamac's role as a creator whose creation remains incomplete without additional divine acts.

Pachacamac governs **earthquakes**: when he moves, the earth shakes. This is because he is not merely a deity *of* the earth but the animating power *within* the earth — the earth is his body, and its trembling is his motion. This is structurally identical to Poseidon's earthquake domain but arising independently in a coastal context where seismic activity was intense.

The **oracle** function: Pachacamac spoke through his idol — a wooden totem kept in the innermost chamber of the temple, accessible only to the highest priests. Pilgrims came from across the Andes seeking his oracular counsel. The oracle's prestige was such that the Inca did not destroy it but instead integrated Pachacamac worship into the broader Inca state religious system, requiring annual offerings at the site. Francisco Pizarro's brother Hernando forced access to the inner idol in 1533 and found it — a disappointingly simple wooden pole. The contrast between the oracle's immense prestige and the plainness of its material representation is itself theologically significant: Pachacamac as the animate earth cannot be represented; the wooden idol is a placeholder for an invisible animating power.

## Cross-tradition identifications

- **[[viracocha]]** — *structural-parallel (the great Andean divide)*: The Pachacamac (coastal) vs. Viracocha (highland) contrast is the central polarity of Andean comparative theology. Salomon (1991) frames it as a fundamental divide in Andean religious geography. Both are creator-gods who shaped humanity; both are associated with a pre-Inca era; both had their cults absorbed (not destroyed) by Inca state religion. The key difference: Viracocha is the wandering highland creator associated with stone figures and Lake Titicaca; Pachacamac is the coastal earth-animator and oracle. The Inca synthesis placed Inti (Sun) above both, but neither Pachacamac nor Viracocha was eliminated. This coastal-vs-highland theological divide parallels Egyptian regional theology: the Memphite Theology of [[marduk]]-type creator vs. the Heliopolitan solar creator — regional traditions with competing cosmogonies harmonized at the imperial level.

- **[[apollo]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: The Pachacamac oracle at Lurín and the Delphic oracle of Apollo are the two most prominent pilgrimage-oracle sites of their respective civilizations. The structural parallel is striking: (1) both drew pilgrims from the entire known world seeking divine guidance; (2) both combined a prophetic/oracular function with a solar or light-world connection (Apollo as sun god; Pachacamac associated with the animate cosmic order that the sun governs); (3) both had elaborate priestly mediator systems controlling access to the divine voice; (4) both were politically powerful — kings and rulers made policy based on oracle pronouncements. The analogy is functional, not theological — these are the two strongest cases in their respective traditions of the oracle-god as political-cosmic authority.

- **[[enki-ea]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Enki (Sumerian) / Ea (Akkadian) creates humans through a wisdom-trick but often creates flawed or incomplete humans requiring correction (*Atrahasis*: the early humans are too numerous and noisy; Enlil sends floods). Pachacamac creates humans and fails to feed them. Both are creator-wisdom figures whose creation needs supplementation by other divine actors. Both also combine coastal/water domain with creative intelligence: Enki's domain is the *abzu* (freshwater abyss under the earth); Pachacamac's power emanates from the coastal earth.

- **[[poseidon]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Poseidon as *Enosichthon* ("Earth-Shaker") and as the deity who controls coastal waters is the closest Old World parallel to Pachacamac's earthquake power. Both are coastal powers; both cause earthquakes; both receive major shrine complexes near the sea. The parallel is functional: the conceptualization of a deity as the animating power inside the earth whose motion causes seismic events appears independently in coastal Andean and Greek Mediterranean religious thought.

## Iconography
The Pachacamac idol — a wooden pole carved with figures — was the primary cult object. The innermost sanctum was wrapped in textiles; the idol within was accessible to priests only after years of fasting and preparation. The visible architectural expression of Pachacamac worship is the massive pyramidal platform complexes at the Lurín site, built over centuries by successive cultures. Unlike most Andean deities, Pachacamac was rarely depicted in portable art — his power was localized in his oracle site.

## Disputes
- The relationship between "Con" (a northern coastal deity recorded by some Spanish chroniclers as the predecessor of Pachacamac) and Pachacamac himself is unclear. Some scholars treat them as distinct figures; others as aspects of the same theological tradition recorded at different times.
- Whether the coastal Pachacamac tradition and the highland Viracocha tradition were originally entirely separate or had common roots remains debated.
- The degree to which the Inca "synchronized" Pachacamac with Inti (forcing the oracle under solar authority) vs. preserving genuine coastal independence is contested.
