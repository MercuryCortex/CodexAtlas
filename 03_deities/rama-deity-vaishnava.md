# Rama (Vaishnava avatar)

See also [[rama]] (stub node).

The seventh *avatāra* of [[vishnu]] (or [[vishnu]]) in Puranic theology; hero of Valmiki's Sanskrit *Rāmāyaṇa* (~3rd–2nd c. BCE in core form) and of Tulsidas's Awadhi vernacular *Ramcharitmanas* (1574 CE). In the bhakti devotional tradition centered on Rama — *Rāma-bhakti* — Rama is not merely a hero or an avatar but the supreme personal deity, simultaneously God-as-king and God-as-ideal-human.

## Maryādā Puruṣottama

Rama's distinctive theological title is *maryādā puruṣottama* — "the highest person of the limit / norm." Where [[krishna-deity-vaishnava]] embodies *lila* (divine play, transcending limits), Rama embodies *maryādā* (the binding norm, the socially and cosmically given limit). Rama keeps every promise and obligation even at devastating personal cost:
- Keeps his father's boon (goes into 14-year exile rather than become king as rightfully his)
- Keeps his warrior dharma (kills Ravana despite Ravana's Brahmin status)
- Keeps his king's duty (banishes Sita on a citizen's rumor, despite his love for her)

This makes Rama the paradigm of *dharmic* heroism — supremely virtuous precisely because he subordinates personal desire to cosmic-social order.

## Textual history

The Rama story has many forms:
- **Valmiki's Rāmāyaṇa** (Sanskrit, ~3rd–2nd c. BCE in Bālakāṇḍa's latest layers): the critical text; Goldman et al.'s 7-volume Princeton translation is the scholarly standard.
- **Kamban's Iramavataram** (Tamil, ~12th c. CE): deeply different theological emphasis; Kamba Ramayanam is one of Tamil literature's masterpieces.
- **Tulsidas's Ramcharitmanas** (Awadhi, 1574 CE): the most-recited Hindu text after the Bhagavad Gita in North India; Rama explicitly identified as the supreme deity (not just Vishnu's avatar); deeply emotional bhakti framework. Lutgendorf (1991) is the standard study.
- **Buddhist Dasaratha Jataka**: Rama as a previous birth of the Bodhisattva; the moral of the jataka differs significantly from the Hindu versions.
- **Thai Ramakien**, **Indonesian Kakawin Ramayana**: Southeast Asian receptions showing the pan-regional reach.

## Cross-tradition significance

**MASSIVE-win — Rama-avatar ↔ Christ incarnation:**

The Vaishnava avatar theology asks: why would the infinite, self-sufficient God *voluntarily descend* (*avatāra* = descent) into a finite, mortal, suffering human form? The theological answers developed in the Puranas parallel the Christian incarnation debate almost point-for-point:

- *Why descend at all?* Answer: to restore cosmic order (*dharma-samsthapanarthaya* — BG 4.8); the Incarnation also restores the disrupted order between humanity and God.
- *Does the avatar truly suffer?* This parallels the Docetist controversy — did Christ truly suffer on the cross, or was it appearance? The Ramaite tradition affirms Rama genuinely suffers (he weeps for Sita, mourns Lakshman's injury), which the bhakti-tradition reads as God's capacity for compassion — exactly the anti-Docetist argument of Irenaeus and Athanasius.
- *The avatar's relationship to the supreme deity*: In Ramaite bhakti, Rama *is* Vishnu in full descent — not a mere manifestation but the complete divine person. This parallels the Chalcedonian "fully God, fully human" formula.

These structural parallels are noted in Hindu-Christian dialogue scholarship (Panikkar, 1981; Clooney, 2010) but are not claimed as historical transmission. They represent independent theological trajectories arriving at formally similar answers to the same problems about divine immanence.

## Modern reception

Rama's image has been politically mobilized in modern India: the Ram Janmabhoomi movement (claiming Ayodhya as Rama's birthplace), the demolition of the Babri Masjid (1992), and the 2024 Ram Mandir consecration make Rama simultaneously a devotional figure and a political symbol of Hindu majoritarianism.
