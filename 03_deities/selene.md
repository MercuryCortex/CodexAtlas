# Selene

## Identity
Selene (Greek *Σελήνη*) is the **personified moon of Greek religion**, sister of [[helios]] (sun) and Eos (dawn) and daughter of the Titans Hyperion and Theia. Her own narrative mythology is limited — the principal story is her love for the mortal shepherd Endymion, whom she perpetually visits in his enchanted sleep — but she is **disproportionately important in late-antique magical, theurgic, and Hekate-syncretic contexts**. By the Roman imperial period Selene merges substantially with [[hekate]] (a fierce chthonic goddess herself) and with the lunar aspect of [[artemis]], producing a **lunar triple-goddess** that is invoked in the Greek Magical Papyri (PGM) more frequently than any other female divine name and that plays a central role in [[phase-4-022-chaldean-oracles]]-style theurgy.

## Earliest attestation
- **Homer** *Iliad* 23.460 (chariot of Selene as simile); references in *Odyssey*.
- **Homeric Hymn 32 (*To Selene*)** — extended early hymn.
- **Hesiod** *Theogony* 371–374 — daughter of Hyperion and Theia.
- Endymion myth attested by Sappho fragment, Apollonius, etc.
- Lunar-Hekate-Artemis syncretism crystallizes in Hellenistic period; standard by Roman imperial times.
- **Greek Magical Papyri** (2nd–5th c. CE Egypt) — Selene-Hekate-Artemis invocations dominate the lunar-magic corpus.

## Mythological role
Selene's principal narrative is the **Endymion myth**: she falls in love with the beautiful mortal shepherd of Caria, who is granted eternal sleep so she may visit him perpetually in his unaware state. The myth is used in late-antique and Renaissance art as an allegory of soul-encounter, contemplation, or unattainable love.

In Hellenistic and Roman magical practice Selene is the principal **goddess of monthly time, women's bodies, and lunar magic** — invoked at new moon and full moon, for love-magic, curse-magic, healing-magic, and theurgic ascent.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **Luna (Roman)** — *ancient-identification*: standard Roman *interpretatio*.
- **[[hekate]]** — *ancient-identification* (Hellenistic syncretism): Selene-Hekate(-Artemis) as the lunar triple-goddess of crossroads, magic, and women's mysteries.
- **[[artemis]]** — *ancient-identification*: late-Hellenistic lunar Artemis absorbs Selene.
- **[[isis-hellenistic]]** — *ancient-identification*: Isis as lunar goddess in Apuleius's universal-goddess aretalogy.
- **Mary as "Stella Maris" and Marian lunar iconography** — *appropriated-by*: medieval and early-modern Christian Mariology absorbs lunar iconography — Mary "clothed with the sun, with the moon under her feet" (Revelation 12:1) is read through and integrates Selene-Isis-Hekate-Artemis lunar-feminine tradition; widespread in medieval and Renaissance Marian art (the *Immaculata* standing on the crescent moon).
- **[[hathor]] / [[isis-egyptian]] (Egyptian lunar contexts)** — *parallel-motif* and *syncretic-identification*: Greek-Egyptian magical papyri merge Selene with Egyptian lunar deities (Iah / Khonsu / lunar Isis).

## Iconography
- **Crescent moon** atop her head (cresting like horns) — her signature attribute
- **Lunar disc** behind the head
- **Torch** in hand (light of the moon)
- **Two-horse chariot** crossing the night sky (parallel to Helios's quadriga)
- Often **billowing veil** above the head, formed into an arc — visualizing the lunar dome

## Disputes
- **Selene as proper deity vs. cosmic personification.** Whether Selene was a recipient of actual cult-worship in classical Greek religion or was primarily a poetic and astronomical personification is debated. Burkert 1985 treats her as having limited but real cult; the literary-personification view holds in much classical material.
- **Date of Selene-Hekate syncretism.** Gradual through Hellenistic period, fully standardized by 1st c. CE; specific milestones disputed (Johnston 1990).
- **Marian lunar iconography source.** The "moon under her feet" iconography is biblically rooted (Revelation 12:1) but its visual elaboration in medieval and early-modern Catholicism draws on classical lunar-goddess imagery. The polemic-inversion vs. continuity question is alive in art-historical literature.
