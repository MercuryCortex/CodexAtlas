# Höðr

## Identity
Höðr (Old Norse: "Hǫðr," anglicized as Hod or Hodur) is the blind Norse god whose unwitting hand kills [[baldr]] — the god of light, beauty, and joy — and thereby sets in motion the final sequence of events leading to Ragnarök. He is one of the most tragic figures in Norse mythology: guilty of the greatest crime (the killing of the most beloved god) yet without intention or knowledge of his act. Blind, he is led by [[loki]] to throw the mistletoe dart that is the only thing in existence capable of harming Baldr — Loki having engineered this situation by discovering the single exception to [[frigg]]'s protective oath. After Ragnarök, Höðr and Baldr return together from death, their severed brotherhood restored in the new world. He is the instrument of fate — the hand through which inevitable doom operates — rather than its agent.

## Earliest attestation
Höðr is attested in Snorri Sturluson's **Prose Edda** (Gylfaginning, chapter 49, ~1220 CE) as the killer of Baldr. He also appears in the Eddic poem **Völuspá** (Seeress's Prophecy, ~1000 CE oral tradition) in cryptic reference to the mistletoe arrow. The skald Saxo Grammaticus's *Gesta Danorum* (~1200 CE) preserves a euhemerized version in which Hother is a mortal prince in competition with the god-like Balderus (Baldr), giving the myth a different character — but confirming Höðr's ancient standing in Scandinavian tradition.

## Mythological role
**The Killing of Baldr.** Baldr's invulnerability is ensured by Frigg's oath: she has made every substance in the world swear not to harm him, and the gods amuse themselves by throwing weapons at Baldr that bounce off harmlessly. Loki discovers the one exception — the **mistletoe** — which Frigg had not asked, thinking it too young and insignificant to harm anything. Loki fashions a dart of mistletoe, finds the blind Höðr standing apart from the games, and offers to guide his throw so he too can participate in honoring Baldr. Höðr throws the mistletoe. Baldr falls dead.

The layered tragedy of this moment: Höðr's exclusion from the games because of blindness (he cannot participate in the throwing-games that celebrate Baldr), his inclusion by Loki in an act that seems an honor, his complete ignorance of what he is doing, and the outcome that destroys the most beloved being in the cosmos — all make Höðr the definitive figure of **unintended doom** in world mythology.

**Punishment.** [[odin]] fathers Váli specifically to avenge Baldr's death — Váli is born within one night, grows to manhood in one day, and kills Höðr without washing, combing, or eating until the deed is done. Höðr descends to [[hel]]'s realm.

**Return After Ragnarök.** The *Völuspá* and the Prose Edda agree: after Ragnarök and the renewal of the world, **Höðr and Baldr return from Hel together**, reconciled, to dwell in the new creation. This return is one of Norse mythology's most striking redemption-motifs — the blind killer and his radiant victim reunited in the world reborn. Höðr's culpability is absorbed into the larger structure of fate, cosmic destruction, and renewal.

**Light and Dark Pairing.** Baldr (light, summer, radiance) and Höðr (darkness, blindness, winter) are sometimes interpreted as a seasonal mythological pair — the death of the summer sun at midsummer. This reading connects to the widespread Indo-European myth of the divine twins or cosmic complements (compare the Dioskouroi, the Aśvins), though Norse scholarship does not universally accept the seasonal-myth interpretation.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[baldr]]** — *ancient-identification*: Höðr and Baldr are brothers whose linked fate structures the entire Baldr myth; their separation by death and reunion after Ragnarök is the core of the story.
- **[[loki]]** — *folk-syncretism*: Loki is the intentional agent who makes Höðr the unwitting instrument of Baldr's death; the Höðr-Loki pairing is the mechanism of the mythological catastrophe. Snorri, *Prose Edda*, Gylfaginning 49.
- **[[odin]]** — Höðr's father; Odin's response to Baldr's death (fathering Váli to avenge it) directly involves Höðr's punishment.
- **[[frigg]]** — Höðr's mother; Frigg's failure to obtain the oath from mistletoe is what makes Höðr's shot lethal.
- **[[hel]]** — ruler of the death realm where both Höðr and Baldr go after death; the place from which they return in the new world.

## Iconography
Höðr has almost no independent iconographic tradition — he is primarily a literary figure. He is typically depicted in modern illustration as a tall, pale god with closed or blank eyes, guided by Loki's hand. His darkness is the foil to Baldr's golden radiance. No archaeological evidence of an independent cult of Höðr has been identified.

## Disputes
Whether Höðr was originally an independent deity with his own cult or was always primarily a narrative figure in the Baldr myth is debated. Some scholars (notably Eugen Mogk) argue he is a relatively late literary creation. The relationship between Snorri's Prose Edda version and Saxo Grammaticus's euhemerized *Gesta Danorum* version — in which Hother is a mortal rival of the divine Balderus — is unresolved; Saxo's version may preserve an older tradition that Snorri mythologizes. The seasonal-myth interpretation (Baldr = summer sun, Höðr = winter darkness) was popular in 19th-century solar-mythology scholarship and has fallen partly out of favor.
