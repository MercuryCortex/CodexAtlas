# Tangaroa

## Identity
Pan-Polynesian sea-god, with significant differences in cosmic-status across Polynesia: in Māori tradition, Tangaroa is one of the sons of Ranginui and Papatūānuku, god of the sea and fish, brother of Tāne; in Tahitian tradition, *Ta'aroa* is the *supreme* creator — Henry's *Ancient Tahiti* (1928) records a Tahitian cosmogony in which Ta'aroa exists alone in a cosmic shell, breaks the shell, and creates all the gods and the cosmos; in Samoan tradition, *Tagaloa* is similarly the supreme creator. The Hawaiian *Kanaloa* is cognate but holds a less central position — paired with Kāne as wandering companion.

## Earliest attestation
Ancestral Polynesian Society religion (~1000 BCE onward by linguistic reconstruction); attested in each Polynesian tradition's oral and (later) textual record. The varying status (sea-god in Māori vs. supreme creator in Tahitian) is a striking case of cultural-religious differentiation from a common ancestral system.

## Mythological role
Māori: in the Ranginui-Papatūānuku separation narrative, Tangaroa is one of the sons who urges separation; once separated, Tangaroa takes the sea as his realm. He has a long-running conflict with his brother Tāne (forest), since Tāne shelters Tangaroa's grandchildren (the trees that humans use to build canoes that venture onto Tangaroa's sea).

Tahitian: Ta'aroa exists alone in a primordial shell; he breaks the shell, the upper half becomes the sky, the lower the earth; he then creates the other gods, the cosmos, and humans from his own body (a Polynesian Purusha-type cosmogony).

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **Kanaloa (Hawaiian)** — *manuscript-transmission*: linguistic cognate; functional shifts noted above.
- **Ta'aroa (Tahitian)** / **Tagaloa (Samoan)** — *manuscript-transmission*: cognate forms.
