# Svantovit

## Identity
Svantovit was the supreme deity of the Rani, the Slavic inhabitants of Rügen Island on the Baltic coast, whose great sanctuary at Arkona (Cape Arkona, northernmost point of Rügen) was the most important surviving Slavic pagan cult-center in northern Europe at the time of its destruction. He is described by Saxo Grammaticus (*Gesta Danorum* XIV, ~1200 CE) and by Helmold of Bosau (*Chronica Slavorum* I.52, ~1168 CE) — our two main sources — as a four-faced idol standing twelve feet tall, holding a drinking horn, housed in a richly appointed wooden temple. Helmold calls him *deus deorum* among the Slavs: "god of gods."

## Earliest attestation
The sanctuary at Arkona is archaeologically attested; its destruction in **1168 CE** by the Danish King Valdemar I and Archbishop Absalon is a historical event (recorded in Saxo, who participated in the campaign). Earlier textual reference: Helmold wrote ~1168 CE; Saxo wrote ~1200 CE, drawing on earlier materials. There is no surviving pre-Christian Slavic textual source describing Svantovit. Archaeological finds at the Arkona headland include traces of a large enclosed compound.

## Mythological role
**The Four Faces.** Svantovit's idol had four faces — two at front and back, two at the sides — each looking toward one of the four cardinal directions. This iconography encodes cosmic omniscience: a god who sees in all directions simultaneously is a god who sees *everything*. The Zbruch Idol (9th–10th c. Ukraine, four-faced stone pillar, now in Krakow) is sometimes identified as a representation of Svantovit or of a functionally similar four-faced Slavic deity, though this remains contested.

**The White Horse Divination.** A white horse was kept at Arkona, tended by the temple priests, never ridden by any mortal — it was Svantovit's horse. Before any major military expedition, the priests would set up a course of three rows of crossed spears and lead the horse through them; if it stepped through with the right hoof first each time, the omen was favorable; left hoof first meant the campaign should be delayed. The horse's behavior was Svantovit's oracle.

**The Mead Horn.** The idol held a drinking horn. Each year the chief priest would examine how much mead remained — a full horn meant a coming year of abundance; if it had diminished, famine was expected. He would then pour out the old mead as libation and refill the horn, praying for victory and plenty.

**The Destruction of Arkona (1168 CE).** The Danish Christian king Valdemar I and Archbishop Absalon led a naval expedition to Rügen, besieged Arkona, and demanded conversion. The Rani capitulated. The idol was dragged out, dismembered, and used for firewood. The temple's accumulated treasure (one-third of all war-booty from Rani raids had been deposited there for generations) was distributed to the Danish soldiers. This is among the best-documented acts of pagan temple destruction in northern Europe.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[brahma]]** — *cross-tradition-parallel*: Brahma's four faces directed to the four cardinal directions is the most direct iconographic parallel to Svantovit's four-faced idol — both encode the supreme deity's omnidirectional cosmic vision in the same sculptural grammar. The parallel is typological; there is no historical connection between Slavic and Indian traditions here.
- **[[janus]]** — *cross-tradition-parallel*: Janus's doubled face encodes omniscient vision across the threshold (past/future, inside/outside); Svantovit's quadrupled face encodes omniscient vision across the four directions — both express divine total-awareness through multiplied faces.
- **[[epona]]** and **[[the-ashvins]]** — *cross-tradition-parallel*: the oracular white horse at Arkona belongs to the wider Indo-European sacred-horse tradition; Epona (Gaulish horse-goddess) and the Ashvins (Vedic divine horsemen) represent the divine-horse complex in Celtic and Vedic branches.
- **[[perun]]** — *mythological-partner*: Svantovit vs. Perun represents the West Slavic vs. East Slavic supreme-deity traditions; both are warrior-patron supreme gods but with regional distribution reflecting different tribal confederacies.
