# Harihara

## Identity
A composite syncretic deity combining Viṣṇu (*Hari*) on the right half and Śiva (*Hara*) on the left half of a single body — the iconographic-theological embodiment of Vaiṣṇava-Śaiva theological harmony. The Harihara form makes the explicit theological claim that the apparent rivalry between Vaiṣṇavism and Śaivism (the two principal pan-Indian theistic Hindu traditions) is superficial: at the metaphysical level, *Hari* (Viṣṇu) and *Hara* (Śiva) are the same supreme reality. The Harihara iconography is among the most explicit pan-Indian sectarian-unification statements in any tradition, and the form has been continuously produced from ~4th–5th c. CE (Gupta period) through the medieval and into the modern period, especially in Karnataka and in Cambodian Khmer-period (Angkor) sculpture.

## Earliest attestation
- **Sanskrit textual sources** ~late-classical: Mahābhārata Anuśāsana-parvan and various Purāṇas treat the Hari-Hara identity narratively.
- **Iconographic-archaeological** ~Gupta period onward (4th–6th c. CE): the formal Harihara icon emerges in Gupta-period sculpture; multiple major Harihara sculptures from the Gupta and post-Gupta periods survive across North and Central India.
- **Cambodian Khmer attestation** ~6th–9th c. CE: substantial Harihara sculptural tradition at Phnom Da, Sambor Prei Kuk, and other pre-Angkor Khmer sites — the Cambodian Harihara cult is among the most institutionally elaborated outside India. The Khmer royal cult of Harihara was a major pre-Angkor religious-political institution.
- **Karnataka temple**: the city of Harihar in central Karnataka, named for and housing a major Harihara temple (~10th c. founding) on the bank of the Tungabhadra river.

## Mythological role
- Multiple narrative myths in the Purāṇic tradition justify the Hari-Hara composite: in some, Viṣṇu and Śiva merge bodily to demonstrate their non-difference; in others, the form arises in response to sectarian conflict among the devotees of the two deities, the supreme reality manifesting in the composite form to resolve the conflict.
- The Cambodian Khmer royal cult interpreted Harihara as the cosmic king-god — the divine sovereign whose dual aspect mirrored the king's combined civilizing and martial functions.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **Vishnu and Shiva**: the entire point of the deity is the explicit identification.
- **Cambodian Khmer royal-cosmological theology**: Harihara at pre-Angkor Khmer was the cosmic-royal supreme deity. The transition from Harihara to Devarāja (the deified-king cult of Angkor-period Khmer Shaivism / Vaishnavism) is a major moment in Southeast Asian Hindu religious history.
- **Vaiṣṇava-Śaiva theological unification literature**: the medieval *Hari-Hara-stotras* (composite-deity hymns) and the Harihara temple liturgy systematize the unification.
- **Modern Hindu sectarian-harmony rhetoric**: contemporary Hindu nationalist and modernist movements have invoked the Harihara iconography as evidence of internal-Hindu pluralism. The Vijayanagara empire (1336–1565) under Vidyaranya's intellectual program self-presented in part as Harihara-cult continuity (the founding king was named Harihara I; the dynasty was named *Sangama-vaṃśa* with Harihara at its head).

## Iconography
- Right half: Viṣṇu — typically blue body, holding *cakra* (discus) and *śaṅkha* (conch), wearing yellow *pītāmbara*, with *kirīṭa-mukuṭa* (royal crown).
- Left half: Śiva — typically white or ash-smeared body, holding trident and (sometimes) drum, with snake around neck, third eye on forehead, *jaṭā-mukuṭa* (matted-hair crown), tiger-skin or naked left side.
- The bisecting line down the body is iconographically central; the two halves are visibly distinct.

## Disputes
- **The Harihara cult's standing**: how widespread the Harihara cult was institutionally (vs. how much it was a theological-iconographic motif within Vaishnava and Shaiva contexts) is variably assessed. The Khmer Cambodian Harihara cult is undisputedly institutionally major; the Indian Harihara cult is more diffuse.
- **Pre-Angkor / Angkor transition** in Cambodia: how the Harihara cult relates to the subsequent Devarāja cult is a continuing question in Southeast Asian Hindu studies.
