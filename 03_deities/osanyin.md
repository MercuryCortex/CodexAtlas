# Òsanyìn

## Identity
Òrìṣà of *ewé* (leaves) — the pharmacological-botanical god whose knowledge underlies every Yoruba medicine, every initiatory bath, every healing ritual. Òsanyìn governs the forest as living pharmacy and the secret correspondences between specific leaves and specific orishas. The babaláwo cannot prescribe a remedy, and no orisha-preparation can be made, without Òsanyìn's herbs — making him the pharmacological floor of the entire Ifá-orisha system (Verger 1995).

## Earliest attestation
Yoruba oral tradition; Ifá corpus references throughout. Verger's *Ewé* (1995) is the standard ethnobotanical reference, cataloguing several thousand leaves with their orisha correspondences.

## Mythological role
A central myth: the orishas quarrelled over knowledge of the leaves. Òsanyìn possessed all herbal wisdom; the others wanted access. In the struggle Òsanyìn was injured — left with one leg, one arm, one eye, and a high-pitched bird-like voice. Yansan (Oya) is the one who scattered the calabash of leaves so all orishas could share them. The mutilation is a sacred mark: it marks Òsanyìn as the original sole possessor, and the cosmic dispersal of herbal knowledge is the founding moment of accessible Yoruba medicine.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **Osain (Cuban Lucumí)** — direct diasporic transmission; Cuban *santería* preserves Òsanyìn's pharmacological role in *omiero* (the green ritual-water that consecrates initiates).
- **Ossaim / Ossanha (Brazilian Candomblé)** — direct transmission via Portuguese-Brazilian slave trade; same forest-herbal role.
- **Saint Joseph / Saint Sylvester** — folk-syncretism in Cuba (Brandon 1993).

## Diasporic continuity — pharmacological infrastructure crosses the Atlantic
The Òsanyìn cult demonstrates one of the most-precise documented religious continuities of the Middle Passage: enslaved Yoruba in Cuba and Brazil recreated the botanical-pharmacological knowledge of the West-African forest in tropical-American flora. Cuban *santeros* substituted New-World plants when West-African originals were unavailable, but preserved the orisha-leaf correspondences and the underlying logic. The *omiero* prepared at Lucumí initiations is structurally identical to the West-African ritual bath. Verger (1995) and Cabrera (*El Monte*, 1954) document the specific substitutions. This is one strand of the broader Òrìṣà-diaspora preservation that also produced the [[theme-orisha-saint-syncretism]] mechanism.

## Iconography
One-legged / one-armed figure; bird perched on an iron staff (the *opa Òsanyìn*); green and yellow; the calabash of leaves; placed in the *igbó* (sacred grove) at the forest edge.
