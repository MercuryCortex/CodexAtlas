# Kumarbi

## Identity
Kumarbi is the Hurrian "father of the gods" — a deposed, second-generation sovereign whose displacement from cosmic kingship by his son Teshub is the central narrative of the Hurrian succession myth. Kumarbi's role is the *middle* generation between the primordial Anu (sky) and the storm-warrior king Teshub. He has Hurrian grain associations (he is sometimes identified with grain itself in lists) and is identified across the ancient Near East with whichever deity occupies the "deposed father" slot: Sumero-Akkadian Enlil, Ugaritic-Syrian Dagan, and — via post-Bronze Age transmission — Greek Kronos and Roman Saturn.

The Hurrian *Song of Kumarbi* (also called the *Song of Going Forth*, *Song of Emergence*, or *Song of Heavenly Kingship*) narrates the succession: Alalu rules heaven for nine years; Anu deposes him; Kumarbi attacks Anu, bites off and swallows his genitals; the seed makes Kumarbi pregnant with five gods including Teshub the storm-god; after Teshub's birth, he overthrows his father Kumarbi. The structural parallel to Hesiod's Theogony (Ouranos → Kronos by castration → Zeus by overthrow) is direct and is the single most cited case in comparative ANE→Greek transmission scholarship.

## Earliest attestation
- Boğazköy archive (Hattusa) tablets — Hurrian and Hittite versions of the Kumarbi cycle, ~-1400 to -1200 BCE.
- Hurrian theonyms in Old Assyrian colony texts at Kanesh and elsewhere, ~-1900 to -1750 — Kumarbi attested by name in personal-name compounds.

## Mythological role
- **Song of Kumarbi (Song of Going Forth).** The cosmological succession: Alalu / Anu / Kumarbi / Teshub. Kumarbi castrates Anu, becomes pregnant, gives birth to Teshub, is overthrown by Teshub.
- **Song of Hedammu.** Kumarbi engenders the sea-monster Hedammu with the daughter of the Sea, to take revenge on Teshub. Teshub's sister Šauška seduces and intoxicates Hedammu; Teshub defeats him.
- **Song of Ullikummi.** Kumarbi engenders a stone monster, Ullikummi, on a great rock. Ullikummi grows monstrously fast in the sea; Teshub initially fails to defeat him; eventually Ea cuts the rope binding heaven and earth (the original cutting-tool used by the gods to separate the cosmos), and Teshub defeats Ullikummi.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[kronos]]** — *ancient-identification* (Lucian; Philo of Byblos via Eusebius) + *scholarly-confirmed* (West 1997; Bachvarova 2016). The cleanest cross-Mediterranean myth-transfer case in the ANE→Greek substrate: father-god deposed by his storm-god son via the castration motif and the swallowing motif. (In Hesiod the castration is by sickle and the swallowing is of *children* by Kronos; in the Hurrian myth Kumarbi *swallows the severed genitals of Anu* — the motifs are recombined but the core elements are preserved.)
- **[[enlil]]** — *ancient-identification* in Hurrian-Mesopotamian god-lists.
- **[[dagon]]** — *ancient-identification* in Hurrian-Syrian god-lists.
- **[[saturn-roman]]** — inherited from the Kronos identification.

## Iconography
Limited surviving iconography; usually shown enthroned as a councilor-god holding a sceptre, often with grain associations.

## Disputes
- **Mechanism of transmission to Greece.** The fact of the Kumarbi → Hesiod parallel is unquestioned. The *mechanism* divides scholarship:
  - **West 1997** (*East Face of Helicon*): direct literary / oral-poetic dependence via Late Bronze Age Aegean-Anatolian contact; the Greek tradition inherits the succession myth from a Hurrian-Hittite source.
  - **Burkert 1992** (*Orientalizing Revolution*): transmission is via 8th-/7th-c. BCE Phoenician intermediaries during the "Orientalizing" period of Archaic Greece, not directly from the Bronze Age.
  - **Bachvarova 2016** (*From Hittite to Homer*): both mechanisms operate; emphasizes a continuous tradition of itinerant singers carrying material across the Aegean-Anatolian zone from the Late Bronze Age forward.
- **Whether Kumarbi is originally a grain-god or a father-god.** Hurrian sources are ambiguous; the imperial-era cycles emphasize the father-god role, but onomastic and god-list evidence preserves grain associations.
