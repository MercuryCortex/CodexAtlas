# Humbaba

## Identity

Humbaba (Sumerian **Huwawa**) is the monstrous guardian of the Cedar Forest in the Gilgamesh tradition — a terrifying being set by the god [[enlil]] to protect the cedars, endowed with seven auras of radiance (*melammu*) and a fearsome appearance. Mesopotamian clay masks depict his face as a single coiled line resembling the convolutions of an animal's intestines, used in extispicy (liver-divination) — an image that made Humbaba's face itself an omen.

## Mythological role

Humbaba is the great adversary of the most famous Mesopotamian heroic episode. In the Sumerian poem *Gilgamesh and Huwawa*, and at greater length in the Old Babylonian and Standard Babylonian *Epic of Gilgamesh*, [[gilgamesh-nephilim]] and his companion Enkidu journey to the distant Cedar Forest to make a name for themselves by felling its sacred cedars and confronting its guardian. With the aid of the sun-god [[utu-shamash]], who pins Humbaba with thirteen winds, they overpower him. Humbaba pleads for his life, but the heroes kill him. The deed brings glory — but also the wrath of the gods, who decree the death of Enkidu in recompense, setting in motion Gilgamesh's confrontation with his own mortality.

## Cross-tradition significance

The episode is a Mesopotamian form of the **monster-guardian-of-the-sacred-wilderness** motif — the hero who transgresses a divine boundary to win renown and pays a price. Comparisons have been drawn to later Greek monster-slaying quests and to the broader pattern of the guardian whose killing is both heroic and hubristic. The Humbaba mask, widely distributed, also made him one of the most recognizable apotropaic faces of the ancient Near East.
