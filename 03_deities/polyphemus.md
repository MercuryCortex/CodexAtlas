# Polyphemus

## Identity

Polyphemus (Greek **Πολύφημος**, *Polyphēmos* — "abounding in fame/songs," an irony the later bucolic poets exploited when they made him a singer) is the man-eating Cyclops of *Odyssey* 9, son of [[poseidon]] and the sea-nymph Thoosa, daughter of Phorcys (Od. 1.71-73). He is the only individually named and characterized Cyclops in Homer, one of the **Homeric Cyclopes** — lawless, cave-dwelling shepherd-giants who plant nothing, hold no assemblies, and live "each one giving law to his own wives and children" (Od. 9.112-115), caring nothing for the gods. These Homeric Cyclopes must be distinguished from two other groups bearing the same name: the **Hesiodic Cyclopes** — Brontes, Steropes, and Arges, sons of Uranus and [[gaia]], the smiths who forge Zeus's thunderbolt (*Theogony* 139-146; see [[phase-2-009-hesiod-theogony-works-and-days]]) — and the **"Cyclopean" wall-builders** credited with the giant masonry of Tiryns and Mycenae. Mainstream scholarship (Mondi 1983) treats the three as originally distinct traditions secondarily gathered under one name. Polyphemus has **no cult**: like [[adamastor]], he stands in the Atlas among the deities as a monstrous being of divine parentage occupying a comparative slot — the blinded ogre, the anti-host — not as a worshipped god.

## In the Odyssey (the Kyklopeia, Od. 9.105-566)

The Cyclops episode is among the most famous of Odysseus's adventures and the engine of the whole epic. [[odysseus]] and twelve companions enter the giant's cave while he is out with his flocks; against his men's advice, Odysseus waits to receive the stranger's due of hospitality. What follows is xenia performed in a mirror: the host comes home, pens his guests in with a door-stone twenty-two wagons could not move (9.240-243), and **asks who they are before feeding them** — inverting the Homeric protocol in which the guest is fed and only then questioned. Told that they are suppliants under the protection of Zeus of strangers, Polyphemus answers that "the Cyclopes pay no heed to aegis-bearing Zeus" (9.275-276) and, in place of the guest-meal, seizes two companions, dashes their brains out, and eats them — six men in all across four meals.

Odysseus's revenge is the archetypal victory of *mētis* over force. He stupefies the giant with the unmixed wine of Maron, priest of Apollo (9.196-211, 345-374); gives his name as ***Outis***, "Nobody" (9.366-367) — so that the "guest-gift" the host grants is to eat Nobody last (9.369-370); and with four companions drives a heated olive-wood stake into the sleeping giant's eye (9.375-397). When the neighbouring Cyclopes come to the cave-mouth asking who is harming him, Polyphemus can only cry that *Nobody* is killing him, and they leave (9.403-412). The blinded giant recalls, too late, an old prophecy of the seer Telemus that he would lose his sight at the hands of one Odysseus — he had expected someone bigger (9.507-516). The Greeks escape slung beneath the bellies of the rams (9.425-463), and Odysseus, unable to resist the boast, shouts back his true name from the ship (9.502-505).

That boast is the hinge of the epic. Armed with the name, Polyphemus prays to his father Poseidon (9.528-536): that Odysseus never reach home — or, if fate forbids that, that he come **late, in evil plight, with the loss of all his companions, on a stranger's ship, to find trouble in his house**. Poseidon hears him, and the curse is, point for point, the plot of the *Odyssey*: the ten-year delay, the drowned crews, the Phaeacian ship, the suitors in the hall (cf. Od. 1.68-75, where Poseidon's wrath over his blinded son is given as the reason the hero is still unhomed). The Kyklopeia is thus simultaneously the paradigm **xenia-violation narrative** — the host who eats his guests, set against the good hosts Nestor, Menelaus, and Eumaeus — and the aetiology of the epic's divine machinery.

## After Homer — the satyr play and the lovesick Cyclops

The classical and Hellenistic tradition localized the Cyclopes at **Sicily**, on Etna, and retold the episode in every register. [[euripides]]'s ***Cyclops*** (c. -408), the only satyr play to survive complete, stages Odyssey 9 at Etna with Silenus and the satyrs enslaved in the Cyclops's cave. Around -400 the dithyrambist **Philoxenus of Cythera** gave the figure its second life: his *Cyclops* is the earliest attested treatment of the monster as a lover — hopelessly courting the Nereid **Galatea** — reputedly as satire on Dionysius I of Syracuse. **Theocritus** built two bucolic masterpieces on that conceit (*Idylls* 6 and 11): the young Cyclops as shepherd-poet who discovers that song is the only *pharmakon* for love. **Virgil** returned him to horror in the Achaemenides episode (*Aeneid* 3.588-681), where Aeneas's crew glimpses the blinded giant washing his socket in the sea; **Ovid** fused both strains in the tale of **Acis and Galatea** (*Metamorphoses* 13.738-897), where the serenading giant, refused, crushes his rival Acis under a mountain-rock.

It is the Philoxenus-Theocritus-Ovid line — the **monstrous giant in unrequited love with a sea-nymph** — that descends to Renaissance epic: Camões's [[adamastor]] (*Os Lusíadas* V, 1572), the Titan petrified into the Cape of Good Hope for his hopeless love of Thetis, is built in part on the Polyphemus/Galatea topos (Quint 1993), and onward through the Baroque (Góngora's *Fábula de Polifemo y Galatea*, 1612; Handel's *Acis and Galatea*, 1718). This downstream lineage is **secure literary transmission**: each author demonstrably read the tradition he reworked.

## Cross-tradition significance (the blinded ogre)

Polyphemus is the Greek anchor of one of the most widely attested tale-types in world folklore: **ATU 1137, "The Ogre Blinded"** — the man trapped in the dwelling of a man-eating giant who blinds him with a heated spit and escapes under or disguised in the skins of the flock. Oskar **Hackman** (1904) collected over two hundred variants across Europe, the Caucasus, and the Near East; famous members include the ogre of **Sindbad's third voyage** in the *Arabian Nights* and the Oghuz-Turkic **Tepegöz** of the *Book of Dede Korkut* — the latter so close to the Homeric episode that most scholars place both within one connected tale-tradition. The central scholarly question since Wilhelm **Grimm**'s essay *Die Sage von Polyphem* (1857) has been priority: do the folk variants descend from Homer, or did Homer inherit a tale already old? The settled mainstream position (Hackman 1904; **Page** 1955; **Glenn** 1971) is the latter — the tale-type circulated independently of and almost certainly before the *Odyssey*, and Homer's genius lies in the integration: binding the folktale to the xenia-theme and to Poseidon's wrath, and (on many readings) to the *Outis/mētis* pun that only works in Greek. Julien **d'Huy** (2013) has pressed the antiquity claim furthest, using phylogenetic methods to argue a prehistoric — possibly Palaeolithic — origin for the tale; the method is contested and the claim is reported here as argued, not established.

Beyond the tale-type proper, two comparisons require honest framing. The Irish [[balor]] — the Fomorian giant slain when Lugh drives a sling-stone through his single destroying eye — is a **typological parallel** (the giant destroyed through the single eye), not a variant of ATU 1137: his story has no cave, no flock-escape, no Nobody-trick, and no documented transmission link to the Greek material. The Akkadian [[humbaba]] — the monstrous guardian of the remote Cedar Forest overcome by Gilgamesh's deceptive speech — belongs to the **argued Near Eastern background** of Greek epic (Mondi 1983; Glenn 1971): the structural resemblance (the ogre of the remote domain, tricked rather than overpowered) is part of the broader, mainstream-but-unproven case for Bronze Age Near Eastern transmission into Homeric epic, and independent convergence on the ogre-adventure type cannot be excluded. The vault's tiering reflects these gradations: Adamastor (transmission, T1), Balor and Humbaba (typology/argued background, T2), and the third-eye speculation below (T3).

## Disputes

- **Folktale vs Homer.** As above: the priority debate running from Grimm (1857) through Hackman (1904) to Page (1955) and Glenn (1971). The independence and antiquity of the tale-type is the mainstream position; how much of the extant folk-record has nonetheless been secondarily contaminated by the *Odyssey* (via school-texts and retellings) remains genuinely open, variant by variant.
- **The single eye is not in Homer's text.** The *Odyssey* never states that Polyphemus has one eye; the narrative implies it (one stake, one blinding, total blindness), and the one-eyed anatomy is explicit only in the Hesiodic description of the *other* Cyclopes (*Theogony* 143-145) and in later art and literature. The standard commentary (Heubeck-Hoekstra 1989) treats this as evidence that Homer could assume an audience already familiar with the one-eyed-ogre tale.
- **Three kinds of Cyclopes.** Whether the Homeric shepherds, the Hesiodic thunderbolt-smiths, and the Cyclopean wall-builders were originally one tradition or three secondarily merged under one name (Mondi 1983 argues the latter; this is the mainstream view).
- **Phylogenetic depth.** d'Huy's (2013) Palaeolithic reconstruction of the tale is methodologically contested (tale-phylogenetics imports biological assumptions folklorists dispute); recorded at T2 as an argued claim.
- **The third-eye hypothesis.** The suggestion that the one-eyed Cyclops preserves a degraded memory of a single-divine-eye/third-eye concept (see [[ajna-third-eye]]) is documented in this vault as a Tier-3 interpretive hypothesis; it is not mainstream scholarship, which derives the single eye from the folktale tradition.
