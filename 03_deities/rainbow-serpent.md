# Rainbow Serpent

## Identity
The Rainbow Serpent is the most widely attested Aboriginal Dreaming-being across the Australian continent, found in some form in the religious traditions of dozens of distinct Aboriginal nations especially across northern and central Australia. The Rainbow Serpent is regularly associated with water-sources (rivers, billabongs, springs, waterholes), with the rainbow itself, with rain, and with creator-ancestral activity. Specific names and characters vary radically by nation: Yingarna and her sons Ngalyod and Borlung (Kunwinjku, Arnhem Land); Wagyl (Noongar, southwest Western Australia); Akurra (Adnyamathanha, Flinders Ranges); Wonambi (Pitjantjatjara, Western Desert). The category "Rainbow Serpent" is partly a comparative-analytic abstraction made by 20th-c. anthropology — A.R. Radcliffe-Brown's 1926 article "The Rainbow-Serpent Myth of Australia" defined the comparative category and remains the foundational text for the discussion.

## Earliest attestation
Rock-art evidence: snake imagery in Arnhem Land rock-art has been argued (Taçon et al. 1996) to attest Rainbow Serpent imagery from at least ~6,000 years ago, possibly earlier; the Wandjina rock-art tradition of the Kimberley includes related serpent figures. Continuous ceremonial transmission to the present.

## Mythological role
In many traditions: the Rainbow Serpent shapes the landscape by moving through it; carves rivers; creates billabongs by lying in them; ascends as a rainbow after rain. The Serpent is dangerous — disturbing the Serpent's water-source brings drought, flood, illness; women in certain ritual states must not approach. In other traditions the Serpent is the ancestral mother of human groups, or the creator of particular language-groups, or the punisher of social-transgression.

## Cross-tradition identifications
None within Aboriginal Australia (each nation's Serpent is its own). External comparative attempts (links to Eurasian dragon-serpent figures, to Quetzalcoatl, etc.) have been made by 19th-c. and early-20th-c. comparative-religion writers but have not survived critical scrutiny — typological similarity does not imply contact or descent.

## Disputes
- **Pan-Aboriginal nature.** Older anthropology (Radcliffe-Brown 1926) treated the Rainbow Serpent as the principal pan-Aboriginal religious figure. Current scholarship (Sutton 2003; Mathews 2008) emphasizes the variation across nations and questions whether "Rainbow Serpent" names a single comparable phenomenon or a family of distinct nation-specific beings sharing some features.
- **Pre-contact-AD antiquity.** Rock-art arguments for ~6,000-year attestation (Taçon et al. 1996) are influential but contested.

## Iconography
Serpent form, often elongated; rainbow association; water-source imagery; rock-art representations across northern Australia.
