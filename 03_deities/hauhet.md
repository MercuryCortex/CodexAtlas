# Hauhet

## Identity

Hauhet ("the boundless" — feminine) is one of the eight primordial deities of the Hermopolitan Ogdoad and the female member of its boundlessness-pair, the counterpart of [[heh-egyptian]]. With the other three pairs — Nun/Naunet (the primordial waters), Kek/Kauket (darkness), and Amun/Amaunet (hiddenness) — Heh and Hauhet describe a quality of the pre-creation abyss: its limitless, unbounded, formless extent, the expanse without edge or measure that existed before the first sunrise.

## Mythological role

Like all the Ogdoad, Hauhet creates not by action but by constituting one of the conditions of the pre-cosmic state. She is the feminine articulation of boundlessness — the endless, formless room in which creation could happen but had not yet happened. The Egyptian conception of the abyss before creation as simultaneously watery (Nun/Naunet), boundless (Heh/Hauhet), dark (Kek/Kauket) and hidden (Amun/Amaunet) is among the most systematically worked-out pre-creation cosmologies of the ancient Near East, and Hauhet supplies one of its four irreducible qualities.

## Cross-tradition identifications

- **[[heh-egyptian]]** — *ancient-identification*: Hauhet is the female complement of Heh; the pair personifies limitless extent.
- **the "formless and void" of Genesis 1:2** — *scholarly-parallel*: the Hebrew *tohu wa-bohu* describing the pre-creation earth occupies the same conceptual slot as the Egyptian "boundless / formless" quality of the abyss (Allen 1988).
- **the Ogdoad complex** — Hauhet operates within the eightfold Hermopolitan theology of the abyss, under the patronage of [[thoth]].

## Iconography

In Hermopolitan depictions Hauhet, like the other three female members of the Ogdoad, is serpent-headed (the males are frog-headed). She is otherwise rarely depicted independently of the full eight.

## Disputes

As with Naunet, Hauhet is comparatively thinly attested in her own right; some scholars regard her chiefly as the grammatically necessary feminine of Heh. Her boundlessness-domain overlaps conceptually with both the watery (Nun) and the dark (Kek) qualities of the abyss, and the precise differentiation among the four Ogdoad qualities is itself a matter of scholarly reconstruction.
