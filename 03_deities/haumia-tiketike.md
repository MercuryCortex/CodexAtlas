# Haumia-tike-tike

## Identity
Haumia-tike-tike (often simply Haumia) is the Māori atua (god/ancestor) of wild, uncultivated food — primarily fernroot (aruhe, the rhizome of the bracken fern *Pteridium esculentum*), wild berries, and other plants found freely in the forest without cultivation. He is one of the sons of Ranginui (Sky Father) and Papatūānuku (Earth Mother) and a sibling in the great brothers' narrative alongside Tāne, Tū, Tangaroa, and Rongo. Where his brother Rongo governs cultivated food (particularly kūmara), Haumia governs what the land provides without human intervention — the distinction encodes a fundamental Māori cosmological pairing of the cultivated and the wild.

## Earliest attestation
Pre-literate Māori oral tradition; first recorded by European-era ethnographers, principally Elsdon Best in *Maori Religion and Mythology* (1924). The pairing of Haumia (wild plants) with Rongo (cultivated plants) is one of the structural pillars of the Māori sons-of-Rangi narrative.

## Mythological role
**Sons-of-Rangi**: When the sons of Ranginui and Papatūānuku debated separating their parents (the sky pressed against the earth), Haumia participated in the decision, though it is Tāne who ultimately performs the separation. After the separation, each son takes a domain: Tangaroa takes the sea, Tāne takes the forest, Tū takes warfare, Rongo takes cultivated plants, and Haumia takes wild/uncultivated plants.

**Hidden in the earth**: In some accounts, when Tāwhirimātea (the storm-god, who opposed the separation) attacked his brothers after the great separation, Haumia and Rongo hid themselves in the earth — concealing themselves in Papatūānuku's body — to escape the winds' wrath. This hiding explains why fernroot is dug from the ground.

**Ritual significance**: Fernroot (aruhe) was a staple food for Māori before the widespread adoption of kūmara; the ritual and cosmological weight placed on Haumia reflects the practical centrality of wild foraging in Māori subsistence. Food gathered from the wild was under his protection.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[rongo-maori]]** — *paired-domain*: Rongo governs cultivated food; Haumia governs wild food. Together they cover the full spectrum of plant-based sustenance; they are conceptually paired across Māori tradition.
- **[[tane]]** — *sibling / domain-adjacent*: Tāne governs the forest in which wild plants grow; Haumia's domain is interior to Tāne's.

## Iconography
No distinctive iconographic attributes are recorded in the pre-European tradition. The fernroot (aruhe) is his primary symbol.
