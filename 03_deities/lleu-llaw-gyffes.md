# Lleu Llaw Gyffes

## Identity
Lleu Llaw Gyffes ("**Bright One of the Skillful Hand**") is the hero-deity of the Fourth Branch of the Welsh *Mabinogi* and the Welsh instantiation of the **Pan-Celtic luminous divine craftsman-warrior** whose name — Proto-Celtic *\*Lugus* ("shining one") — is attested across the entire Celtic world: as **Lugh Lámhfhada** ("Lugh of the Long Arm") in Ireland, as **Lugus** in Gaulish inscriptions and in the cluster of *Lugdunum* place-names (Lyon, Leiden, Laon, Loudun, and others — "the fort/hill of Lugus"), and as **Lleu** in Wales. The *Lugus/Lugh/Lleu* divine figure is arguably the most coherently attested pan-Celtic deity.

His existence is defined by three paradoxes: he is the most destined and the most prohibited (born under three maternal curses); the most skilled at everything yet unable to be killed in any ordinary way; the most sovereign yet the most betrayed. These paradoxes are the Fourth Branch's theological engine.

## Earliest attestation
The Fourth Branch of the *Mabinogi* preserves Lleu in the oldest surviving Welsh mythological prose narrative (manuscripts ~1350 CE, encoding oral tradition from ~800–1100 CE at minimum). The Irish cognate **Lugh** is attested earlier: *Cath Maige Tuired* (the Second Battle of Mag Tuired) documents Lugh's arrival at the divine assembly and his claim to all skills (*samildánach* = "equally skilled in all arts") — attested in manuscripts of the 9th–11th centuries. The Gaulish **Lugus** is attested in Roman-period inscriptions (1st c. BCE – 3rd c. CE) and in the mass of *Lugdunum* place-names across former Gaul.

## Mythological role
**The Three Prohibitions.** Arianrhod ("Silver Wheel"), Lleu's mother, places three *tyngedau* (fates/prohibitions) on her son: he shall have no name unless she gives it; no arms unless she gives them; no human wife. These prohibitions are a systematic attempt to deny Lleu existence, sovereignty, and continuation. Gwydion circumvents each through disguise and illusion — tricking Arianrhod into naming Lleu "the fair one with the skillful hand" when she watches him throw a stone; tricking her into arming him during a false siege; and finally having [[gwydion]] and Math create **Blodeuwedd** ("Flower-Face") from the blossoms of oak, meadowsweet, and broom — a wife of pure nature-beauty, unable to be bound by mortal conventions.

**Blodeuwedd's Betrayal.** Blodeuwedd falls in love with the hunter Gronw Pebr during Lleu's absence. Together they devise Lleu's death: because Lleu can only be killed under a very specific set of impossible conditions (not indoors nor out; not on horse nor on foot; not by day nor by night; with a weapon forged only during Sunday masses — a tynged designed to make him unkillable), they must trick Lleu into demonstrating these conditions himself. He does — and is struck through the side by Gronw's spear as he stands with one foot on a bath-cauldron's rim and one on a goat's back, at dusk.

**The Eagle Transformation.** Struck by the spear, Lleu does not die. He transforms into a **great eagle** and flies away. His form is neither human nor dead — he is the sovereignty-power of the land in a suspended, incomplete state. [[gwydion]] searches for him, tracking his presence through a sow that feeds on rotting flesh beneath an oak tree; he finds Lleu perched high in the oak, maggot-ridden and half-dead. Gwydion sings three *englynion* (verse-incantations) that bring Lleu down through the branches until he falls into Gwydion's arms. Gwydion restores him to human form; he recovers; he kills Gronw and reclaims Gwynedd.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[lugh]]** and **[[lugus]]** — *etymological cognate*: direct linguistic reflexes of the same Pan-Celtic divine name and type. Lugh is the most fully attested: he arrives at the hall of the Tuatha Dé Danann and claims admission on grounds of mastery of all arts — when told every craft has its master inside already, he says "but do you have anyone who is master of *all* simultaneously?" He is admitted, becomes the divine champion, and kills his grandfather Balor at the Battle of Mag Tuired with a sling-stone through Balor's evil eye.
- **[[baldr]]** — *cross-tradition-parallel*: MASSIVE WIN. Baldr is the Norse reflex of the same dying-divine-youth-of-light mytheme. Both Lleu and Baldr are surrounded by conditions of apparent invulnerability; both are killed by a weapon forged or discovered through supernatural means at a betrayal moment; both represent the world's greatest loss when they die.
- **[[osiris]]** — *cross-tradition-parallel*: MASSIVE WIN. The spear-wound that does not kill but transforms; the sovereign lord's betrayal at the height of power; the devoted search and restoration — Gwydion/Isis, eagle/dismembered Osiris, the great oak/the Nile sycamore. The structural convergence between Lleu's myth and the Osiris cycle is among the most compelling cross-tradition parallels in the vault.
- **[[jesus-christ-deity]]** — *cross-tradition-parallel*: the spear through the side (John 19:34) as a structural mytheme shared with Lleu's wounding; the dying-and-rising sovereign figure; the transformation from death-state to restored life. The parallel is typological — part of the ancient Mediterranean–European dying-god complex — not a matter of direct influence.
- **[[apollo]]** — *cross-tradition-parallel*: the PIE luminous-divine-craftsman type; *samildánach* = Apollo's multiple domains of mastery.
- **[[adonis]]** — *cross-tradition-parallel*: the flower-wife (Blodeuwedd, literally made of flowers) turning against her luminous husband; the death of the beautiful divine youth through nature's betrayal.
- **[[gwydion]]** — *mythological partner*: Gwydion is Lleu's creator, guardian, and rescuer — the older magical tradition that makes the luminous hero possible and preserves him after his fall.
