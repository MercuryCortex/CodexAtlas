# Erebus

## Identity

Erebus (Greek **Erebos**) is the Greek primordial personification of **darkness** — the deep gloom that, in Hesiod's cosmogony, comes into being among the very first things, born directly of [[chaos-primordial]]. He is both the dark that precedes the lit world and, more specifically, the shadowy region through which the dead pass on their way into the underworld; "Erebus" is often used simply to mean the darkness of the netherworld.

## Mythological role

In the *Theogony*, "from Chaos came forth Erebos and black Night (Nyx)." Erebus and his sister [[nyx-primordial]] then unite, and from their joining come the two brighter principles: **Aether** (the shining upper air) and **Hemera** (Day). This is one of the most elegant moves in Greek cosmogony — darkness and night, by their union, generate light and day, so that the lit world is born out of the primordial dark. Erebus himself remains largely a principle rather than an actor; he has no myths of his own beyond his genealogical place, and his name is frequently a synonym for the dark realm of the dead.

## Cross-tradition significance

Erebus is the Greek instance of the **primordial darkness** that precedes creation — the same cosmogonic slot occupied by the Egyptian Ogdoad's darkness-pair [[kek-egyptian]] and [[kauket]], and by the "darkness … over the face of the deep" of Genesis 1:2. The Greek refinement — that darkness and night together beget light and day — is a distinctive contribution to the ancient family of "light born from primordial dark" cosmogonies.
