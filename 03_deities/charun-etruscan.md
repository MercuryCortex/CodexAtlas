# Charun

## Identity
Charun (also *Charu*) is the Etruscan demon of death and guardian of the underworld entrance — one of the most visually distinctive and theologically significant figures in Etruscan religion. He is depicted as a monstrous, terrifying figure: **blue or grey skin**, a **hooked beak-like nose**, **pointed ears**, **wings**, and carrying a **large wooden or iron mallet (hammer)** with which he may strike the dying or the newly dead. He is not a neutral ferryman in the Greek manner but an **active demon of death** who participates in the harvesting of souls and guards the threshold of the underworld with menace.

Despite sharing a name with Greek **[[hades]]'s** ferryman Charon (no file), Charun is a fundamentally different figure: the Greek Charon is a calm, mercantile boatman who requires payment to ferry the dead; the Etruscan Charun is a predatory demon whose presence at a death scene marks it as final and terrible. This distinction represents a genuinely Etruscan theological elaboration — not simple borrowing from Greece.

## Earliest attestation
- Charun appears in Etruscan tomb paintings from approximately the **4th century BCE** onward, particularly in tombs at Tarquinia (Tomba degli Auguri, Tomba dell'Orco) and in Vulci.
- His image proliferates in the 3rd–2nd century BCE as Etruscan funerary art becomes increasingly preoccupied with the terrors of death under Roman pressure and social disruption.
- He is also depicted on Etruscan funerary urns, stone sarcophagi, and occasionally on bronze mirrors in scenes of death and transition.

## Mythological role
Charun serves as the **terrifying gatekeeper** of the Etruscan underworld. He appears in tomb paintings at the moment of death — standing over the dying, accompanying the corpse, or waiting at the entrance to the underworld realm. His hammer is the instrument of finality: it signals that death has been enacted and is irrevocable. In some depictions he grasps the deceased by the arm, participating actively in the soul's transfer.

Etruscan underworld theology distinguishes Charun from **[[turms]]**: Turms is the divine, protective psychopomp who guides souls gently; Charun is the demonic gatekeeper who threatens and enforces the boundary. The Etruscan afterlife required passage past Charun — a gauntlet, not a river-crossing. This two-figure system (gentle guide + terrifying guardian) is a distinctively Etruscan contribution to Mediterranean underworld theology.

Charun sometimes appears alongside **Vanth**, a female Etruscan underworld demon — winged, armed with a torch — who is his partner or counterpart in Etruscan tomb scenes. Together they embody the Etruscan experience of death as a confrontation with demonic powers.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[hades]]** (Greek) — *scholarly-parallel*: Both preside over the realm of the dead, but Hades is a sovereign god (neutral, juridical), while Charun is a demon (active, terrifying). The structural slot is similar; the theological character is opposite.
- **[[anubis]]** (Egyptian) — *scholarly-parallel*: Anubis guards the threshold between living and dead and escorts souls to judgment. Both Anubis and Charun occupy the doorkeeper role at the boundary of death, though Anubis is a guide-and-judge rather than a demon.
- **[[yama-vedic]]** (Vedic) — *scholarly-parallel*: Yama as lord of the dead who dispatches messengers (Yamaduta) to collect souls parallels Charun's active soul-harvesting role. The Yamaduta-as-collectors parallel Charun more closely than Yama himself.
- **[[osiris]]** (Egyptian) — *scholarly-parallel*: Osiris's green or black skin (signifying decomposition and chthonic power) parallels Charun's blue-grey skin. Dark or colored skin as a visual marker of death-deity status recurs across Mediterranean and South Asian traditions.
- **[[kali]]** (Hindu) — *scholarly-parallel*: Kali's blue-black skin, ferocious aspect, and role as active consumer of death (not a neutral sovereign) parallels Charun's active, terrifying character. Both are death-deities defined by horror rather than sovereignty.
- **[[baron-samedi]]** (Vodou) — *scholarly-parallel*: Baron Samedi is the Vodou lwa of death, irreverent and powerful, who presides over the dead with dark humor and unpredictability — an active death-presence rather than neutral sovereign, paralleling Charun's non-judicial role.

## Iconography
Charun is one of the most visually striking figures in ancient Mediterranean religion:
- **Blue or grey-blue skin**: the defining marker of his chthonic, death-associated nature.
- **Hooked beak-like nose**: monstrous, non-human.
- **Pointed ears**: bestial.
- **Wings**: often shown folded or partially extended; mark him as a supernatural being capable of spanning worlds.
- **Large mallet or hammer**: his principal weapon and symbol — the finality of death made visible.
- **Serpents**: sometimes wreathed around his arms or head, emphasizing his chthonic character.

He is usually depicted at scenes of death — beside the dying, at the tomb entrance, or in the underworld — in contrast to the calm, youthful Turms who also appears in Etruscan funerary art.

## Disputes
- **Relationship to Greek Charon**: the name similarity (Etruscan *Charun* / Greek *Charon*) has generated debate. Some scholars see direct Greek borrowing with Etruscan demonic elaboration; others argue Charun is an independent Etruscan figure whose name happens to resemble Charon. The radically different character (demon vs. ferryman) supports the view that Charun is a genuine Etruscan theological development.
- **Dating of the demonic character**: Charun becomes increasingly monstrous in later Etruscan art (3rd–2nd century BCE); some scholars argue the demonic aspect intensified under social and political crisis (Roman conquest of Etruria). Earlier depictions may be somewhat less terrifying.
