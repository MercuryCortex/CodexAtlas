# Kāne

## Identity
One of the four principal *akua* (gods) of pre-contact Hawaiian religion — Kāne, Kū, Lono, Kanaloa. Kāne is the god of creation, fresh water, life, sunlight, procreation, and ancestral genealogy. He is regularly associated with the *kalo* (taro) plant, the staple food of Hawaiian agriculture. In the cosmogony, Kāne is one of the principal generative powers; springs of fresh water emerging from rock are called *wai a Kāne* — "water of Kāne." Kāne's cult was less politically prominent than Kū's (war) but was widespread in everyday religious life.

## Earliest attestation
Hawaiian oral tradition; documented in Kumulipo (~1700) and in 19th-c. recovery (David Malo's *Hawaiian Antiquities* 1838; Samuel Kamakau's *Ka Po'e Kahiko*).

## Mythological role
In the Kumulipo, Kāne is among the foundational generative powers. In other Hawaiian narratives, Kāne and Kanaloa wander together as companions, opening springs of fresh water from rocks (Kāne strikes the rock, Kanaloa drinks).

## Iconography
Sunlight; rainbow; *kalo* plant; fresh-water springs. Hawaiian *akua* typically had carved-image *ki'i* representations in the *heiau* (temples) but specific Kāne iconography is less elaborate than Kū's war-god images.
