# Dattatreya

## Identity
A syncretic deity combining the three classical Hindu supreme deities — Brahmā, Viṣṇu, and Śiva — in a single three-faced six-armed body, traditionally identified as the son of the Vedic sage Atri and his paradigmatically-devoted wife Anasūyā. Dattatreya is simultaneously: (a) the *avadhūta* supreme yogi — the renouncer-beyond-all-stages-and-castes who transcends conventional religious-social order; (b) the *Trimūrti-svarūpa* — the single body incarnating all three classical supreme deities; (c) the *guru-of-gurus* — patron of the Nāth, Mahānubhāva, Vārkarī, Datta-sampradāya, and Daśanāmī ascetic lineages; (d) a major Tantric guru. The deity's iconographic distinctiveness (three faces, six arms, four dogs and a cow) and theological catholicity (he is simultaneously Vaiṣṇava, Śaiva, and Brāhmaṇic; he is supreme yogi and supreme devotional object; he transcends sectarian divisions) make him one of the most clearly pan-Indian, deliberately-unsectarian Hindu deities. Dattatreya worship is especially intense in Maharashtra (where Audumbar, Narsobawadi, and Ganagapur are the principal pilgrimage centers), in Karnataka, and in Gujarat.

## Earliest attestation
- **Mahābhārata** (Anuśāsana-parvan; ~1st c. BCE – 4th c. CE) and the embedded *Dattātreya-Avadhūta-Gītā* — early instructional dialogue.
- ***Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa*** (~3rd–7th c. CE) — narrative-mythological development of the Atri-Anasūyā-Dattatreya story.
- ***Bhāgavata Purāṇa*** (~9th–10th c. CE) Book 11.7–9: the *Avadhūta-Upākhyāna* in which Dattatreya teaches the king Yadu the 24 gurus from whom Dattatreya learned wisdom (earth, water, fire, wind, sky, sun, moon, kingfisher bird, ocean, etc.) — one of the supreme Hindu instructional texts on learning from nature.
- ***Dattātreya-Saṃhitā*** (medieval) — the Tantric ritual manual.

## Mythological role
- **The Atri-Anasūyā test**: the foundational origin myth: the Trimūrti (Brahmā, Viṣṇu, Śiva) appear to Anasūyā as Brahmin guests and demand she serve them naked; through her *pati-vrata* (devoted-wife) power, she transforms them into infants and serves them; the three deities, recognizing the supreme devotion, grant her the boon of having them as her sons. The three combined into one form become Dattatreya.
- **24 gurus instruction**: in the Bhāgavata Purāṇa's *Avadhūta-Upākhyāna*, Dattatreya teaches that wisdom can be learned from anything in the natural world if observed with the right attention. The teaching is one of the most-cited Hindu instructional narratives.
- **Guru-of-gurus**: numerous narratives have Dattatreya appearing in disguise as a beggar or madman to instruct legendary masters; the deity is fundamentally a transmission-figure.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **Trimūrti unification**: the entire deity is the explicit unification.
- **Sufi parallel — Khiḍr / al-Khaḍir**: Schimmel 1975 and Rigopoulos 1998 have noted structural-functional parallels between Dattatreya and the Sufi figure of Khiḍr (the immortal guide who appears to wayfarers in disguise, teaches by paradoxical signs, transcends conventional religious-categorical authority). Both are immortal guru-figures who manifest to elect students across sectarian lines; both transcend the conventional religious-social-political order. In late-medieval Maharashtra (where the Datta-sampradāya is dominant) and the Indo-Persian Sufi presence overlap geographically, the structural parallel may have produced documented cross-fertilization at the level of devotional practice (the Avadhūta-Sufi *bekhud* / "selfless" ascetic mode shares vocabulary).
- **Nāth sampradāya lineage claim**: the Nāth tradition treats Dattatreya as one of the foundational masters of the lineage, alongside [[matsyendranath]] and [[gorakhnath]].
- **Sai Baba of Shirdi syncretic continuity**: the Maharashtra-region 19th-20th-c. saint Sai Baba of Shirdi (~1838–1918), who explicitly transgressed Hindu-Muslim boundaries and was claimed by both communities, is sometimes interpreted as a Datta-tradition continuation (Rigopoulos has written on the Sai Baba-Dattatreya connection).

## Iconography
- Three faces (Brahmā, Viṣṇu, Śiva), each with the iconographic markers of that deity.
- Six arms holding: *kamaṇḍalu* (renouncer's water-pot), *akṣamālā* (rosary beads), trident, conch (*śaṅkha*), discus (*cakra*), *ḍamaru* (Tantric drum).
- Four dogs at his feet (representing the four Vedas, or the four *yugas* / world-ages, or — more antinomian — the four *varṇas* equally serving the supreme).
- A cow standing behind or beside him (Earth, dharma).
- Ash-smeared body, matted hair, often naked or in tiger-skin (avadhūta).

## Disputes
- **Date of cultic emergence**: textual references span late-Vedic to medieval; the developed Trimūrti-iconographic Dattatreya likely emerges ~5th–10th c. CE.
- **Sectarian categorization**: whether Dattatreya is best classified as Vaiṣṇava (the *Bhāgavata* tradition treats him as a Viṣṇu-avatāra), Śaiva (Nāth and most Tantric traditions treat him as primarily Śaiva), or supra-sectarian (the deity's own theological logic) is debated.
- **Connection to Sai Baba of Shirdi**: contested; Rigopoulos and others argue for substantial continuity, others for parallel-but-distinct phenomena.
