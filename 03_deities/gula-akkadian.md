# Gula

## Identity
Gula is the supreme healing goddess of the Akkadian/Babylonian pantheon and the divine patron of medicine, midwifery, and the asu/asipu (physician/exorcist) professions. Her name is itself an epithet ("the Great One"); she is also called Ninkarrak, Azugallatu ("Great Physician"), and Belet-balati ("Lady of Life"). Her principal cult center is **Isin** (the Egalmah temple), where she is the city-goddess; secondary cults exist at Babylon, Borsippa, Nippur (where she is called Nintinugga), and at the Assyrian capitals. She absorbs and consolidates the older Sumerian healing-goddess complex: the Lagashite [[bau-sumerian]] (whose dog-iconography is transferred to Gula), [[ninisina]] of Isin, and Ninkarrak. From the Old Babylonian period onward, "Gula" functions as the umbrella name for the Akkadian healing-goddess identity. She is consort of [[ninurta]] in the Isin theological system after Ninurta absorbs the earlier Pabilsag.

## Earliest attestation
- Old Babylonian god lists from Isin and Nippur (~-2000 to -1700) name Gula alongside (or as identification of) Ninisina and Ninkarrak.
- The epilogue of the [[phase-1-007-code-of-hammurabi]] invokes Ninkarrak (= Gula) as a curse-witness who afflicts violators with incurable disease — one of the most ferocious curses in the inscription.
- Kudurru (boundary-stone) iconography of the Kassite and Middle Babylonian periods regularly depicts Gula's emblems (seated goddess, dog, scalpel) in the divine-symbol registers.
- The *Gula Hymn of Bullussa-rabi* (~-1400 to -1200) is the most extended Akkadian hymn to her — a litany of her epithets across her many cult sites and identifications.
- Votive dog figurines, in terracotta and bronze, are deposited at her sanctuaries from the Old Babylonian period through the Neo-Babylonian — the most diagnostic archaeological marker of her cult.

## Mythological role
Unlike Inanna/Ishtar or Nergal, Gula is not the protagonist of major narrative myths. Her role is **cultic, medical, and intercessory**:
- She is invoked in incantations (the *Maqlu* and *Surpu* series) as the divine source of healing power; the *asipu* (exorcist) addresses her in opening formulae.
- Babylonian medical texts (the *Diagnostic Handbook* attributed to Esagil-kin-apli, ~-1100) place her at the head of the divine-medical hierarchy.
- In wisdom literature (the [[phase-1-032-babylonian-theodicy]] and *Ludlul bel nemeqi*), the sufferer appeals to Gula for restoration; the *Ludlul* speaker is healed by her intervention.
- Royal inscriptions of Neo-Babylonian and Neo-Assyrian kings record dedications to Gula for personal health and dynastic continuance.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[bau-sumerian]]** — *ancient-identification*: by the Old Babylonian period Bau's healing function (and her dog-attribute) is absorbed into Gula; Bau's civic role at Girsu fades with Lagash's political decline while Gula carries the medical function forward. The transfer is partial — Bau's queen-of-Girsu role does *not* migrate, only the healing.
- **[[ninisina]]** — *ancient-identification*: Ninisina ("Lady of Isin") is identified with Gula at her principal cult site; they may originally have been distinct but become functionally indistinguishable by the OB period.
- **[[ninkarrak]]** — *ancient-identification*: a West-Semitic-flavored name for the same healing goddess, prominent in Mari texts and the Hammurabi epilogue.
- **Nintinugga** — *ancient-identification*: the Nippur form, attested in Nippur god lists and the *Letter-prayer to Nintinugga*.
- *Scholarly parallel*: Greek Asklepios and Egyptian Sekhmet (in her plague-and-healing duality) are sometimes compared cross-culturally; these are *scholarly-parallel*, not ancient identifications.

## Iconography
Seated goddess in horned crown, often with a **dog** at her feet or under her throne — the dog is the single most diagnostic attribute of her cult, inherited from Bau. Surgical implements (scalpel, bandages) appear on Kassite kudurrus among her divine symbols. Votive dog figurines (terracotta and bronze, some bearing inscriptions naming Gula) have been excavated in large numbers at Isin and other cult sites — these are appeals for healing, deposited by petitioners.

## Disputes
- **Gula vs. Ninisina vs. Ninkarrak vs. Bau — one goddess or four?** The Mesopotamian healing-goddess complex is itself layered: in earliest sources these are distinguishable local deities (Bau at Lagash, Ninisina at Isin, Ninkarrak in West-Semitic contexts). From the Old Babylonian period they are increasingly treated as identifications of a single healing-goddess identity, normally called Gula. Westenholz 2013 treats this as a real syncretic merger; older scholarship sometimes preserved them as distinct. The vault separates Bau (as the Sumerian Lagashite city-goddess with her own distinct civic role) from Gula (as the Akkadian/Babylonian healing-goddess umbrella), per the splitting rule.
- **Consort identity**. Gula's consort is variously [[ninurta]] (in Isin and Babylon), Pabilsag (the older Isin theology that Ninurta absorbs), or no fixed consort. The variation reflects local cultic emphases, not contradiction.
