# Tištrya (Tishtrya)

## Identity
Tištrya is the Zoroastrian yazata (divine being) of the star Sirius — the brightest star in the night sky (*sothis* in Egyptian; *Tištar Yašt* in the Avestan yasht cycle). He is a divine archer and warrior who fights the demon Apaosha (demon of drought) in a cosmic battle for the rain. The Tištar Yašt (Yasht 8) narrates: Tištrya descends from the sky in three ten-night periods (as a young man, a bull, and a white horse with golden ears and golden caparison), enters the cosmic sea Vourukasha, battles Apaosha (who appears as a black horse, hairless from disease), and — when aided by the prayers of the faithful and the power of Ahura Mazda — defeats him, releasing the rain that falls on all the earth.

Tištrya functions within the Zoroastrian cosmic scheme as a guardian of Asha (truth/order) against the forces of Druj (lie/chaos): drought is not merely a weather event but a manifestation of the cosmic Lie withholding what creation needs to flourish. His defeat of Apaosha is therefore a cosmic-moral act, not merely a meteorological myth. He is explicitly said to require the worship of the faithful to defeat Apaosha — a theologically significant detail: without human ritual participation, even the divine yazata cannot overcome the drought-demon.

## Earliest attestation
Tištrya is attested in the Yasht cycle of the younger Avesta — specifically Yasht 8 (the Tištar Yašt), dated by scholars to approximately 1000–500 BCE (the dating of individual Yashts is debated; most are thought to represent pre-Zarathushtra materials preserved within the Zoroastrian canon). The name *Tištrya* is likely a borrowing or cognate from an Indo-Iranian *Tṛkṣya* / *Tiṣya* tradition; the Vedic *Tiṣya* (a name for a star-deity, later identified with Sirius or the star Puṣya) may be related, though the relationship is debated (Panaino 1990). The heliacal rising of Sirius in the ancient Iranian calendar marked the beginning of the summer heat/drought season — the Tištrya myth is calibrated to this astronomical-seasonal fact.

## Mythological role
The Tištar Yašt (Yasht 8) narrates in detail:
1. **Three descents**: Tištrya descends to the cosmic sea Vourukasha in three periods of ten nights each — first as a fifteen-year-old youth (human form), second as a bull with golden horns, third as a white horse with golden ears and gold caparison.
2. **The battle**: In his horse form, Tištrya meets Apaosha (the drought-demon) appearing as a black horse, bald and scabby. They fight for three days and nights in the sea. Apaosha drives Tištrya back.
3. **The turning point**: Tištrya laments that if humanity had worshipped him properly, he would have the strength to defeat Apaosha. Ahura Mazda himself makes the offering (*yasna*) to Tištrya, granting him the power of ten horses, ten camels, ten bulls, ten mountains, ten rivers.
4. **Victory and rain**: Empowered, Tištrya defeats Apaosha and emerges from the sea, causing the rains to fall on all creation, on the mountains of Hukairya, on the seven *karshvars* (divisions) of the earth.
5. **Agricultural correspondence**: The rising of Sirius corresponds to the late summer heat; the rains that follow in the Iranian ecological calendar were associated with Tištrya's annual victory over Apaosha.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[indra]]** — *scholarly-parallel* (Proto-Indo-Iranian mytheme): The Vedic Indra-Vṛtra battle is the closest and most structurally identical parallel. Indra is the storm-warrior-god who, armed with the thunderbolt (vajra), shatters the cosmic "blocker" (Vṛtra = the drought-serpent / dam-of-waters) to release the cattle and rain. Tištrya is the stellar-warrior-god who defeats Apaosha the drought-demon to release the rain. West (2007) categorizes both as instances of the Proto-Indo-Iranian weather-combat mytheme. The difference is the medium: Indra fights with lightning; Tištrya fights in horse-form.
- **[[vritra]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Vṛtra and Apaosha are cognate adversaries in the Indo-Iranian drought-combat mythology — both are the cosmic principle of withholding/blocking (Vṛtra "envelops/blocks"; Apaosha "drought").
- **[[thor]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Thor vs. Jörmungandr (the Midgard Serpent who encircles the world) is the Norse instantiation of the wider Indo-European storm-combat myth. Tištrya vs. Apaosha, Indra vs. Vṛtra, Thor vs. Jörmungandr, and Baal vs. Yam all share the deep-structure: divine warrior defeats cosmic-chaos/blockage to maintain the world's order and fertility.
- **[[perun]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: The Slavic Perun vs. Veles combat (storm-god defeats the serpent who steals cattle/rain and hides underground) is the same Indo-European mytheme. Ivanov and Toporov's reconstruction of the Proto-Slavic combat myth parallels the Indo-Iranian Tištrya/Indra pattern exactly: celestial warrior (associated with lightning/stars) defeats the adversarial demon of the earth/underground who withholds water and fertility.
- **[[the-ashvins]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: The Vedic Ashvins are divine twin horsemen of the dawn (associated with the morning stars Rigel and Betelgeuse, or with the planet Venus). Tištrya is a divine horseman of a specific star (Sirius). Both derive from the Proto-Indo-Iranian sacred-horse complex and its association with beneficial stellar phenomena.
- **[[epona]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: The Gaulish horse goddess Epona and Tištrya's white-horse-with-golden-ears avatar share the Indo-European sacred horse typology — the horse as a divine vehicle and symbol of cosmic power, health, and abundance.
- **[[ahura-mazda]]** — *direct-influence*: Tištrya is a Yazata subordinate to Ahura Mazda; his cosmic battle requires Ahura Mazda's empowerment. This subordination is theologically significant — even the stellar divine warrior cannot win alone; cosmic victory requires the alignment of the supreme divine will with human ritual devotion.
- **[[mithra-zoroastrian]]** — *direct-influence*: Mithra (the yazata of the covenant) is associated with the same cosmic battle against the Druj; the Tištrya myth and the Mithra Yasht (Yasht 10) both portray divine warriors maintaining cosmic order against dark forces.
- **[[anahita]]** — *direct-influence*: Anāhitā is the yazata of the cosmic waters (and war-victory); Tištrya's battle releases those very waters. The Tištar Yašt and the Ābān Yašt (Yasht 5, Anāhitā) are cosmologically complementary: Tištrya unlocks the rain; Anāhitā is the divine power in the waters themselves.

## The Sirius Connection
The Tištar Yašt's association of Tištrya with the star Sirius is explicit — it is not mythological speculation. Sirius is the brightest star in the night sky; in the ancient Iranian and Mesopotamian calendars, the heliacal rising of Sirius (when Sirius first becomes visible in the pre-dawn sky after a period of invisibility) marked significant seasonal transitions. The three ten-night periods of Tištrya's descent correspond to the period of Sirius's annual battle with the heat/drought. Antonio Panaino's *Tištrya* (1990) provides the fullest analysis of the astronomical-mythological correspondence.

Note: The Nommo/Sirius connection claimed by Robert Temple's *The Sirius Mystery* (1976) — concerning the Dogon [[nommo]] — should not be confused with the entirely separate and well-documented Avestan Tištrya-Sirius identification. The latter is attested directly in the primary Avestan source and requires no speculation.

## Iconography
Tištrya is not depicted in surviving Zoroastrian iconography with any established visual tradition (consistent with Zoroastrianism's general aversion to divine images). His attributes as described in the Tištar Yašt: white horse form with golden (*zairi*) ears and golden caparison; radiant youth form (fifteen-year-old); golden-horned bull. The star Sirius itself — the brightest fixed star, unmistakable in the summer night sky — is his celestial avatar.
