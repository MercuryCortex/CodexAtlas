# Illapa

## Identity
Illapa (Quechua: *Ilyap'a*) is the Inca god of thunder, lightning, and rain — the third most important deity of the Inca state pantheon after Inti (sun) and Viracocha (creator), and the most immediately powerful in terms of weather control. He was conceived as a deity who kept the celestial Milky Way as a river in a great jar; when he struck it with his sling, thunder cracked, lightning flashed, and rain fell upon the earth. As the rain-bringer, Illapa was indispensable to Andean agriculture, and his shrines were widespread throughout the Inca empire. After the Spanish conquest, he was assimilated to Santiago (Saint James) — a fusion that persists in Andean folk Catholicism to the present.

## Earliest attestation
Inca state-period attestation (1200–1533 CE); recorded by colonial chroniclers including Cobo, Cieza, and Polo de Ondegardo. Pre-Inca Andean thunder/lightning deity traditions are attested in Moche iconography (the "Decapitator" or *Ai apaec* figure is associated with lightning) and Tiwanaku staff-god imagery, suggesting deep Andean antiquity for this deity-type before the Inca systematization.

## Mythological role
**The sling and the jar**: Illapa lived in the celestial sphere and carried the Milky Way as a river held in a great ceramic jar. When he wished to produce rain, he struck the jar with his sling; the crack of the sling was thunder, the flash of the sling-stone's arc was lightning, and the water that spilled was rain. This myth is well-attested in colonial sources and gives Illapa direct control over rain — the most critical agricultural variable in the Andes.

**Warrior aspect**: Illapa was also the divine warrior — his thunder was battle, his lightning was his weapon. The Inca state exploited this dual identity: Inca warriors were the people of the sun (Inti) and the thunder (Illapa). The Spanish saw the warrior-thunder iconography and mapped it to Santiago (Matamoros) — Saint James the Apostle, patron of the Spanish Reconquista, who appeared in battle surrounded by lightning. The identification was mutually reinforced.

**Companion and instrument of Inti**: While Viracocha is the creator and Inti is the cosmic sovereign, Illapa is the executor — the deity who acts in the atmosphere, who brings the water needed to sustain the crops that Inti's warmth nurtures. The triad Viracocha-Inti-Illapa represents creator, sustainer, and weather-activator.

**Illapa shrines (huacas)**: Lightning strikes were sacred — sites struck by lightning became *huacas* (sacred places). Individuals struck by lightning and surviving were considered touched by Illapa and given shamanic authority. The ceque system of Cusco included multiple shrines dedicated to Illapa.

## Cross-tradition MASSIVE WIN: the Santiago-Illapa conflation
The identification of Illapa with Santiago (Saint James) is one of the most vivid cases of colonial religious fusion in the Americas:

| Illapa | Santiago |
|---|---|
| Thunder-god, sling-wielder | Warrior-saint, horseback, sword |
| Lightning = his weapon | Depictions show lightning/fire around him |
| Rain-bringer, weather-controller | Associated with storms in popular tradition |
| Divine warrior of the Inca state | Divine warrior of the Spanish Reconquista |

The Andean population encountering the image of *Santiago Matamoros* (Saint James as warrior on horseback, associated with storm and lightning in his conquest iconography) recognized Illapa beneath the Catholic surface. The feast of Santiago (July 25) became one of the most Andean of all Catholic festivals — celebrated with specific Andean elements that encode the thunder-god's character beneath the saint's name.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **Santiago (Saint James)** — *folk-syncretism*: See above; the dominant surviving syncretism.
- **Thor (Norse)** — *structural-parallel*: Thunder-god who is the weapon-bearer of the divine family, third after the sky-sovereign and creator; no transmission.
- **Indra (Vedic)** — *structural-parallel*: Rain-bringer and warrior who uses a weapon (vajra / thunderbolt) to release water held by an adversary; parallel function.
- **[[tangaroa]]** — *no connection*: Different elemental domain (sea vs. sky).

## Iconography
Illapa was depicted in human form, with shining garments (representing lightning) and carrying a sling. Some accounts describe him in golden or silver armor. His sling (warak'a) was the primary iconographic attribute. Post-conquest depictions merge with Santiago imagery — a mounted warrior figure is the hybrid form found in colonial Andean art.
