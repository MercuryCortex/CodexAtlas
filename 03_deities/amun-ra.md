# Amun-Ra

## Identity
Amun-Ra (Egyptian *Jmn-Rꜥ*) is the **composite high-god of Egyptian state religion** from the 18th Dynasty (c. -1550) onward — a deliberate theological fusion of [[amun]] (the hidden Theban creator) with [[ra]] (the visible Heliopolitan solar god). The fusion encodes a theological claim: the **hidden being and the visible cosmos are aspects of a single divine reality**. As articulated in the Leiden Hymn to Amun (Papyrus Leiden I 350, 19th Dynasty), Amun-Ra is one of three aspects of a single god — **"His identity is hidden as Amun, his face is Re, his body is Ptah"** — the most theologically sophisticated articulation of late-second-millennium Egyptian religion.

We split Amun-Ra into a separate node from [[amun]] (alone) and [[ra]] (alone) because the composite has distinct theology, cult, and reception history.

## Earliest attestation
- The compound *Jmn-Rꜥ* is attested already in 18th-Dynasty (early New Kingdom, c. -1550 to -1300) royal inscriptions and hymns.
- The Cairo Hymn to Amun-Re (Papyrus Boulaq 17, ~-1500) is the earliest extended devotional text addressed specifically to the composite.
- The Leiden Hymn (Pap. Leiden I 350, 19th Dynasty, c. -1250) articulates the full triadic theology: Amun + Re + Ptah as one.
- Amun-Ra remains the dominant Egyptian state god through the Late Period; persists into Ptolemaic-Roman temple religion (Karnak temple is operational, with diminished resources, until late antiquity).

## Mythological role
Amun-Ra is principally a **cosmological** deity rather than a narrative-mythological one. He is the **maker of all that is**, the cosmic king who legitimates the Pharaoh as his son, the hidden source of breath and life behind the visible sun. Where [[osiris]] is the god of narrative — the dying-and-rising king of the underworld — Amun-Ra is the god of cosmology, addressed in hymns rather than myths. The lack of an extensive Amun-Ra narrative is itself theologically significant: a god whose nature is hiddenness does not appear in stories.

The Leiden Hymn's claim that Amun-Ra is the **one god** of whom all other gods are aspects has been called by Assmann "cosmological monotheism" and "summodeism" — *one supreme god encompassing the others*. It is structurally analogous to (but historically independent from) Stoic logos-pantheism and Middle Platonist *unus deus*.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[zeus]]** — *ancient-identification*: as Zeus-Ammon, particularly through the Siwa oracle. [[herodotus]] (2.42) reports that Egyptians call Zeus "Ammon"; the equation persists through Hellenistic and Roman religious culture and is institutionalized in Alexander's deification by the Siwa oracle (-332).
- **[[ra]]** — *syncretic-fusion* + *ancient-identification*: Amun-Ra IS the integration of Theban Amun and Heliopolitan Ra.
- **[[amun]]** — *syncretic-fusion*: same.
- **[[ptah]]** — *ancient-identification*: Leiden Hymn's threefold theology.
- **Stoic logos / Middle Platonist One** — *scholarly-parallel*: Assmann (1995, 2014) argues for structural parallels between New Kingdom Amun-Ra theology and later Greek philosophical monotheisms; direct historical influence is suggestive but hard to demonstrate.
- **[[hermes-trismegistus]]** — *scholarly-parallel* + *shared-milieu*: the Hermetic tradition's cosmological monotheism in [[phase-3-021-hermetic-corpus-earliest]] / [[phase-4-011-corpus-hermeticum-i]] is composed in Egypt under the Roman Empire by Greek-writing intellectuals immersed in Egyptian theology; Egyptian Amun-Ra theology is one of its substrate elements.

## Iconography
- **Solar disc** above Amun's double-plumed crown — the visible-sun aspect added to Amun's hidden-creator headdress
- Often **ram-headed** (Amun's animal) with the solar disc
- Alternative: **hawk-headed** with the solar disc (taking on Ra's iconography)
- **Gold or blue skin** — gold for solar radiance, blue for hiddenness / sky
- *Ankh* and *was*-scepter
- Central cult site: the great Hypostyle Hall at Karnak, the largest religious building of the ancient world, dedicated to Amun-Ra

## Disputes
- **Is Amun-Ra theology proto-monotheism?** The major axis of dispute. Assmann (1995, 2014) makes the strongest case for "cosmological monotheism" / "differentiated monotheism" — one god comprehending all others. Hornung (1982) is more conservative — Egyptian religion is irreducibly polytheist in cult practice, with theological texts sometimes pushing toward unity but not redefining cult into monotheism. The question matters for the Codex Atlas's Christianity-to-older-traditions chain: if Amun-Ra is genuinely proto-monotheist, that strengthens cross-cultural lines into Hellenistic philosophical monotheism and from there into Christian theology.
- **Continuity with Akhenaten's Aten cult.** [[akhenaten]]'s [[aten]] reform was explicitly *anti*-Amun (the Theban Amun priesthood being the rival power center). Whether Atenism's monotheism is a radical break from or a polemical inversion of incipient Amun-Ra monism is disputed (Hornung 1999; Assmann 2014).
- **Influence on Hebrew monotheism.** The strongest version (Freud 1939, *Moses and Monotheism*) argues that Mosaic monotheism descends from Atenism; the more moderate version (Assmann 2014, *From Akhenaten to Moses*) argues for typological resonance rather than direct descent. Egyptian Amun-Ra theology is part of the substrate of both arguments.

## The Mosaic Distinction — MASSIVE WIN connection

[[jan-assmann]]'s *Moses the Egyptian* (1997) and *Of God and Gods* (2008) identify Amun-Ra theology as the religious baseline from which the **Mosaic distinction** departed. Before the Mosaic distinction, Egyptian religion operated by **translatability**: any god could be identified with any other across traditions; Amun = Zeus = Jupiter; the theological field is one of mutual recognition. The Leiden Hymn's triadic formula ("All gods are three — Amun, Re, Ptah") is the exemplar: all the gods are aspects of a single, unnamed divine reality that can be accessed through any of them.

What Akhenaten's [[aten]] reform introduced — and what Assmann argues was transmitted through cultural memory into Mosaic monotheism — was the **counter-principle**: one form of worship is true, all others are false. This "Mosaic distinction" created for the first time a religious field of **truth claims** rather than merely **power claims**. The theological violence of subsequent religious history (persecution, heresy, crusades, jihad) all derives, Assmann argues, from this single structural innovation — the claim that my god is real and yours is not. Amun-Ra theology is the last major tradition **before** that line was crossed, representing the full sophisticated expression of the translatable polytheism it displaced.

**For the vault**: Amun-Ra → Aten → Mosaic distinction → Judaism/Christianity/Islam is one of the most consequential theological sequences the vault tracks. It is documented in peer-reviewed scholarship (Assmann 1997, 2008 — Tier 1–2) and explains not just Egyptian religion but why the Abrahamic traditions share a distinctive exclusivism that their Egyptian predecessor lacked.
