# Mama Sara

## Identity
Mama Sara ("Mother Grain" or "Mother Maize" — from Quechua *mama* = mother, *sara* = maize) is the Andean personification of maize and food-grain as a sacred feminine presence. In Andean theology, she is not merely a goddess *of* grain but grain itself conceived as animate — as a mother-spirit dwelling in exceptional maize cobs. She is among the most practically intimate of all Andean sacred figures: not a remote cosmic deity but a presence that could be found in any household's storage, in the form of specially shaped cobs, weaving, and ritual bundles. Cobs of maize found growing in human shapes, in the shapes of animals, or fused as double-cobs were kept as her sacred embodiments — called *conopas* (small household sacred objects) or *mullu sara* (large sacred cobs). These were wrapped in textiles, fed chicha (corn beer), spoken to, and consulted for the health of the household's crops.

Mama Sara belongs to the cluster of Andean "Mama" (Mother) deities: [[mama-cocha]] (Sea-Mother), [[mama-quilla]] (Moon-Mother), and Mama Sara (Grain-Mother). Together these three constitute a feminine divine triad governing the great domains of Andean life — the sea (mother of fish and coastal abundance), the moon (time-keeper and menstrual cycle), and grain (agricultural sustenance). [[pachamama]] (Earth-Mother) stands as the encompassing figure of all three; the Mama deities are her specific manifestations in particular domains.

Crucially: Mama Sara's ritual practice is *living*. Unlike most ancient deity cults in the vault, Andean communities in the highlands of Peru and Bolivia continue to practice Mama Sara veneration — planting rituals, harvest thanksgivings, and conopa maintenance are documented in contemporary ethnography. This makes Mama Sara one of the vault's clearest examples of an ancient deity tradition that has survived 500 years of colonial Christianity to remain an active sacred presence.

## Earliest attestation
- The practice of keeping specially-shaped maize cobs as sacred objects is archaeologically attested in Andean sites from approximately -1000 (early Formative period). Named attestation of "Mama Sara" as a deity figure appears in colonial-period Quechua sources and in the *extirpación de idolatrías* (idolatry-extirpation) records, where Spanish priests seized and destroyed conopas including sara conopas.
- The *Huarochirí Manuscript* ([[phase-8-006-huarochiri-manuscript]], c. 1608) preserves Andean theological material including agricultural spirits; Mama Sara is implicated in the agricultural deity cluster discussed there.

## Mythological role
In Andean agricultural cosmology, Mama Sara is not associated with a single major myth but with a distributed theology of crop-animation: every maize plant has a soul-force; exceptional cobs are concentrations of this force; the most sacred are kept as conopas to ensure future harvests. The ritual cycle includes:

- **Planting rituals**: Before sowing, conopas are addressed, chicha is offered, the land is petitioned. Mama Sara's blessing is sought for seed germination.
- **Harvest rituals**: At harvest, the first picked cobs are saved, examined for sacred shapes, and potentially installed as new conopas. The harvest is partly a selection process — finding the new sacred bodies of Mama Sara in the year's crop.
- **Storage protection**: Grain stored for the winter and for seed is kept with sara conopas who protect it from rot, pests, and shortage.

The *inca* state cult incorporated Mama Sara into imperial agricultural ritual: the Inca's royal maize fields (*chakra*) were ceremonially plowed with golden implements by the Sapa Inca himself at the Inti Raymi festival, and the state maintained large storehouses (*qollqa*) whose contents were under divine protection. The grain deity tradition underlies the entire Inca system of state food redistribution.

## Cross-tradition identifications — The Grain-Mother: a Global Pattern

Mama Sara is the Andean instance of the **Grain-Mother** archetype — the personification of the cultivated grain as a divine feminine principle. This pattern appears independently across Old World and New World agricultural traditions:

- **[[demeter]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Demeter (Greek grain-mother, "Earth-Mother" or "Grain-Mother" — from *De-* possibly = earth, *meter* = mother) is the closest Old World parallel. Both Demeter and Mama Sara are: (1) personifications of grain as a living divine mother; (2) the focus of agricultural ritual at planting and harvest; (3) conceived as resident in the grain itself (Demeter's spirit in the last sheaf; Mama Sara in the conopa). The Eleusinian Mysteries centered on Demeter's grief at the loss of Persephone — which is the mythologized form of the annual grain cycle (seed descends to the earth; grain rises in spring). Mama Sara's annual harvest-selection parallels this cycle without the underworld-descent narrative. No transmission.

- **[[ceres-roman]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Ceres (Roman equivalent of Demeter) is structurally identical. The Roman *Cerealia* festival at the grain harvest parallels Andean harvest ritual for Mama Sara.

- **[[nisaba]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Nisaba (Sumerian grain-goddess, also goddess of writing and accounting — because grain-counting was the basis of early Mesopotamian writing) is the Mesopotamian grain-mother. Nisaba and Mama Sara represent independently-developed theological responses to the same agricultural reality: grain is the foundation of civilization, and this civilization-sustaining power is conceived as feminine and divine. That both grain-mothers (Nisaba in Sumer, Mama Sara in the Andes) belong to the world's first and one of the Americas' most sophisticated civilizations respectively underlines the deep connection between grain cultivation, record-keeping, and state theology.

**The Corn-Mother in Native American traditions**: The "Corn Mother" figure appears across numerous indigenous North American traditions (Navajo: Changing Woman; Cherokee: Selu the Corn Woman; Pueblo: Corn Maidens). These North American figures are regional parallels within the Americas — the theological pattern of the grain as divine feminine mother extends from Andean Mama Sara through Mesoamerican maize deities to North American Corn Mothers as a hemisphere-wide agricultural religious pattern. The independence of Andean and Mesoamerican grain theology from common indigenous ancestry (vs. parallel development from the adoption of maize agriculture) is an open question.

## The Mama Deity Cluster

Mama Sara is best understood within the Andean feminine divine triad:

| Mama Deity | Domain | Primary Association |
|---|---|---|
| [[mama-cocha]] | Sea / Lakes | Fish, coastal abundance, Lake Titicaca |
| [[mama-quilla]] | Moon | Time, calendar, menstrual cycle, silver |
| Mama Sara | Grain / Maize | Harvest, conopas, agricultural renewal |
| [[pachamama]] | Earth (encompassing) | Land, fertility, the earth as living body |

The three Mama deities are Pachamama's specific manifestations — the earth-mother governing her respective domain. Together they cover the three great sources of Andean subsistence: the sea, the agricultural cycle (regulated by the moon), and the grain itself.

## Iconography
Mama Sara has no anthropomorphic iconography in the Western sense — she is not depicted as a human figure. Her sacred "images" are the conopas: maize cobs in human or animal forms, and especially double-cobs (two cobs growing fused together), which were considered her most powerful manifestations. These were wrapped in fine textiles, kept in household niches, and adorned with miniature clothing. The textile-wrapping links Mama Sara to weaving as a sacred female art in Andean culture — the grain is dressed as a person, and her care is personal and domestic.

## Disputes
- The line between Mama Sara (grain deity) and Pachamama (earth deity) is fluid in living Andean practice. Some communities treat them as distinct figures; others as aspects of a single feminine earth-power. The colonial-period extirpation records show considerable regional variation in how the grain-spirit was named and conceived.
- Whether the pre-Inca Mama Sara tradition was pan-Andean or regional (particular to certain highland agricultural communities) before Inca standardization is uncertain.
