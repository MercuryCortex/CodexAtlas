# Kū

## Identity
Hawaiian war-god — one of the four principal *akua* (Kāne, Kū, Lono, Kanaloa). Kū is the deity associated with warfare, conquest, kingship-power-by-conquest, and human sacrifice. Kamehameha I, who unified the Hawaiian Islands ~1810, was a particular devotee of *Kūkā'ilimoku* ("Kū-the-snatcher-of-islands"); the conquest of the chain was performed under Kū's auspices. After the death of Kamehameha I (1819), the *kapu* system was abolished by Kamehameha II under his mother Keōpūolani's and stepmother Ka'ahumanu's pressure, and Kū's principal temples were burned — making Kū's cult one of the most dramatic religious-political transformations documented in Pacific history.

## Earliest attestation
Hawaiian oral tradition; documented in 19th-c. Hawaiian-language sources (Malo, Kamakau); the surviving Kū *ki'i* in major museum collections (Bishop Museum, British Museum) are pre-1819 carvings.

## Mythological role
Kū is the cosmic-warrior principle; his rites included human sacrifice on the *luakini heiau* (war temples). His aspects are many: Kū-of-the-fish (fishing patronage), Kū-of-the-forest (canoe-tree blessing), Kū-of-the-snatcher-of-islands (conquest). He shared genealogical-cosmogonic standing with Kāne, Lono, Kanaloa.

## Iconography
The Kū *ki'i*: tall carved-wood figures with fierce expressions, mother-of-pearl eyes, prominent teeth, often crested with feathers. The feathered *ki'i* (basket-frame covered in red 'i'iwi and yellow 'ō'ō feathers) were the most prestigious. The Kū iconography is one of the most globally recognizable Polynesian religious artistic traditions.
