# Thraetaona

## Identity

Thraetaona (Avestan **Θraētaona**, patronymic *Āθwyāni* "of the house of Āθwya"; Pahlavi *Frēdōn*; New Persian **Fereydun**) is the **dragon-slaying hero of Zoroastrian-Iranian tradition** — the champion who smites and binds the three-headed serpent **[[azi-dahaka|Aži Dahāka]]**. He is the Iranian occupant of the **[[chaoskampf|Chaoskampf]]** champion-slot: the structural counterpart of Vedic **[[indra|Indra]]**, Babylonian [[marduk|Marduk]], Greek [[zeus|Zeus]], and Norse [[thor|Thor]], and — within Zoroastrianism itself — the *narrative* dragon-slayer to whom **[[verethragna|Verethragna]]** stands as the *abstracted* yazata of dragon-slaying victory.

In the Avesta, Thraetaona is not a mortal of ordinary history but a figure of the mythic-heroic age who is **invoked in sacrifice and grants the *xvarənah*** (the royal-glory that departed from [[yima-jamshid|Yima]]); he is also a **healer and averter of fever and disease** (Vendidad 20; Ābān Yasht). His central, defining act is everywhere the same — the smiting of Aži Dahāka. In the later Persianate reception he is **euhemerised into Fereydun**, the fifth king of the primordial Pīšdādiān dynasty in Ferdowsi's *Shahnameh*, who overthrows the dragon-tyrant Zahhāk. The Atlas files him among the deity-rank chaoskampf champions (with Indra, Verethragna, and the other slayers) on the strength of his divinized Avestan profile — sacrificial recipient, *xvarənah*-bearer, member of the same inherited dragon-slayer complex — while noting the figure's drift toward legendary kingship in the epic tradition (see Disputes).

## In the Avesta and the Iranian tradition

Thraetaona is a **Younger-Avestan** figure (he does not appear in the older Gāθās). The principal sources are:

- **Yasht 5 (Ābān Yašt) 33-35** — Thraetaona of the house of Āθwya sacrifices a hundred horses, a thousand oxen and ten thousand lambs to **Arəduuī Sūrā Anāhitā** "in Varəna of the four corners," begging the strength to **overcome Aži Dahāka, the three-mouthed, three-headed, six-eyed monster** of a thousand devices; the goddess grants the boon. This is the fullest narrative invocation.
- **Yasna 9 (Hōm Yašt) 7-8** — in the Avesta's own genealogy of those who pressed **[[haoma-zoroastrian|Haoma]]**, **Āθwya** is the second presser, and his reward is the son **Θraētaona, who smote Aži Dahāka, the three-mouthed, three-headed, six-eyed** dragon. (This text is already cited in the Atlas's haoma node.)
- **Yasht 19 (Zamyād Yašt) 36-37, 92** — Thraetaona among the bearers of the *xvarənah*; and the **eschatological coda**: the bound dragon will break loose at the end of time and be destroyed by Kərəsāspa.
- **Vendidad 20** — Thraetaona invoked as the first healer who drove back sickness, fever, and death.
- **Pahlavi books** (Bundahišn, Dēnkard) elaborate the figure as **Frēdōn**; the **Shahnameh** (~+1010) gives the fully epic **Fereydun**, who defeats Zahhāk with the ox-headed mace (*gorz-e gāvsār*) and binds him on **Mount Damāvand**, then partitions the world among his three sons Salm, Tur and Iraj.

The distinctive shape of the Iranian combat is that Thraetaona **binds rather than annihilates**: Aži Dahāka is not killed but **chained beneath Mount Damāvand**, surviving for a final eschatological release. The Iranian chaoskampf is therefore **split across two times** — primordial binding (Thraetaona) and end-time slaying (Kərəsāspa, within the [[saoshyant|Saoshyant]]-led renovation).

## Cross-tradition significance

Thraetaona is **doubly load-bearing** for the chaoskampf web, and the two kinds of connection must be kept distinct.

**(1) Genuine Indo-Iranian inheritance (the secure cognate side).** Thraetaona is the Iranian reflex of an **inherited Indo-Iranian dragon-slayer**. The cleanest comparand is **Vedic Trita Āptya**, who slays the three-headed **Viśvarūpa/Triśiras** (the son of Tvaṣṭṛ): the patronymics correspond exactly — Avestan **Āθwya** = Vedic **Āptya** — and *both* heroes kill a *three-headed* adversary. There is a precise philological subtlety here, noted in the *Encyclopaedia Iranica* and by West (2007): in Iran the inherited name-pair has **split across two figures** — the patronymic line **Āθwya → Θraētaona** carries the dragon-fight, while the cognate of the *name* Vedic *Trita* survives separately as **Θrita**, the father of the other Iranian dragon-slayer Kərəsāspa. The shared poetic formula clinches the inheritance: Avestan **\*jan- aži-** ("smote the serpent") answers Vedic **\*áhann áhim** (Rigveda 1.32.1, of Indra slaying Vritra), an instance of the Proto-Indo-European formula **\*gʷhen- h₃gʷhim-** reconstructed by Watkins (1995). With **Indra** (the great Vedic dragon-slayer) and **[[verethragna|Verethragna]]** (the Iranian *Vr̥tra-ghna* "smasher-of-resistance"), Thraetaona belongs to the **inherited, genealogical core** of the chaoskampf — the part of the pattern that is demonstrably *cognate*, not merely *similar*.

**(2) Wider Old-World convergence (the structural side).** Beyond the Indo-Iranian family, Thraetaona fills the **same comparative slot** as champions in **non-Indo-European** traditions — [[marduk|Marduk]] vs [[tiamat|Tiamat]], [[baal-hadad|Baal]] vs [[yam|Yam]], Egyptian Ra vs Apophis. Here the relationship is **structural/typological, plausibly areal**, not genealogical: there is no claim of linguistic cognation between Avestan and Akkadian. The Atlas follows the mainstream framing (West 2007; Watkins 1995): the chaoskampf is *partly inherited* (within Indo-European, where Thraetaona sits) and *partly convergent/diffused* (between Indo-European and the ancient Near East). Conflating the two — treating the Marduk parallel as the same KIND of fact as the Indra cognate — is exactly the error the comparative literature warns against.

## Disputes

- **Deity vs legendary hero.** Thraetaona straddles the line between *divinized hero* (the Avestan profile: sacrificial recipient, *xvarənah*-bearer, healer) and *legendary king* (the Shahnameh's Fereydun, a mortal monarch). The Atlas types him **deity** to occupy the chaoskampf champion-slot consistently with Indra and Verethragna, but the figure's later trajectory is unambiguously toward **euhemerised kingship** rather than cult. Reasonable cataloguers could file the Fereydun reception as a `person`/heroic-king node; the *Avestan* figure is the one wired here.
- **Thraetaona vs Trita — one figure or two.** Whether the Iranian tradition originally had a *single* Āθwya-Trita dragon-slayer that later **fissioned** into Θraētaona (the fighter) and Θrita (Kərəsāspa's father), or whether two distinct figures were always present, is debated. The name-split is secure; its prehistory is reconstructed, not attested.
- **Direction and depth of the Aži Dahāka–Zahhāk development.** The Avestan *serpent* Aži Dahāka becomes, in the Pahlavi and Islamic-Persian tradition, the *human tyrant* **Zahhāk** with serpents growing from his shoulders. How much of this is inherited mytheme versus later narrative elaboration (and any Mesopotamian colouring of the Zahhāk figure) is discussed in the Iranica 'Aždahā' literature; it does not affect the core Thraetaona–dragon combat, which is securely Avestan.
