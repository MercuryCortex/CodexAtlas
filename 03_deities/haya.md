# Haya

## Identity

Haya (Ḫaya) is a Mesopotamian god of the storehouse, of accounts, and of the scribal craft, best known as the husband of [[nisaba]], the goddess of grain and of writing. The pairing is telling: Nisaba personifies both the grain itself and the scribal counting that records it, while Haya is the god of the store in which the grain is kept and the reckoning is done — together they form a divine couple of agriculture-as-administered, the harvest and its accounting.

## Mythological role and cult

Haya appears chiefly in scribal and administrative contexts. He is invoked alongside Nisaba in the colophons and doxologies of scribes ("praise to Nisaba and Haya"), and he is associated with the storehouse and with doorkeeping — the guarding and reckoning of stored goods. In some genealogical traditions Haya and Nisaba are the parents of [[nungal]], the goddess of the prison, and of Sud (a form of Ninlil), placing Haya at the head of a small family of gods concerned with administration, justice, and the scribal art. Though never a major cult figure, he was worshipped at Umma, Kish, and later at Assur.

## Cross-tradition significance

Haya is a useful node for the Mesopotamian **scribal and administrative theology** — the divinization of writing, counting, and the storehouse that underlay the bureaucratic civilization of Sumer and Babylon. As the consort of the writing-goddess Nisaba, he belongs to the cluster of deities (with Nabu and Nisaba) through whom Mesopotamian culture sacralized literacy and record-keeping — the technological substrate on which the entire textual tradition, including the scriptural traditions downstream of it, ultimately rests.
