# Igigi

## Identity

The Igigi are a **collective** of Mesopotamian gods — most characteristically the *younger* generation of working gods, as opposed to the senior [[anunnaki]]. (In some texts the usage is reversed or blurred, the two terms shifting in scope across periods, but the Atra-ḫasīs tradition fixes the Igigi as the laboring junior gods.) Like the Anunnaki, the Igigi are invoked as a group rather than worshipped as an individual deity.

## Mythological role

The Igigi are central to the great Babylonian account of the **creation of humankind**, the *Atra-ḫasīs* epic. In the beginning, the epic says, "the gods instead of man did the work, bore the loads" — and it is the Igigi who are set to the back-breaking toil of digging the Tigris and Euphrates and the irrigation canals. After forty years they rebel: they burn their tools, surround the house of [[enlil]] by night, and refuse to labor any longer. In the divine council that follows, [[enki-ea]] proposes the remedy — the birth-goddess will create humanity from clay mixed with the flesh and blood of a slain god, so that human beings will carry the gods' burden. The Igigi's revolt is thus the hinge on which the creation of the human race turns: humanity exists to do the work the gods refused.

## Cross-tradition significance

The Igigi-revolt narrative is one of the most striking Mesopotamian anthropogonies and a key comparandum for the biblical creation of humanity. The motif of humans formed from clay (and, in *Atra-ḫasīs*, animated by divine blood) to serve the divine, and the broader theme of the gods' labor relieved by human creation, stand behind — and contrast with — the Genesis account of humanity made in the divine image. Together with [[nammu]]'s clay-creation of humans in the Sumerian tradition, the Igigi epic anchors the Mesopotamian end of the "creation of humankind from clay" wire.
