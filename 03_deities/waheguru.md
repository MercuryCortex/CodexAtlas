# Waheguru

## Identity
Waheguru (Punjabi: ਵਾਹੇਗੁਰੂ) — usually rendered "Wondrous Teacher" or "Wonderful Lord," from Punjabi *wāhe* (wonder, astonishment) + *gurū* (teacher, dispeller of darkness) — is the Sikh name and concept for the supreme divine reality. Not a person, not gendered, not anthropomorphic, not a creator-god in the mythological sense: Waheguru is the **Ik Onkar** (One Creator), the formless (*nirankar*), timeless (*akal*), self-existent (*saibham*) reality that underlies all existence and from which all being flows. The **Mool Mantar** (Root Formula), the foundational statement of Sikh theology, opens the [[phase-6-016-guru-granth-sahib]] and defines Waheguru's attributes:

> *Ik Oṃkār, Sat Nāmu, Kartā Purakhu, Nirbhau, Nirvair, Akāl Mūrat, Ajūnī, Saibhaṃ, Gur Prasādi.*
> ("One Creator Being. Truth is His Name. Creator of all things. Without fear. Without enmity. Timeless Form. Beyond birth and death. Self-existent. By Guru's Grace.")

This formula is one of the most precisely compressed theological statements in world religious literature: each attribute directly negates a category of limitation (fear, enmity, time, birth/death, dependence on another) to describe the absolute. Waheguru is approached not through image, ritual sacrifice, or theological speculation but through **Naam** (the divine Name meditated upon in Simran — recitative meditation) and through the **Shabad** (the divine Word as preserved in Gurbani, the sacred hymns of the Guru Granth Sahib).

## Earliest attestation
- **Guru Nanak Dev Ji** (1469–1539 CE): the foundational theological articulation of Waheguru and the Mool Mantar. Nanak's hymns in the Guru Granth Sahib are the primary source.
- The name "Waheguru" as a specific divine epithet appears across the Gurbani and is associated especially with the Sikh Gurus from Guru Nanak through the later Gurus.
- **Guru Granth Sahib** compiled 1604 CE by Guru Arjan Dev (5th Sikh Guru); finalized in its current form by Guru Gobind Singh (10th Sikh Guru, 1708 CE). [[phase-6-016-guru-granth-sahib]]

## Mythological role
Waheguru is not a mythological deity with a narrative — no birth, no consort, no battles, no descent into history. Waheguru is the formless absolute reality that **sustains** all creation through its own will (*hukam*, the divine order) and that is the goal of all liberation (*mukti*): the soul (jiv) is separated from Waheguru by *haumai* (ego / self-centeredness) and is reunited with Waheguru through *naam simran* (meditation on the Name), Sangat (holy community), and Gurbani. This is a devotional-mystical soteriology structurally closer to Sufi *fana* (annihilation in the divine) and Vedantic *moksha* (liberation into Brahman) than to mythological-narrative religion.

The **Ik Onkar** calligraphic symbol — the numeral "1" combined with the *Oṃkāra* sign — is the visual emblem of Waheguru's absolute unity: "there is one, and it is the Creator." This is the most radical possible monotheistic formula: not merely "our god is supreme" but "there is only one, period."

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[ein-sof]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: the Kabbalistic infinite that is beyond all attributes, beyond being itself, from which all the *Sefirot* (divine qualities) emanate — parallels Waheguru as Nirankar (formless) and Akal Purkh (timeless) (Singh 1993). Both traditions developed sophisticated mystical theologies of the unknowable absolute.
- **[[the-one-plotinus]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Plotinus's One — absolutely simple, beyond predication, the source from which *Nous* (Mind) and Soul emanate — is structurally homologous with Waheguru as the formless source of all creation. The Sikh tradition absorbed Neoplatonic theological concepts through Sufi philosophical channels.
- **[[allah]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Islamic theological attributes of Allah as *ahad* (absolutely one), *samad* (self-sufficient, eternal), beyond comparison (*tanzih*) — directly parallel the Mool Mantar's Waheguru (McLeod 1968). Guru Nanak's formative theological world included deep engagement with Islamic monotheism.
- **[[aditi]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: the Vedic Aditi as the boundless (*a-diti* = "non-bound"), the mother of all gods, the infinite that precedes and contains all being — parallels Waheguru as the unlimited, the container of all that exists.
- **[[ometeotl]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: the Aztec supreme dual-principle that is beyond gender, beyond form, the source from which all creation flows — another non-anthropomorphic formless absolute that parallels Waheguru's theological structure.
- **[[the-monad-gnostic]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: the Gnostic primal monad — singular, unbounded, beyond all categories — as the absolute source, paralleling Waheguru as the singular formless reality.
- **[[amenominakanushi]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: the Japanese Shinto deity of the center of heaven, the first kami, formless and self-existent — a parallel to Waheguru as the primordial self-existent reality.

## Iconography
Waheguru has **no iconic representation** — this is a theologically central commitment in Sikhism (*nirankar* = formless, beyond image). Sikh gurdwaras (temples) display no divine images; the sacred focal point is the **Guru Granth Sahib** enthroned on a throne (*manji sahib*) under a canopy. The **Ik Onkar** calligraphic symbol serves as the closest thing to a Sikh "icon" — it is everywhere in Sikh sacred spaces but represents the *unity* of the divine, not a figure of the divine.

## Disputes
- **"Waheguru" as epithet vs. theological concept.** Whether *Waheguru* is a *name* of God (like *Yahweh* or *Allah*) or a theological *description* (wondrous teacher) is debated within Sikh theological tradition. The Guru Granth Sahib uses it both ways.
- **The Sikh-Hinduism-Islam triangle.** Western scholarship has debated whether Sikhism is best understood as a synthesis of Hindu and Islamic influences (the "Sant tradition" synthesis model, associated with McLeod) or as a genuinely independent revelation (the Sikh theological position). The theological conception of Waheguru draws on but transcends both Islamic and Hindu-Vedantic models.
- **Waheguru's gender.** Gurbani uses both masculine and feminine pronouns for Waheguru in different hymns; the official Sikh theological position is that Waheguru is beyond gender (*nirankar* = formless, hence beyond gendered embodiment).
