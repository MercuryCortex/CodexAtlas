# The Father (Valentinian)

## Identity
In Valentinian Gnosticism, **the Father** — also called **Bythos** ("Depth") or the **Propator** ("Forefather") — is the ineffable, unbegotten, and perfect first principle from which the entire divine fullness ([[the-pleroma|Pleroma]]) unfolds. He is prior to all that exists, incomprehensible and uncontainable, dwelling in silence (*Sigē*) before the emanation of the aeons. The whole Valentinian cosmology is the story of how the aeons proceed from this primal Depth and how the lowest of them, [[sophia-gnostic|Sophia]], falls and is restored.

## Earliest attestation
The Father is the central theological subject of the [[phase-4-003-gospel-of-truth|Gospel of Truth]] (a Valentinian meditation, possibly by Valentinus himself, ~mid-2nd c. CE) and is described in detail by the heresiologist Irenaeus when he summarizes the Valentinian system of Ptolemy (*Against Heresies* I.1). The Gospel of Philip likewise presupposes this supreme Father.

## Context
Valentinianism, founded by [[valentinus]] (~100–160 CE), was the most sophisticated and influential of the Gnostic schools, operating partly within the broader catholic church before being marginalized as heretical. Its theology of the unknowable Father generating the Pleroma through emanation drew on Middle Platonist metaphysics (the One beyond being) and on Jewish-Christian speculation about the hidden God. The Father is **apophatic** — defined chiefly by negation, beyond name and comprehension.

## Cross-tradition findings
- **The unknowable Source across Gnostic and Platonic thought.** The Valentinian Father/Bythos parallels the Sethian [[the-monad-gnostic|Monad]] and the Neoplatonic One — all positing an utterly transcendent first principle beyond being and predication. This convergence reflects the shared Middle/Neo-Platonist substrate of 2nd–3rd-century religious philosophy rather than a one-way borrowing. Wired as a `scholarly-parallel` to the Monad.
- **Apophatic theology downstream.** The negative-theology vocabulary applied to the Father feeds, by a contested route, into later Christian apophaticism (Pseudo-Dionysius). The Atlas treats this as a parallel/substrate relation, not a documented dependency.
