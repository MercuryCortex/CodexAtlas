# The Primordial Sea (Popol Vuh)

## Identity

The **primordial sea** of the *Popol Vuh* is the calm, silent, empty expanse of water that — together with the empty sky — is all that exists before creation in K'iche' Maya cosmogony. It is not a personified deity but a cosmological condition: the pooled, motionless sea under the "womb of the sky," in which nothing yet stirs. In the Atlas it is placed among the primordial entities beside [[tehom]], [[tiamat]], [[nun]], [[apas]], and [[ginnungagap]] because it fills the Maya position in the cross-tradition primordial-waters slot — and, crucially, fills it from a civilization that had no contact with any of the others.

## In the Popol Vuh

The *Popol Vuh* opens on absolute stillness: "*all is silent and calm. Hushed and empty is the womb of the sky... Alone lies the expanse of the sea, along with the womb of all the sky. There is not yet anything gathered together*" (Christenson 2007). Into this calm water the creators are already present: the **Framer and the Shaper** (Tz'aqol and B'itol), together with **Sovereign Plumed Serpent** (Tepew and Q'uq'umatz — the K'iche' feathered serpent, [[feathered-serpent]]) and **Heart of Sky** (Uk'u'x Kaj, also called Huracan), lie within the sea "surrounded with light," wrapped in green and blue quetzal feathers.

Creation then proceeds **by word**: the gods take counsel together, and simply by speaking — "*Earth!*" — they cause the mountains and the land to rise from the water, "*like a cloud, like a mist*." The sea is parted from the land exactly as in the Genesis separation of the waters. The remainder of the book follows the creators' repeated attempts to make beings who will speak and remember them — the animals, the mud people, the wood people, and finally the maize people — but the cosmogonic ground of the whole narrative is this first calm sea.

## Cross-tradition significance (the Maya deep — and the limit of transmission)

The K'iche' primordial sea is the **Maya member of the cross-tradition primordial-waters cluster**, and it carries a special evidential weight. The Hebrew *tĕhôm* and the Akkadian *Tiāmat* are linked by a documented loan-cognate — a genuine case of cultural transmission. The Vedic *salila* and the Norse Ginnungagap are linked by common Indo-European inheritance. But the Maya developed the **identical cosmogonic image — a calm primordial sea under an empty sky, parted to reveal land, creation by divine speech — in complete isolation from the Old World**. There is no possible transmission. The Popol Vuh therefore anchors the boundary of the whole cluster: it proves that the primordial-waters cosmogony is not only *transmitted* (Babylon → Israel) but also *independently re-invented* (Mesoamerica), which means the cross-tradition parallels in this cluster are a mixture of documented descent and genuine convergence — exactly the distinction an investigation of "the deep" must keep straight. That the creation-by-word motif **also** recurs here (alongside the Memphite Theology and Genesis 1) sharpens the point: convergence, not contact.

## Disputes

The *Popol Vuh* survives only in a single alphabetic K'iche' manuscript (Francisco Ximénez's early-18th-century copy of a mid-16th-century original), so the degree to which its prologue preserves genuinely pre-Columbian cosmology versus colonial-era reshaping — including possible contamination by the Genesis account the K'iche' authors knew from Spanish missionaries — is debated. Most scholars (Christenson, Tedlock) judge the core cosmogony authentically Maya, citing its continuity with Classic-period creation imagery and its distinctively Maya features (the maize-people, the feathered-serpent creators, the failed creations), and treat the resemblance to Genesis as convergence rather than borrowing; but the manuscript's late date means the question cannot be closed with certainty.
