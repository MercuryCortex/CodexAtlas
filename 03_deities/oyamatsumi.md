# Ōyamatsumi

## Identity
Ōyamatsumi is the great Shintō **kami of the mountains** — the lord of the high, wild land that stands beyond the cultivated fields. Born of [[izanagi]] and [[izanami]] among the kami of land and sea, he is the father of the nature-princesses through whom one of Shintō's most consequential myths unfolds. His shrine at Ōmishima is a historic centre of warriors' and seafarers' devotion, and he is also honoured as a kami of **sake-brewing**.

## Earliest attestation
Recorded in the *Kojiki* (712 CE) and *Nihon Shoki* (720 CE) among the offspring of the creator pair; the Ōyamazumi Shrine preserves an ancient mountain-cult.

## Mythological role — the two daughters and the origin of mortality
Ōyamatsumi gives his two daughters to the heavenly grandson **Ninigi**: [[konohanasakuya-hime]] ("blossoming-tree princess," beauty that flares and fades) and **Iwanaga-hime** ("long-rock princess," cold permanence). Ninigi keeps the lovely Konohanasakuya but sends Iwanaga back — and so, Ōyamatsumi declares, the lives of Ninigi's descendants (the emperors, and by extension humankind) will be **brief and blossom-like rather than enduring as the rocks**. The rejected gift of permanence is the Shintō account of why human life is mortal.

## Cross-tradition
The Iwanaga/Konohanasakuya episode is a Shintō instance of the widespread **forfeited-immortality** myth — humanity could have had unending life but a choice or refusal lost it — the same shape as the Tree of Life set beyond reach in Eden and the "origin of death" tales catalogued across world mythology. See [[tree-of-life]].

## Iconography
Represented by the mountain itself and by his shrines; associated with the deer, the forest, and the sake cup.
