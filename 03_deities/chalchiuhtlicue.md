# Chalchiuhtlicue

## Identity
Chalchiuhtlicue ("She of the Jade Skirt" in Classical Nahuatl, from *chalchihuitl* = jade/turquoise, *cueitl* = skirt) is the Aztec goddess of water — specifically of rivers, lakes, springs, and the great floods. She is the consort of [[tlaloc]], the rain god, and where Tlaloc governs rain and storm from above, Chalchiuhtlicue governs the waters of the earth's surface. Her jade skirt is both a garment and a cosmic symbol: jade was the Aztec emblem of water and preciousness, the color of still lake water, and her skirt flowing with streams is depicted in the codices as a literal current bearing human figures downstream. She was patron of childbirth and of infants — all newborns underwent a ritual water-cleansing in her name, marking the threshold of life as belonging to her watery domain.

Most significantly, Chalchiuhtlicue was the presiding deity of the Fourth Sun (Nahui Atl — "Four Water"), the world age immediately preceding the present Fifth Sun. The Fourth Sun was destroyed by flood — Chalchiuhtlicue wept for her people; some accounts say it was a flood of 52 years. Humans became fish, surviving in the waters. The destruction of the Fourth Sun is the Aztec Great Flood, and Chalchiuhtlicue is its divine agent.

## Earliest attestation
- Teotihuacan murals (~100–700 CE) at the Palace of Tetitla show a great water-goddess figure — the "Great Goddess of Teotihuacan" — who many scholars identify as the precursor of Chalchiuhtlicue, though the identification remains debated (Pasztory 1974; Berrin & Pasztory 1993). If this identification is correct, her attestation pushes back to approximately -900 (late pre-Classic Teotihuacan antecedents in the Basin of Mexico).
- Clear named attestation: Florentine Codex ([[phase-8-004-florentine-codex-sahagun]]) and Codex Borgia ([[phase-8-002-codex-borgia]]), 15th–16th c. CE.

## Mythological role
In the Five Suns cosmogony (attested in the *Leyenda de los Soles*, *Codex Chimalpopoca*): the Fourth Sun was the age of Chalchiuhtlicue. When it ended — either because the gods quarreled with her, or because Tezcatlipoca shamed her into weeping — a rain of water fell for 52 years and drowned the world. The people of the Fourth Sun became fish. In some variants, [[quetzalcoatl]] and [[ehecatl]] carried the survivors of the flood across the sky.

She also appears in birth ritual: midwives invoked Chalchiuhtlicue at the moment of delivery, washing the newborn in water blessed in her name. Water was associated with purification, with the entrance into the social/cosmic order, and with her protective power.

In the divine cosmological hierarchy, as daughter of [[ometeotl]], she is one of the active deities who manifests the primordial dual source into specific cosmic functions.

## Cross-tradition identifications

- **[[mama-cocha]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Mama Cocha ("Sea Mother" / "Lake Mother") is the Andean feminine deity of the sea and great lakes — particularly Lake Titicaca. The structural parallel is striking: both are feminine water-power figures, both are conceived as the living water that sustains life rather than the destructive flood as such. The "jade skirt" water imagery of Chalchiuhtlicue and the literal lake/sea body of Mama Cocha reflect a shared Amerindian theological pattern of the feminine sacred as water. No documented transmission.

- **[[tiamat]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Tiamat (Babylonian primordial sea-dragon) is the closest Old World parallel. Both are the primordial waters that precede and potentially dissolve ordered creation; both are feminine water-chaos powers associated with a flood or pre-creation state. In the Babylonian *Enuma Elish*, [[marduk]] kills Tiamat and creates the earth from her body. In the Aztec cosmogony, the Fourth Sun flood is ended when [[quetzalcoatl]] and [[tezcatlipoca]] act to restore order after Chalchiuhtlicue's weeping dissolves the world. The structural parallel is flood-chaos-as-feminine-water-power that must be contained or succeeded for the next world age to begin.

- **[[nun]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Nun (Egyptian primordial abyss, the undifferentiated water before creation) parallels Chalchiuhtlicue's role as the watery totality that both precedes and follows the ordered world. In Egyptian theology, Nun is not a destructive force but the eternal formless substrate from which Re rises each morning — and into which the ordered world will eventually dissolve. Chalchiuhtlicue's flood-dissolution plays a similar cosmological role: the water age is the dissolution-state between world-structures.

- **[[ixchel]]** — *structural affinity*: Ixchel, the Maya goddess of water and weaving, is a regional parallel within Mesoamerica — another feminine water deity who governs flooding and the cycles of water. The two are not identified in ancient sources but represent the Mesoamerican tradition of feminine water sovereignty.

## Iconography
Chalchiuhtlicue is depicted in the codices with: a jade-and-turquoise skirt; a water-lily or reed headdress; streams flowing from her garment containing human figures; blue/green color throughout (jade-water color coding). In Teotihuacan murals, the candidate "Great Goddess" figure is surrounded by streams, water imagery, and jade-green color coding. Frogs and water birds attend her.

## Disputes
- The identification of the Teotihuacan "Great Goddess" with Chalchiuhtlicue remains contested. Pasztory (1974) argues for a single all-encompassing female deity at Teotihuacan; others read multiple female deities in the murals. The continuity from Teotihuacan water-goddess to Aztec Chalchiuhtlicue is probable but not certain.
- The mechanism of the Fourth Sun's destruction varies across sources: in some texts Chalchiuhtlicue weeps of her own sorrow; in others Tezcatlipoca mocks her false piety and shame causes the weeping. The agency question (deity choosing vs. deity shamed into acting) reflects different traditions' handling of the same narrative.
