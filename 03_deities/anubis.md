# Anubis

Anubis (Egyptian *Inpu*) is the jackal-headed Egyptian god of embalming, mummification, and the necropolis — and the primary psychopomp of the Egyptian underworld. He is among the oldest attested Egyptian deities, already present in late Predynastic and Early Dynastic iconography, predating the codification of the Osirian myth into which he is later incorporated as a junior figure. His black skin does not signify death in the Egyptian symbolic register but regeneration — the color of the fertile Nile silt and the bituminous resin used in embalming, both substances associated with new life. Cult centers at Cynopolis ("City of the Dog/Jackal") in Middle Egypt and at Asyut.

## Mythological role

Anubis presides over the embalming process: the embalmer-priest performing mummification rites wore an Anubis mask, making the human craftsman temporarily become the divine patron of his craft. He guides the deceased into the netherworld as **psychopomp** and tends the scales at the weighing of the heart in the Hall of Two Truths (*Ahet*), with [[thoth]] recording the verdict and [[osiris]] enthroned as judge.

In the Osirian myth as developed in the Middle and New Kingdoms, Anubis is presented as the son of [[osiris]] by [[nephthys]], born of a clandestine encounter and raised by [[isis-egyptian]] — a mythological etiology grafted onto a much older independent jackal-god. In the central Osirian narrative, when [[osiris]] is murdered and dismembered by [[set]], it is Anubis whom [[isis-egyptian]] and [[nephthys]] call upon to embalm the restored body. Anubis performs the first mummification — making the preservation of the physical body a theological act directly ordained by divine necessity. The body must be preserved because it is the vehicle for resurrection: [[osiris]] cannot rise, and the sun cannot rise, and the dead cannot proceed, unless the body is intact.

## The weighing of the heart

The most cosmologically significant scene in Egyptian religion — depicted in chapter 125 of the *Book of the Dead* — shows Anubis officiating at the scales where the deceased's heart is weighed against the feather of [[ma-at]] (cosmic truth and order). Anubis holds the scales, ensuring the accuracy of the measurement with impartial divine precision. If the heart is lighter than or equal to the feather, the deceased is declared *maat-kheru* (true of voice) and proceeds to the Field of Reeds. If heavier — weighted by wrongdoing — it is devoured by Ammit, the composite beast, and the person ceases to exist.

This role makes Anubis a **cosmic moral accountant** and the guarantor of impartial justice — not a judge (that is Osiris's role) but the officer who ensures the measurement is honest. The structural logic maps across traditions:

| Tradition | Figure at the judgment | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Egyptian | Anubis | Holds the scales; impartial measurement |
| Zoroastrian | Mithra | Cosmic witness of good and evil deeds |
| Byzantine Christian | Archangel Michael | Holds scales in psychostasia iconography |
| Buddhist Abhidharma | Yama's clerks | Record deeds for judgment by Yama |
| Islamic | Recording angels Raqib / Atid | Write deeds in the Book |

See [[psychostasia-soul-weighing]] for the full cross-tradition comparative analysis. See [[theme-graduated-afterlife]] for the theological implication that afterlife reward is calibrated to earthly conduct.

## MASSIVE WIN: Anubis → Hermanubis → Hermes Psychopomp

The Anubis-Hermes equation is the most directly documented Egyptian-Greek divine identification after Osiris-Dionysus. Both deities serve as **psychopomps** (Greek: "soul-guides") who escort the dead from the world of the living to the realm of the dead. Both are associated with liminality, with the border between worlds. The parallel was noted in antiquity: Plutarch (*De Iside et Osiride* §61, c. 100 CE) explicitly associates Anubis with Hermes as guide of souls, and the Roman-period syncretic deity **Hermanubis** — half-Hermes, half-Anubis — was actively worshipped in Roman Egypt, depicted with Hermes's caduceus and Anubis's jackal head.

Griffiths (1970, pp. 488–491) documents the §61 parallel in detail. This identification was not merely scholarly abstraction but cultic reality: Hermanubis shrines and statuettes are archaeologically attested across Roman Egypt, and the composite deity appears in the *Corpus Hermeticum* orbit. The Hermanubis fusion is one of the cleaner demonstrations that ancient Egyptians and Greeks understood their psychopomp traditions as describing the same cosmic function. See [[hermes-greek]].

## MASSIVE WIN: Embalming as resurrection theology

The theological logic of Egyptian mummification is precisely articulated through Anubis: when [[isis-egyptian]] and [[nephthys]] reconstitute the dismembered body of [[osiris]] and call on Anubis to embalm it, they are establishing the metaphysical principle that the **physical body matters for the afterlife**. The preservation of the body is not mere sentiment — it is a theological requirement. Osiris can only be resurrected (and the king can only participate in that resurrection) if the body survives.

This is the Egyptian position in a debate that runs through every major religious tradition: does the body matter after death?

- **Egyptian**: Yes — the body must be preserved (mummification); without it, resurrection is impossible
- **Zoroastrian**: Yes — the physical resurrection of the body at the Frashokereti is bodily
- **Jewish/Christian**: Yes — the resurrection of the dead is bodily (Paul, 1 Cor 15; the empty tomb tradition)
- **Greek Platonic**: No — the soul is the real self; the body is the tomb (*soma sema*)
- **Gnostic**: No — the material body is a prison to escape

The Egyptian tradition (via mummification theology centered on Anubis) represents the most ancient documented articulation of the "body matters" position, which runs through Zoroastrian and into Christian resurrection theology — both against the Greek contempt for material existence.

## The jackal: threat transformed into protector

The cult of Anubis exemplifies one of Egyptian religion's most elegant symbolic moves. Jackals were observed near desert-edge cemeteries — they scavenged burial grounds, digging at fresh graves. The threat was real. Egyptian religious imagination responded not by fighting the jackal but by sacralizing it: the threat-animal becomes the protector-god. The same logic appears across religious traditions:

- The *nehushtan* (Numbers 21): the bronze serpent — the same creature that kills becomes the instrument of healing when looked upon
- The Cross: the instrument of Roman execution becomes the instrument of salvation
- Indra: the storm that destroys crops is also the storm that breaks the drought

This transformation of feared into sacred is a structural constant in religious symbolism. Anubis is one of its clearest ancient examples.

## Greco-Roman reception and Hermanubis

By the Ptolemaic period (323–30 BCE), Anubis was being actively identified with Hermes throughout the Greek-speaking world. The composite **Hermanubis** was not an academic construct but a living cult figure: statuettes of Hermanubis (with dog or jackal head, caduceus staff, palm branch) survive from across Roman Egypt. The figure appears in Apuleius's *The Golden Ass* (Book XI), where the procession of Isis includes "Anubis, the messenger between gods above and gods below, walking upright, his dog's head alternately black and golden." The Roman-period identification of Anubis with Hermes/Mercury as dual psychopomp is documented across funerary art and literary sources.
