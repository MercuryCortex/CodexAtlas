# Pan

## Identity
Pan (Greek *Πάν*) is the **rustic goat-god of Greek religion**, originally an Arcadian shepherd-deity of the Peloponnesian highlands and spread by Athenian state-adoption (early 5th c. BCE, in gratitude for his help at Marathon — Herodotus 6.105) into pan-Hellenic and later Greco-Roman religion. He represents **untamed wild nature** in contrast to the civilized Olympian gods: goat-legged, horned, sexually voracious, the lord of nymphs and satyrs, and the giver of the irrational *panic* terror that strikes flocks and humans alone in wild places. For the Codex Atlas's mission Pan is doubly load-bearing: (1) he is one of [[herodotus]]'s Greek-Egyptian theological identifications (Pan = Banebdjedet of Mendes), and (2) his "death" reported by [[plutarch]] becomes a central **Christian-apologetic topos** that frames the supersession of paganism.

## Earliest attestation
- Homeric Hymn 19 (*To Pan*) — earliest extended literary attestation (probably 6th–5th c. BCE).
- [[hesiod]] traditions: Pan as son of [[hermes-greek]] and a nymph.
- Arcadian cult-sites with Predynastic-Greek (Bronze Age) ritual continuity (Borgeaud 1988): Pan's origin is likely a genuinely ancient Arcadian shepherd-protector deity.
- Adopted at Athens after the Battle of Marathon (-490) — Herodotus 6.105 reports Pheidippides encountered Pan en route to Sparta, was assured of divine help; subsequent Athenian gratitude established a cult-cave on the north slope of the Acropolis.
- Spread to Roman religion as syncretic with Faunus; remains a major figure in Latin pastoral poetry (Virgil's *Eclogues*).

## Mythological role
Pan's mythology is principally **erotic and rustic**: he pursues nymphs (Syrinx transformed into reeds becomes his panpipe; Echo flees and becomes only voice; Selene seduced by his white-fleece disguise), competes with [[apollo]] in music and loses, and helps shepherds against wolves. He is the **father of *panic*** — the irrational mass-terror that grips flocks and armies in wild places; the word's etymology preserves his cosmological role as the spirit of the chaotic non-civilized world.

The single most influential narrative for later reception is **[[plutarch]] *De defectu oraculorum* 17**: a sailor named Thamus, sailing past the island of Paxi in the reign of Tiberius, hears a divine voice cry out three times "Thamus! When you reach Palodes, announce that Great Pan is dead." On reaching Palodes Thamus complies; from the shore comes a great lamenting wail. The story is dated within Plutarch's lifetime by his interlocutor. It is taken up by **Eusebius** *Praeparatio Evangelica* 5.17, then by every medieval and modern Christian apologetic, as **a divine proclamation that the pagan gods had died with the death of Christ** (the chronological coincidence — Tiberius's reign — is rhetorically asserted). The story is also reread in modern times by Romantic and Symbolist poets, by Nietzsche, by E.M. Forster, etc.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **Faunus (Roman)** — *ancient-identification*: Roman *interpretatio*; structurally close enough to be effectively the same god.
- **Banebdjedet / Mendes** — *ancient-identification*: [[herodotus]] *Histories* 2.46 identifies Pan with the Egyptian ram-god of Mendes. The identification is **iconographic** (both have caprid features — Pan's goat-legs, Banebdjedet's ram-head) rather than functional, but it is one of the canonical Greek-Egyptian theological equations.
- **[[dionysus-mystery]]** — *shared-milieu* + *scholarly-parallel*: Pan is a member of Dionysus's thiasos in Hellenistic-Roman iconography; structurally Pan and Dionysus both represent **untamed natural force** harnessed into ritual.
- **Christian devil-iconography** — *appropriated-by* / *polemic-inversion*: the goat-horned, goat-legged, sexually rapacious Christian Devil-iconography of medieval and later Christian art is **directly derived from Pan/Faunus**. This is the most consequential single piece of Greek-Egyptian-pagan visual material to be inverted into Christian polemic. (See Russell 1977 *The Devil*; Cohn 1975 *Europe's Inner Demons*.)

## Iconography
- **Human torso, head with horns and beard, goat-legs and hooves**
- **Panpipe (syrinx)** — multi-reed flute, his signature instrument
- **Shepherd's crook (*lagobolon*)**
- Often crowned with **pine**
- Frequently depicted in pursuit of nymphs, dancing with satyrs, or seated on a rock playing the syrinx

## Disputes
- **Date of Plutarch's "death of Pan" anecdote.** The story is set in Tiberius's reign (14–37 CE) but reported decades later by Plutarch; whether the event is genuine, a literary construction, or a Christian-tinged later insertion is disputed. The dominant view (Borgeaud 1988) is that it is a genuine pagan eschatological anecdote later appropriated by Christians, not Christian fabrication.
- **Etymology of "Pan."** Ancient (Homeric Hymn 19): Pan = *pan* ("all"), since he delights all gods. Modern philology: more likely from a root meaning "shepherd" / "feeder," cognate with Latin *pasco*, "to feed." The ancient "all" etymology is a folk-etymology, but it becomes theologically generative — Hellenistic Pan as "the god of the all," the cosmic pan-deity, anticipates philosophical pantheism.
- **Pan and Christ death-coincidence.** Plutarch's story takes place in Tiberius's reign — the same reign as Jesus's death. The apologetic synchronization is post-hoc, but the temporal proximity has been irresistible to commentators from Eusebius onward.
