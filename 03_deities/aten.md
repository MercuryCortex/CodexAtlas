# Aten

The Aten (Egyptian *itn*) is the visible disc of the sun, treated in pre-Amarna theology as a minor solar concept (one of many epithets for Ra's solar manifestation) and elevated by [[akhenaten]] (~1353–1336 BCE) into the sole worshipped deity of his radical religious reform. The Atenist innovation is twofold: (1) **abstraction** — the Aten is depicted only as the disc itself, never as a person or animal-headed god, with rays descending to the royal family terminating in tiny hands holding ankhs (the unique Amarna iconography); (2) **exclusivity** — Akhenaten suppressed the other Egyptian cults, especially that of Amun-Ra at Thebes, defacing the names of competing gods wherever the regime could reach.

## Mythological role
The Aten has no narrative mythology — no birth, no consort, no children, no combat, no death. This is itself the theological point: the Aten is presented as pure creative and sustaining solar power, the sole self-existent divine principle that brings forth and sustains all life. The [[phase-1-011-great-hymn-to-aten]] is the principal devotional articulation. Whether this constitutes the first true monotheism (Assmann, Breasted), a strong henotheism (Hornung), or a politically motivated solar-cult radicalization (Reeves) is one of the most-debated questions in Egyptology. After Akhenaten's death, the Aten cult was abandoned, Akhenaten's capital at Akhetaten was deserted, the names of Aten were erased from monuments, and Egyptian polytheism resumed under Tutankhamun and the 19th Dynasty. The Aten survives in modern scholarly imagination as a possible influence — direct or distant — on later Hebrew monotheism (Freud, Assmann) and on Hellenistic sun-god monotheism (Hornung).
