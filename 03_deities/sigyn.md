# Sigyn

## Identity
Norse goddess of loyalty and devotion; wife of [[loki]] and one of the most sparsely attested yet emotionally concentrated figures in the Norse pantheon. Only two Eddic stanzas directly concern her — *Völuspá* 35 and *Gylfaginning* 50's prose account — yet her act defines her completely: she stands beside the bound [[loki]], holding a bowl above his face to catch the venom dripping from the serpent fastened above him as punishment for his role in [[baldr]]'s death. When the bowl fills and she must leave to empty it, the venom falls on Loki's face; he writhes in agony, causing earthquakes. Her name (*sigr* + *vín*, "victory-friend" or "victory-woman") carries an irony heavy enough to collapse under its own weight — there is no victory in her vigil, only endurance.

## Earliest attestation
- *Völuspá* (c. 1000 CE), stanza 35: the *völva*'s vision of the cosmic state before Ragnarök mentions Sigyn sitting beside her bound husband with a sorrowful heart — one of the poem's most quietly devastating images.
- *Prose Edda* (Snorri Sturluson, c. 1220), *Gylfaginning* 50: the fullest account of Loki's binding and Sigyn's role. Snorri names her explicitly, describes the bowl, and connects the earthquake tradition to the moments when she leaves to empty it.
- She appears in no further primary narratives. Her sons by Loki (Narfi and Váli) are slain in the binding episode — Váli transformed into a wolf, Narfi's entrails used to bind Loki — a double horror that frames Sigyn's vigil.

## Mythological role
Sigyn's entire mythology is the vigil. After [[loki]] was bound beneath a mountain for engineering [[baldr]]'s death, [[frigg]] having ensured all things swore not to harm Baldr except the mistletoe, and Loki having guided [[odin]]'s blind son Höðr to throw the mistletoe dart — the Æsir bound Loki with the entrails of his son Narfi, transformed into iron fetters, with a serpent fastened above his face to drip venom. Sigyn chose to remain. She holds the bowl. She saves him from full venom-agony for as long as she can stand it — and when she cannot, the earth shakes. The Norse tradition thus explains earthquakes not as tectonic events but as the consequence of a wife's impossible act of compassion reaching its limit. In this she stands apart from the gods who bound her husband — she is the only Aesir-adjacent figure who refuses the community's judgment.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[isis-egyptian]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Isis's patient reassembly of [[osiris]]'s scattered body, her flight through Egypt, her refusal to abandon her murdered husband — the greatest parallel in ancient religion to Sigyn's vigil. Both are wives who stay when the male god is broken. Both operate outside the revenge/justice framework that otherwise structures their mythology. The typological resonance is profound even without genetic connection.
- **[[parvati]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Parvati's devoted perseverance alongside Shiva through his ascetic withdrawal, his periods of inaccessibility, the violence of his nature — a pattern of female divine loyalty outlasting male divine extremity.
- **[[osiris]]** — *scholarly-parallel* (as recipient): as the bound/destroyed god who is sustained by female devotion, Osiris is the counterpart-figure to Loki. Both are divine males whose helplessness is met by female loyalty rather than abandonment.
- **Grail-as-vessel** — *scholarly-parallel* (motif): Sigyn's bowl is one of mythology's most quietly powerful vessel-images — a container whose function is to hold suffering back, to delay catastrophe. It parallels the Grail's function as a vessel of redemption and grace, and the Buddhist offering bowl as a compassion-gesture. The bowl that catches what would otherwise destroy is a rare and resonant mythological object.

## Iconography
No ancient iconographic depictions survive. The bowl is the defining attribute. Modern Norse-revival imagery consistently shows Sigyn kneeling or standing beside the recumbent Loki, arms raised, holding the bowl — a posture of sustained effort. The German painter Christopher Wilhelm Eckersberg (1783–1853) and the Swedish artist Mårten Eskil Winge produced influential 19th-century depictions that established the iconographic convention.

## Disputes
- **Sigyn's parentage and divine status.** She is called *ásynja* (a goddess of the Aesir) by Snorri, but her parentage is nowhere given. She appears to have no independent cult, no festival, no place-name evidence — she exists entirely through her relationship to Loki. Whether she represents an ancient figure or a narrative invention to explain earthquake phenomena is unresolvable.
- **The sons.** Sources are inconsistent about which son is named Narfi and which Váli, and whether Váli is also Loki's son by [[odin]]'s Skadi-episode. The *Prose Edda* presents two sons by Sigyn (Narfi and Váli); the transformation-and-binding uses both.
