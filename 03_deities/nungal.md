# Nungal

## Identity
Nungal is the Sumerian goddess of the **prison-house** — specifically the *é-kur-ra*, "Great House," within the Ekur complex at Nippur — and the divine personification of purgative judicial detention. She is daughter of [[an-sumerian]] and (in some traditions) [[ereshkigal]], consort of Birtum. Her hymn (the *Hymn to Nungal*, Sjöberg 1973) is one of the most theologically distinctive in the Sumerian corpus: it presents her not as a vindictive demon-goddess but as a **purificatory and restorative** divine power — the prison is described as a place where the wicked are purified, broken, and either released remade or kept until their destruction. The hymn balances terror ("my house presses down on the wicked") with maternal restoration ("I am their good guardian"), articulating a remarkably nuanced theology of judicial corrective punishment for ~-2000 BCE. The Akkadian rendering Manungal carries the epithet "merciful," reinforcing the dual character.

## Earliest attestation
- The *Hymn to Nungal* is preserved in Old Babylonian school copies from Nippur and Ur; the composition is presumed Ur III in origin.
- She appears in Ur III administrative texts at Nippur as the recipient of regular offerings.
- The Isin Reform texts of Lipit-Ishtar invoke her as guarantor of the laws — appropriate for a prison-goddess.

## Mythological role
- *Hymn to Nungal (Nungal A)*: the principal source. The text is a first-person hymn in which Nungal describes her own house, her function, the procedures of detention and ordeal, and the theology of corrective punishment. Civil (1993) re-reads several passages and clarifies the legal-procedural vocabulary embedded in the hymn.
- *Trial by river ordeal*: Nungal is associated with the river ordeal as a judicial procedure (the accused is cast into the river; the goddess decides survival). The hymn intersects with the legal-procedural texts of the Lipit-Ishtar and Ur-Nammu codes.
- She is occasionally invoked in lament-literature as the divine witness to violations of justice.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- Nungal is one of the more locally-bound Sumerian deities; her cult does not transfer cleanly to the Akkadian or Babylonian pantheon, though her Akkadian form Manungal is sporadically attested.
- *Scholarly parallel*: comparison with later netherworld-justice figures (e.g., the Egyptian forty-two judges of the Book of the Dead; later Jewish *malakhei chabbalah* "angels of destruction") is occasionally made; these are *scholarly-parallel*, not ancient identifications.

## Iconography
No distinctive iconography is preserved; she appears in seals as a seated goddess in horned crown indistinguishable from other minor goddesses in cultic scenes.

## Disputes
- **Date of the Hymn**. Ur III versus Old Babylonian composition is debated; Sjöberg 1973 favors Ur III, Civil 1993 leaves the question open.
- **Genealogy**. Daughter of An is the dominant tradition; daughter of Ereshkigal appears in some Old Babylonian sources and reflects her netherworld associations.
