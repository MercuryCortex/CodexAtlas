# Óðinn

## Identity
Chief of the Æsir; god of wisdom, poetry, war, death, sorcery, runes, and kingship. Óðinn is the *all-father* — Allföðr — but also a sinister, ambiguous, transgressive figure: he practices *seiðr* (a magic considered womanish), accepts dishonored deaths, betrays his sworn warriors when their time comes, gathers half the slain at his hall Valhǫll in preparation for Ragnarǫk. He is the god of the *intellectual* and ecstatic, the elite and the marginalized; his cult was distinct from Thor's broader popular reach.

## Earliest attestation
Tacitus *Germania* 9 (98 CE) records the Germans worshipping a chief god whom he identifies with Mercury (*interpretatio romana*) — this is universally identified as the deity later named Óðinn / Wōden / Wodan. Subsequent attestation: runic and place-name evidence across Germanic Europe; Old English (Wōden in royal genealogies); Old Saxon (Wodan); abundant Old Norse-Icelandic literary attestation from ~12th c. onward.

## Mythological role
Acquired wisdom at extraordinary cost: gave one eye to Mímir for a drink from the well of wisdom at the root of Yggdrasill; hanged himself on Yggdrasill for nine nights, pierced by his own spear, to win the runes (*Hávamál* 138–141). Took half the heroic dead to Valhǫll, where they fight and feast in preparation for Ragnarǫk. At Ragnarǫk, he is killed by the wolf Fenrir; his son Víðarr avenges him.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **Mercury / Hermes** — *ancient-identification* (Tacitus): Roman *interpretatio* identifies the Germanic chief god with Mercury — apparently on the basis of psychopomp / messenger / trickster functions and possibly on the Wednesday / *dies Mercurii* / *Wōdnesdæg* equivalence in the Germanic week-day calque from Roman.
- **Christ (Hávamál 138 hanging)** — *contested-parallel*: Óðinn's self-sacrifice on Yggdrasill, "myself to myself," for the runes has been compared structurally to the crucifixion. Whether the parallel reflects independent Indo-European cosmic-self-sacrifice tradition, Christian influence on late Eddic poetry, or convergent imagery is debated.

## Iconography
One eye (lost or covered); spear *Gungnir*; ravens on shoulders (*Huginn* "thought" and *Muninn* "memory"); wolves at feet (*Geri* "greedy" and *Freki* "ravenous"); eight-legged horse *Sleipnir*; wanderer disguise — wide-brimmed hat, hood, blue cloak.
