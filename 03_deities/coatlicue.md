# Coatlicue

## Identity
"Serpent-Skirt" — the Aztec earth-mother goddess; mother of Huitzilopochtli, Coyolxauhqui, and the four-hundred Centzon Huitznahua. The colossal *Coatlicue* sculpture excavated 1790 in Mexico City (now Museo Nacional de Antropología) is one of the most iconic surviving Aztec artworks and one of the most theologically dense — her head replaced by two confronted serpents (signifying her decapitation by her own children); a necklace of severed hands and excised human hearts; skirt of serpents; pendulous breasts visible above and below the heart-and-hands necklace. The sculpture is simultaneously horrifying and theologically precise — she is mother (lactating breasts) and devourer (necklace of body parts), the earth that produces life and consumes death.

## Earliest attestation
Mexica state religion; the *Coatlicue* monolith (~15th c.); narrative attestation in Sahagún Book III.

## Mythological role
The Coyolxauhqui-Huitzilopochtli myth: Coatlicue sweeps the Coatepec temple-mountain; a feather descends from heaven and impregnates her; her existing daughter Coyolxauhqui leads her brothers (the 400 Centzon Huitznahua) to kill the mother whose pregnancy they consider shameful; Huitzilopochtli is born fully armed from her womb and slays Coyolxauhqui, dismembering her body and casting her down the mountain. Coatlicue's role: she is the mother who is *almost killed but whose son saves her*; Mexica ritual replayed the cycle (the Coyolxauhqui stone at the foot of Templo Mayor receives the dismembered bodies of sacrificial victims rolled down the temple steps).

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **Tonantzin / Our Lady of Guadalupe** — *folk-syncretism* (contested): the Tepeyac hill where the Virgin of Guadalupe appeared in 1531 to Juan Diego was reportedly the site of a Tonantzin temple. Whether Tonantzin = Coatlicue or a separate goddess is debated; Lafaye (1976) argues for the Coatlicue-Tonantzin-Guadalupe substitution as a deep colonial-religious syncretism; others (Brading 2001) treat Tonantzin and Coatlicue as separate Mexica figures both subsequently substituted by Guadalupe.

## Iconography
The 1790-excavated Coatlicue colossus is the principal image — though so theologically intense that the Spanish colonial government reburied it for being too horrifying, and it was repeatedly re-excavated and re-buried before its current museum placement. Twin serpent-heads (where the human head was severed); necklace of hands and hearts; serpent skirt; eagle-claw feet.
