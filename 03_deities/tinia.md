# Tinia

## Identity
Tinia is the supreme deity of the Etruscan pantheon: sky-god, lord of thunder and lightning, king of the gods, and divine sovereign whose authority was the ultimate sanction behind Etruscan law, augury, and state power. He is the Etruscan equivalent of Greek Zeus and Roman Jupiter, heading the Etruscan Capitoline Triad alongside his consort **[[uni-etruscan]]** and the wisdom-goddess **[[menvra-etruscan]]**. His name appears in Etruscan inscriptions and most significantly on the **Piacenza Liver** — a bronze model of a sheep's liver (c. 100 BCE) used in *haruspicy* (divination from entrails), the most important surviving artifact of Etruscan religious practice. Tinia's sections on the liver occupy the highest position, marking him as the apex of the divine hierarchy.

## Earliest attestation
- Epigraphic attestation: Tinia's name (*Tinia*, *Tin*, *Tins*) appears in Etruscan votive inscriptions from at least the 6th century BCE.
- The **Piacenza Liver** (c. 100 BCE) preserves his name inscribed in the outer rim of the bronze organ model, in a position associated with the highest divine authority in Etruscan divination.
- Etruscan tomb paintings and temple terracottas from c. 600–300 BCE depict a bearded enthroned male in the Zeus-iconographic type, identified with Tinia by context and inscription.

## Mythological role
Tinia's most theologically distinctive attribute is his **triple thunderbolt** — an arrangement unique in the ancient Mediterranean. According to Etruscan doctrine (preserved in Roman sources including Seneca's *Naturales Quaestiones*):
- The **first thunderbolt** Tinia may hurl on his own authority — a warning shot.
- The **second thunderbolt** requires the consent of the **twelve Etruscan gods** (*dei consentes*) — a major action requiring divine council.
- The **third thunderbolt** requires consent of a higher, more mysterious group of superior gods — an action so cosmically significant it overrides even Tinia's sovereign will.

This three-tier model of divine permission is without parallel in Greek or Roman religion and represents a sophisticated Etruscan theology of constrained divine sovereignty. Tinia also presided over **augury** — the interpretation of lightning strikes as divine messages — which the Romans adopted from Etruscan practice (the *haruspices* remained officially Etruscan specialists in Roman religion into the Imperial period).

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[zeus]]** — *ancient-identification*: Tinia is the Etruscan Zeus in both iconography and divine function; Etruscan art borrows the Zeus-thunderbolt-eagle type directly from Greek prototypes encountered through Magna Graecia.
- **[[jupiter]]** — *ancient-identification*: The Roman Capitoline Triad (Jupiter/Juno/Minerva) is a direct inheritance from the Etruscan Capitoline Triad (Tinia/Uni/Menvra); the Capitoline Hill temple was built by Etruscan craftsmen. This is one of the most clearly documented religious transmission events in antiquity.
- **[[dyaus-pita]]** (Vedic) — *scholarly-parallel*: Tinia belongs to the Mediterranean sky-father-as-sovereign pattern that derives from or parallels PIE *Dyēus ph₂tēr.
- **[[teshub]]** (Hittite) — *scholarly-parallel*: Both are storm-god-kings with divine council structures in the Near Eastern and East Mediterranean horizon.
- **[[baal-hadad]]** (Canaanite) — *scholarly-parallel*: Mediterranean storm-sovereign with divine assembly, documented Phoenician-Etruscan contact in the Pyrgi Tablets.

## Iconography
Tinia is depicted in the Greek Zeus-type: a mature bearded male, enthroned, holding the thunderbolt. He wears a toga or mantle. The eagle appears as his symbol. Unlike Greek Zeus who typically holds one thunderbolt, Etruscan iconography sometimes emphasizes the triple-bolt doctrine through grouped lightning motifs.

## Disputes
- **Etymology of "Tinia"**: uncertain; possibly derived from an Etruscan root for "day" or "sky" (paralleling Greek *Dios* genitive of Zeus), but Etruscan is a language isolate and the connection is not linguistically secure.
- **Pre-Greek vs. Hellenized**: debate over whether Tinia was originally a more distinct Etruscan deity who was later fully Hellenized, or whether the Zeus-type iconography represents a genuine indigenous sky-father tradition. The three-thunderbolt doctrine — without Greek parallel — suggests genuine Etruscan theological elaboration beyond simple Hellenization.
