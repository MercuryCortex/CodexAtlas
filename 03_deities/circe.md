# Circe

## Identity

Circe (Greek **Κίρκη**, *Kírkē*) is the goddess-enchantress of the island **Aeaea** in the *Odyssey* — daughter of [[helios]] the sun and the Oceanid Perse (Od. 10.138-139), sister of Aeëtes king of Colchis (and thus aunt of Medea) and — in later tradition — of Pasiphae. Homer is unambiguous about her divinity: she is *theos*, the "dread goddess with human speech" (*deinē theos audēessa*, Od. 10.136), and *polypharmakos*, "of many drugs" (10.276). She is the earliest extended portrait of a **pharmakis** — worker of transformative plant-drugs — in surviving Greek literature, and the figure whose reception history Yarnall (1994) traces as the archetype of the Western enchantress. Nor is she only literary: Roman colonists at **Circeii** (Monte Circeo, Latium), where her island came to be localized, maintained an actual cult — Cicero notes that "our colonists at Circeii" worship Circe devoutly (*De Natura Deorum* 3.48), and Strabo describes a shrine of Circe with an altar of Athena and a cup said to be Odysseus's on the Circaeum promontory (5.3.6). The Atlas types her as a deity on both grounds: Homeric divinity and attested cult. This node also covers **Aeaea** itself, which has no separate node; Homer places the island not in the west but by "the risings of the sun" (Od. 12.3-4) — the Italian localization is a later, secondary tradition.

## In the Odyssey

Circe dominates the center of the *Odyssey* (Books 10-12), and her episode falls into two sharply different halves — the dangerous goddess, then the indispensable adviser.

**The drugged kykeon and the transformation (Od. 10.210-347).** Odysseus's scouting party finds a house of polished stone in a wood, ringed by wolves and mountain lions that fawn like dogs — men she has drugged, or beasts made tame (10.212-219 — the text is ambiguous and the ambiguity is old). Within, a goddess singing at her loom. She brings the men in and serves them a **kykeon** — Pramnian wine stirred with barley, cheese, and yellow honey, into which she mixes "baneful drugs" (*pharmaka lygra*, 10.234-236); a blow of her wand (*rhabdos*, 10.238) completes the change, and the crew are penned as swine, "their minds unchanged" (10.239-240) — the detail that makes the scene horror rather than farce. Odysseus, going to their rescue, is met by [[hermes-greek]], who gives him the apotropaic herb **moly** — black of root, milk-white of flower, "hard for mortal men to dig, but the gods can do all things" (10.302-306) — often called the first magical plant in Western literature — its occult nature disclosed by a god. Proof against her drug, Odysseus draws his sword; Circe, astonished, invites him to her bed, and he refuses until she swears the great oath of the gods to work no further harm (10.343-346). She restores the crew — younger and fairer than before (10.395-396) — and the company remains on Aeaea a full year (10.467-469).

**The mistress of the rite (Od. 10.487-540; 12.37-141).** When Odysseus asks leave to go home, Circe redirects the entire epic: he must first consult the shade of the Theban seer Teiresias in the house of Hades (10.490-495). She then dictates, in exact ritual detail, the **Nekyia**: the route across Ocean to the grove of Persephone (10.508-512), the pit a cubit square, the triple libation of milk-and-honey, sweet wine, and water, the sprinkled barley, the vows to the dead, and the black ram and black ewe whose blood the shades must drink (10.517-537) — the protocol Odysseus executes to the letter in Book 11, the foundational Greek underworld-consultation (see [[odysseus]]). A wind she sends carries the ship to Ocean's edge (10.507; 11.6-8). After the return — and the burial of Elpenor, who had died on her roof (12.8-15) — she debriefs Odysseus by night and delivers the second great briefing: the Sirens, the Planctae, Scylla and Charybdis, and the fatal cattle of Helios (12.37-141). Circe is thus the *Odyssey*'s dispenser of the knowledge of the world's edge: the goddess who knows the way to the dead and past the monsters, and tells it.

## In the wider Greek and Roman tradition

Hesiod's *Theogony* gives her genealogy (Perseis bore her to Helios, with Aeëtes, 956-957) and, in its closing catalogue, her sons by Odysseus — **Agrius and Latinus**, rulers of the Tyrsenians (1011-1014), the beginning of Circe's long entanglement with Italy. The Epic-Cycle *Telegony* (lost; known from Proclus's summary) made her son **Telegonus** unwittingly kill his father Odysseus, then marry Penelope while Telemachus marries Circe — the tradition also responsible for a suspected Telegonus-interpolation in the *Theogony* text. In Apollonius Rhodius's *Argonautica* (4.659-752) she purifies Jason and Medea for the murder of Apsyrtus — Circe as mistress of purification rite, the older rite-knowledge of the Odyssey redeployed. Roman tradition fixed Aeaea at the promontory of **Circeii** (Monte Circeo) with the cult noted above, and Ovid's *Metamorphoses* (13.898-14.440) made her the jealous transformer of Scylla and of Picus — the vindictive "witch" of later European reception, a marked demotion from Homer's goddess (Yarnall 1994 traces the whole arc).

## Cross-tradition significance

Circe stands at the Greek end of two of the best-argued Near Eastern parallels in Homeric scholarship — both **structural-literary parallels with a transmission case, not cognates**.

**The route-giver at the world's edge (Siduri).** In *Gilgamesh* X the divine ale-wife **Siduri**, keeper of a house by the shore at the edge of the world, receives the wandering hero and directs him on the crossing of the waters of death to Utanapishtim; in *Odyssey* 10 the island-goddess Circe receives the wandering hero and directs him on the crossing of Ocean to the dead. **Martin West** (*The East Face of Helicon*, 1997) and **Walter Burkert** (*The Orientalizing Revolution*, 1992) treat the Circe/Calypso-Siduri correspondence as one of the strongest single exhibits for Bronze-Age-to-Archaic Near Eastern literary transmission into Greek epic, probably via Anatolian intermediaries — the same dossier that links Odysseus-to-Teiresias with Gilgamesh-to-Utanapishtim (see [[odysseus]], [[gilgamesh-king]]).

**The goddess who turns lovers into animals (Ishtar).** In *Gilgamesh* VI the hero refuses the love of [[ishtar-akkadian|Ishtar]] by reciting the fates of her former lovers — transformed into wolf and mole; Circe's house is ringed by drugged wolves and lions, her cup turns men to swine, and Odysseus will not lie with her until bound by oath — Hermes' warning is precisely that her bed is a trap for the unprotected (Od. 10.296-301). West (1997) draws the comparison as part of the same Near Eastern background.

The honest framing: the **Aeaea-to-Rome line is secure reception** (Hesiod's Latinus, the *Telegony*, Circeii, Ovid — a documented literary and cultic chain), while the **Mesopotamian parallels are argued transmission** — mainstream since West and Burkert, but reconstructions resting on structural resemblance, with cautious scholars (Dowden among them) preferring to leave open independent convergence on the widespread type-scene of the wandering hero and the dangerous divine hostess. No linguistic cognate connects Circe to any Near Eastern figure. Finally, her **kykeon** ties her lexically to the Eleusinian ritual drink ([[eleusinian-kykeon]]): the same word, the same stirred-mixture preparation, entirely different contexts — a semantic-field link the entheogen literature (Wasson-Hofmann-Ruck 1978, Tier-2) has pressed further than the evidence strictly allows.

## Disputes

- **Goddess or witch?** Homer calls Circe *theos* throughout, and the Circeii cult shows real worship; the "witch" is a product of later reception (already hardening in Ovid, complete by the medieval and Renaissance moralizations — Yarnall 1994). The Atlas follows the primary evidence: deity, with the enchantress-reception noted as reception.
- **The name.** Ancient and modern attempts link *Kírkē* to *kírkos*, "hawk/falcon" (so already ancient etymologizing); no modern etymology commands consensus. The name is not connectable to any Near Eastern name — the cross-tradition parallels are structural only.
- **What was the drug?** The pharmacological reading of the episode — Circe's *pharmakon* as an anticholinergic, datura-type delirium; **moly** as the snowdrop *Galanthus nivalis*, whose galantamine is a genuine physiological antidote (an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor) — was proposed in a real medical journal (Plaitakis and Duvoisin 1983) and is much cited; it is a plausible retro-diagnosis, not a demonstrated identification, and classicists generally treat moly as a mythical plant.
- **Transmission vs convergence.** As above: the Siduri and Ishtar parallels are the mainstream West-Burkert transmission case, contested as unproven; the parallel type-scene could in principle arise independently. The Atlas records both positions.
- **Telegonus.** The *Theogony*'s Telegonus line is widely suspected as an interpolation harmonizing Hesiod with the *Telegony*; this node attributes Agrius and Latinus to Hesiod (Th. 1011-1014) and Telegonus to the Epic Cycle.
