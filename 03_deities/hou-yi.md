# Hou Yi (后羿)

## Identity
Hou Yi ("Lord Yi") is the Chinese **divine archer** — the supreme cultural hero of Chinese solar mythology, celebrated for shooting down nine of the ten suns that scorched the ancient earth. He embodies the archetype of the **tragic divine champion**: gifted beyond all others, servant of the gods, savior of humanity, ultimately exiled, betrayed, and destroyed. His story is the Chinese Prometheus — the figure who restores the cosmic order at the cost of his own divine standing.

## Earliest attestation
- Primary source: **Huainanzi** (*Huái Nán Zǐ*, 淮南子), compiled c. **139 BCE** by Liu An, King of Huainan — contains the fullest narrative of Hou Yi and the ten suns.
- Also attested in: **Shanhaijing** (Classic of Mountains and Seas, compiled c. 4th–1st c. BCE), **Chuci** (Songs of the South, c. 4th–3rd c. BCE, contains earlier fragmentary references to "Yi the Archer").
- The Chuci references predate the Huainanzi and suggest the myth was already established in the 4th–3rd century BCE.

## Mythological role
**The ten suns**: In the beginning, ten suns — children of the solar deity Dijun and his consort Xihe — took turns crossing the sky. When all ten appeared simultaneously, the heat scorched the earth and threatened all life. The supreme deity Dijun sent Hou Yi (then a divine being in heaven) to frighten the suns back into order. Instead, Hou Yi shot down nine of the ten, leaving only one sun. Dijun, enraged by the killing of his solar children, **exiled Hou Yi and Chang'e to earth**, stripping them of their immortality.

**Monster-slaying**: On earth, Hou Yi performed further heroic feats — defeating the wind deity Fei Lian, the enormous boar Zao Shou, the nine-headed serpent Xiu She, and other monsters that plagued humanity.

**The elixir**: Hou Yi petitioned the **Queen Mother of the West** ([[xi-wangmu]]) for an immortality elixir, which she granted. Before he could use it, his wife [[chang-e]] drank it and ascended to the moon.

**Betrayal and death**: Hou Yi trained the archer Fengmeng, who grew to surpass his master and, out of jealousy or ambition, killed Hou Yi with a blow from a **peach-wood club** — the only material capable of harming the divine archer. A tragic end: killed by his own student, using a mundane weapon that circumvented his supreme skill.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[apollo]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: both are divine archers who define the archetype; both carry the tragic dimension of divine skill — Apollo's arrows bring plague as well as music; Hou Yi's arrows save the world but doom him. The divine archer as beautiful, excellent, and dangerous.
- **[[indra]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: parallel solar-restoration myth — Indra defeats [[vritra]] who blocks the sun's waters; Hou Yi shoots down the excess suns. Both are warrior-saviors whose triumph is ambivalent. No transmission; convergent solar-myth structure.
- **[[prometheus]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: the divine servant who performs the supreme rescue of the world and is punished by exile and suffering. Prometheus brings fire stolen from the gods; Hou Yi kills the gods' children (the suns) to save the world. Both are tragic civilization-founders.
- **[[sun-wukong]]** — *folk-syncretism (loose)*: both are supremely capable divine figures who overreach, are punished/exiled, and whose power is ultimately constrained by a higher authority. Both belong to the Chinese tradition of the contained champion.

## MASSIVE WIN: the solar-excess myth

The "too many suns / the sun goes wrong" crisis is a cross-cultural mythological category:
- **China**: Hou Yi shoots down nine of ten suns (Huainanzi)
- **Greece**: Phaethon drives the solar chariot off course; Zeus corrects with a thunderbolt (*Metamorphoses* II)
- **Vedic India**: Indra defeats Vritra, who has blocked the cosmic waters and plunged the world into darkness (Rigveda 1.32)
- **Mesoamerica**: multiple sun-creation stories in the Aztec Five Suns cosmogony involve successive solar failures corrected by divine intervention
- The pattern: the solar order fails or exceeds its proper boundary; a divine champion corrects it; the correction costs the champion something

## Iconography
Depicted as a heroic male archer, typically shown drawing a great bow toward the sky, with suns falling. Later artistic tradition sometimes shows him in Han-dynasty military dress. His peach-wood club (the instrument of his death) occasionally appears as a sinister secondary attribute.
