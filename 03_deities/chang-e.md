# Chang'e (嫦娥)

## Identity
Chang'e (also Heng'e) is the Chinese **moon goddess** — the most widely recognized female deity in Chinese folk religion and the central figure of the **Mid-Autumn Festival (中秋節)**, the second-largest Chinese festival. Her name is inseparable from the moon itself: in Chinese, "Chang'e Ben Yue" (嫦娥奔月, "Chang'e flees to the moon") is the canonical phrase for her myth. She inhabits the **Palace of the Moon (Guanghan Palace)** accompanied only by the **Jade Rabbit (玉兔)**, who pounds the elixir of immortality in a mortar. Her defining attribute is luminous, solitary beauty — the moon as the goddess's loneliness made visible.

## Earliest attestation
- First literary attestation: **Huainanzi** (*Huái Nán Zǐ*, 淮南子), compiled c. **139 BCE** under Liu An, King of Huainan. Chapter 6 contains the earliest surviving narrative of Chang'e stealing and drinking the immortality elixir and ascending to the moon.
- Iconographic attestations: Han dynasty (206 BCE – 220 CE) tomb paintings and bronze mirrors include moon-goddess imagery with the jade rabbit and mortar, confirming the myth's pictorial currency during the Han period.

## Mythological role
The core myth has three variant versions, all attested from the Han period or later:
1. **Theft-from-ambition**: Chang'e steals the immortality elixir granted to her husband [[hou-yi]] by the Queen Mother of the West ([[xi-wangmu]]) and drinks it herself, ascending to the moon as punishment or consequence.
2. **Theft-to-prevent-theft**: The villain Peng Meng (or Fengmeng) attempts to steal the elixir while Hou Yi is away; Chang'e drinks it herself to prevent his acquisition, ascending to the moon as the only escape.
3. **Accident**: Chang'e drinks the elixir accidentally, unknowing of its effect.

In all versions she ascends alone to the moon, where she remains in celestial exile — the archetypal figure of beauty, immortality, and irrecoverable isolation. The **Jade Rabbit** pounds immortality medicine in a mortar beside her; a **cassia tree** (桂花樹) grows in the lunar palace. The **Mid-Autumn Festival** (15th day of the 8th lunar month) centers on moon-gazing and offering mooncakes — devotional recognition of Chang'e's presence.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[selene]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: the lunar goddess who inhabits the moon's realm; both embody celestial, isolated feminine beauty. Selene's love for the sleeping Endymion mirrors Chang'e's solitary longing. No transmission; parallel structure.
- **[[tsukuyomi]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: both moon deities inhabit a realm structurally separated from the solar deity by a foundational quarrel or transgression; both embody the moon's alterity from the solar order.
- **[[mama-quilla]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: both are feminine moon deities whose worship anchors major cyclical festivals governed by the lunar calendar. No transmission; parallel convergence.
- **[[chandra]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Vedic moon deity whose domain cluster (immortality-substance, cyclical celestial dwelling, feminine-associative qualities) parallels Chang'e's. Soma and the immortality elixir serve analogous cosmological roles.

## MASSIVE WIN: the rabbit-in-the-moon

The **rabbit visible in the moon** — a cross-cultural pareidolia reading of the lunar surface — generates independent mythologies across traditions that had no contact:
- **Chinese**: the Jade Rabbit (玉兔) pounds immortality elixir for Chang'e
- **Mesoamerican (Maya/Aztec)**: the rabbit is explicitly visible in the moon — the Aztec creator Quetzalcoatl places the rabbit in the moon; Maya moon iconography shows a rabbit scribing
- **Buddhist Jataka tales**: the *Śaśajātaka* tells of a rabbit who self-immolates for Indra; Indra places the rabbit's image in the moon in its honor; this tale spread from India through Central Asia to Japan and China
- The rabbit-in-the-moon is thus a case of convergent pareidolia generating independent mythological elaboration — plus one documented transmission chain (Buddhist Jataka → East Asia)

## Iconography
Chang'e appears as a beautiful woman in flowing white or silver robes, often depicted floating upward toward the moon or standing in the lunar palace. The **jade rabbit** and **mortar** are constant attributes. The **cassia tree** appears in many depictions. Han mirrors frequently show the moon goddess with a crouching rabbit and toad (another lunar resident — the three-legged toad *Chán Chú*).
