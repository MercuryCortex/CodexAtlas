# Sun Wukong

## Identity
Sun Wukong — the Monkey King — is the central protagonist of the 16th-century Chinese novel *Xiyouji* (Journey to the West, attributed to Wu Cheng'en, ~1592), and one of the most beloved supernatural figures in Chinese civilization. He combines trickster irreverence, martial invincibility, and the capacity for spiritual transformation into a single mythic career arc: born from a cosmogonic stone, he becomes king of the monkeys, acquires immortality through Daoist cultivation, storms heaven and declares himself "Great Sage Equal to Heaven," is defeated by the Buddha, imprisoned under Five Elements Mountain for five hundred years, and finally earns true liberation by escorting the monk Tang Sanzang ([[avalokitesvara]]'s protégé) to India for the Buddhist scriptures. He ends the journey as "Victorious Fighting Buddha" — having completed a trickster's ultimate trajectory from cosmic chaos to realized enlightenment through ordeal.

## Earliest attestation
Earlier folk antecedents circulate in oral tradition and in a Yuan-dynasty zaju play (*Pŭtōng Wénzì*, c. 13th c.). The canonical text is Wu Cheng'en's *Xiyouji* (c. 1592, Ming dynasty). The historical pilgrim Xuanzang's actual journey to India (629–645 CE) provides the narrative skeleton that the supernatural story-tradition grew around.

## Mythological role
Sun Wukong's career falls into three mythological acts:

**1. The Stone Birth and Cosmic Ambition.** He is born fully formed from a magic stone on Flower-Fruit Mountain — a cosmogonic birth outside normal biology, parallel to Mithras's birth from rock. He learns the Daoist arts of immortality (seventy-two transformations, cloud-somersault covering 108,000 li) from the Patriarch Subodhi. After stealing the Dragon King's magic iron pillar (which becomes his staff), plundering the peaches of immortality, and eating the pills of immortality in Laozi's furnace, he challenges the Jade Emperor himself. The *Jade Emperor* — [[jade-emperor]] — must invoke the Buddha to subdue him.

**2. Imprisonment and Ordeal.** The Buddha traps him beneath Five Elements Mountain for five centuries. [[guanyin]] visits him, offering release if he agrees to escort the monk Tang Sanzang westward and wear the golden headband of discipline. This is the trickster's binding — his freedom constrained in service of a spiritual mission.

**3. Pilgrimage and Buddhahood.** As Tang Sanzang's chief disciple and protector, Sun Wukong battles demons, exposes illusions, and performs 81 ordeals across the journey to India. His chief rival in the journey narratives is Erlang Shen — [[erlang-shen]] — who subdues him early (before Guanyin pacifies him), and whose divine eye pierces Sun Wukong's transformations. The journey culminates in his recognition as a fully realized Buddha — the trickster's enlightenment earned through suffering and service, not transcendence of conflict.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **[[anansi]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: West African/Caribbean spider-trickster; both use cunning against power, both inhabit animal-human-divine liminal space. No historical contact; structural convergence.
- **[[loki]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Norse shapeshifter who defies divine order and is ultimately constrained. Both are heaven-rebels punished and eventually integrated; both trigger the cosmological crisis that reshapes the world.
- **[[narada]]** — *scholarly-parallel*: Hindu sage-trickster who moves freely between cosmic realms, instigating transformation through irreverence; parallels Sun Wukong's liminal cosmological mobility.
- **[[jade-emperor]]** — chief antagonist in the heaven-rebellion arc; later becomes uneasy ally.
- **[[guanyin]]** — spiritual mentor who mediates his release and mission; the compassion that redirects his power.
- **[[amitabha]]** — the goal: Pure Land Buddhism's supreme Buddha, whose realm the pilgrimage ultimately gestures toward.
- **[[xi-wangmu]]** — Queen Mother of the West; her peaches of immortality are among the first things Sun Wukong steals.

## Iconography
Sun Wukong appears with the body of a monkey, often dressed as a warrior-pilgrim, carrying the golden staff (Ruyi Jingu Bang — capable of shrinking to a pin or expanding to a pillar that holds up the sky), wearing the golden headband, and often depicted leaping through clouds. Modern depictions range from Peking opera masks (white face, acrobatic costume) to animated film versions (Journey to the West adaptations from 1960s–present).

## Disputes
Whether the novel is primarily a Buddhist allegory (the pilgrimage as inner cultivation), a Daoist text, a Confucian political satire, or simply popular adventure fiction is actively debated. The stone-birth motif is sometimes linked to shamanic traditions of Siberian origin. Some scholars identify a South Asian or Indian Hanuman-influence on the Monkey King archetype (both are monkey-warriors serving a holy mission), though direct transmission is unproven.
