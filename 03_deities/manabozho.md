# Manabozho (Nanabozho)

## Identity

**Manabozho** (also *Nanabozho*, *Wenabozho*, *Winabojo*) is the Great Hare and supreme culture hero of the Anishinaabe (Ojibwe/Chippewa) people and the broader Algonquian-speaking nations of the Great Lakes region. He is simultaneously trickster, transformer, thief, healer, and the reshaper of the earth after the primordial flood — a figure whose myth cycle spans the full arc of creation, the origin of death, the gift of fire, and the establishment of the present world. Manabozho occupies among the Algonquian nations a position comparable to Coyote in the Southwest or Raven on the Pacific Northwest Coast: the necessary transgressor through whom the conditions of human existence are established.

## Earliest attestation

- The Manabozho cycle belongs to living oral tradition reaching back millennia; the Great Lakes region shows continuous Indigenous occupation for over 10,000 years.
- First extensive European documentation: Henry Rowe Schoolcraft, *Algic Researches* (1839) — the foundational collection of Ojibwe oral tradition; Schoolcraft later expanded in *Myth of Hiawatha* (1856).
- Johann Georg Kohl, *Kitchi-Gami* (1860) — German independent documentation of the Ojibwe Manabozho cycle.
- Schoolcraft's romanticized material directly inspired Henry Wadsworth Longfellow's *Song of Hiawatha* (1855), making Manabozho (under the name Hiawatha) one of the most widely read Native American mythological figures in 19th-century Euro-American literary culture.

## Mythological role

**Fire-theft**: Manabozho travels to the east and steals fire from a powerful spirit (in some versions, an old man; in others, a supernatural being who hoards fire), escaping pursuit through shapeshifting. This episode places him in the global fire-theft archetype alongside Prometheus (Greek), Maui (Polynesian), and the Coyote fire-theft traditions (California/Great Basin).

**The great flood and Earth-Diver creation**: After a conflict with the underwater serpent powers (*Mishibizhii* / underwater panthers), Manabozho is chased by a great flood that rises to cover the earth. He climbs to a raft (or a floating log or mountain) with the animals. He sends various animals diving to the bottom of the waters to bring up mud — the **Earth-Diver** motif. Most fail or drown; finally the muskrat (in many versions) succeeds, bringing up a tiny piece of mud in its paw. Manabozho breathes on it, and the earth grows from that mud into the present world. This Earth-Diver cosmogony is one of the most widely distributed Native American creation motifs (attested from the Algonquian east through the Plains and into Siberian traditions, suggesting very deep antiquity).

**Healing and the Midewiwin**: Manabozho is credited with establishing (or revealing) the Midewiwin — the Grand Medicine Society of the Ojibwe — and with introducing medicinal plants and healing knowledge. This dimension gives him a role parallel to Asclepius (healing culture hero) alongside his trickster role.

**Death**: In one of the most consequential episodes, Manabozho's wolf-companion or younger brother is killed by the underwater spirits. Manabozho's grief and revenge cycle establishes the condition of death — and his brother becomes the first being to travel to the Land of the Dead, creating the path that all souls follow.

## Cross-tradition identifications

- **[[coyote-navajo]]** — *structural-parallel*: the two most documented Native American trickster-culture-hero figures; both drive creation narratives through transgressive action; both introduce death, fire, and the current shape of the world through a combination of gift and catastrophe.
- **[[raven-trickster]]** — *structural-parallel*: Raven (Pacific Northwest) is the closest geographic parallel; both are shapeshifting trickster-creators of comparable narrative scope; both steal luminous/energetic gifts for humanity.
- **[[prometheus]]** — *structural-parallel* (fire-theft archetype): Manabozho's fire-theft from a spirit being is structurally identical to Prometheus's theft from Olympus. Frazer's global survey (*Myths of the Origin of Fire*, 1930) classifies both in the same fire-theft pattern.
- **[[loki]]** — *structural-parallel*: both are shapeshifting tricksters who navigate between divine and mortal worlds; both have episodes where transgressive action sets the conditions for death; both are helpers and disruptors in equal measure.
- **[[anansi]]** — *structural-parallel*: Anansi (West African spider-trickster) is the archetypal trickster who obtains something essential through deception — structurally parallel to Manabozho's theft of fire and knowledge.
- **[[maui-polynesian]]** — *structural-parallel*: Maui steals fire and snares the sun; Manabozho steals fire and reshapes the earth. Both are the primary culture-heroes of expansive myth cycles covering creation, fire, the origin of death, and the present shape of the world.
- **[[bergelmir]]** — *structural-parallel* (flood survival): Manabozho's Earth-Diver flood narrative (survival on raft + rebuilding from tiny piece of mud) parallels Bergelmir's survival of the blood-flood after Ymir's killing. Both establish a renewed world from the ruins of destruction.
- **[[gitche-manitou]]** — *contextual*: the Algonquian concept of the Great Spirit (Gitche Manitou) provides the cosmological context within which Manabozho operates — he mediates between the Great Spirit's world and the human world.
- **[[sky-woman-haudenosaunee]]** — *contextual*: the nearby Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) Sky Woman tradition attests the same Earth-Diver motif (animals dive for mud); Manabozho's flood narrative represents an Anishinaabe parallel to this pan-Great-Lakes cosmogonic pattern.
- **[[wakan-tanka]]** — *contextual*: the broader Plains and Woodland sense of the sacred pervading all things frames Manabozho as a specific embodiment of generative creative power.

## Iconography

- **Great Hare form**: Manabozho most frequently appears in hare/rabbit form in iconographic representations — though many Ojibwe-tradition visual artists depict him in human form as well
- **Transformation sequences**: images showing mid-transformation moments (human-to-animal) are characteristic
- **Earth-Diver scenes**: Manabozho on a raft with animals is a recurring artistic motif in Anishinaabe traditional arts

## The Longfellow problem

Schoolcraft's romanticized and substantially altered adaptations of Manabozho narratives, which Longfellow used as the basis for *The Song of Hiawatha* (1855), created enormous 19th-century Euro-American interest in this figure — but under the name of a completely different historical person (the Haudenosaunee diplomat Hiawatha) and stripped of the trickster dimension. Schoolcraft merged separate traditions; Longfellow then further romanticized. Contemporary Anishinaabe scholarship distinguishes the literary "Hiawatha" sharply from the living Manabozho/Nanabozho tradition.
