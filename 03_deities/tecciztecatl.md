# Tecciztecatl

## Identity

Tecciztecatl ("the one from the place of the sea-snail / conch") is the Aztec god who becomes the **moon** in the creation of the Fifth Sun. Wealthy and proud, adorned with gold and quetzal plumes, he is the deliberate counterpart to the humble [[nanahuatzin]]: where Nanahuatzin's poverty and courage win him the sun, Tecciztecatl's riches and fear earn him only the lesser light.

## Mythological role

In the *Florentine Codex* account of the world's re-creation at Teotihuacan, the gods build a sacrificial fire and ask who will leap in to become the new sun. The rich Tecciztecatl volunteers first and offers costly treasures in his penance (where Nanahuatzin can offer only thorns and his own blood). But when the moment comes, **Tecciztecatl approaches the flames four times and four times draws back in terror**. The humble Nanahuatzin then throws himself in without hesitation and becomes the sun; shamed, Tecciztecatl follows into the embers and rises as a second sun, equally bright. The gods, displeased that the coward should shine as brilliantly as the brave, **hurl a rabbit into Tecciztecatl's face**, dimming him — which is why the moon is paler than the sun, and why the Mesoamericans saw the figure of a **rabbit** in the moon.

## Cross-tradition significance

Tecciztecatl's myth is the Aztec **aetiology of the moon** and of the sun–moon difference, framed as a moral contrast between courage and cowardice, humility and pride. The "rabbit in the moon" he explains is a striking parallel to the lunar-rabbit traditions of East Asia (where a rabbit pounds the elixir of immortality on the moon), one of several intriguing Pacific-rim resonances. As the moon paired with the self-sacrificed sun, he belongs to the cross-tradition complex of **paired solar and lunar deities** born together at the world's beginning.
