# Lono

## Identity
Hawaiian god of agriculture, rain, peace, and the *Makahiki* festival — the four-month annual season (approximately late October to February) when war was prohibited, tribute was collected, and athletic-religious celebrations occurred. Lono is the god whose absence is wartime and whose presence is harvest-peace; he is invoked at planting and harvest of *'uala* (sweet potato), *kalo* (taro), and other crops.

## Earliest attestation
Hawaiian oral tradition; documented in Malo (1838); Cook journals (1779).

## Mythological role
In one narrative, Lono came down to earth on a rainbow, married a chiefess (Kaikilani-Ali'i-Wahine-o-Puna), discovered her unfaithfulness, killed her, and in remorse left Hawai'i — promising to return on a great floating *heiau* (temple). The Makahiki festival commemorates his expected return.

## Cross-tradition identifications
- **Captain James Cook (1779)** — *folk-syncretism* (historical, contested): Cook arrived at Kealakekua Bay in January 1779 *during* the Makahiki festival, and his great ship resembled a floating *heiau*. Hawaiian sources and some scholarly readings (Sahlins 1985) hold that Cook was identified with the returning Lono and accorded divine honors; on his return after the Makahiki had ended (February 1779), the identification was untenable, leading to his killing. Obeyesekere (1992) contested this reading, arguing Hawaiians were not so credulous — initiating one of the most-discussed methodological debates in 20th-c. anthropology (the Sahlins-Obeyesekere debate). The current consensus (Borofsky 1997 review) accepts that some Lono-Cook identification occurred but is appropriately cautious about its precise character.

## Iconography
The Makahiki staff — a long pole with a cross-piece at the top from which white bark cloth (*kapa*) was hung, ferns and bird-skins attached. The staff was carried around each island during the Makahiki, collecting tribute. It strikingly resembles a ship's mast with sail — hence the Cook-as-Lono possibility.
