# Ekeko

The Aymara-origin **god of abundance** — one of the most continuously and publicly venerated pre-Columbian Andean deities in modern practice. Ekeko is depicted as a **small jolly hunchback man** carrying household goods on his back: miniatures of bread, money, livestock, building materials, even tiny university diplomas. The cult survived the Spanish Conquest substantially intact — Ekeko's pre-Christian origin is uncontested; his post-Christian flourishing is one of the clearest cases of an Andean deity persisting through colonial Catholicism without significant syncretism.

**The Alasitas festival (La Paz, Bolivia — every 24 January).** The principal Ekeko ritual: vendors at the Alasitas market sell **miniatures** of every conceivable object the participant might want for the coming year (cars, houses, banknotes, food, diplomas, marriage certificates). The participant buys the miniature representing the desired item, has it blessed (often by both an Aymara priest *and* a Catholic priest, sequentially), and places it on or next to a household Ekeko figure. The participant must offer Ekeko cigarettes (placed in his open mouth) and alcohol throughout the year. The miniature's blessing makes the real-life version manifest by year's end.

The Alasitas festival is recognized by **UNESCO as Intangible Cultural Heritage** (inscribed 2017) — among the few pre-Columbian Andean religious practices to receive that recognition. Ekeko's cult is therefore not only continuous but officially-validated continuity, an unusual position for any pre-Conquest American deity.
