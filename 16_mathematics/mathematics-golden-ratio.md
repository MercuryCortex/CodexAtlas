# The golden ratio (φ)

The proportion φ = (1 + √5) / 2 ≈ 1.618... — defined geometrically as the division of a line such that the whole is to the larger part as the larger part is to the smaller. First given a rigorous mathematical treatment in Euclid's *Elements* Book VI, Definition 3 ("extreme and mean ratio") and Book VI Proposition 30 (the construction). The Pythagorean school appears to have discovered it earlier through the geometry of the regular pentagon, in which the ratio of diagonal to side is exactly φ.

## The pentagonal connection

The regular pentagon and the pentagram are saturated with φ: the ratio of the pentagram's points to its inner pentagon, the side-to-diagonal ratio of the pentagon, the recursive nesting of pentagons inside pentagrams — all are φ-relationships. This is the probable Pythagorean discovery context, and probably the source of the pentagram's status as the Pythagorean *health-symbol* (*hugieia*) — the figure is the geometric expression of the principle that the same proportion governs every level of a nested system.

## Pacioli and the Renaissance

Luca Pacioli's *De divina proportione* (1509), illustrated by Leonardo da Vinci, is the foundational Renaissance text — and gives the proportion its theological name. Pacioli argues that the ratio is *divine* on five grounds: (i) it is one, like God; (ii) it is one ratio defined by three terms (the trinitarian analogy); (iii) it cannot be expressed as a rational number, like the unknowability of God; (iv) it is always *in itself* — every part contains the whole proportion; (v) it gives existence to celestial virtues through the dodecahedron (which is built from pentagons and is associated with the cosmos in *Timaeus*).

## The φ-overclaim problem

The popular literature has attributed the golden ratio to the Egyptian pyramids, the Parthenon, the medieval cathedrals, the proportions of the human body, the spiral of the nautilus shell, and many other settings. Herz-Fischler (1998) demonstrates that almost all of these claims are unsupported by careful measurement — the historical-architectural use of φ is far less common than popular sources suggest. *Genuine* historical-architectural φ-use is securely attested only from the Italian Renaissance onward (Pacioli's circle, some Counter-Reformation church proportions). The Parthenon claim, in particular, can be made to fit φ only by selecting boundary-lines flexibly. The mathematical and aesthetic interest of φ is real; its alleged-universal-presence in classical sacred architecture is largely a 19th-20th c. construction.
