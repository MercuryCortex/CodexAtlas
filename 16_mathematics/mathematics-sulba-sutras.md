The **Sulba Sūtras** (Sanskrit: *sulba* = "cord, string"; *sūtra* = "thread, aphorism") are a collection of Vedic texts dated c. 800–200 BCE that contain the earliest Indian mathematical corpus — and its most striking feature is that every geometric theorem in them was generated by a single practical requirement: the precise construction of **fire altars** (*agni*) for Vedic ritual sacrifice.

## Ritual Context: The Geometry of Fire

Vedic ritual ([[tradition-vedic]]) required the construction of fire altars in geometrically precise forms — square, circle, falcon-shape, chariot-wheel — and the rules governing their construction were preserved in the Sulba Sūtras as part of the *Śrauta Sūtra* literature, the technical manuals for solemn sacrifice.

The stakes were theological: an incorrectly constructed altar could invalidate the sacrifice and produce ritual harm rather than benefit. Geometric precision was not an intellectual virtue but a **religious obligation**. The mathematical precision of the Sulba Sūtras is therefore a by-product of cultic anxiety — and the same anxiety that drove Babylonian scribes to develop astronomical precision to ensure correct festival timing.

Three of the most important Sulba Sūtras are attributed to **Baudhayana** (c. 800 BCE), **Āpastamba** (c. 600 BCE), and **Kātyāyana** (c. 200 BCE).

## Baudhayana's Theorem — The Prior Claim

The most celebrated passage in the Baudhayana Sulba Sūtra (1.9) reads:

> *"The diagonal of a rectangle produces by itself both the areas which the two sides of the rectangle produce separately."*

This is a general statement of what Western tradition calls the Pythagorean theorem — that the square on the hypotenuse of a right triangle equals the sum of the squares on the two other sides — dated to c. 800 BCE, approximately **200–300 years before [[pythagoras]]** (c. 570–495 BCE).

Baudhayana also explicitly lists specific Pythagorean triples: 3-4-5, 5-12-13, 8-15-17, 7-24-25 — the same triplets that appear in the Babylonian Plimpton 322 tablet (c. 1800 BCE), predating both Greek and Indian texts by a millennium.

## The Squaring the Circle Problem

The Sulba Sūtras contain practical approximations for constructing a square whose area equals a given circle (and vice versa) — the ritual required altar shapes to be geometrically equivalent in area even when their forms differed. Baudhayana's approximation yields an implied value of π ≈ 3.088; Āpastamba's method gives π ≈ 3.004 in one context and a more refined π ≈ 3.0883 in another. These are practical engineering values, not abstract approximations — they were used to build altars.

## Cross-Tradition MASSIVE WIN

**The Pythagorean theorem is NOT Greek in origin.** The standard historiography names the theorem after [[pythagoras]] (c. 570–495 BCE). The Sulba Sūtras (c. 800 BCE) and the Babylonian Plimpton 322 tablet (c. 1800 BCE) both contain the theorem — the latter 1,300 years before Pythagoras. The naming is a European historiographic convention that reflects whose texts were read in early-modern European universities, not who discovered the relationship.

**Religious precision as the mother of mathematical abstraction**: The Sulba Sūtras are the clearest case in the ancient world where mathematical sophistication is a direct and documented product of ritual requirement. The theorem was not derived through abstract contemplation; it was derived because you cannot build a falcon-shaped altar that doubles the area of a square altar without knowing the relationship between a diagonal and its sides. The Vedic ritual complex produced the Indian mathematical tradition the same way Babylonian temple administration produced cuneiform arithmetic.
