# Vedic altar geometry

The corpus of geometrical techniques developed in ancient India for the precise construction of Vedic fire-altars (*citi*) and their associated ritual figures. The principal textual sources are the *Śulba-sūtras* — appendices attached to the *Kalpa-sūtras* of the major Yajurveda *śākhās* — and named by their authors: Baudhāyana (earliest, c. -800 to -600), Āpastamba, Mānava, and Kātyāyana (latest, c. -300). The name *śulba* means "cord" — the construction tool by which the altar layouts were laid out on the ground.

## The constructions

The texts describe in technical detail the construction of altars of specified shape and exact area: the *gārhapatya* (square, of one *puruṣa* on a side), the *āhavanīya* (rectangle), the *dakṣiṇa* (semicircle), and the elaborate composite figures of the great fire-altar (*agnicayana*) — including the falcon-shaped altar of 7.5 square puruṣas. The techniques include:

- The Pythagorean theorem — explicitly stated in Baudhāyana 1.12: "The diagonal of a rectangle produces the sum of [the areas of] what is produced by the longer and the shorter sides separately." Specific Pythagorean triples are listed: 3-4-5, 5-12-13, 8-15-17, 12-35-37.
- Construction of a square equal in area to a given rectangle.
- Construction of a square equal in area to a given circle (with explicit rational approximations of π).
- Doubling and tripling the area of a given square.
- Constructions of irrational quantities — √2 is given to 5 decimal places by an additive series in Baudhāyana.

## The dating question

The Pythagorean-theorem statement in Baudhāyana precedes Pythagoras's traditional date (c. -530) by several centuries. The geometrical techniques are demonstrably older than the textual codification — fire-altar ritual is described in earlier Brāhmaṇa prose, which assumes the geometry as a working tradition. Seidenberg (1962) argued for an even older common ancestor of South-Asian and Greek geometrical mathematics; the argument is influential in the historiography of mathematics but not universally accepted.

## Why the geometry mattered

The Brāhmaṇa-prose tradition is explicit: a *correct* altar produces the desired ritual effect; an *incorrect* altar does not. Geometrical exactness is not optional. The Vedic ritualist is therefore a working geometer whose calculations have ritual consequences; the Śulba-sūtra-tradition is the systematic preservation of the techniques required to be a successful sacrificer. This is mathematics as theology — the geometrical procedure *is* the ritual.
