# Rhind Mathematical Papyrus

The **Rhind Mathematical Papyrus** (c. 1650 BCE, British Museum EA 10057) is the most complete ancient Egyptian mathematical document — purchased by Alexander Henry Rhind in Luxor in 1858 and now in the British Museum. It is a scribal teaching text, copied by a scribe named Ahmes (*"Ahmes Papyrus"* is an alternative name) who states he copied it from an earlier document of the Middle Kingdom (c. 2000–1800 BCE). The text itself contains **87 mathematical problems** with worked solutions.

## What It Contains

The problems fall into practical categories:
- **Arithmetic**: multiplication and division using unit fractions (1/n form); the "method of false position" for solving linear equations
- **Geometry**: areas of triangles, trapezoids, rectangles, and circles; volumes of cylindrical granaries; slopes of pyramid faces (*seqed*)
- **Distribution problems**: dividing loaves among workers, distributing grain, sharing provisions by ratio
- **Algebraic problems**: "heap" problems (aha problems) — solving for an unknown quantity; the first documented use of a symbol (represented by a hieratic sign meaning "heap") for an unknown

The problems are worked, not proved. The scribe gives the procedure and the answer; the reader must apply the same procedure to similar problems. This is the administrative-mathematical tradition: **know how to get the answer**, not why the procedure works.

## MASSIVE WIN — The First π Approximation

Problem 48: Ahmes compares the area of a circle to the area of its circumscribing square. His procedure: take the diameter, subtract 1/9 of it, square the result. In modern terms: area = (8/9 × d)² = (8d/9)². This gives π ≈ 256/81 ≈ **3.1605** — accurate to within 0.6% of the true value.

This is the **oldest documented approximation of π** in any civilization. The Babylonians used π ≈ 3 (or sometimes 25/8 = 3.125). The Vedic Sulba Sūtras use various approximations. The Rhind's 256/81 predates all of them by chronology of documentation.

Every subsequent mathematical tradition had to confront the same fundamental problem: the ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter is not a nice rational number. The Egyptian, Babylonian, Vedic Indian, Chinese, and Greek traditions all approximated it, all arrived at approximately 3.1, and all recognized the approximation was imperfect. The Rhind's approximation is the first documented step in humanity's 4,000-year engagement with a number that cannot be exactly expressed.

## MASSIVE WIN — The Seqed and Pyramid Construction

The *seqed* (slope measurement) problems in the Rhind are directly related to pyramid construction. The seqed expresses the horizontal displacement per unit vertical rise — the steepness of a slope — in units of *palms* per *cubit*. Standard pyramid proportions correspond to specific seqed values.

This is **applied trigonometry before trigonometry** — the computational procedure for managing slope without any trigonometric concept. The mathematical tool produced pyramids; the pyramids produced the mathematical tool. Sacred architecture and mathematical development are inseparable.

## The Scribal School Context

The Rhind is a teaching text for Egyptian scribes — government administrators who managed the temple economy and state construction projects. Egyptian scribal training included:
- Hieratic script writing
- Reading and composing official documents
- Mathematical computation (the Rhind's content)
- Measurement and surveying
- Drafting architectural plans

The scribe was a sacred-administrative figure: holder of the written word (controlled by [[thoth]], god of writing and mathematics), mediator between the human and divine orders, manager of the surplus that built the temples. Mathematics was not separate from religion in this context; it was the technical expression of divine order (*ma'at*) in the material world.

## MASSIVE WIN — Unit Fraction Arithmetic

Egyptian mathematics required all fractions to be written as sums of distinct unit fractions (1/n). So 2/3 = 1/2 + 1/6; 2/7 = 1/4 + 1/28; etc. The Rhind opens with a "2/n table" — the standard decompositions of 2/3 through 2/101 — that scribes memorized and used.

This seems cumbersome compared to modern fraction notation. But it forced the development of sophisticated computational skills and required deep familiarity with divisibility relationships. The Egyptian unit-fraction system appears in Greek mathematics (Archimedes used it) and influenced medieval European computation through its Latin transmission. Fibonacci's *Liber Abaci* specifically discusses Egyptian-style fraction decomposition as an alternative computational method.
