# Abjad numerals

The system in which each Arabic letter bears a fixed numerical value — the parallel to Hebrew [[mathematics-gematria|gematria]] in the Islamic world, inherited from the same Aramaic-Phoenician parent and preserving the same numerical assignments. The Arabic alphabet was reordered for everyday use into a phonetic sequence (*alif*, *bāʾ*, *tāʾ*, *thāʾ*, *jīm*, ...) but the older Semitic letter-order survives explicitly in the numerical *abjad* sequence: alif=1, bāʾ=2, jīm=3, dāl=4, hāʾ=5, wāw=6, zāy=7, ḥāʾ=8, ṭāʾ=9, yāʾ=10, kāf=20, lām=30, mīm=40, nūn=50, sīn=60, ʿayn=70, fāʾ=80, ṣād=90, qāf=100, rāʾ=200, shīn=300, tāʾ=400, thāʾ=500, khāʾ=600, dhāl=700, ḍād=800, ẓāʾ=900, ghayn=1000.

The first eight letter-values (alif-ḥāʾ) and the next bracket (yāʾ-tsade) and the hundreds (qāf-tāʾ) match the Hebrew positions exactly — the Arabic numerical-letter values are a direct inheritance.

## Chronograms and historical record

The principal everyday use of abjad in Islamic culture was the *chronogram*: a phrase whose letter-totals encode a date. Tombstones, manuscript colophons, foundation inscriptions, and panegyric poetry routinely encode dates this way; the practice peaked in Ottoman and Mughal contexts (16th-19th c.) and is still occasionally used in conservative Islamic contexts today. A scholarly subspecialty (*tāʾrīkh*) reads thousands of such chronograms across Islamic textual culture.

## Sufi 'ilm al-ḥurūf

The Sufi science of letters ([[alphabet-ilm-al-huruf]]) — particularly as developed by Ibn al-ʿArabī (1165-1240), Aḥmad al-Būnī (d. 1225), and the Ḥurūfī movement of Faḍlallāh Astarābādī (d. 1394) — takes abjad numerology as the entry point into a full metaphysics: each letter has a number, a planet, a phonetic element, a divine attribute. The huge corpus of Sufi-letter-mysticism literature (Ibn al-ʿArabī's *Fuṣūṣ al-ḥikam*, al-Būnī's *Shams al-Maʿārif*, the Ḥurūfī *Jāvidān-nāma*) treats abjad as the operative substrate of all Quranic interpretation. The 14 *huruf al-muqattaʿat* — the "disjoined letters" that open 29 Quranic surahs — are the most extreme case: their abjad totals and combinations are read as the cipher of the divine architecture of the Quran itself.
