# Platonic solids

The five regular convex polyhedra — the tetrahedron (4 faces), cube/hexahedron (6 faces), octahedron (8 faces), dodecahedron (12 faces), and icosahedron (20 faces). The set is *complete*: Euclid's *Elements* Book XIII Proposition 18 demonstrates that only these five regular convex polyhedra exist in three-dimensional space. This exhaustiveness is one of the strongest a-priori mathematical facts and was, for the Pythagoreans and Platonists, deeply loaded with theological meaning.

## The Timaeus assignment

Plato's *Timaeus* (c. -360) assigns each of the four traditionally-recognized elements to four of the regular solids on intuitive-empirical grounds:

- **Fire** ↔ tetrahedron (the sharpest, most penetrating).
- **Earth** ↔ cube (the most stable, easiest to stack).
- **Air** ↔ octahedron (lighter than water, harder to grasp).
- **Water** ↔ icosahedron (the roundest, most fluid).
- **Cosmos / the all** ↔ dodecahedron (the fifth, "which God used for arranging the constellations on the whole heaven").

The dodecahedron-cosmos identification is the structurally important move: the *aether* (or the cosmic envelope) gets the fifth solid. Aristotle later (in *On the Heavens*) makes this the basis of the *quintessence* doctrine — a fifth element distinct from the four mundane elements, the substance of celestial bodies. The medieval Latin word *quint-essence* preserves the assignment in modern English: literally "the fifth essence."

## Renaissance reception

The Platonic solids re-entered the sacred-geometry program through the Italian Renaissance. Piero della Francesca's *Libellus de Quinque Corporibus Regularibus* (c. 1480), Luca Pacioli's *De divina proportione* (1509, with illustrations by Leonardo), and later Kepler's *Mysterium Cosmographicum* (1596 — Kepler proposed that the spacing of the six known planets was determined by inscribing each Platonic solid inside the next, an idea he later abandoned but never fully repudiated) all rehearse the Pythagorean-Platonic program.

## The dodecahedron-cosmos connection in modern physics

Curiously, the dodecahedron-cosmos identification has a footnote in modern cosmology: Luminet et al. (2003) and Atiyah & Sutcliffe (2003) explored a model of the universe with the topology of a Poincaré dodecahedral space. The model produces specific predictions about the cosmic microwave background that are testable but have not been confirmed. Plato's intuition that the dodecahedron is the shape of the cosmos remains — at least as a serious mathematical possibility — open.
