# Pi (π) — cross-tradition

The ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter — a transcendental irrational number whose exact value cannot be expressed in any finite decimal or fractional form, but which appears in every culture that pursued geometry far enough to need it. The history of π is a useful comparative measure of the mathematical depth of pre-modern traditions, because every culture that built circular structures or tracked celestial motion had to develop *some* working approximation.

## The ancient approximations

- **Egyptian** (Rhind Papyrus, c. -1650): π ≈ 256/81 ≈ 3.16049. The papyrus gives a rule for computing the area of a circle of diameter *d* as the area of a square of side 8*d*/9 — equivalent to the stated approximation.
- **Babylonian** (YBC 7302, c. -1800 to -1600): the implicit ratio used in circumference-from-diameter problems is 3 + 1/8 = 3.125. Plimpton 322 and related tablets show implicit awareness that the actual value is somewhat higher.
- **Hebrew** (1 Kings 7:23, c. -550 final-form): the Solomonic molten-sea (the great bronze basin in Solomon's temple) is given as 10 cubits across and 30 cubits in circumference — implying π = 3. This has occasioned extensive rabbinic-mathematical commentary; some readings (Ḥakham Eliya of Vilna) read the *implicit* π via a gematria of the spelling-variant in 1 Kings 7:23 to obtain π ≈ 3.14150943..., though this is post hoc.
- **Vedic** (Śulba-sūtras, c. -800 to -300): Baudhāyana gives constructions equivalent to π ≈ 3.0883; Mānava gives π = 3.125; Āpastamba uses 3.0884.
- **Greek** (Archimedes, c. -250): proved by the method of exhaustion that 3 + 10/71 < π < 3 + 10/70, i.e., 3.140845 < π < 3.142857. This is the first rigorous bounding of π.
- **Chinese** (Liu Hui, 263 CE): π ≈ 3.1416 by inscribed polygon method; Zu Chongzhi (c. 480 CE) refined this to π ≈ 355/113 = 3.14159292... (accurate to seven decimal places — a precision Europe did not match until the 16th c.).
- **Indian Kerala school** (Mādhava of Sangamagrāma, c. 1380): the first known infinite-series expression for π — the Madhava–Leibniz series (later rediscovered in Europe by Leibniz in 1674): π/4 = 1 - 1/3 + 1/5 - 1/7 + ... The Kerala-school mathematicians computed π to 11 decimal places c. 1400 — two centuries before any European could match it.

## Theological loading

The Solomonic-sea π = 3 problem is the most-discussed theological case. Modern apologetic literature has argued the biblical figure can be read consistently — for instance by taking the *outer* circumference and *inner* diameter, or by treating the figures as rounded — but the simpler reading is that the text takes 3 as a working approximation. The biblical writer is not committed to a precise mathematical statement.

The Pythagorean-Platonic tradition treats π's irrationality as theologically significant: like the diagonal of the unit square (√2) and the golden ratio (φ), π is *alogos* — without ratio, expressible only as a process never as a quantity. Three of the foundational geometrical quantities are irrational; this was experienced by the Pythagoreans as a scandalous limit on the rational comprehensibility of the world.
