# Numeral & Place-Value Systems — comparative spine

A **numeral system** is a notation for writing quantities: a finite set of signs and rules by which the signs combine to denote numbers. The decisive distinction in the history of notation is between **additive** systems — where the value of a numeral is the sum of its signs, irrespective of position (Roman *XXIII*, Egyptian hieroglyphic, Greek and Arabic alphabetic numerals) — and **positional (place-value)** systems, where the value of a sign depends on the column it occupies (modern *203* uses the *same* sign 2 to mean two hundred). Place-value is the single most powerful idea in elementary mathematics, because it makes the four operations mechanical and unbounded; it is also rare. This node is the comparative spine that arranges the Atlas's numeral nodes **by base** and tracks the one feature that separates a working notation from a transformative one: **zero**.

## The bases — at least three independent inventions of place-value

Positional notation was invented independently at least three times, on three different bases, by three civilizations with no plausible contact:

- **Base 60 — Babylonian sexagesimal** ([[mathematics-sexagesimal-babylonian]]). The oldest fully positional system (Old Babylonian, c. -1900 to -1600), written in cuneiform wedges. Sixty is highly composite (twelve divisors), which makes division exact and survives in our 60-minute hour and 360° circle.
- **Base 20 — Maya vigesimal** ([[mathematics-mayan-vigesimal]]). A fully positional system with an explicit zero glyph, attested from the Late Preclassic (c. -400 to 200 CE) and developed for calendrical-astronomical computation across millions of days.
- **Base 10 — Indian decimal** ([[mathematics-zero-india]]). The decimal place-value system with zero, fixed by Brahmagupta (628 CE) and the only one of the three to achieve global transmission.
- **Base 10, positional — Chinese rod numerals** ([[mathematics-chinese-nine-chapters]]). Counting-rod notation behind the *Jiuzhang Suanshu* (c. 100 BCE) is decimal and effectively positional on the counting board, with an indigenous place-value structure (the written round-zero is later and partly transmitted).

Two further members are **non-positional contrast cases** that show place-value is not inevitable even in advanced cultures:

- **Base 10, additive — Egyptian** ([[mathematics-egyptian-rhind-papyrus]]). Distinct repeated symbols for 1, 10, 100, 1000; no place-value, no zero — yet a fully competent practical mathematics (the Rhind Papyrus, c. 1650 BCE).
- **Alphabetic — Arabic abjad** ([[mathematics-abjad-numerals]]). Each letter a fixed value; the Islamic continuation of the Greek→Hebrew→Arabic letter-numeral lineage. Notably, the *same* Islamic mathematical culture that kept abjad for chronograms also transmitted the Indian positional decimal for arithmetic.

## Zero as placeholder vs zero as number — the cardinal distinction

The key scholarly distinction this spine turns on is not *who had a zero* but *what kind of zero*:

1. **Zero as placeholder** marks an empty column so that 203 is distinguishable from 23. The **Babylonians** had this by c. 300 BCE (a space, later a double-wedge sign), but it was never a quantity one could compute with — it could not stand alone and was not used in the final position. Robson (2008) and Neugebauer (1957) are explicit that the Babylonian sign is positional punctuation, not a number.
2. **Zero as a number** participates in arithmetic. This is the radical Indian step: **Brahmagupta**'s *Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta* (628 CE) states the first rules for operating with zero (*n* + 0 = *n*, *n* × 0 = 0, and — famously and incorrectly — 0 ÷ 0 = 0). Only after zero becomes a number can it enter algebra as a legitimate result.

The **Maya** zero is the interesting middle term: it is a genuine place-value sign (more than a Babylonian gap) embedded in calendar arithmetic, but there is no surviving Maya text stating *rules* for zero as an operand in the Brahmaguptan sense. The honest summary: **three independent place-value systems; one (India) that made zero a number; one (Maya) with a true positional zero-sign but no preserved zero-arithmetic; one (Babylon) with a placeholder only.**

## The documented transmission — India → Baghdad → Europe

Against this background of independent invention stands the one numeral system that became **global**, and it became global by **documented transmission, not convergence**:

- **India → Baghdad.** [[mathematics-house-of-wisdom|al-Khwārizmī]]'s *Kitāb al-Ḥisāb al-Hindī* ("On Indian Reckoning", c. 825 CE) systematised the Indian decimal-with-zero for the Arabic-reading world; the numerals were known in Arabic precisely as *al-arqām al-hindiyya*, "the Indian figures."
- **Baghdad → Europe.** The Latin translation of al-Khwārizmī (*Algoritmi de numero Indorum*, c. 1126) carried both the numerals and — from his Latinised name — the word **algorithm**. The Arabic *ṣifr* ("empty"), the translation of Sanskrit *śūnya*, became Latin *zephirum* → Italian *zefiro* → English **zero** (and, by another route, **cipher**).
- **Europe → commerce.** [[mathematics-fibonacci|Fibonacci]]'s *Liber Abaci* (1202) opens: "With these nine figures, and with the sign 0 which the Arabs call zephyr, any number whatsoever can be written." That sentence began the displacement of Roman numerals from European accounting. The numerals are called **Hindu-Arabic** because the chain is real and traceable end to end: Indian invention, Arabic transmission, European adoption.

## Cross-tradition significance (transmission vs convergence)

This spine is a textbook case for separating the two relationships the Atlas keeps distinct:

- **Independent convergence** governs the *invention of place-value itself*. The Babylonian, Maya, Chinese and Indian positional systems are not genealogically related; they are four separate solutions to the same problem of writing large numbers compactly. Place-value is "discovered" the way the right-triangle theorem is discovered repeatedly (Babylon, India, China) — because the underlying mathematics rewards it. The mainstream of comparative mathematics (Menninger 1969; Ifrah 2000; Robson 2008; the contributors to Closs 1986) treats these as parallel inventions, and the Maya case in particular is the standard counter-example to any unilineal "numbers spread outward from one source" story.
- **Documented transmission** governs the *spread of the specific decimal-and-zero system that became universal*. Here there is no debate about convergence: the manuscripts (Brahmagupta 628; al-Khwārizmī c. 825; the Latin *Algoritmi*; *Liber Abaci* 1202) and the loan-words (*śūnya* → *ṣifr* → *zephirum* → *zero*/*cipher*) trace an unbroken line. Plofker (2009) documents each link.

The lesson the spine encodes: **the idea of positional notation arose by convergence; the particular notation the whole modern world writes in arrived by transmission.** Conflating the two — treating the global success of Hindu-Arabic numerals as evidence that all numeral systems descend from one — is precisely the error the comparative literature warns against.

## Disputes

- **Dating of the Bakhshali zero.** The Bakhshali manuscript's dot-placeholder was long cited as possibly the oldest physical Indian zero (c. 200-400 CE on palaeographic grounds), but 2017 radiocarbon dating of its birch-bark folios yielded multiple dates spread across the 3rd-10th centuries CE, and the manuscript may be composite. The *earliest secure* dated Indian zero in a positional inscription is therefore the **Gwalior inscription (876 CE)**; Brahmagupta's *textual* zero-arithmetic (628 CE) is firmer than the physical placeholder evidence. (Plofker 2009; Kaplan 1999.)
- **Whether the Babylonian sign counts as "a zero."** Some popular accounts (and Ifrah 2000 in places) credit Babylon with "the first zero." Specialists (Robson 2008; Neugebauer 1957) restrict this: the sign is a medial placeholder, absent in final position and never an operand, so it is a zero of *notation* but not of *number*. The disagreement is partly terminological, but the placeholder-vs-number line is the substantive point.
- **The independence of Chinese place-value's written zero.** The decimal place-value *structure* of the counting board is indigenous and ancient, but the written **round zero** appears in China only from about the 8th century CE and is widely held to reflect contact with Indian mathematics (transmitted via Buddhism). Whether any earlier indigenous written zero existed is debated; Martzloff (1997) and Needham (1959) treat the structure as native and the written symbol as later and partly external.
- **Sitchin-style and pyramidological diffusion claims** (that one prehistoric source seeded all numeral systems) are pseudo-historical and rejected across the field; the Maya case alone refutes them.
