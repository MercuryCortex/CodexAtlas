# Kora — Tibetan Buddhist Circumambulation

The Tibetan Buddhist practice of **clockwise circumambulation** (Tibetan *skor ba*, kora) of a sacred object — stūpa, monastery, sacred image, mountain, or sacred-region. The practice is the **direct inheritance of Hindu pradakṣiṇā** via the Indian → Tibetan Buddhist transmission beginning in the 7th c. CE, preserving the clockwise direction, the prescribed-count system (1/3/7/21/108), and the multi-radius scaling (object → building → mountain → region). The Tibetan religious landscape from the 8th c. onward is structured around kora circuits: every stūpa, every monastery, every sacred image has its kora-path inscribed; every major mountain has its kora-circuit; every major city has its inner-kora + outer-kora (Lhasa famously has three: the Nangkhor immediately around the Jokhang shrine, the Barkhor around the Jokhang complex, and the Lingkhor circumambulating the entire old city).

## Hindu → Buddhist direct inheritance

The Hindu pradakṣiṇā tradition (Vedic origin, -1500 onward) was inherited wholesale into Indian Buddhism. Early Buddhist stūpas (3rd c. BCE onward — Sanchi, Bharhut, Amaravati) were built with circumambulation-platforms designed for the prescribed clockwise-circling rite. When Vajrayāna Buddhism transmitted from India to Tibet starting in the 7th c. (Padmasambhava's 8th-c. founding of Samye; Atiśa's 11th-c. Kadampa reform), the kora practice came with it as a structural element of the tradition. The Tibetan word *skor ba* (kora) means simply "to go around" — preserving the Sanskrit *pradakṣiṇā* "right-side-keeping" semantic. The clockwise direction (= the daily westward path of the sun, the cosmological macrocosm) is preserved across the religious-frame change.

The Bon tradition (pre-Buddhist Tibetan religion that survived alongside Buddhism after the 8th-c. Buddhist arrival) **reverses the direction** — Bonpos circumambulate **counter-clockwise**. The inversion is not arbitrary; it's the historically-Bon distinction marking the difference between the two traditions. At Mount Kailash, Hindu + Buddhist pilgrims walk the 52-km kora circuit clockwise; Bon pilgrims walk it counter-clockwise. The two streams flow past each other on the same path.

## Mount Kailash — the canonical kora

The **Mount Kailash kora** is the most-pilgrimaged single-mountain circumambulation in the Asian sacred-geography. The mountain (~6,638 m, in Tibet's far west) is simultaneously sacred to four traditions:

- **Hindus** — abode of Śiva + Pārvatī (Liṅga Purāṇa, Skanda Purāṇa)
- **Buddhists** — abode of Cakrasaṃvara (the wrathful tantric Buddha-form of the highest yoga tantra)
- **Jains** — the place where Tīrthaṅkara Ṛṣabha (the first of the 24 Jain Tīrthaṅkaras) attained mokṣa
- **Bonpos** — the sacred mountain of their tradition, central to the *Mdo dus* cosmology

The kora is a 52-km, 3-day circuit at high altitude (the highest point, Dolma La pass, is 5,648 m). Standard piety: 108 koras over a lifetime cleanses all karma; one daṇḍavat-kora (body-prostration kora, taking 2-4 weeks of daily prostration) is equivalent to 108 walking koras. The cross-tradition convergence at Kailash is one of the most-densely-attested cross-religious sites in the world.

## Scale + somatic discipline

Beyond Kailash, major Tibetan kora circuits include:

- **Jokhang Lingkhor** (Lhasa) — the 8-km outer-circumambulation of the old city, traditionally walked daily by Lhasa laity
- **Tsari kora** — the 12-year-cycle kora at Tsari (closed during periods of political tension; resumed when conditions allow)
- **Amnye Machen kora** — the eastern-Tibetan equivalent of Kailash
- **Bodhnath stūpa kora** (Nepal — Tibetan-Buddhist diaspora) — Tibetan refugees in Kathmandu walk the Bodhnath circumambulation-platform multiple times daily
- **Swayambhunath kora** (Kathmandu) — similar to Bodhnath

The daily local-kora practice for Tibetan laity is a contemplative discipline — prayer-wheel turning + mantra recitation + body-walking-in-clockwise-around-the-sacred — that fuses the physical + verbal + mental "Three Mysteries" framework of Vajrayāna practice.

## MASSIVE-WIN — cross-tradition circumambulation convergence

Kora anchors the Buddhist pole of one of the cleanest cross-tradition **structural-pattern convergences** in comparative religion. The cluster:

- **Hindu [[circumambulation-pradakshina-hindu|pradakṣiṇā]]** — Vedic origin, the millennia-older parent tradition
- **Buddhist kora** — direct Hindu inheritance via Indian Buddhist transmission
- **Islamic [[tawaf-kaaba-circumambulation|ṭawāf]]** — clockwise-from-sacred-frame Hajj rite (no proven historical transmission from Hindu/Buddhist — structural parallel)
- **Jewish hakafot** — 7-circumambulations of Torah on Simchat Torah
- **Christian Easter-Vigil altar-circling**
- **Bon counter-clockwise kora** — the deliberate inversion documenting the centrality of the practice
- **Mesoamerican + Andean sacred-mountain circling** (independent)

The Mount Kailash convergence (Hindu + Buddhist + Jain + Bon all performing kora at the same mountain) is the densest single-site cross-tradition convergence in the comparative-religion record. The Bernbaum 1980 *The Way to Shambhala* + Snellgrove 1987 + McKay 1998 are the principal scholarly treatments.
