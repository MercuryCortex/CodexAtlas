# Ānāpānasati — Mindfulness of Breathing

The principal Buddhist breath-meditation practice: sustained attention to the breath, unfolding through the canonical 16-step progressive scheme of the *Ānāpānasati Sutta* (Majjhima Nikāya 118). The Buddha is recorded as describing ānāpānasati as the meditation method he himself used to attain awakening under the bodhi tree, and as the method "by which any *bhikkhu* will reach the holy life's fulfilment in this very life." It is one of the most-practiced contemplative methods on Earth in the 21st century, both within Buddhist traditions and in secularized derivative forms (MBSR, Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy, Headspace, Calm, etc.).

## The canonical 16-step scheme

The *Ānāpānasati Sutta* organizes breath-mindfulness into 16 progressive steps, grouped into four tetrads aligned with the Four Foundations of Mindfulness (*satipaṭṭhāna*):

**Body tetrad (kāyānupassanā):**
1. Breathing in/out long, knows "I am breathing in/out long"
2. Breathing in/out short, knows "I am breathing in/out short"
3. Trains: "I will breathe in/out experiencing the whole body"
4. Trains: "I will breathe in/out calming the body-formation"

**Feeling-tone tetrad (vedanānupassanā):**
5. Trains: "I will breathe in/out experiencing rapture (*pīti*)"
6. Trains: "I will breathe in/out experiencing pleasure (*sukha*)"
7. Trains: "I will breathe in/out experiencing the mental-formation"
8. Trains: "I will breathe in/out calming the mental-formation"

**Mind tetrad (cittānupassanā):**
9. Trains: "I will breathe in/out experiencing the mind"
10. Trains: "I will breathe in/out gladdening the mind"
11. Trains: "I will breathe in/out concentrating the mind"
12. Trains: "I will breathe in/out liberating the mind"

**Dharmas tetrad (dhammānupassanā):**
13. Trains: "I will breathe in/out contemplating impermanence (*anicca*)"
14. Trains: "I will breathe in/out contemplating fading-away (*virāga*)"
15. Trains: "I will breathe in/out contemplating cessation (*nirodha*)"
16. Trains: "I will breathe in/out contemplating relinquishment (*paṭinissagga*)"

The scheme moves from gross body-awareness through subtler feeling and mental states into direct insight into the marks of existence (impermanence, suffering, not-self), culminating in liberation. Note that the scheme is BOTH *samatha* (concentration) AND *vipassanā* (insight) — the same breath-anchor leads through deepening concentration into liberating insight.

## Buddhaghosa's commentarial systematization

The 5th-c. Theravāda commentator [[buddhaghosa]] dedicates chapter VIII of the *Visuddhimagga* to ānāpānasati, identifying it as the supreme meditation object for all temperament-types. He systematizes the progression as four stages of attention-quality:

1. **Counting (gaṇanā)** — preliminary stabilization through breath-counting
2. **Following (anubandhanā)** — sustained attention to the full inhale/exhale
3. **Touching (phusanā)** — attention to the contact-point at nostrils
4. **Settling (ṭhapanā)** — sustained absorption at a fine breath-image (*nimitta*); gateway to the four *jhānas*

This four-stage scheme has shaped the major modern Theravāda methods: Pa Auk's strict-samatha jhāna training, Ajahn Brahm's jhāna teaching, Mahasi's modified abdominal-breath method, and the Thai forest tradition's variations.

## The 20th-century revival + global diffusion

Ānāpānasati was significantly revived in late-19th + 20th-c. Burmese Buddhism, where vipassana practice was opened from monastic specialization to lay accessibility:

- **Ledi Sayadaw** (1846-1923) — initiated the lay vipassana movement
- **U Ba Khin** (1899-1971) — Burmese government official; teacher of Goenka
- **S.N. Goenka** (1924-2013) — Indian-Burmese; established the global Goenka 10-day retreat network; ānāpāna is the first 3 days, body-scan vipassana follows
- **Mahasi Sayadaw** (1904-1982) — Burmese systematic vipassana with abdominal-rise-fall as primary object
- **Ajahn Buddhadasa** (1906-1993) — Thai forest reformer; modernist 16-step Ānāpānasati commentary

The transmission to the West runs through these lineages plus:
- **Jack Kornfield, Sharon Salzberg, Joseph Goldstein** — American teachers trained in Thai/Burmese lineages; co-founded the Insight Meditation Society (1975)
- **Jon Kabat-Zinn** — secularized breath-mindfulness into Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR) at UMass Medical Center starting 1979; the foundational secular-clinical mindfulness program
- **Thích Nhất Hạnh** — Vietnamese Zen-trained; popularized breath-mindfulness in modern Western practice through Plum Village + numerous books

## MASSIVE-WIN — the secular mindfulness diffusion

The diffusion of ānāpānasati from Buddhist monastic practice into secular global clinical/wellness/corporate contexts is one of the largest cross-cultural religious-practice diffusions of the 20th-21st centuries. Jeff Wilson's *Mindful America* (2014) is the decisive scholarly history of how Buddhist breath-mindfulness was progressively re-coded as secular health-intervention through MBSR (1979), the 1990s mindfulness-research wave, and the 2010s app-economy popularization (Headspace, Calm — both centered on guided breath-meditation derived from ānāpānasati).

The secular-mindfulness diffusion has generated significant scholarly + Buddhist-tradition debate:
- **McMindfulness critique** (Ronald Purser, *McMindfulness*, 2019) — argues secular MBSR strips ethical + soteriological context from the practice
- **Buddhist preservation critique** — many traditional Buddhist teachers argue secular extraction misses the breath-meditation's place within the Eightfold Path framework
- **Clinical-empirical defense** — substantial RCT literature documents secular MBSR efficacy for anxiety, depression, chronic pain

This parallels the [[yoga-asana-postural|postural yoga]] secular-fitness diffusion, [[zazen-zen-meditation|Zen]] aesthetic-philosophical diffusion, and [[tai-chi-quan-internal-martial|tai chi]] / [[qigong-energy-cultivation|qigong]] wellness diffusion as the four principal religious-contemplative practices that crossed from Asian religious traditions into Western secular wellness contexts during the 20th-21st centuries.

## Cross-tradition position

Ānāpānasati anchors the Buddhist pole of the cross-tradition **breath-discipline** cluster. The distinctive feature vs other traditions:

- **vs Hindu [[pranayama-yogic-breath|prāṇāyāma]]** — same anchor (breath) but ānāpāna OBSERVES natural breath, prāṇāyāma CONTROLS breath (the structural-distinction noted in the prāṇāyāma node)
- **vs Daoist [[qigong-energy-cultivation|qigong]] breathing** — qigong adds qi-circulation framework and movement; ānāpāna is purely seated observational
- **vs Sufi breath-coordinated [[dhikr-sufi-remembrance|dhikr]] + hesychast breath-coordinated [[hesychasm-jesus-prayer|Jesus Prayer]]** — both Sufi + hesychast add a recitative phrase to the breath; ānāpāna is non-recitative

The Buddhist breath-mindfulness method has been the most-systematically theorized of all the cross-tradition breath-disciplines, with both extensive canonical-textual elaboration (Ānāpānasati Sutta + Visuddhimagga + later commentaries) and extensive 20th-c. clinical-scientific operationalization (the MBSR research literature).
