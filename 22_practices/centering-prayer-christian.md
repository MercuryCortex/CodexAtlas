# Centering Prayer — Modern Christian Contemplative Practice

A 20-minute silent contemplative-prayer method developed in the 1970s by three Trappist monks at Saint Joseph's Abbey in Spencer, Massachusetts — William Meninger, M. Basil Pennington, and Thomas Keating — drawing principally on the 14th-c. English mystical classic *The Cloud of Unknowing* but synthesizing it with the broader Western Christian contemplative tradition (Cassian, Pseudo-Dionysius, Carmelite contemplation, John of the Cross, Thérèse of Lisieux) into a method accessible to lay practitioners outside monastic enclosure. Diffused globally from the 1980s through the Contemplative Outreach network (founded by Keating, 1984), Centering Prayer has become one of the most-widely-practiced contemporary Catholic + ecumenical contemplative practices and a major channel through which contemplative-meditation practice has re-entered modern Western Christianity after centuries of relative neglect in post-Reformation Catholic + Protestant piety.

## How it works

The standard four guidelines (Keating's canonical formulation):

1. **Choose a sacred word** as the symbol of your consent to God's presence and action within
2. **Sitting comfortably with eyes closed**, settle briefly and silently introduce the sacred word as the symbol of your consent
3. **When engaged with thoughts**, return ever so gently to the sacred word
4. **At the end of the prayer period**, remain in silence with eyes closed for a couple of minutes

The sacred word can be any short word the practitioner finds resonant: "God", "Jesus", "Abba", "Love", "Peace", "Yes", "Open", "Be Still". It is NOT a mantra to be repeated continuously (as in [[mantra-japa-hindu|Hindu mantra-japa]] or [[dhikr-sufi-remembrance|Sufi dhikr]]); rather it serves as a gentle return-anchor used only when the practitioner notices having become caught in thoughts. The aim is not the word itself but rather the underlying silent intention of consent to God's presence + action within the soul during the prayer period.

Recommended duration: 20 minutes, twice daily (morning + afternoon/evening). The shorter daily frequency is theologized as a regular consent to God's transformative action over time rather than a single intensified event.

## The four R's (Keating's later formulation)

When a thought arises:

- **Resist** no thought
- **Retain** no thought
- **React** emotionally to no thought
- **Return** ever so gently to the sacred word

The progression is not "stop thinking" — thoughts will continue — but rather "do not engage" with the thought-stream. The practice is closer to a contemplative posture toward thought than a cognitive control of thought.

## Pre-modern Christian sources

Keating et al. were explicit that Centering Prayer is not a new invention but a modern systematization of pre-existing Western Christian contemplative tradition:

- ***The Cloud of Unknowing*** (anonymous English mystic, ~1370) — chapter 7 contains the single-word-of-prayer instruction: "And if you would have this purpose summed up in one word, take a little word of one syllable rather than of two; for the shorter it is, the more does it accord with this work of the Spirit. Such a word is the word *God* or the word *Love*. Take which one you prefer, or any other to your liking..." This passage is the direct source of the Centering Prayer sacred-word method
- **John Cassian** (~360-435) — *Conferences* IX-X on continual prayer; the "Deus in adjutorium meum intende" formula; the foundational Latin source for repetitive contemplative prayer in Western monasticism
- **The Carmelite contemplative tradition** — particularly John of the Cross's *Living Flame of Love* and *Dark Night of the Soul*; Teresa of Ávila's *Interior Castle* and *Way of Perfection*
- **Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite** (~late 5th c.) — *Mystical Theology*; the foundational Western source for apophatic / negative-theology contemplative tradition
- **Meister Eckhart** (~1260-1328) — German Dominican mystic; *Gelassenheit* (releasement / letting-be); proximate medieval ancestor

The Trappist developers framed Centering Prayer as a recovery of this neglected Western Christian contemplative tradition in a form accessible to lay practitioners in the late-20th-c. Catholic context after Vatican II's renewal of liturgical + lay-spiritual life.

## Reception, controversy, and ecumenical reach

The method spread quickly through the Catholic religious-orders and lay-renewal networks of the 1980s. The Contemplative Outreach network (founded 1984) became the principal organizational vehicle. Major Catholic reception was supportive — Keating's books carried *imprimatur* + nihil obstat from his Trappist abbey + diocesan authorities. Ecumenical reach extended quickly into Episcopalian + mainline Protestant + Anglican circles.

Controversies have arisen in certain Catholic + Evangelical circles:

- **Eastern-religion-influence critique**: some Catholic + Evangelical critics (e.g., the late Cardinal Joseph Ratzinger's 1989 *Letter on Some Aspects of Christian Meditation* to bishops; various Evangelical apologetic writings) raised concerns that the method as taught risked importing Eastern (Buddhist + Hindu) meditation conceptual frameworks into Christian prayer in ways that obscure its distinctively Christian character. The criticisms have not resulted in any official Catholic condemnation of the method
- **The "consenting" framework**: Keating's specific theological framing of Centering Prayer as "consent to God's presence and action within" has been debated in Catholic theological circles regarding its relation to traditional Catholic mystical theology categories
- **Therapeutic-frame critique**: as Centering Prayer entered psychotherapy + spiritual-direction contexts in the 1990s-2010s, some critics argued the method was being de-theologized into a generic mindfulness-relaxation technique stripped of its Christian theological content

The method has nonetheless become a major channel of modern Catholic + ecumenical contemplative practice; the Contemplative Outreach network operates across many countries; the foundational books remain in continuous print; and the practice has become widely-taught in Catholic + Episcopal retreat houses, monasteries, and lay-renewal contexts.

## Cross-tradition position

Centering Prayer sits within the cross-tradition cluster of **silent contemplative-prayer disciplines**:

- **Eastern Orthodox [[hesychasm-jesus-prayer|hesychast Jesus Prayer]]** — the closest cross-tradition cognate; both: short repetitive phrase + silent contemplative orientation + recovery of pre-modern contemplative tradition for modern practice. Distinctive differences: Jesus Prayer uses a continuously-repeated longer phrase + breath-coordination; Centering Prayer uses a sacred word only as return-anchor + no breath-coordination
- **Buddhist [[zazen-zen-meditation|shikantaza zazen]]** (Soto Zen "just sitting") — structurally cognate as non-discursive open-awareness silent contemplative practice. Thomas Merton + Robert Kennedy + other Catholic-Zen interlocutors have documented the convergence. Distinctive differences: Centering Prayer has explicit theistic framing (consent to God's presence); shikantaza has Buddhist non-theistic framing
- **Quaker silent worship** — the Protestant cross-tradition cognate; silent corporate waiting on God; structurally similar though different in theological framing
- **Modern secular mindfulness-meditation** — secularized derivative of Buddhist meditation; the question of how Centering Prayer relates to secular mindfulness has been the subject of substantial discussion in modern contemplative-practice scholarship

The pattern: silent non-discursive contemplative-prayer practice oriented toward presence or awareness or consent rather than discursive thought or visualization. Centering Prayer is the principal modern Catholic + Western-Christian contribution to this cross-tradition cluster. See also [[lectio-divina-christian|lectio divina]] for the complementary discursive-meditative Christian practice with which Centering Prayer is typically paired.
