# Tonglen — Tibetan Practice of Taking and Giving

A Tibetan Buddhist meditation in which the practitioner reverses the ordinary self-protective reflex: **breathing in the suffering of others** (visualized as dark, hot, heavy smoke) and **breathing out one's own well-being, light, and virtue** (visualized as bright cool radiance). The Tibetan word *tonglen* (གཏོང་ལེན་, *gtong len*) literally means "giving-and-taking" — the *order* of the term names the giving FIRST, but in practice the in-breath takes suffering and the out-breath gives well-being. The reversal is the entire point: where the ego instinctively pulls in pleasure and pushes out pain, tonglen trains the practitioner to do the opposite.

## How it works

The standard modern four-stage scheme (Chödrön 2001):

1. **A flash of basic openness** — resting briefly in the open, non-defensive ground of mind before the practice proper begins.
2. **Work with the texture** — breathing in heat, thickness, darkness, weight; breathing out coolness, light, spaciousness. This stage trains the practitioner to take and give *quality* before specific *content*.
3. **A specific situation or person** — directing the practice to one being's suffering (someone known, sometimes oneself in a past or future moment of pain).
4. **Extending universally** — extending the same in-and-out to all beings in the same condition of suffering.

The breath-coordination is essential. The in-breath physiologically marks the *willingness to receive* what the ego ordinarily refuses; the out-breath marks the *willingness to give* what the ego ordinarily hoards. Repeated daily practice gradually undoes the self-protective reflex and re-trains the heart toward the bodhisattva orientation.

## Doctrinal grounding — the Indian Mahāyāna source

The doctrinal foundation is Śāntideva's 8th-c. *Bodhicaryāvatāra* (*Way of the Bodhisattva*), chapter 8, which contains the canonical argument for the **exchange of self and other** (*parātma-parivartana*): if self and other are equally afflicted by suffering, and if the conventional self-other boundary is the root cognitive error that *generates* the unequal protection of self, then the bodhisattva trains to dissolve that boundary by deliberate reversal. Śāntideva supplies the philosophy; tonglen is the breath-anchored *psychophysical practice* that operationalizes it.

The transmission from India to Tibet runs through Atiśa Dīpaṅkara (982-1054), the Bengali master who studied at Suvarṇadvīpa (Sumatra) under Dharmakīrti / Dharmarakṣita and brought the lojong (mind-training) tradition to Tibet in 1042. Atiśa founded the Kadampa school, whose central practice was lojong + tonglen. The Kadampa later flowed into all four major Tibetan schools (Gelugpa, Sakyapa, Kagyupa, Nyingmapa), making tonglen a pan-Tibetan practice rather than a school-specific technique.

## Lojong frame — the Seven Points

The canonical textual frame is **Chekawa Yeshe Dorje's** *Seven Points of Mind Training* (12th c.), which organizes the entire bodhisattva-path into seven progressive instructions. Tonglen sits at point 2 ("Train in the two bodhichittas") and point 3 ("Transform adverse conditions into the path of awakening"). The text is short — seven points + about 59 pithy aphorism-lines (*slogans*) — and modern teachers (Chödrön, Trungpa, Dilgo Khyentse) typically teach tonglen by working through the slogans alongside the formal sitting practice.

## 20th-c. transmission to the West

Tonglen became one of the most-circulated Tibetan practices in the contemporary West through three transmission lines:

- **Chögyam Trungpa Rinpoche** (1939-1987) — Tibetan Karma Kagyu / Nyingma master; founded Vajradhatu / Shambhala International; *Training the Mind and Cultivating Loving-Kindness* (1993) is the original Western lecture-series on lojong + tonglen.
- **Pema Chödrön** (b. 1936) — American student of Trungpa, ordained Tibetan Buddhist nun, abbess of Gampo Abbey (Cape Breton); her books *Start Where You Are* (1994) and *The Places That Scare You* (2001) are the principal Western exposition of tonglen, especially adapted for lay practitioners in trauma + grief + conflict contexts.
- **14th Dalai Lama** (b. 1935) — teaches tonglen as the central method of compassion cultivation in his public teaching on lojong and bodhichitta, including its application in clinical-medical contexts (Dalai Lama–Mind & Life dialogues 1990-present).

## MASSIVE-WIN — cross-tradition compassion-cultivation parallels

Tonglen anchors the Tibetan Mahāyāna pole of a cross-tradition cluster of **breath-coordinated compassion-cultivation practices**:

- **vs Theravāda [metta-bhavana|mettā-bhāvanā]** — both are systematic compassion-cultivation methods grounded in canonical Buddhist texts, but mettā *radiates* well-wishing outward in concentric circles (self → loved-ones → neutral → difficult → all-beings); tonglen *exchanges* suffering and well-being directly via the breath. Mettā is older (Pali canon) and is the Theravāda baseline; tonglen is the Mahāyāna-Vajrayāna structural inversion that adds the bodhisattva-vehicle frame.
- **vs Christian [[hesychasm-jesus-prayer|hesychast Jesus Prayer]]** — both are breath-coordinated contemplative methods aimed at the heart's transformation toward mercy. The hesychast's "Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me a sinner" repeats with the breath; tonglen's taking-giving runs with the breath. Different theological framings (Christic kenosis vs Mahāyāna bodhichitta) — same psychophysical method.
- **vs Christian [[centering-prayer-christian|centering prayer]]** — both have been used in 20th-21st-c. cross-tradition clinical-contemplative settings (hospital chaplaincy, palliative care, prison ministry — see Halifax 2008 *Being with Dying*) as accessible-to-laypeople compassion practices.

The convergence is documented across at least 3 major living traditions (Tibetan Mahāyāna + Theravāda + Eastern Orthodox + modern secular-contemplative). The structural pattern: **breath-anchored interior practice + deliberately cultivated orientation toward suffering + non-defensive openness as both means and goal**.

## Modern clinical reception

The Halifax / Cassidy / Mind-and-Life clinical-research engagement with tonglen has shaped contemporary palliative care, trauma therapy, and "compassion fatigue" prevention programs. Roshi Joan Halifax's *Being with Dying* trainings (Upaya Zen Center, Santa Fe) have integrated tonglen into hospice and end-of-life professional curricula since the 1990s. This places tonglen alongside [[anapanasati-mindfulness-breath|ānāpānasati]] / MBSR as the second-major Buddhist practice diffusion into Western clinical contexts in the late 20th–early 21st centuries — the first targeting attention + stress; the second targeting compassion + care-of-others.
