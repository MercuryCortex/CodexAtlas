# Mendicancy — Cross-Tradition Holy Begging and Dependence on Alms

The cross-tradition practice of **voluntary poverty and dependence on alms as a spiritual discipline** — the deliberate renunciation of property, income and self-provisioning so that the religious specialist lives on what lay supporters give. It is the **receiving side of the almsgiving relation**: every institution that commends alms (zakāt, dāna, tzedakah, caritas) implies a class of holy poor who legitimately receive them, and in the śramaṇa, monastic-Christian and Sufi worlds that class is formalized into a discipline with its own rules, implements (the begging bowl, the staff, the patched robe) and theology. The pattern arises independently in at least three religious matrices — the Indian śramaṇa movements (from the 5th c. BCE), Latin Christianity (institutionalized in the 13th c.), and Sufi Islam — and has an explicitly secular Greek analogue in the Cynic beggar-philosopher.

## Major instances by tradition

### Buddhist bhikkhu / bhikkhunī — piṇḍapāta
The monk's daily **alms-round** (Pali *piṇḍapāta*, "gathering lumps [of food]") with the **begging bowl** (*patta*) is among the most iconic Buddhist practices. The *Vinaya Piṭaka* codifies it: the monk owns three robes, a bowl, a needle, a razor, a water-strainer; he handles no money; he eats one meal, gathered on the round, before noon. He walks silently door to door with downcast eyes, accepting whatever is placed in the bowl without choosing, thanking, or (ideally) even meeting the giver's expectation of praise. The relation is reciprocal: the layperson who fills the bowl earns merit (*puñña*) and the monk, freed from livelihood, is available for full-time practice and teaching. In Theravāda countries the pre-dawn alms-round remains a daily public sight; the strictest **forest tradition** (dhutaṅga) monks eat only what the bowl yields.

### Jain muni / sādhvī — gochari
Jain mendicancy is among the most physically exacting. The wandering monk (*muni*) or nun (*sādhvī*) begs *gochari* ("cow-grazing" — as a cow crops a little grass here and there without uprooting the field), taking small amounts from many households so as to burden no one and so that no food is cooked specifically for the monk. The alms must pass the rules of *eṣaṇā*, avoiding forty-six enumerated faults; food that violates *ahiṃsā* or was prepared with the monk in mind is refused. This flows directly from *aparigraha* (non-possession), a Jain great vow: the Digambara monk owns literally nothing, not even clothing, and eats from his cupped hands.

### Hindu sannyāsī / sādhu — mādhukarī
The Hindu renouncer of the fourth *āśrama* (*saṃnyāsa*) abandons the sacred fire, property and fixed abode and begs *mādhukarī* ("in the manner of a bee," collecting a little nectar from each flower — a little food from each of several houses, never depleting one household). The classical implements are the ochre robe, the staff (*daṇḍa*), the water-pot (*kamaṇḍalu*) and the begging-bowl. Śaṅkara's medieval **Daśanāmī** orders institutionalized this on a quasi-monastic model; itinerant *sādhus* and *yogins* outside formal orders continue it. The *Saṃnyāsa Upaniṣads* (Olivelle 1992) prescribe the begging discipline in detail.

### Christian mendicant friars — Franciscans and Dominicans
Where earlier Benedictine monasticism was communally wealthy (monasteries held land), the 13th-century **friars** made corporate as well as personal poverty their charism. **Francis of Assisi** founded the Order of Friars Minor (approved 1209/1210); the Franciscan ideal was to own nothing individually *or in common*, living by manual work, begging and preaching — a stance that triggered the bitter 14th-c. controversy over apostolic poverty and the *usus pauper*. **Dominic** founded the Order of Preachers (1216), likewise mendicant, to sustain an itinerant preaching mission. The friar's begging (*quaestio*) distinguished the mendicant orders sharply from the landed monastic tradition and from the secular clergy.

### Sufi faqīr / darwīsh — faqr
Islamic mysticism makes **poverty (*faqr*)** a spiritual state, not merely an economic one: the *faqīr* ("poor man") is the one who owns nothing and needs nothing but God — grounding the ideal in the Qur'anic "O mankind, you are the poor (*al-fuqarāʾ*) before God" (35:15) and the Prophet's reported preference for poverty. The wandering **dervish** (Persian *darwīsh*) in his patched robe (*khirqa*), supported by alms and by the hospitality codes of *futuwwa*, is the outward figure; the inward reality is emptying the self before the Real. Unlike śramaṇa and friar mendicancy, Sufi *faqr* is often interior and compatible with ordinary work and family life, but the wandering mendicant dervish makes it visible.

## Cross-tradition significance

Mendicancy is best read as **convergent institutional evolution around a structural necessity**, with one important corridor of possible transmission and one clear case of independent parallel.

- **The receiving side of almsgiving.** The strongest analytic point is relational rather than genetic. Every major tradition institutionalizes giving to the poor and to religious specialists ([[ritual-zakat-charity|zakāt, tzedakah, dāna, the tithe, langar]]); a formalized class of holy poor is the necessary counterpart. Strenski (1983) and Ohnuma (2007) show the Buddhist alms-round functioning as *generalized exchange*: the bowl is the mechanism that lets the layperson generate merit. Mendicancy and almsgiving are two names for one circuit, which is why they recur together across unrelated traditions.

- **Shared Indian root, then convergence.** Within India the Buddhist, Jain and Hindu-saṃnyāsa mendicant patterns are **genuinely related** — they emerge from the common Gangetic **śramaṇa** milieu of the mid-1st millennium BCE, sharing the wandering-renouncer type, the begging discipline and the vocabulary of non-possession. This is real historical connection, not mere analogy.

- **Christian and Sufi mendicancy are independent convergences.** There is no credible evidence that the 13th-c. friars or the Sufi *fuqarāʾ* derived their begging practice from Indian precedent. They arise from within their own scriptural and monastic logics (apostolic poverty modelled on Christ and the sending of the Twelve in Matt 10:9–10; Qur'anic *faqr*). The resemblance to Buddhist and Jain mendicancy is a structural/typological parallel — the same solution reached from different premises — and should not be overstated into transmission.

- **The secular control case.** [[diogenes-of-sinope|Diogenes the Cynic]] shows that the mendicant form does not even require a religious rationale: the Cynic beggar-philosopher practised propertyless, itinerant begging as an exercise in rational self-sufficiency (*autarkeia*) rather than for merit or salvation (Desmond 2008). The Cynic case is the cleanest evidence that holy begging is a recurrent human *form* that different worldviews fill with different meaning.

## Disputes

- **Was mendicancy the original Buddhist ideal or a later idealization?** Schopen and others have argued from archaeology and monastic law that many historical monks were far more property-entangled (owning cells, gardens, even servants) than the bowl-and-robe image suggests; the pure mendicant is partly a normative ideal. Others read the *Vinaya* itself as evidence the discipline was real and early. The iconography is secure; its universality in practice is debated.
- **Apostolic-poverty controversy.** Whether Christ and the apostles owned nothing even in common was a live, church-dividing question in the 14th century (the Franciscan Spirituals vs. John XXII, *Cum inter nonnullos*, 1323, which condemned the strict thesis). The dispute shows mendicancy was contested *within* Christianity, not a settled ideal.
- **Begging vs. work.** Every mendicant tradition faced criticism that professional religious begging is parasitic; Protestant reformers attacked friar mendicancy sharply, and Weber's typology treats "world-rejecting" begging asceticism as one pole against the "inner-worldly" work ethic.
