# Fasting — Cross-Tradition Religious Abstention

The cross-tradition practice of voluntary abstention from food (+ sometimes water + sometimes other consumptions: sexual activity, speech, sleep, certain foods) for religious-spiritual purpose. **Every major religious tradition in the vault has formalized fasting practices**, making fasting one of the broadest cross-tradition convergences in religious-practice history. The structural-anthropological pattern: humans across cultures find ascetic food-abstention spiritually meaningful + religiously generative.

## Major instances by tradition

### Islamic Ramadan
The most institutionalized fast in any religious tradition: **one full lunar month per year** (the 9th month of the [[islamic-hijri-calendar|Hijri calendar]], Ramadan) of complete abstention from food + water + sexual activity from sunrise (Fajr) to sunset (Maghrib). One of the Five Pillars of Islam. The *iftar* (sunset meal) + *sahūr* (pre-dawn meal) bracket the daily fast. The 1-month duration + daily ~16-hour rigorous abstention makes Ramadan the most-physically-demanding institutional fast in world religion. ~1.8 billion Muslims observe globally.

### Christian Lent
**40 days of preparation before Easter** (mirroring Christ's 40 days in the wilderness; Matt 4:2). Food-restriction varies by tradition + era:
- **Eastern Orthodox**: rigorous (no meat, dairy, eggs, wine, oil for most of Lent; sometimes water-only days)
- **Pre-Vatican-II Catholic**: meat-abstention on Wednesdays + Fridays + Saturdays; one meal/day; no animal products
- **Post-Vatican-II Catholic**: minimal (meat-abstention on Fridays in Lent; fast on Ash Wednesday + Good Friday)
- **Protestant**: voluntary individual practice; revived in 20th-21st c. Anglican + ecumenical movements

### Jewish Yom Kippur
**~25 hours of complete abstention from food + water** (the "Day of Atonement"; [[hebrew-calendar]] Tishrei 10). The most-severe Jewish fast; non-observance is one of the few directly-Torah-mandated practices (Leviticus 16:29-31). Approximately 50% of secular Jews still observe Yom Kippur in some form — the highest religious-observance rate of any single Jewish practice. Additional Jewish fasts: Tisha B'Av (mourning for Temple destructions); 17th of Tammuz; fast of Esther; 3 dawn-to-dusk fasts during the year.

### Hindu Ekadashi + many regional fasts
**Ekadashi**: 11th day of each lunar fortnight (so ~24 days/year of partial-fast). Different schools observe differently — full fast, fruit-only, single-meal. Vaishnava Ekadashi observance is especially elaborated; the *Padma Purana* + *Skanda Purana* devote substantial material to Ekadashi practice.

Other Hindu fasts: **Navaratri** (9-night festival fast, twice yearly); **Karva Chauth** (women's day-long fast for husbands' welfare); **Maha Shivaratri** (Shiva-night fast); **Janmashtami** (Krishna-birthday fast); many regional + community-specific fasts.

### Jain Paryushan
**8-10 day annual fast** (the most-rigorous community-wide Jain religious observance). Some Jain practitioners undertake *Santhara* (also called *Sallekhana*) — voluntary terminal fasting unto death, a Jain ascetic-spiritual practice unique among world religions for its formalized fasting-unto-death; legally contested in modern India but Jain-tradition-recognized.

### Buddhist Uposatha
**Every full + new moon** (8 days/lunar month + 4 days/lunar month = ~12 days/month). Lay Theravada observance: 8 precepts (instead of normal 5) including no eating after noon, no sexual activity, no entertainment. Monastic observance: the Pātimokkha recitation. The *Uposatha* is the principal day-of-observance in Buddhist lay devotional life.

### Bahá'í Fast
**19 days in the month of ʿAláʼ** (February-March; the last month of the [[bahai-badi-calendar|Bahá'í Badíʿ calendar]]). Sunrise-to-sunset abstention from food + water + sexual activity. Structurally cognate with Ramadan (the Bahá'í tradition emerges from Islamic Iranian context); shorter (19 days vs 30) + falls in late winter rather than rotating lunar calendar.

### Indigenous vision-quest fasting
**Variable-duration food-and-water abstention** as transformational-initiatory practice in Native American + Aboriginal Australian + other indigenous traditions. The fasting context is typically initiatory (puberty rite, healer-training, life-transition); the spiritual goal is direct experience-of-the-sacred / spirit-encounter / vision-reception.

## Cross-tradition pattern

The cross-tradition fasting phenomenon exhibits several recurring structural elements:

1. **Calendrical anchoring**: fasts are typically anchored to specific dates / lunar phases / seasonal moments (Ramadan, Ekadashi, Lent, Yom Kippur, Uposatha) rather than scheduled at individual discretion. The communal-temporal-coordination is part of the practice
2. **Body-as-religious-instrument**: the body's hunger-state becomes religiously meaningful; the practitioner's relationship to food / appetite / desire becomes the meditation-object
3. **Communal solidarity**: fasting is typically observed in synchrony with one's religious community; the shared-abstention creates social-spiritual bonds
4. **Ethical-purificatory dimension**: fasting is typically paired with intensified prayer + ethical-attention + charitable giving; not merely physiological discipline but moral-spiritual-comprehensive practice
5. **Transformational claim**: each tradition claims fasting produces specific spiritual transformations (atonement, taqwa, purification, vision-reception, sympathy with the poor)

## The cross-tradition methodological-discipline finding

Caroline Walker Bynum's *Holy Feast and Holy Fast* (1987) and Rudolph Bell's *Holy Anorexia* (1985) document the late-medieval Christian female-saints' fasting tradition with critical-historical methodology that has illuminated cross-tradition fasting practice generally. The methodological insight: **religious fasting is genuinely transformational + has documented physiological + psychological effects + the religious-interpretive framework (sin / purity / atonement / divine-presence) shapes the practice's meaning**. The cross-tradition convergence on fasting reflects (a) universal human physiological response to food-abstention, AND (b) cross-cultural religious-interpretive frameworks for interpreting that response.

The cross-tradition fasting phenomenon is one of the most-pancultural religious practices documented in the vault — comparable in cross-tradition reach only to the prayer-bead family ([[mantra-japa-hindu]] + [[dhikr-sufi-remembrance]] + [[rosary-catholic-prayer]] + [[hesychasm-jesus-prayer]]).
