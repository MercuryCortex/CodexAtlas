# Homa / Havan — Fire-Offering Discipline

## Identity

**Homa** (Sanskrit *homa*, होम; Hindi/vernacular *havan* / *hawan*) is the Vedic-Hindu **fire-offering discipline**: the recurring pouring of consecrated oblations — clarified butter (*ghee* / *ājya*), grains, and wood-sticks (*samidh*) — into a consecrated fire while reciting mantras, so that **Agni**, the divine fire, conveys the offering upward to the gods. The word derives from the root *hu-* ("to pour as an oblation, to sacrifice into fire"), the same root that yields *hotṛ* (the "pourer" / invoking priest) and *āhavanīya* (the "offering fire"). It is deliberately catalogued here as a **practice** — a repeatable devotional discipline — and kept distinct from the ceremonial event-frame filed under rituals ([[ritual-yajna-vedic-fire]]): the *yajña* is the sacrificial occasion, while homa/*agnihotra* is the standing offering-act performed within and beyond it, from the twice-daily household fire to the greatest śrauta rites.

## In the Vedic and Hindu tradition

The discipline runs the full scale of Vedic and Hindu ritual life:

- **Agnihotra** — the minimal, twice-daily household offering at dawn and dusk (milk and ghee poured into the consecrated fire). It is the irreducible kernel: the householder who has established the sacred fires is obliged to feed them morning and evening. H.W. Bodewitz's *The Daily Evening and Morning Offering* (1976) is the standard study of its Brāhmaṇa codification, and David Knipe's *Vedic Voices* (2015) documents the families in Andhra who still maintain it.
- **Śrauta homa** — the solemn offerings maintained in the three (or five) sacred fires, culminating in the great soma-sacrifices and the apex **Agnicayana**, whose falcon-shaped altar of 10,800 bricks Frits Staal filmed in Kerala in 1975 and analysed in *Agni* (1983), arguing it may be the oldest surviving ritual in the world.
- **Havan / pūjā-homa** — the living popular form: a small fire-pit (*kuṇḍa*) fed with samidh, ghee, and grain to the rhythm of *svāhā*, closing weddings (the *saptapadī* around the marriage fire), house-blessings, and festival rites for hundreds of millions of contemporary Hindus.

In every form the logic is constant: the fire is not the object of worship but the **mouth of the gods** — Agni is *hotṛ*, the priest who eats the oblation and carries its essence as smoke to the heavens. The Ṛgveda opens (RV 1.1.1) with exactly this figure: "I praise Agni, the household priest, the divine minister of the sacrifice."

## Tantric and Buddhist homa

From the medieval period the offering-discipline is absorbed into **Tantra**. The Āgamic and Tantric manuals classify the *homa* by aim — commonly five types: *śāntika* (pacifying), *pauṣṭika* (increase/nourishing), *vaśīkaraṇa* and *ābhicārika* (subjugation and aggressive rites), and so on — with the shape of the *kuṇḍa* and the offering-substances prescribed to each aim.

Crucially, the discipline then **crosses into Buddhism**. The Vajrayāna fire-offering, chartered by the *Mahāvairocana-sūtra*, is carried through Indian and Central-Asian esoteric Buddhism into **Tibetan *sbyin sreg*** ("burnt offering") and, via **Kūkai** (774–835), into the Japanese **goma** (護摩) of the Shingon and Tendai schools. The Shingon goma is performed on a fixed hearth-altar (the *goma-dan*) before **Fudō Myōō** (Acala), within the cosmology of Mahāvairocana ([[vairocana]]). Richard K. Payne's *The Tantric Ritual of Japan* (1991) shows the Japanese rite retaining the Indian structure — the priest offers wood, then grains and ghee, into the fire as the deity's mouth — a thousand years and several religions removed from the Vedic hearth.

## Cross-tradition significance

The homa is one of the Atlas's cleanest cases of the two DIFFERENT kinds of cross-tradition link, and they must not be confused:

**1. Documented common-ancestor inheritance (Indo-Iranian).** Sanskrit *yajña* and Avestan *yasna* are **cognate** words (from Proto-Indo-European *\*yag̑-*, "to worship by fire"), and Vedic **Agni** and Zoroastrian **Ātar** ([[atar]]) are cognate divine-fire figures inherited from the common Proto-Indo-Iranian religion before the Indian/Iranian split (c. -1500). The Vedic homa and the Zoroastrian [[ritual-yasna-fire-ceremony|yasna]] are therefore **genetically related** fire-cults, not independent inventions (Boyce 1975). The disciplines then diverged: the Vedic tradition emphasises **offering-into** the fire (oblation-conveyance), while the Zoroastrian tradition emphasises **tending** an ever-burning fire and guarding its purity — Zoroastrians pray *facing* the fire, not *into* it.

**2. Documented later transmission (Buddhist, Hindu → East Asia).** The passage of the homa from Tantric Hinduism into Buddhist esoterism and thence to Tibet, China, and Japan is a **traceable historical borrowing** across a religious-frame change — the strongest kind of cross-tradition connection. Payne & Witzel's *Homa Variations* (2016) treats the whole span as one discipline changing across the *longue durée*, from the Vedic agnihotra to the Kyoto goma-dan.

Set against these secure links, the wider "fire as the required medium of human–divine communication" pattern — the Greek *thysia*, the Hebrew *ʿolah* burnt-offering, the Roman hearth — is best read as **typological convergence** (with, at most, diffuse Bronze-Age contact), not as descent from the Indo-Iranian homa. The honest hierarchy: *yajña/yasna* is a linguistic cognate (secure), the Buddhist goma is a documented transmission (secure), and the Mediterranean burnt-offerings are structural parallels (convergence).

## Disputes

- **Antiquity and continuity of the Agnicayana.** Staal (1983) reads the 10,800-brick falcon altar and its astronomical orientation as evidence of unbroken transmission from Vedic antiquity; more cautious scholars note that living performances are reconstructions guided by textual manuals and that "continuity" is partly a claim of the tradition itself. That the *agnihotra* has been maintained continuously is far better evidenced (Knipe 2015) than that the full Agnicayana has.
- **Direction of the Indo-Iranian derivation.** The mainstream view is that Agni and Ātar both descend from a Proto-Indo-Iranian divine-fire; older scholarship that made Iranian Ātar a borrowing from Vedic Agni (or vice versa) is not the dominant position (Boyce 1987).
- **How much the East-Asian goma preserves vs. re-invents.** Payne (1991) stresses genuine structural continuity from the Indian homa; others emphasise the Sinicised and Japanised accretions (the goma's assimilation to indigenous fire and kami cult), so that the goma is a transmitted *and* substantially transformed discipline rather than a frozen import.
