# Ramadan — Islamic Month-Long Daytime Fast

The Islamic **annual month-long observance** during **Ramaḍān** (the 9th month of the Islamic lunar calendar) of daytime (dawn-to-sunset) complete abstention from food, water, sexual activity, and smoking — combined with intensified prayer, Qurʾān recitation, charitable giving, and the pursuit of the **Laylat al-Qadr** (Night of Power) in the final ten days. Ramaḍān is one of the **Five Pillars of Islam** (*arkān al-Islām*) and the single most-widely-observed religious fasting practice on Earth (~1.8 billion practitioners). The month culminates in **Eid al-Fitr** — the three-day festival ending the fast and one of the two great Islamic religious festivals (with Eid al-Adha).

The foundational text is Qurʾān 2:183-185: *"O you who believe! Fasting is prescribed for you, as it was prescribed for those before you, that you may attain God-consciousness... The month of Ramaḍān in which was revealed the Qurʾān, a guidance for mankind, and clear signs of guidance and the criterion..."* The fast's theological purpose is *taqwā* (God-consciousness / God-mindfulness) — the cultivation of moral + spiritual attentiveness through bodily discipline. The month commemorates the Qurʾān's beginning of revelation in Laylat al-Qadr ("better than a thousand months" — Qurʾān 97:3).

## The daily structure — suhūr + ifṭār

The Ramaḍān day is structured around three meal-times that frame the fast:

1. **Suhūr** — the pre-dawn meal, eaten before *fajr* (true dawn, ~80 minutes before sunrise). Prophetic Sunna emphasizes eating suhūr even briefly: "Eat suhūr, for there is a blessing in it" (Bukhārī 1923).
2. **The fast** — from fajr to maghrib (sunset), ~12-17 hours depending on latitude + season. Complete abstention from food, water, sexual activity, smoking, and any intentional consumption.
3. **Ifṭār** — the sunset break-fast at *maghrib*. Traditionally broken first with dates + water following the Prophet's practice, then followed by maghrib prayer + the main evening meal.

The fast is generally considered medically + halakhically binding on all adult Muslims who are healthy + non-traveling. Exemptions: pre-pubescent children, the elderly + ill, pregnant + nursing women, menstruating women (who must make up the days later), travelers (similar make-up rule). The exemption rules are derived from Qurʾān 2:184-185 and elaborated in the hadith.

## Tarāwīḥ + Laylat al-Qadr + Iʿtikāf

The Ramaḍān night is structured around intensified worship:

- **Tarāwīḥ** — extended congregational night prayers (typically 20 rakʿas, varies by tradition) recited each night of Ramaḍān at the mosque. The full Qurʾān (30 *juzʾ*) is typically recited across the month so that the practitioner hears or recites the entire text by month's end (the *khatm* tradition).
- **Iʿtikāf** — the spiritual retreat in the mosque during the final 10 days. A practitioner withdraws from daily life entirely + devotes the period to prayer + Qurʾān + dhikr. The practice follows Muḥammad's Sunna: "The Prophet used to perform iʿtikāf in the last ten days of Ramaḍān" (Bukhārī 2025).
- **Laylat al-Qadr** — the Night of Power, observed in one of the odd nights of the final 10 days (commonly the 27th, though the exact night is intentionally not specified). Qurʾān 97 establishes its significance: it commemorates the night the Qurʾān began to be revealed; worship performed on this night is "better than a thousand months" of worship. Practitioners spend the night in prayer + Qurʾān recitation + dhikr.

## Theological frame — taqwā + the Qurʾān revelation

The Qurʾān 2:183 explicitly states the fast's purpose: *"...that you may attain *taqwā*"* (God-consciousness, God-mindfulness, righteous fear). The practitioner's bodily discipline through daytime abstention is understood as training the soul in the larger discipline of moral + spiritual attentiveness. The 14th-c. Ḥanbali jurist Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya systematized this connection: the fast is the bodily preparation for the soul's reception of divine guidance.

The month's commemorative dimension is equally central: Ramaḍān is the month in which the Qurʾān's revelation began (Laylat al-Qadr in this month, by Sunna). The annual Ramaḍān re-enacts that moment of revelation through the practitioner's recitation + listening to the full text + intensified attention to its meaning.

## Eid al-Fitr — the festival of breaking fast

The month ends at the next new moon (29 or 30 days after the start, by lunar observation). **Eid al-Fitr** ("Festival of the Breaking of the Fast") follows immediately:

- *Zakat al-Fitr* — obligatory charity given before the Eid prayer; a small measure of staple food (or equivalent) per household member, distributed to those who cannot afford festival meals
- *Salat al-ʿĪd* — congregational morning prayer at an open-air ground or large mosque
- *ʿĪd takbīrāt* — extended takbīr formulas chanted from the previous night
- Three days of festive meals, family visiting, gift-giving, charitable activity
- Variants across cultures (Indonesian *Lebaran*, Turkish *Ramazan Bayramı*, South Asian *Eid*, etc.) elaborate the global Islamic festival in regionally-distinctive forms

## MASSIVE-WIN — cross-tradition annual-fasting cluster + Yom Kippur transmission

Ramaḍān anchors the Islamic pole of the global **annual-fasting cluster**:

- **Islamic Ramaḍān** — month-long daytime complete fast (~1.8 billion practitioners)
- **Christian [[lent-christian-fast|Lent]]** — 40-day reduced/abstention fast + Easter climax
- **Jewish [[yom-kippur-fast|Yom Kippur]]** — 25-hour annual complete fast + atonement focus
- **Bahá'í Fast** — 19-day annual sunrise-sunset abstention (March)
- **Hindu Ekadashi + Mahashivaratri + Navaratri** + many regional annual fasts
- **Jain Paryushan** — 8-10 day annual fast
- **Buddhist Vassa** — 3-month monastic intensified-practice period

A specifically-documented **transmission within the Abrahamic family**: Goitein 1968 *Studies in Islamic History and Institutions* shows that the early Medinan community initially observed a **Yom Kippur-style ʿĀshūrāʾ fast** on 10 Muḥarram, following the Jewish community Muḥammad encountered in Medina. The hadith records: "When the Prophet came to Medina, he found the Jews fasting on the day of ʿĀshūrāʾ. He asked them about it. They said: 'This is the day on which God saved Moses from Pharaoh.' The Prophet said: 'We have more right to Moses than you do,' and he fasted on this day and ordered the Muslims to fast" (Bukhārī 2004). When Qurʾān 2:183-185 was revealed (c. 624 CE / 2 AH) establishing Ramaḍān as the primary Muslim fast, ʿĀshūrāʾ became voluntary; Shīʿa Muslims observe it today specifically for the Karbalāʾ commemoration (10 Muḥarram 680 CE = martyrdom of Ḥusayn).

This explicit historical transmission — Yom Kippur → ʿĀshūrāʾ → Ramaḍān as the developmental sequence of early-Islamic temporal-discipline — is one of the cleanest documented cases of cross-Abrahamic ritual inheritance.
