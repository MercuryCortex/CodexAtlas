# Antony the Great

## Identity
Egyptian Coptic peasant, born to a moderately prosperous Christian family in Coma (Middle Egypt) around 251. Heard the Gospel of Matthew read in church around 270 ("If thou wilt be perfect, sell what thou hast..."), gave away his inheritance, and entered a life of progressive withdrawal into the desert: first the village outskirts, then the tombs, then the abandoned fort at Pispir, finally the "Inner Mountain" near the Red Sea. Died around 356 at the canonical age of 105 — preserving full faculties to the end (per Athanasius). The single most consequential figure in the launch of Christian monasticism.

## Historical evidence
- **Athanasius's *Vita Antonii*** (~360) — written within four years of Antony's death and presented as direct testimony; the primary source.
- **Seven Letters** preserved in Coptic, Georgian, Latin, and Syriac versions. Their authenticity was long disputed; Samuel Rubenson 1995 mounted a strong case for genuine Antonian authorship — showing that the letters are theologically more sophisticated and philosophically more Origenist than Athanasius's hagiographic portrait suggests.
- *Apophthegmata Patrum* (Sayings of the Desert Fathers) — preserves Antonian sayings.
- Jerome's *Vita Pauli* (a partisan rival hagiography, claiming Paul of Thebes as the earlier hermit).

## Works
Seven letters (Rubenson 1995) — the only writings the historical Antony plausibly produced. They show familiarity with Alexandrian theological vocabulary and an Origenist anthropology.

## Role in the tradition
Founding hero of the eremitical (solitary) strand of Christian monasticism — the inheritance from him runs through the Egyptian desert (Macarius, Pambo, Sisoes, the *Apophthegmata*), into Palestine and Syria, into Cappadocia (Basil's rule explicitly invokes him), into Latin Christianity (via Athanasius's translated *Vita*; cf. Augustine, *Confessions* VIII.6 — the *Vita Antonii* is one of the immediate triggers of Augustine's conversion), and into the modern world as the archetype of the desert ascetic.

## Connections — substrate and influence
- The historical Antony's letters (Rubenson 1995) **show clear Origenist theological background** — anthropology of the rational soul fallen into materiality and ascending back to its source. This is Alexandrian Christian-Platonism, not the simple Coptic peasant piety of Athanasius's *Vita*. The Origen-Plotinus-Platonism substrate is direct.
- The Egyptian desert tradition Antony inaugurates has been read, especially by Goehring and Brown, against the **deep substrate of Egyptian asceticism**: the priest-purificatory traditions of the New Kingdom, the *katochoi* of the Serapeum (cultic recluses), and the Therapeutae described by Philo on the shores of Lake Mareotis (*De Vita Contemplativa*). Direct continuity is contested; geographic-cultural continuity (Egyptian sacred withdrawal in the desert margins) is real. Harmless 2004: 419–48.
- Antony's struggle with demons in the *Vita* draws on a Mediterranean substrate of spirit-warfare imagery — Egyptian, Jewish (1 Enoch, Qumran, the Pauline epistles), and Greco-Roman (the *daimones* of late-antique philosophical and magical thought).
- His withdrawal *eis tēn erēmon* (into the desert) echoes Israelite prophetic withdrawal (Elijah, John the Baptist) and ultimately Mesopotamian / Egyptian holy-man traditions.

## Disputes
- **Authorship of the Letters.** Rubenson 1995 made the strong case; earlier scholarship (Dörries) was skeptical. The case now favors authenticity.
- **Historicity of the *Vita Antonii*.** Athanasius is using Antony for his own theological purposes (anti-Arian, anti-Melitian, anti-Origenist in surface presentation). The basic biographical facts are credible; the speeches are Athanasian theological construction.
- **Was Antony literate?** The Athanasian *Vita* portrays him as illiterate; the Letters (if authentic) show him sophisticated. Best resolution: literate in Coptic, presented as illiterate for hagiographic purposes.
