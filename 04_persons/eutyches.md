# Eutyches

## Identity
Archimandrite of a large monastery outside the walls of Constantinople, godfather to the imperial chamberlain Chrysaphius (and hence politically protected at court). His name attaches to *Eutychianism* — the extreme one-nature Christology condemned at the Council of Chalcedon in 451 — under which the human nature of Christ is "swallowed up" by the divine "as a drop of honey in the sea." The Robber Council (*Latrocinium*) of Ephesus in 449 had vindicated him under Dioscorus of Alexandria; Chalcedon two years later reversed that judgment.

## Historical evidence
Documented in conciliar acta of the Constantinople synod (448), the Robber Council of Ephesus (449), and Chalcedon (451). The Tome of Leo (449) is written explicitly against him. Eutyches's own statements survive only in citations from these proceedings.

## Works
No independent treatises survive. His positions are preserved in his confession to the 448 synod (Flavian's tribunal) and his correspondence with Pope Leo.

## Role in the tradition
Eponym of "Eutychianism" / extreme Monophysitism (one-nature, with the human absorbed). The Oriental Orthodox churches (Coptic, Ethiopian, Syriac, Armenian) reject Chalcedon but also reject Eutyches — their *miaphysite* Christology (one composite nature, fully divine and fully human) follows Cyril rather than Eutyches.

## Connections — substrate and influence
- His "drop of honey in the sea" metaphor structurally resembles late-antique Platonist accounts of the soul's reabsorption into the One (Plotinus, *Enneads* VI.9) — though Eutyches is not philosophically sophisticated and the parallel is structural rather than genetic (Meyendorff 1989).
- The Christological dispute he triggered is a continuation of the Antiochene (dyophysite) vs. Alexandrian (monist-tending) tension that traces back to the differing schools of biblical interpretation — Alexandrian allegorical-Platonic vs. Antiochene historical-Aristotelian (Norris 1980: 1–30).

## Disputes
- **Eutyches's actual Christology.** Some scholars (Sellers 1953) argue his Christology was less extreme than caricatured; the *Latrocinium* acta show genuine confusion about what he believed.
