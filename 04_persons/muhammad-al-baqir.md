# Muhammad al-Baqir

## Identity
Son of [[ali-zayn-al-abidin]] and a daughter of [[hasan-ibn-ali]] — uniquely among the Imams, both of his parents were descendants of the Prophet (Hasani on his mother's side, Husayni on his father's). Born in Medina ~676; present as a young child (~4 years old) in the captive women's-party returning from Karbala. For Shi'i Islam, the **Fifth Imam**, succeeding his father ~712. His epithet *al-Bāqir* — from *baqara*, "to split open" — signifies "the one who splits open knowledge," i.e., who reveals the depths of religious science. Died in Medina ~733 (poisoned per Shi'i tradition; historical evidence inconclusive).

## Historical evidence
Better-documented in Islamic sources than several of the later Imams because of his teaching activity in Medina (a major early-Islamic teaching center) and his role as transmitter of *hadith* recognized in both Sunni and Shi'i traditions. The corpus of legal-theological-exegetical material attributed to him in the Shi'i hadith collections (Kulayni's *al-Kafi*, ibn Babawayh, al-Tusi) is vast — al-Baqir together with his son [[jafar-al-sadiq]] are the two single largest sources of attributed material in the Twelver hadith corpus.

## Works
No surviving compositions; the *attributed corpus* — preserved through his students and recorded in the Shi'i hadith collections — is enormous. Al-Baqir's reported teaching covers exegesis (tafsir), legal theory, ritual law, *imamate* doctrine, eschatology, and esoteric (*batin*) hermeneutics.

## Role in the tradition
**The doctrinal-pedagogical founder of mature Twelver Imami religious science.** Where Ali had been a political figure and Husayn a martyr, al-Baqir was a teacher in a stabilized political situation (the Umayyad caliphate, while hostile to the Imami line, did not — after Karbala — actively confront the Medinan Imams who pursued quietism). The teaching circle around al-Baqir in Medina included a wide range of students — Imami partisans, Mu'tazila proto-rationalists, Sunni jurists. The institutional foundation of *ḥawza* (Shi'i seminary) culture traces back to al-Baqir and his son al-Sadiq.

Amir-Moezzi 1994 argues that the *esoteric* dimension of Imami doctrine — the Imam as carrier of *ʿilm* (a divine knowledge transmitted within the Imami line), as the *batin* (inner) complement to the *zahir* (outer) Qur'an, as the cosmic-soteriological figure on whom the *ʿalam* (world) depends — receives its first systematic articulation under al-Baqir and al-Sadiq. The doctrine of *ʿisma* (Imam's infallibility) likewise crystallizes here. This is the period when Shi'i Islam becomes recognizably *Shi'i* in the doctrinal sense, not merely *partisan* in the political sense.

### The Zaydi divergence
Al-Baqir's half-brother **Zayd ibn Ali** (d. 740) — son of Zayn al-Abidin by a different mother — pursued the opposite strategic line from al-Baqir's quietism. Zayd led a Kufan revolt against the Umayyad caliph Hisham in 740 and was killed; his head displayed in Damascus. Zayd's partisans — the **Zaydiyya** — became the founding community of *Zaydi Shi'ism* (the "Fivers"), which holds the imamate to belong to any qualified descendant of Hasan or Husayn willing to claim it by uprising (*khurūj*), not necessarily through the designated Imami line. Zaydi Shi'ism became politically dominant in Yemen from the 9th c. (the Rassid imamate, founded by al-Hadi ila al-Haqq in 893) through the 1962 Yemeni Republican revolution, and survives as the religious tradition of much of northern Yemen including the Houthi movement. The Zaydi divergence is the *first* major split in the Shi'i Imami line (the second being the Ismaili divergence at al-Sadiq's succession).

## Disputes
- **Esotericism: how early?** Amir-Moezzi argues that the *batin* / esoteric dimension is constitutive of early Imami doctrine from al-Baqir / al-Sadiq onward; the modern rationalist Twelver tradition (post-Mulla Sadra and especially post-19th-c. Shi'i modernism) has tended to downplay or rationalize the early esoteric layer. Bayhom-Daou 2003 and other critics argue Amir-Moezzi over-reads later Buyid-era esotericism into the earlier strata. Both readings register.

- **Cause of death.** Standard Shi'i tradition (poisoned by Caliph Hisham or his agents). Critical assessment: plausible but not securely demonstrable; the *imams-as-martyrs* topos generates strong narrative pressure toward poisoning attributions even when the contemporary evidence is thin.

## Cross-tradition connections
- ↔ [[ali-zayn-al-abidin]] (father), [[jafar-al-sadiq]] (son) — the father-son pair al-Baqir and al-Sadiq are the doctrinal founders of mature Imami Shi'ism.
- ↔ [[tradition-shia-islam]] — definitive teacher.
- ↔ **Zaydi Shi'ism** — first major divergence in the Shi'i Imami line at al-Baqir's generation (Zayd ibn Ali's revolt and the Zaydi tradition).
- ↔ [[tradition-sufism]] — al-Baqir is in several Sufi *silsila* lineages of esoteric transmission.
- ↔ Early Sunni jurisprudence — al-Baqir taught (and was respected by) Sunni jurists including (per Shi'i tradition, more contestedly per Sunni) Abu Hanifa.
