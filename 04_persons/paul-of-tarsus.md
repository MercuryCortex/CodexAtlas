# Paul of Tarsus

## Identity
Paul of Tarsus (~+5 to ~+65 CE) was a Pharisaically-trained Jewish Roman citizen from Tarsus in Cilicia who, after an initial period of persecuting the early Jesus-movement, experienced a visionary christophany (Gal 1:11–17), reinterpreted his life as apostle to the Gentiles, and through ~25 years of missionary travel, congregational founding, and extensive letter-writing established the Gentile-Christian movement that became the dominant form of Christianity. He is the author of the **earliest extant Christian literature** (the undisputed seven letters, ~+50/+58) and **the single most influential theologian of Christianity** after Jesus himself.

## Historical evidence
Among the best-documented figures of the 1st century. Three streams:
1. **His own letters** — primary, self-authored, datable, geographically specific.
2. **Acts of the Apostles** — narrative biography by a sympathetic but distinct author (~+85/+95), with notable theological framing and some discrepancies from the letters.
3. **External early Christian attestation** — 1 Clement (~+95), Ignatius (~+110), Polycarp (~+115) all refer to Paul as an established apostolic figure.

For this vault, Paul matters as: (a) the *engine* of Christianity's break from Torah-observance and its Gentile expansion; (b) the early formulator of christological language (Phil 2:6–11 the "Christ hymn", 1 Cor 15:3–5 the resurrection kerygma); (c) the source-text behind much of later Christian soteriology (justification, atonement, union-with-Christ); (d) the figure Marcion would later canonize (in stripped-down form) as the *only* genuine apostle, prompting the proto-orthodox response to canonize a fuller Pauline corpus.

## Works
- Undisputed seven: Romans, 1–2 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, 1 Thessalonians, Philemon.
- Disputed (mainstream critical: pseudonymous): Ephesians, Colossians, 2 Thessalonians (deutero-Pauline); 1–2 Timothy, Titus (Pastorals — near-certainly pseudonymous, early 2nd c.); Hebrews (ancient attribution, modern consensus: not Pauline).
- Later legendary: *Acts of Paul and Thecla*, *3 Corinthians*, *Apocalypse of Paul*.

## Role in the tradition
**Christianity**: the apostolic theologian; through Augustine and Luther his epistles drive Western soteriology.

**Gnostic Christianity**: Valentinians (Heracleon, Theodotus, the *Tripartite Tractate*) read Paul as *their* apostle; the spiritual/psychic/material anthropology of 1 Cor 2–3 is a Valentinian touchstone. Pagels (*The Gnostic Paul*, 1975) demonstrates the Valentinian appropriation in detail.

**Marcionism**: the *only* genuine apostle; Paul + a stripped-down Luke = Marcion's canon.

**Modern Pauline scholarship**: the Lutheran-Augustinian reading of Paul (as critic of "works-righteousness Judaism" and proclaimer of individual justification by faith) has been substantially revised since E.P. Sanders (1977), with the "New Perspective" (Sanders, Dunn, Wright) and "Paul within Judaism" (Fredriksen, Eisenbaum, Nanos) reframing Paul's argument as primarily about Jewish-Gentile community boundaries.

## Disputes
- **Damascus-road experience.** Visionary (Paul's own description, Gal 1) — what historians can say beyond "subjective conviction of christophany" is contested.
- **Relationship with Jerusalem apostles.** Galatians 2 (Paul's view: he confronted Peter at Antioch; James's emissaries were the problem) vs. Acts 15 (Luke's view: amicable Jerusalem Council resolution). Discrepancy is real and theologically loaded.
- **Authorship of disputed letters.** Mainstream: Pastorals certainly pseudonymous; Colossians/Ephesians/2 Thessalonians probably; conservative scholarship retains all 13.
- **End of life.** Traditional martyrdom under Nero ~+64/+65 is widely accepted but not documented in surviving sources.
