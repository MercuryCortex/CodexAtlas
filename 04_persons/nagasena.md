# Nāgasena

## Identity
Nāgasena was a Buddhist monk of the late 2nd c. BCE, born (per the Pali tradition) in a brahmin family near Kashmir or Punjab, ordained in the early-Buddhist sectarian milieu (Sarvāstivāda or related Vibhajjavāda traditions), who became the philosophical interlocutor of King [[menander-i-soter|Menander I Sōter]] of the Indo-Greek kingdom — the dialogues preserved in the Pali *Milindapañha* (and its Chinese parallel *Nāgasena-bhikṣu-sūtra*). Nāgasena is the figure who articulates the no-self (*anātman*) doctrine through the famous chariot-simile ("As the chariot is a designation arising from the assembling of axle, wheels, frame, etc., so 'Nāgasena' is a designation arising from the assembling of hair, nails, teeth, skin, bones, viscera, mental states, etc., and there is no permanent self underlying it"), the standard reference-argument for Buddhist anātman analysis from the 2nd c. BCE forward.

## Historical evidence
- **Pali**: the Milindapañha treats Nāgasena as historical; the frame-narrative places him in the geography and political milieu of Menander's reign with internal consistency.
- **Chinese**: the *Nāgasena-bhikṣu-sūtra* (T 1670 A and B, translated into Chinese in two separate recensions ~3rd-4th c. CE from a non-Pali Indic source) preserves a parallel version of the dialogue with broadly the same content and the same two principal figures. This independent recension is the strongest argument that Nāgasena was a historical figure with a real connection to a Greek-king dialogue — the Pali and Chinese traditions inherit a shared earlier core.
- **No Greek attestation**: Plutarch and Strabo know Menander but do not name his Buddhist interlocutor. This is consistent with Nāgasena being a historical Indian monk known mainly to the Indian tradition.

## Works
None directly. The Milindapañha is his canonical record. Modern scholarship treats the work as substantially composite (a core dialogue of perhaps 3-4 chapters dated to ~-100 to +50; expanded with later philosophical accretions through ~4th c. CE).

## Role in the tradition
- **In Theravāda**: Nāgasena's arguments in the Milindapañha are standard reference-points for Theravāda doctrinal exposition. The Pali Milindapañha is canonical (in the Khuddaka Nikāya in the Burmese and Thai recensions). His chariot-simile (Milindapañha 2.1.1) is the foundational Buddhist *anātman* analysis and is cited in almost every subsequent doctrinal exposition.
- **In Sarvāstivāda and broader early Buddhism**: the Chinese recensions show Nāgasena was claimed by the northern Buddhist tradition as well; Demieville 1924 argues the original was Sarvāstivāda, with the Pali version a southern adaptation.
- **In modern comparative philosophy**: Nāgasena's chariot-simile is the classic Buddhist analogue of Derek Parfit's reductionist analysis of personal identity. Parfit himself acknowledged the parallel in *Reasons and Persons* (1984); the connection is now a standard reference in analytic philosophy of mind (Siderits, *Buddhism as Philosophy*, 2007).

## Disputes
- **Historicity vs. literary construction**: was Nāgasena a real monk who met Menander, or is he a literary figure created by the Pali tradition to frame a doctrinal exposition? Modern consensus (Mendelson 1994, Mairs 2014, Halkias 2014): the historical core is likely real (a Buddhist monk did engage Menander in some context), but the elaborated dialogue is a literary genre product. The Chinese parallel-recension argues for a real historical core.
- **School affiliation**: Sarvāstivāda or Theravāda? The Chinese recensions suggest Sarvāstivāda or related northern school; the Pali transmission is Theravāda. The original probably pre-dates the southern-northern split being fully sharp.
- **Dates**: anchored by Menander's reign (-165 to -130 per Bopearachchi 1991), so Nāgasena floruit is ~-150 to -130.
