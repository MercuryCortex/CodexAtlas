# Yajnavalkya

## Identity
The principal sage-protagonist of the *Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad* — the earliest major Upaniṣad, datable to roughly the 8th–7th c. BCE — and traditionally treated as the founder of the *Śukla* (White) Yajurveda recension, the *Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa* lineage, and (much later, anachronistically) the *Yājñavalkya Smṛti* dharmaśāstra. The historical Yājñavalkya is best located in the Videha (modern Mithila) court of King Janaka, who in the BĀU's narrative is the patron and interlocutor of Yajnavalkya's most famous teaching events. The literary figure of Yajnavalkya in the BĀU is the most fully developed Upaniṣadic protagonist: brilliant, combative in debate (the *brahmodya* dialogue contests in BĀU 3 with Gārgī and others are central episodes), instructive to his wife Maitreyī (BĀU 2.4 and 4.5 — the famous *ātman*-realization dialogue), the holder of two wives (Maitreyī and Kātyāyanī), and the first sage in the canon to articulate the *neti-neti* ("not this, not this") apophatic teaching about ultimate reality.

## Historical evidence
- The BĀU itself (~8th–7th c. BCE composition; redacted by ~6th c. BCE) is the primary documentary source.
- The Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa references and citations of Yajnavalkya as a ritual authority.
- Subsequent Vedic and dharmaśāstra tradition's consistent treatment of Yajnavalkya as a foundational sage.
- Witzel 2003 reconstructs the historical Kuru → Videha cultural transition (~9th–8th c. BCE) in which the figure of Yajnavalkya is anchored — the move from western Kuru-Pañcāla Vedic ritualism to eastern Videha philosophical-Upaniṣadic reflection.

## Works
The traditional attributions:
- **The Śukla Yajurveda recension and the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa**: traditionally received from the sun-god Sūrya in a vision after a rupture with his teacher Vaiśampāyana (the legend is structurally interesting but not biographically reliable).
- ***Yājñavalkya Smṛti*** (Dharmaśāstra): a major medieval Hindu law-text. The text itself dates ~3rd–5th c. CE — many centuries later than the historical Yajnavalkya — and is a classic case of pseudepigraphy (authoritative attribution to an ancient sage of a much later composition).

## Role in the tradition
- **Foundational Upaniṣadic philosophical voice**: in the BĀU dialogues Yajnavalkya articulates the doctrines that became foundational for the subsequent Vedānta:
  - The identification of *brahman* (cosmic reality) with *ātman* (the self) — BĀU 4.4–5.
  - The *neti-neti* (apophatic) characterization of ultimate reality — BĀU 2.3.6, 4.4.22.
  - The teaching that liberation comes through *knowledge* of *brahman*, not through ritual works alone — the central Upaniṣadic move and a sharp tension with the preceding Brahmaṇa ritualist tradition.
  - The doctrine of *karma* and rebirth in the form that becomes the standard Indian-religions framework — BĀU 3.2.13 (the first clear textual occurrence of post-mortem action-fruition in the Indian record).
- **Founder of one tradition of Vedic ritual transmission**: the Śukla Yajurveda / Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa lineage is the most institutionally important ritual transmission tradition in classical Hinduism.
- **Pseudepigraphic anchor for medieval dharmaśāstra**: the *Yājñavalkya Smṛti* is a major source for medieval Hindu legal-ethical practice via the commentary of Vijñāneśvara (*Mitākṣarā*, 11th c.) and Aparārka.

## Cross-tradition connections
- **Earliest documentable Indian articulation of philosophical non-dualism**: BĀU 4.4.5 ("This great unborn Self is none other than the one consisting of cognition...") is among the earliest documentable articulations of philosophical non-dualism in any tradition. The position recurs across world philosophy — in Parmenides (~6th–5th c. BCE), in the Daoist *Daodejing* (~6th–4th c. BCE per current dating), in late-antique Neoplatonism (Plotinus, 3rd c. CE), in the Buddhist Yogācāra "consciousness-only" school, in Sufi *waḥdat al-wujūd* (Ibn Arabi, 13th c.), in Spinoza's substance-monism (17th c.). The cross-tradition recurrence raises the comparative-philosophical question whether non-dualism is a position to which sustained reflection converges; mainstream comparative philosophy (Halbfass 1988, Tuck 1990) treats the parallels as structural recurrences rather than direct transmissions.
- **Karma-rebirth doctrine emerges into the Indian-religions common framework**: BĀU 3.2.13's articulation of post-mortem action-fruition is the earliest clear textual articulation of what becomes the shared karma-rebirth-liberation framework of Hindu, Buddhist, and Jain traditions — a cross-tradition substrate later articulated differently by each school but tracing back to Upaniṣadic Yajnavalkya-style sources.
- **Mithila as proto-philosophical region**: the Videha-Kuru cultural transition (~9th–7th c. BCE) is the structural-cultural matrix from which both the Upaniṣadic reform of Vedic ritualism and (a century or two later) the Buddhist and Jain śramaṇa rejections of Brahmanism emerge. Yajnavalkya is the Upaniṣadic-Brahmanical wing; the Buddha (~5th c. BCE, Magadha) and Mahāvīra (~6th c. BCE, Vaiśālī-Videha) are the śramaṇa wings of the same regional intellectual ferment.

## Disputes
- **Historicity**: the consensus is that there is a historical kernel — a sage-priest figure in the Kuru-Videha cultural transition — overlaid with extensive Upaniṣadic-literary construction. The BĀU's Yajnavalkya is not a verbatim historical figure but a literary protagonist organized around a proto-historical center.
- **Dating of the Upaniṣadic core**: the BĀU is conventionally dated 800–600 BCE; some scholars (Witzel) argue for ~750 BCE; others would push later toward 600 BCE.
- **The *Yājñavalkya Smṛti*'s pseudepigraphic attribution** is firm in scholarship; the text is part of the late-Gupta dharmaśāstra revival.
