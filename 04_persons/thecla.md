# Thecla

## Identity
A young woman of Iconium (modern Konya, central Turkey) of marriageable age, who — per the *Acts of Paul and Thecla* — heard Paul preach a strongly *encratite* (sexually renunciatory) version of Christianity through her window, broke off her engagement to Thamyris, was persecuted by family and city, survived two attempted executions (burning, wild beasts), baptized herself in a vat of seals in the arena, dressed as a man, taught and preached widely, and died honored at Seleucia. Standardly considered a literary-legendary figure rather than a historical one, but her cult was enormous in late antiquity.

## Historical evidence
The *Acts of Paul and Thecla* (~+185) is our only source; **Tertullian's *On Baptism* 17** (~+200) is the first datable external attestation, and is uniformly hostile: Tertullian condemns the book as the composition of an Asian presbyter who confessed to writing it "out of love for Paul" and was deposed — and complains that women were using its example to claim baptismal and teaching authority. This testimony (a) anchors the AP&T to before 200, (b) implicates it in early 3rd-c. debates about women's ministry, (c) shows that the text was a *practical* problem for proto-orthodox church order, not merely a curiosity.

## Works
None herself. The AP&T became one of the most-translated and most-circulated Christian texts of late antiquity — surviving in Greek, Syriac, Coptic, Arabic, Armenian, Slavonic.

## Role in the tradition
**Cult of saints**: Thecla's shrine at Seleucia (modern Silifke, southern Turkey) was a major Mediterranean pilgrimage site through Late Antiquity; her cult competed with male-apostolic cults. **Women's ministry**: the AP&T's depiction of her teaching, baptizing, and preaching was deployed in late-antique and modern debates about women's authority — affirmatively by some, condemned by Tertullian and the Pastoral Epistles tradition.

**Modern critical scholarship** (MacDonald 1983; Davis 2001; Pervo 2014): reads the AP&T as preserving (under male redaction) authentic women's oral storytelling about an exemplary heroine — a *counter-narrative* to the Pastoral-Epistles silencing of women.

## Disputes
- **Historicity.** No external attestation; mainstream view treats her as legendary.
- **Date of the AP&T.** Early 180s based on Tertullian; the underlying oral tradition may be earlier.
- **Author.** Tertullian says an unnamed Asian presbyter, deposed for confessing — repeated by Jerome.
