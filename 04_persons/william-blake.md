# William Blake

## Identity

William Blake (1757–1827) was a London engraver, poet, and visionary who created one of the most elaborate private mythological systems in Western literature — simultaneously the most fully developed modern reception of Gnostic cosmogony and an independent re-invention of Kabbalistic theosophy from a Protestant English workshop. Blake was a working artisan (he engraved his own illuminated books by hand, on copper plates, and hand-colored each copy), a political radical (he sympathized with both the American and French Revolutions and wore the red Phrygian cap until the Terror), a theological rebel (he attacked the Church of England and Deism with equal ferocity), and a visionary who claimed to see and speak with angels, the prophet Isaiah, and the dead. 

He was almost entirely unknown in his lifetime. The posthumous rediscovery began with Alexander Gilchrist's 1863 biography; Yeats and Ellis's 1893 Hermetic edition made him a canonical figure. He is now considered one of the major English poets and one of the most significant visionary artists in Western history.

## The Mythological System

Blake created an original mythology that is simultaneously a cosmogony, a theodicy, a psychology, and a theory of history. Its core figures:

### Albion — the Cosmic Human (= Adam Kadmon / Anthropos)
**Albion** is Blake's name for the primordial universal Human — the cosmic being whose body *is* the universe. In the unfallen state, Albion contains all things within himself; the "Four Zoas" are his four component faculties. The fall of Albion (from cosmic unity into divided self-consciousness) *is* the fall — the event that creates the broken, divided world of ordinary experience.

This is the [[adam-kadmon]] tradition in Romantic disguise. Kathleen Raine (1968) demonstrated that Blake derived the Albion concept from Thomas Taylor's Neoplatonic translations and from the Kabbalistic tradition he received through Jacob Boehme. But Blake radicalized it: in the Kabbalistic tradition, Adam Kadmon falls into the broken vessels (*shevirat ha-kelim*); in Blake's tradition, Albion falls into the sleep of materialism and self-enclosure. The restoration of Albion (*Jerusalem*) is the cosmic tikkun.

**MASSIVE WIN**: Albion / Adam Kadmon / Gnostic Anthropos / Vedic Purusha / Zoroastrian Gayōmart / Paul's "Body of Christ" — the cross-tradition claim that **the cosmos has a human form**, and that the restoration of cosmic wholeness is the same event as the restoration of the primordial Human. See [[anthropos-myth]].

### Urizen — the Demiurge (= Gnostic Yaldabaoth / Plato's Demiurge inverted)
**Urizen** (pun: "Your Reason" + "Horizon") is Blake's principal antagonist — the god of reason, law, measurement, and abstraction. He is the fallen aspect of Albion who has separated himself from the other Zoas and attempted to rule alone. Urizen *creates* the fallen material world as an act of self-imposed limitation:

> "He formed a line and a plummet / To divide the Abyss beneath: / He form'd a dividing rule... / He form'd scales to weigh, / He form'd massy weights, / He form'd a brazen quadrant..."
> — *The Book of Urizen* 1794

This is *exactly* the Gnostic Demiurge narrative. In the Apocryphon of John, Yaldabaoth (the Demiurge) creates the material world by separating and measuring, thinking himself the highest God, unaware of the divine light above him. Blake arrived at the same structure independently — through Boehme, Milton (*Paradise Lost*'s God, whom Blake despised), and his own visionary logic.

Blake explicitly identified the Urizen-god with the tyrannical God of conventional religion: "The God who commands you / Is the God of this world only — and the Prince of the Air." His *Marriage of Heaven and Hell* (1790–1793) argues that the "God" worshipped by the church is Urizen — a projection of repressive reason — while the true divine energy is identified with what the church calls "the Devil" (the creative imagination, Los).

### Los — the Creative Prophetic Spirit (= Logos / tikkun)
**Los** is the faculty of Imagination and Prophecy — the creative principle that works within history to repair the fractured cosmic order. He is the smith-god who forges the fallen world's materials into forms that can become vehicles of liberation. His furnaces are the instruments of creative-redemptive transformation.

Structurally: Los is the [[logos-cosmic-reason]] — the divine creative principle working within time — combined with the Lurianic *tikkun* dynamic: the incremental creative-redemptive repair of the broken world. Where Urizen measures and freezes, Los creates and liberates.

Blake's *Milton* enacts Los-as-creative-redemption: the poet Milton descends from heaven to correct his own errors (the Puritan rigidity that had made Urizen into God) — entering Blake's foot and inspiring his work. The poet's creative act is cosmic repair.

### Jerusalem — the Divine Feminine (= Sophia / Shekinah)
**Jerusalem** (the title figure of Blake's longest and most complex work) is Albion's *Emanation* — his feminine counterpart, the divine feminine principle that has been rejected and driven into exile by Albion's fall into self-enclosure. She wanders through history, weeping, while Albion sleeps. The conclusion of the poem is Albion's awakening and his reunion with Jerusalem — cosmic tikkun, the divine marriage restored.

**Jerusalem** = [[shekhinah]] in structural identity: the exiled divine feminine presence who must be reunited with the divine masculine before cosmic restoration can occur. Blake knew the Kabbalistic tradition through Boehme (who was deeply influenced by Kabbalistic ideas), through Agrippa, and through Yeats-era Hermeticism in retroactive reception.

### The Four Zoas — the Tetramorphic Cosmic Human
Urizen / Luvah / Tharmas / Urthona are the four Zoas — the four faculties of Albion (Reason / Emotion / Body / Imagination). In the unfallen state they exist in dynamic harmony; the fall is their dissociation from each other. Blake explicitly connects them to the four faces of Ezekiel's chariot (Man / Lion / Ox / Eagle = the Tetramorph = the four Evangelists' symbols). The Zoas are Blake's version of the partzufim (divine faces) of Lurianic Kabbalah — four aspects of the unified divine that have become fragmented.

## The Sources Blake Drew On

1. **Jacob Boehme** — the primary theological source. Blake owned and annotated Boehme's works. From Boehme he got: the *Ungrund* (the groundless abyss, structurally equivalent to Ein Sof), the idea that God contains both wrath and love as complementary principles, the notion of the Fall as a voluntary divine self-limitation, and the alchemical language of spiritual transformation. See [[jakob-boehme]], [[ungrund]].

2. **Emanuel Swedenborg** — initially an influence, then a target. Blake was briefly a member of the New Jerusalem Church (Swedenborgian) in the 1780s. He filled his copy of Swedenborg's *Heaven and Hell* with scathing annotations when he concluded Swedenborg had made Urizen into God (rational order as the divine). See [[emanuel-swedenborg]].

3. **Thomas Taylor** — the "English Pagan," the first translator of Plato and the Neoplatonists into English. Taylor's Neoplatonic translations gave Blake the concept of the Fall as a descent of the Soul into matter, and the cosmic-human form (*Anthropos*).

4. **The Bible (KJV)** — Blake knew the KJV backwards and forwards and subverted it constantly. He identified the Jehovah of the Old Testament as Urizen; Christ as Los (the creative divine imagination); Satan as the human capacity for accusation and moral self-righteousness (not "the Devil" as conventionally conceived).

5. **Milton's *Paradise Lost*** — Blake's most important literary source and antagonist. He claimed "Milton was of the Devil's party without knowing it" — meaning that Milton's Satan (dynamic, creative, rebellious) is the true hero, while Milton's God (authoritarian, legalistic) is Urizen. Blake's *Milton* (1804) is a correction of *Paradise Lost*.

## MASSIVE WIN: Blake as Independent Convergence

Blake arrived at the Gnostic cosmogony, the Kabbalistic divine-exile narrative, and the Hermetic cosmic-human doctrine through independent synthesis — not through direct access to primary Gnostic or Kabbalistic texts. He read Boehme (who had absorbed Kabbalistic ideas) and Taylor (who transmitted Neoplatonism). The result is a mythology that is structurally isomorphic with the Gnostic-Kabbalistic tradition while being recognizably *English* and *Romantic*.

**The convergence points:**
- Urizen creating the fallen world by measurement and law = Gnostic Demiurge (Yaldabaoth) creating the material world
- Albion as the fallen cosmic human = Adam Kadmon / Purusha / Anthropos
- Jerusalem as the exiled divine feminine = Shekinah / Sophia
- Los's furnaces of creative redemption = tikkun olam (the incremental creative repair of the broken world)
- The Four Zoas' dissociation = shevirat ha-kelim (the shattering of the vessels)
- The Four Zoas' reintegration into Albion = tikkun / apokatastasis (cosmic restoration)

The fact that a London engraver in 1800, working from Boehme and the KJV, independently reconstructed a myth structurally identical to the Jewish-Gnostic tradition of 2nd-century Alexandria — *this* is the MASSIVE WIN. It suggests these are not arbitrary cultural constructs but deep mappings of human spiritual experience.

## Reception

Blake was rediscovered in the Victorian period and canonized in the 20th century. Key receptions:
- **W.B. Yeats** (1893): read Blake as a Hermetic-Kabbalist; his edition with Ellis established Blake's connection to the occult-esoteric tradition
- **Northrop Frye** (*Fearful Symmetry*, 1947): the academic canonization; read Blake as a systematic mythologist in the tradition of Dante and Milton
- **Kathleen Raine** (*Blake and Tradition*, 1968): the definitive source-study; proved Blake's deep debts to Neoplatonism, Hermeticism, and Boehme's Kabbalistic theosophy
- **Allen Ginsberg**: Blake as the prophetic ancestor of the Beat Generation; Ginsberg's 1948 Blake-vision inaugurated his career
- **Philip Pullman** (*His Dark Materials*, 1995–2000): Pullman's Dust/Dæmons/Authority system is a Blake-influenced re-reading of Gnostic cosmogony
