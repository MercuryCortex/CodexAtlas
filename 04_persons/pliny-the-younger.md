# Pliny the Younger

## Identity
Roman senator, lawyer, and imperial administrator of equestrian and senatorial rank, nephew and heir of [[pliny-the-elder|Pliny the Elder]] (who died observing Vesuvius's eruption in 79). Governor of **Bithynia-Pontus** ~+111/+113 as imperial legate under Trajan. His correspondence with Trajan, preserved as Book 10 of his collected *Epistulae*, includes **Epistle 10.96** — Pliny's report on Christians and request for guidance on how to handle them — and Trajan's brief reply (Ep. 10.97). Together these are the **earliest extant Roman administrative discussion of Christianity** and a foundational source for the legal-procedural status of Christians in the empire.

## Historical evidence
The Epistles 10 collection survives intact through the manuscript tradition; the Pliny-Trajan correspondence is uncontested. Pliny describes Christians: meeting before dawn to sing a hymn "to Christ as to a god" (*carmen Christo quasi deo*); eating ordinary (not Thyestean) food; binding themselves by oath not to commit theft, adultery, fraud; deaconesses (*ministrae*) tortured to extract information. Pliny found their religion to be merely *superstitio prava et immodica* (a depraved and excessive superstition) without further criminality. Trajan's reply: do not seek them out; if accused and convicted, punish; anonymous denunciations to be disregarded.

## Works
- *Epistulae* in 10 books
- *Panegyricus* (~+100) — speech praising Trajan
- Lost: legal speeches

## Role in the tradition
- **The first extra-Christian Roman testimony to Christian practice** (~+112) — predating Tacitus (~+115/+117) and Suetonius (~+121). The "carmen Christo quasi deo" detail is the earliest external attestation of Christian Sunday-morning hymnody and of *Christ* (not "the leader" or some other vague figure) as the object of worship.
- **The legal procedure**: the *nominis ipsius* question (is being a Christian itself a crime, or only behaviors of Christians?) is set by Pliny and Trajan as a precedent that lasts essentially through Decius's universal-sacrifice edict (250). Christians could be punished simply for the *name* if denounced and refusing to recant.
- **Apologetic engagement**: Tertullian (*Apol.* 2.6–8) uses the Pliny-Trajan correspondence as a demonstration of Roman procedural incoherence — punishing the name without proven crimes.

## Disputes
- **Authenticity of Ep. 10.96–97**: never seriously contested.
- **Date of Pliny's tenure**: ~+111/+113 is mainstream.
- **Pliny's accuracy on Christian practice**: he is gathering information from accused and former Christians; the details are remarkably well-corroborated by Christian sources of the period.
