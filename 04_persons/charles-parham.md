# Charles Fox Parham (1873–1929)

## Overview
Charles Fox Parham was an American Holiness preacher who formulated the foundational doctrinal claim of classical Pentecostalism: that speaking in tongues is the *initial physical evidence* of Spirit-baptism. His Bethel Bible School in Topeka, Kansas (1900) was the site where student Agnes Ozman spoke in tongues on January 1, 1901 — the event Parham identified as the restored New Testament pattern. He was the theological antecedent who taught [[william-seymour]] the tongues-as-evidence doctrine. But Parham was later marginalized from the movement he initiated — rejected by the Azusa Street community he attempted to control — and his legacy is complicated by racial hostility and later scandals.

## Early life and Holiness formation
Parham was born on June 4, 1873, in Muscatine, Iowa. He showed early religious inclinations and experienced a healing he attributed to divine intervention in his youth. He was ordained in the Methodist Episcopal church but left over financial and administrative conflicts and became an independent Holiness preacher. He accepted the Holiness (Wesleyan) doctrine of entire sanctification as a second blessing but increasingly pressed toward a *third* experience — a further baptism of the Spirit that would empower believers for the end-times mission.

## Bethel Bible School, Topeka (1900) and the tongues doctrine
In October 1900, Parham opened Bethel Bible School in a large mansion known as "Stone's Folly" in Topeka, Kansas. He asked his students to study the New Testament evidence for Spirit baptism. When he returned from a brief trip, students reported their conclusion: in every case in Acts where the Spirit was poured out, tongues followed. Parham endorsed this exegesis. On January 1, 1901, during a watchnight prayer service, student Agnes Ozman (later Agnes La Berge) asked Parham to lay hands on her and pray for Spirit baptism. She spoke in tongues — languages she was reported unable to speak naturally. Over the following days, other students, and Parham himself, had similar experiences. Parham's theological interpretation became the classical Pentecostal doctrine: tongues is not merely one spiritual gift among many (as in the Holiness movement generally) but the specific sign that evidences genuine Spirit baptism.

## Houston Bible School and Seymour
Parham moved his ministry to Texas, where he operated a Bible school in Houston (1905). [[william-seymour]] came to Houston and sought to study there. Parham, operating within the norms of segregated Texas, did not permit Seymour to sit in the classroom. Seymour sat outside the classroom door to hear the lectures — a founding gesture of the racial dynamics that would define early Pentecostalism. Despite this humiliation, Seymour accepted Parham's tongues-as-evidence doctrine and carried it to Los Angeles, where it became the spark of the [[event-azusa-street-revival-1906]].

## Rejection of the Azusa Street revival
When Parham visited Azusa Street in October 1906, he found the revival disturbing. He condemned what he described as "hypnotism," "animal magnetism," and "disgusting" manifestations. His deeper objection was to the interracial character of the worship: a Black pastor leading a racially mixed ecstatic assembly violated both Parham's racial worldview and (in his view) proper order. He attempted to assert apostolic authority over Seymour as his former teacher. The congregation rejected him; [[william-seymour]] retained leadership. This was a decisive moment in Pentecostal history: the theological originator was repudiated by the movement he had initiated, while the Black preacher he had marginalized became the movement's apostolic father.

## Later career and scandals
In 1907, Parham was arrested in San Antonio on charges of sodomy. He denied the charges and they were later dropped, but the arrest ended his public ministry as a major figure. He continued as an itinerant preacher in Texas and Kansas for the remainder of his life, maintaining a small following. He developed connections with white-supremacist movements, including the Ku Klux Klan, in his later years. He died in Baxter Springs, Kansas, on January 29, 1929.

## Legacy
Parham's legacy is bifurcated. He is the doctrinal architect of the single most distinctive Pentecostal claim (tongues as initial evidence) and the founder of the school that produced the first tongues-speaking outbreak he recognized as Spirit-baptism. Without Parham's theological framework, Seymour would not have had the interpretive category to understand what happened at Azusa Street. Yet Parham was ejected from the movement he created, and his racial hostility and later scandals disqualified him from leadership. Pentecostalism's founding is thus an unusual case in which the theological originator and the apostolic figure are two different people — and the apostolic figure (Seymour) represents precisely the population (Black Americans, descendants of slaves) that the originator (Parham) sought to exclude.

## Theological significance
Parham's tongues-as-evidence doctrine represents a specific form of the [[restorationism]] theme: the claim that the early church's supernatural gifts had been suspended during the apostolic period but are being restored in the last days. This restorationism was common in 19th-century American revivalist and Holiness thought; Parham's specific contribution was the identification of tongues as the diagnostic sign. The exegetical move was from 1 Cor 12–14 (tongues as one gift among many) to Acts 2, 10, and 19 (tongues as the recurrent sign accompanying Spirit-baptism) — prioritizing the narrative Acts pattern over the Pauline descriptive pattern.
