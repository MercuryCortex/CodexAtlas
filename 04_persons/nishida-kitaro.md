# Nishida Kitarō

## Identity

Nishida Kitarō (西田幾多郎, 1870–1945) is the founder of the **Kyoto School** and, by broad scholarly consensus, of modern Japanese philosophy — widely regarded as the first to construct an original, systematic philosophy in Japanese that engages Western thought on its own technical terms while drawing its deepest intuitions from Zen (Heisig 2001; Davis 2022) — earlier Meiji figures (Nishi Amane, Inoue Tetsujirō) engaged Western philosophy, but not with his originality and system. A lifelong practitioner of *zazen* and kōan discipline (chiefly at the Rinzai monasteries around Kyoto, though he never formally received transmission), he held the chair of philosophy at Kyoto Imperial University, where the department he built gives the "Kyoto School" its name. His work is organized around a single problem worked in successive forms across three decades: how to think the **pre-reflective ground of reality** — first as **pure experience** (*junsui keiken*), then as **self-awareness** (*jikaku*), and finally as the **logic of place** (*basho no ronri*) whose ultimate term is **absolute nothingness** (*zettai mu*). In the Atlas he is the origin-figure of the [[philosophy-kyoto-school]] hub — the named philosopher who deliberately joined the emptiness tradition of Mahāyāna Buddhism to the German-Idealist and phenomenological tradition of the West.

## Life and works

Born 19 May 1870 in Mori village (Ishikawa Prefecture) near Kanazawa, Nishida studied philosophy as a *senka* (irregular, non-elite) student at Tokyo Imperial University, then taught for years in provincial schools while practising Zen intensively — a period of hardship and bereavement that marks his thought. His first book, ***Zen no kenkyū*** (*An Inquiry into the Good*, 1911), made him famous and is conventionally taken as the founding text of the Kyoto School. He was appointed to Kyoto Imperial University in 1910 and to its chair of philosophy in 1913, retiring in 1928. He died 7 June 1945 in Kamakura, weeks before the war's end.

- ***Zen no kenkyū*** (*An Inquiry into the Good*, 1911) — grounds reality in **pure experience** prior to the subject/object split.
- ***Jikaku ni okeru chokkan to hansei*** (*Intuition and Reflection in Self-Consciousness*, 1917) — reworks pure experience as **self-awareness** in critical dialogue with Fichte and the neo-Kantians.
- ***Hataraku mono kara miru mono e*** (*From the Acting to the Seeing*, 1927) — contains the pivotal 1926 essay **"*Basho*"** ("Place").
- ***Mu no jikakuteki gentei*** (*The Self-Aware Determination of Nothingness*, 1932) and ***Tetsugaku no konpon mondai*** (*Fundamental Problems of Philosophy*, 1933–34) — the mature system of basho, "acting intuition," and the historical world.

## Pure experience, basho, and absolute nothingness

Nishida's *Zen no kenkyū* opens by refusing to begin from a knowing subject set over against known objects. Reality is instead **pure experience** (*junsui keiken*, 純粋経験) — immediate experience *prior* to the subject/object distinction, which is a later abstraction from it. He adapted the term from **William James's** radical empiricism ("pure experience") but gave it a Zen inflection: the immediacy of experience before the reflective ego divides it is close to what *zazen* discloses.

Over the next fifteen years he reworked this into the **logic of place** (*basho no ronri*). In "*Basho*" (1926) he argued that Aristotelian subject–predicate logic mistakes the grammatical **subject** for the seat of reality; instead reality is the **predicate-plane**, the *place* (*basho*, 場所) that envelops and lets appear whatever is judged. He ordered these places in a series — the *basho* of **being**, the *basho* of **relative nothingness** (the field of consciousness), and, enveloping both, the ***basho* of absolute nothingness** (*zettai mu no basho*), a self-aware ultimate that is no thing and no being at all, in which every determination arises and passes. This is Nishida's philosophical name for what Zen calls **emptiness** (*kū*, [[emptiness-sunyata|śūnyatā]]): not a void opposed to fullness but the **groundless ground** of all appearing. His late thought recast the knowing self as **acting intuition** (*kōiteki chokkan*) embedded in a self-forming historical world — the seed of Tanabe's and Nishitani's later systems.

## Cross-tradition significance (an authored bridge, not an accidental parallel)

Nishida is the rare case in which a cross-tradition link is neither independent convergence nor a contested ancient transmission, but a **deliberately constructed synthesis** by a philosopher who read both corpora in the original and named his sources. On the Buddhist side he draws on Zen practice, on Nāgārjuna's [[philosophy-madhyamaka|Madhyamaka]] and the [[emptiness-sunyata|śūnyatā]] of the *Prajñāpāramitā* tradition, and on [[dogen|Dōgen's]] Sōtō Zen (whose *Shōbōgenzō* the Kyoto milieu helped recover as philosophy). On the Western side he engages Fichte, Schelling, [[philosophy-german-idealism|Hegel and the German Idealists]], the neo-Kantians (Cohen, Rickert, Lask), Bergson, and [[william-james|William James]], and stands alongside the [[philosophy-phenomenology-of-religion|phenomenological]] tradition his student Nishitani would carry into direct contact with Heidegger.

The comparative payload is the **emptiness ↔ nothingness bridge**. *Zettai mu* is offered as the East-Asian counterpart to, and corrective of, the Western ontology of **being**: where **Hegel's** Absolute is a *fullness* that knows itself through the totality of its self-development, Nishida's **absolute nothingness** is a groundless field in which self-knowing occurs without any underlying substance. The resemblance to Hegel is close enough to have generated the school's sharpest internal problem — is *zettai mu* faithful to Mahāyāna emptiness, or has emptiness been quietly reshaped into a Hegelian "Absolute," a positive ground under a negative name? The connection to Western thought is thus **secure and documented as intellectual history**; what is debated is whether the synthesis preserves or betrays the emptiness it claims to articulate. This is exactly the transmission-vs-distortion question the Atlas cares about, and here the philosopher himself supplies the terms of it.

## Disputes

**Is it emptiness, or a substantialized ground?** The **Critical Buddhism** movement (*Hihan Bukkyō*) of Hakamaya Noriaki and Matsumoto Shirō (1980s–90s) attacked the whole *basho*/topos style of thinking — an underlying field or original "place" in which things appear — as covert *dhātu-vāda* (substrate-substantialism), a betrayal of the no-self (*anātman*) and dependent-origination (*pratītyasamutpāda*) that define genuine emptiness. On this reading Nishida's "absolute nothingness" is a reified ground incompatible with Nāgārjuna's refusal of any *svabhāva*, including the *svabhāva* of emptiness itself. Defenders answer that *zettai mu* is precisely a place-that-is-no-thing and therefore not a substrate at all. The debate is unresolved and among the most consequential in modern Buddhist philosophy.

**Wartime politics.** Although Nishida died in June 1945 and was more cautious than several younger colleagues, his late philosophy of the "historical world" and his 1943 lecture-memorandum on "The Principle of the New World Order" (*Sekai shinchitsujo no genri*) have been read both as tacit accommodation to the imperial-state project and as an attempt to restrain its ideologues. The extent of complicity is genuinely contested (Heisig & Maraldo, eds., *Rude Awakenings*, 1994) and colours assessment of his political thought.

**Philosophy or Zen apologetics?** Because Nishida articulates a Zen soteriology in Western philosophical vocabulary, critics have asked whether his work is systematic philosophy or religious advocacy in philosophical dress — the same question raised of his medieval source, Dōgen's *Shōbōgenzō*. Heisig (2001) and Davis (2022) treat it as philosophy proper; the question remains live in the reception literature.
