# Akhenaten

## Identity
Akhenaten — born Amenhotep IV around 1370 BCE, died ~1336 BCE — was the tenth pharaoh of the 18th Dynasty and the architect of ancient Egypt's most radical religious reform. Son of Amenhotep III and Queen Tiye, he ascended the throne ~1353 BCE as Amenhotep IV; in the fifth year of his reign he changed his name to Akhenaten ("Effective for [the] Aten"), founded a new capital city Akhetaten ("Horizon of the Aten") on a virgin site at modern Tell el-Amarna in Middle Egypt, and instituted the worship of the Aten — the visible solar disc — as the sole official cult of Egypt. The other gods, especially Amun-Ra of Thebes, were demoted or actively suppressed; their temples were closed and their names defaced. He was married to Queen Nefertiti, with whom he had at least six daughters; he is the father (probably) or stepfather of Tutankhamun. The Amarna reform lasted only Akhenaten's lifetime; his successors progressively reversed it, and by Tutankhamun's reign the standard Egyptian cults had been restored.

## Historical evidence
Robust. (1) Monumental inscriptions and reliefs from Akhetaten (the boundary stelae, the tomb of Ay where the Great Hymn is preserved, the royal tomb); (2) the **[[phase-1-012-amarna-letters]]** — the diplomatic archive of his reign; (3) the famous artistic productions of the Amarna period (the Nefertiti bust now in Berlin; the family portrait reliefs; the highly distinctive "Amarna style" of elongated forms); (4) the systematic *damnatio memoriae* by his successors is itself negative evidence of his historical reality. Later king lists (Manetho) omit him as "the heretic."

## Works
- The [[phase-1-011-great-hymn-to-aten]] (in the tomb of Ay) and its Shorter Hymn variants in several other Amarna tombs.
- The boundary stelae of Akhetaten — declarations of the city's foundation and Akhenaten's commitment to the Aten cult.
- Various royal inscriptions in tombs and on monuments at Amarna.

## Role in the tradition
Within Egyptian memory: erased. Later king lists omit him; later pharaohs reused his masonry and erased his name. Within modern reception: spectacular. Since the rediscovery of Akhetaten in the 19th century, Akhenaten has been a major figure of speculative imagination — Sigmund Freud's *Moses and Monotheism* (1939) speculated that Moses was an Atenist priest who carried Akhenaten's monotheism to the Hebrews; Jan Assmann's "memory of Egypt" thesis treats the Amarna reform as the type-case of religious revolution that shapes later monotheistic self-understanding. Whether Atenism constitutes the first true monotheism, henotheism, or a political-pragmatic move to break the Amun priesthood is one of the most-debated questions in Egyptology and comparative religion.

## Disputes
- **First monotheist?** Assmann (2014) defends a strong monotheistic reading; Hornung (1999) treats it as henotheism; Reeves (2001) emphasizes political pragmatism. All three remain active positions.
- **Influence on later monotheism.** Direct influence on Mosaic monotheism (Freud, Assmann, and following them) is contested; mainstream Egyptology and biblical scholarship is skeptical of any direct chain of transmission.
- **Family relationships.** Whether Tutankhamun is Akhenaten's son by Nefertiti, by a secondary wife Kiya, or by another woman ("the Younger Lady" mummy KV35YL, DNA-tested) is partly settled (DNA suggests Akhenaten's son, mother unidentified) and partly contested.
- **Cause of artistic style.** The unique Amarna art style — elongated features, swollen abdomens, distended skulls — has been variously attributed to (a) deliberate theological iconographic innovation, (b) royal-medical conditions (Marfan, Antley-Bixler syndromes have been proposed), (c) artistic convention without medical basis. The medical hypotheses are speculative.
