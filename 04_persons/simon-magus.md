# Simon Magus

## Identity
Simon, a Samaritan religious figure of the mid-1st century, presented in Acts 8 as a magician and would-be apostle who attempted to purchase apostolic power. Christian heresiological tradition (Justin, Irenaeus, Hippolytus) elaborated him into "the father of all heresies" — the founder of a Gnostic-precursor school called Simonianism that taught a divine syzygy (Simon as the *Great Power*, his consort Helen as the fallen *Ennoia* / divine Thought rescued from a Tyrian brothel) and competed with apostolic Christianity in 1st–2nd century Samaria and Rome.

## Historical evidence
**Acts 8:9–24** gives the New Testament attestation: Simon as a successful magician in Samaria who believed Philip's preaching, was baptized, then offered Peter money to obtain the laying-on-of-hands gift. Peter's furious rebuke ("May your silver perish with you...") gave the word *simony* (purchasing church office) to Christian vocabulary. **Justin Martyr** (~+150), himself a Samaritan, reports that Simon was honored at Rome with a statue inscribed *Simoni Deo Sancto* ("To Simon the Holy God") — though Justin's reading is now generally considered a misidentification of a statue dedicated to the Sabine god Semo Sancus. Justin and **Irenaeus** (*Adv. Haer.* 1.23) elaborate Simonian doctrine: Simon as *the Great Power*, Helen as Ennoia / Sophia descended into matter and trapped, Simon's mission to redeem her. Whether the Simonian doctrine reported by 2nd-century heresiologists genuinely descended from the Simon of Acts, or whether his name was retrospectively attached to later proto-Gnostic systems, is contested. For this vault, Simon Magus matters as: (a) the *boundary figure* between apostolic Christianity and Gnostic-leaning currents; (b) a possible historical anchor for the *Sophia myth* central to later Gnostic systems; (c) the *founding heresiarch* in the heresiological imagination — every later heresy gets traced back through Simon. The historical Simon-of-Acts certainly existed; the elaborate Gnostic theology attached to his name developed in subsequent generations.
