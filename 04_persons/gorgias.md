# Gorgias of Leontini

## Identity

Gorgias of Leontini (c. 483–375 BCE) was a Sicilian-Greek Sophist, rhetorician and itinerant teacher — with Protagoras of Abdera one of the two leading figures of the fifth-century Sophistic movement. Born at Leontinoi (Leontini) in eastern Sicily, he won panhellenic fame as a master of *epideixis* (display oratory) and as one of the most influential early theorists and stylists of persuasive *logos* (speech). Ancient tradition made him a pupil of the Sicilian philosopher-poet [[empedocles]] (Diogenes Laertius VIII.58–59), and credited him with an extraordinarily long life of over a hundred years. He accepted large fees, took no fixed city as home, and — unlike Protagoras — reportedly professed to teach only rhetoric, not *aretē* (virtue): at Plato, *Meno* 95c he is praised precisely for laughing at those who promise to teach virtue.

His importance is twofold and, unusually, split across two registers. As a **stylist and rhetorician** he introduced into Attic prose the ornate figures that antiquity called "Gorgianic" (*Gorgieia schēmata*) — balanced antithesis, clauses of equal length (*isocolon* / *parisosis*), and rhyming clause-endings (*homoeoteleuton*) — and theorised speech as a power that works on the soul. As a **philosopher of the negative** he wrote *On Nature, or On the Non-Existent*, whose three descending theses (nothing exists; if anything exists it cannot be known; if it can be known it cannot be communicated) invert the metaphysics of the Eleatics. In the Atlas he is placed as a principal figure of [[philosophy-sophism]], wired outward to the [[philosophy-eleaticism|Eleatic]] argument he parodies, to the [[philosophy-pyrrhonian-skepticism|skeptical]] tradition that later engaged his theses, and to the [[philosophy-platonism|Platonism]] that made him the emblem of rhetoric-against-philosophy.

## Works and doctrine

None of Gorgias' works survives whole in his own transmission, but two showpiece speeches are preserved complete and his philosophical treatise survives in two ancient paraphrases.

**On Nature, or On the Non-Existent (*Peri tou mē ontos*).** Gorgias' one theoretical work argues three theses in descending order: (1) **nothing exists**; (2) even if something exists, it is **incomprehensible** to human beings; (3) even if it is comprehensible, it is **incommunicable** to another. The treatise is lost, and is reconstructed from two divergent ancient summaries — Sextus Empiricus (*Adv. Math.* VII.65–87) and the pseudo-Aristotelian *De Melisso Xenophane Gorgia* (*MXG* 979a11–980b21). Its very title mirrors, in reverse, Melissus's Eleatic treatise *On Nature, or On What-Is*: where Parmenides and Melissus deduced that being *is* and cannot not-be, Gorgias deploys the same deductive machinery to demonstrate the opposite. Whether the work is sincere nihilism, a formal *reductio* exposing the emptiness of Eleatic method, or a virtuoso demonstration that rhetoric can prove anything at all, is the central interpretive question (see ## Disputes).

**Encomium of Helen (*Helenēs enkōmion*).** A display speech defending Helen of Troy against the charge of having ruined Greece. Gorgias lists four possible causes of her going to Troy — the will of the gods or necessity, physical force, persuasion by speech, or erotic love — and argues that under each she is blameless. The set-piece is his theory of **logos**: speech is "a powerful lord" (*dynastēs megas*) that, though it works through the tiniest and least visible body, accomplishes the most divine effects, acting on the soul as drugs (*pharmaka*) act on the body — some persuasions bewitch, some grieve, some embolden. If Helen was persuaded by speech, she was as much overpowered as if taken by force. The speech ends by calling itself a *paignion* — a "plaything" or amusement — a signature of Gorgianic self-conscious display.

**Defence of Palamedes (*Hyper Palamēdous apologia*).** A model forensic defence, put in the mouth of the mythical inventor Palamedes falsely accused of treason by Odysseus — a systematic exercise in the argument from probability (*eikos*).

**Fragments** of an *Epitaphios* (funeral oration), an *Olympikos* and a *Pythikos* (festival speeches urging panhellenic concord) survive as testimonia, and a rhetorical *Technē* was attributed to him in antiquity.

## In the ancient sources

As with the Sophists generally, Gorgias reaches us largely through witnesses who quote, summarise or prosecute him. Plato names a whole dialogue the *Gorgias*, staging him (and his pupils Polus and Callicles) as the champion of rhetoric against Socratic dialectic; the *Meno* and *Philebus* add further glimpses. Aristotle discusses his style critically in *Rhetoric* III and his fallacies in the *Sophistical Refutations*. Sextus Empiricus and the pseudo-Aristotelian *MXG* preserve the two summaries of *On the Non-Existent*. Diodorus Siculus (XII.53) records the 427 BCE Athenian embassy — the one securely dated event of his life — and the sensation his antithetical prose caused. Diogenes Laertius (VIII.58–59) reports the discipleship under Empedocles, and Philostratus, writing in the Roman-era Second Sophistic, opens his *Lives of the Sophists* (I.9) by treating Gorgias as the fountainhead of the whole sophistic art of *epideixis*. The direct fragments and testimonia are gathered as Diels-Kranz chapter 82 (English in Sprague, *The Older Sophists*).

## Cross-tradition significance

**Principal figure of the Sophistic movement (secure, internal to Greece).** Gorgias belongs, by home tradition, to Greek [[philosophy-sophism]], and is with Protagoras its most important member. But his profile is distinct from the Protagorean core: he is not primarily a relativist of the "man the measure" kind. His two contributions — a theory and technique of persuasive *logos*, and a negative ontology — make him the movement's rhetorician-in-chief and its one systematic philosopher of non-being. Because he reaches us through Plato's hostile frame and two mutually divergent doxographical summaries, the modern "rehabilitation of the Sophists" bears directly on how he is read.

**Antithesis of Eleatic metaphysics (structural, internal to Greece).** *On the Non-Existent* is deliberately built against [[philosophy-eleaticism]]: its title inverts Melissus's, and it uses Eleatic deductive form to reach anti-Eleatic conclusions. This is a secure textual relationship — the treatise unmistakably engages Parmenidean/Melissan argument — but its *point* is contested. It is best described as a methodological confrontation (parody, *reductio*, or refutation of Eleatic reasoning), not a doctrine transmitted from or to the Eleatics.

**Upstream tributary of Greek skepticism (genealogical-within-Greek, qualified).** Gorgias' second and third theses — being is unknowable, and even if known incommunicable — are among the earliest Greek arguments that the later skeptics engaged directly; Sextus Empiricus preserves the treatise inside his own discussion of the criterion of truth. But the relation is a qualified ancestry, not an identity: the Pyrrhonists (see [[philosophy-pyrrhonian-skepticism]]) practice suspension of judgement, whereas Gorgias advances — or performs — positive negative theses, and a large body of scholarship reads his treatise as rhetorical display rather than sincere skepticism. The honest statement is that he is a recognised tributary, on the same footing as Protagorean relativism, not a card-carrying skeptic.

**Emblematic antagonist of Platonism (secure).** By naming a dialogue for him and casting him as the figurehead of persuasion-for-victory against inquiry-for-truth, Plato ([[philosophy-platonism]]) fixed Gorgias' later reputation. Much of what we "know" of his intentions is therefore refracted through the philosophy built to refute him — the root of the interpretive disputes below.

There is a natural temptation to wire Gorgias' *logos* to the cross-tradition **Logos** hubs of the Atlas (the [[logos-cosmic-reason|cosmic Reason]] of Heraclitus, the Stoics and the Fourth Gospel). That wire is **deliberately not drawn**: Gorgias' *logos* is persuasive human *speech* — a psychological and rhetorical force — not the divine ordering Reason of the Logos-theology tradition. The shared Greek word masks two different concepts, and conflating them would be a false cognate.

## Disputes

- **Serious argument or rhetorical display?** The governing question about *On the Non-Existent* is whether Gorgias meant it. Readings divide between (a) sincere skeptical/nihilist philosophy; (b) a formal *reductio ad absurdum* exposing the self-destructive character of Eleatic method; and (c) a *paignion* — a virtuoso exhibition that speech can demonstrate even that nothing exists, of a piece with the self-described "plaything" ending of the *Encomium*. Kerferd (1981, ch. 8) surveys the debate; the question remains open.

- **Sextus versus the MXG.** The treatise survives only in two ancient summaries that diverge in structure and detail. Which better preserves Gorgias' actual reasoning — and how much each has been reshaped by its excerptor's own agenda — is unresolved, and every reconstruction of the argument depends on how the two are weighed (Mansfeld 1985; Cassin 1980).

- **Pupil of Empedocles?** Diogenes Laertius (VIII.58–59) and Satyrus make Gorgias a pupil of [[empedocles]]. The claim is chronologically plausible (both Sicilian, overlapping generations) and attractive because Gorgias' account of perception and of the physical action of *logos* on the soul resembles Empedoclean effluence-theory — but it is an ancient report, not securely documented.

- **Was he a philosopher at all?** Because Gorgias reportedly disclaimed teaching virtue and professed only rhetoric (Plato, *Meno* 95c), some read him as a pure rhetorician wrongly drafted into the history of philosophy; others (e.g. Consigny 2001) reframe the opposition itself, presenting him as a self-conscious rhetorical artist for whom the rhetoric/philosophy border is exactly what is in question.
