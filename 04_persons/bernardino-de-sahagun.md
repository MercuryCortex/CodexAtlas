# Bernardino de Sahagún

## Identity
Spanish Franciscan friar, missionary to New Spain, and pioneering ethnographer of Mexica civilization. Sahagún arrived in Mexico in 1529 — just eight years after the conquest of Tenochtitlan — and lived in New Spain until his death in 1590, over sixty years. He learned Classical Nahuatl with extraordinary fluency at the Colegio de Santa Cruz de Tlatelolco (where he also taught) and spent decades collecting Nahua ethnographic, historical, religious, and natural-historical material with named Indigenous collaborators. The result was the *Florentine Codex* — *Historia general de las cosas de Nueva España* — the principal extant source for pre-conquest Mexica civilization.

## Historical evidence
Extensive documentation: Sahagún's own writings (Spanish and Nahuatl); Franciscan-order records; royal correspondence (Philip II's 1577 suppression order); his collaborators' subsequent writings. Standard biography: León-Portilla 2002.

## Works
- *Florentine Codex* (compiled ~1545–1577, 12 books). Standard English translation: Anderson and Dibble (1950–1982).
- *Psalmodia Christiana* (1583) — Christian songs in Nahuatl using pre-Christian poetic structures; the only work of Sahagún's published in his lifetime.
- Various Nahuatl-language sermon and catechetical works.
- The *Códices Matritenses* — earlier-stage drafts of the Florentine, at the Real Academia and Real Biblioteca, Madrid.

## Role in the tradition
The principal saver of Mexica religious-cultural knowledge. His method — Indigenous-language collection from elders, validated by collaborators, structured comprehensively — was ahead of its time; León-Portilla calls him "the first anthropologist." His project was simultaneously *missionary* (intended to enable more effective evangelization by understanding what it replaced) and *preservationist* (he understood what was being lost); these motivations are not separable.

## Disputes
- **Reliability.** How sympathetically vs. selectively Sahagún represented Mexica religion (especially human sacrifice) is debated. Lockhart and León-Portilla defend the underlying reliability when read through the Nahuatl column. Critics (David Carrasco at times) note European-audience selection biases.
- **Authorial agency.** How much "Sahagún" vs. his Indigenous collaborators authored the Florentine. Modern scholarship (Klor de Alva, Burkhart, León-Portilla) increasingly emphasizes the collaborators' role.
