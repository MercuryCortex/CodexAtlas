# Critias (the speaker in Plato's *Timaeus* and *Critias*)

## Identity
The speaker named "Critias" in the dramatic frame of Plato's *Timaeus* (20d–27b) and the principal narrator of the unfinished *Critias* dialogue. Within the dramatic fiction, he is the figure who transmits the Atlantis story — claiming it descends to him from his grandfather (also named Critias), who heard it from his father Dropides, who was a relative or friend of [[solon]]. The Critias-Dropides-Solon family tree is real Athenian aristocratic prosopography (the family is securely attested in Davies 1971); the question is *which* historical Critias is the dialogue's speaker.

## Historical evidence
The Critias / Dropides family is one of the better-documented Athenian aristocratic lineages, traceable through inscriptions, prosopographical mentions in the orators, and the dialogues' internal claims. The two principal candidates for the dialogue's speaker:

**Identification (a) — Critias IV, "the Tyrant" (~-460 to -403).** The notorious leader of the Thirty Tyrants who ruled Athens in -404/-403 after the city's defeat by Sparta; politically brutal; intellectually associated with the Sophistic movement; cousin of Plato's mother Perictione. Burnet 1914 and Cornford 1937 defended this identification, taking the dialogue's Critias to be the same as the historical tyrant. On this reading, Plato is staging the Atlantis-and-ideal-state story in the mouth of a near-contemporary, who would have been ~70+ in the dramatic date.

**Identification (b) — Critias III, "the elder" or "the grandfather" (~-520 to -429).** A generation earlier; Plato's mother's grandfather; alive but elderly at the dramatic date Plato suggests for the *Timaeus* (~-430s). On this reading, the dialogue's Critias is *not* the notorious tyrant but his grandfather, whose son Glaucon then continues the family line. Davies 1971, Lampert & Planeaux 1998, and Welliver 1977 argued this identification.

The choice matters: (a) makes Plato's mouthpiece for the ideal-Athens-against-Atlantis story a man notorious for political brutality and intellectually associated with the Sophistic-rhetorical tradition Plato opposes — pointedly ironic or deeply problematic. (b) dissolves the discomfort. The (b) identification is now the majority scholarly position but is not unanimous.

The Atlas adopts the slug `critias-younger` to denote *the dialogue-speaker as a distinct prosopographical node*, leaving the IV-vs-III identification flagged. (The label "younger" here disambiguates the speaker from his nephew-or-grandson Critias-the-Tyrant; readers should note that some literature uses "Critias the Younger" specifically for the Tyrant. The slug should be read in context of this Disputes section.)

## Works
None directly attributed. The figure exists primarily as a Platonic dramatic creation. The historical Critias IV (the Tyrant) wrote elegies, prose works, and tragedies (fragments survive in Diels-Kranz 88), but the dialogue does not directly attribute these works to its speaker.

## Role in the tradition
The dialogue-speaker Critias is the *named transmitter* of the Atlantis story within the Platonic dramatic frame. Every later citation of "Solon's Atlantis" rests on the genealogical-mnemonic chain the dialogue-Critias asserts: priests at Saïs → Solon → Dropides → Critias-the-elder → Critias-the-speaker → present audience. For the Atlas's tracing of the Atlantis chain, this figure is the *bridge* between the (Plato-invented) Egyptian source and the Greek-philosophical reception.

## Disputes
- **Identification with Critias IV (the Tyrant) vs. Critias III (the elder).** Discussed above. Majority current scholarship (Lampert & Planeaux 1998, Welliver, Davies, and the standard prosopographical literature) prefers (b); minority (Burnet, Cornford) preferred (a). The dispute is one of the live prosopographical questions in Platonic dramaturgy.
- **Historicity-as-real-speaker.** Both Critias III and Critias IV are securely historical persons; the question is which of them is the speaker. The figure as the dialogue presents him (transmitting Atlantis) is a Platonic construction.
- **Atlantis transmission claim.** Whether the genealogical-mnemonic chain (Saïs → Solon → Dropides → Critias-elder → Critias-speaker) reflects any real transmission is uniformly rejected by mainstream Platonic scholarship; the chain is Plato's literary authority-anchor.
