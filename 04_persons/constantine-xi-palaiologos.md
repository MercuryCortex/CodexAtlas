# Constantine XI Palaiologos

## Identity
Constantine XI Palaiologos (Δραγάσης) — born 1405, died 29 May 1453 — last Roman / Byzantine emperor (r. 1449–1453). Son of Manuel II Palaiologos and Helena Dragaš (a Serbian princess; hence the matronymic Dragases / Δραγάσης occasionally used). Despot of the Morea from 1428, succeeding his brother John VIII as emperor in 1449. Reigned four years and four months over a state reduced to Constantinople, its immediate Thracian hinterland, and the Despotate of the Morea — the final residue of an empire that had been the eastern Roman state for over a millennium.

## Historical evidence
Documented from multiple contemporary perspectives:
- **Greek pro-Byzantine**: George Sphrantzes (in Constantine's service throughout; *Chronicon Minus* the contemporary close-record); Doukas (*Historia Turcobyzantina*, hostile to the Union of Florence but documentary on the siege).
- **Greek pro-Ottoman**: Kritoboulos of Imbros (later in Mehmed II's service; *History of Mehmed the Conqueror*, mid-1460s — pro-Mehmed but with respectful treatment of Constantine).
- **Greek-pagan-influenced**: Laonikos Chalkokondyles (*Histories* — Renaissance-classicizing register).
- **Latin/Venetian**: Nicolò Barbaro's *Diary of the Siege* (a Venetian doctor present in the city); Leonard of Chios's *Letter to Pope Nicholas V*.
- **Ottoman**: Tursun Beg, *History of Mehmed the Conqueror*.

## Role in the tradition

### The Union of Florence and the religious-political dilemma
Constantine inherited the [[event-council-of-florence-1438-1439|Council of Florence Union (1439)]] which his brother John VIII had concluded with Pope Eugene IV — formal reunion of the Latin and Greek churches under papal primacy, in exchange for western military aid against the Turks. The Union was bitterly opposed by the majority of the Greek clergy and population (the famous Notaras line attributed by Doukas — "I would rather see the Turkish turban in the midst of the city than the Latin mitre" — captures the sentiment, whether or not historically said by Loukas Notaras). Constantine personally endorsed the Union and on 12 December 1452 had the Union liturgy formally celebrated in Hagia Sophia — five and a half months before the city fell. Most of the population boycotted Hagia Sophia thereafter. The promised western aid was inadequate: Pope Nicholas V sent Cardinal Isidore of Kiev with 200 Neapolitan archers; Venice and Genoa sent partial private contingents; the major western powers did not mobilise.

### The siege and the death
Mehmed II began the formal siege 6 April 1453 with ~80,000 troops, a fleet, and the unprecedented heavy artillery of Orban (the Hungarian engineer whose "Basilica" gun fired 600-pound stone balls). Constantine commanded ~7,000 troops including the Genoese mercenary captain [[giovanni-giustiniani]] (~700 men) and Cardinal Isidore's contingent. The 53-day siege saw the famous overland-portage of Ottoman ships into the Golden Horn (22 April), the chain-breach attempts, the constant artillery bombardment of the land walls. The final assault began in the early hours of 29 May 1453; the Kerkoporta postern was found unbarred (or partly so); the walls were breached at the Romanos Gate; Giustiniani was wounded and evacuated (precipitating the Greek collapse); Constantine, refusing to flee, removed his imperial insignia and died fighting as an unrecognised soldier somewhere near the breached wall. His body was never positively identified.

### The "marble emperor" legend
Greek folk tradition — first attested in the immediate post-1453 period and elaborated through the centuries of Ottoman rule — held that Constantine did not die but was rescued by an angel and turned to marble, hidden in a cave near the Golden Gate; he would return on the day God restored the City to the Christians ("Πάλι με χρόνους με καιρούς, πάλι δικά μας θα 'ναι"). The legend is the principal Greek folk-eschatological tradition under Ottoman rule and remains alive in modern Greek nationalist memory.

### The "Third Rome" transfer and the prophetic resonance
The death of the **11th Constantine** on the loss of Constantinople — which had been founded by the **first Constantine** ([[constantine-i]]) — was immediately read by Greek and Slavic Orthodoxy as a numerological-prophetic closure: the empire began with a Constantine and ended with a Constantine. In Russian Orthodox theology this licensed the **"Third Rome" doctrine** (Filofei of Pskov, early 16th c.): Old Rome fell to Latin schism; Second Rome (Constantinople) fell to the Turks for embracing the Florentine Union; Moscow inherits the imperial-religious mandate. The Third Rome doctrine becomes load-bearing for Russian imperial-religious ideology from Ivan IV through the Russian Empire (and recurs in modern variants). See [[constantine-i]], [[event-fall-of-constantinople-1453]].

### Greek manuscript diaspora and the western Renaissance
The Byzantine scholarly diaspora — beginning before 1453 (Manuel Chrysoloras taught Greek in Florence from 1397; the Council of Florence 1438–1439 brought Plethon and Bessarion west) and dramatically accelerating after 1453 — carried the Greek manuscript corpus to Italy. Cosimo de' Medici's commission of Ficino to translate the Hermetica (1463) and Plato (1484), Bessarion's bequest of his manuscript collection to Venice (the future Biblioteca Marciana), and the wider Italian humanist absorption of Greek learning are direct downstream consequences. Without the 1453 fall and its scholarly diaspora, the Italian Renaissance's Greek-philosophical and Hermetic dimensions are unimaginable in their actual historical form (Wilson 1992 *From Byzantium to Italy*; Yates 1964 *Giordano Bruno and the Hermetic Tradition* on the Renaissance Hermetic chain). See [[marsilio-ficino]], [[tradition-renaissance-hermeticism]].

## Disputes
- **Was Constantine ever formally crowned?** He was acclaimed at Mystras in January 1449 by his brother Demetrios and a small court; he never had a Constantinopolitan coronation by the Patriarch (the Patriarchal succession was politically frozen by the Union question). Whether he was "fully" emperor in canonical-liturgical terms was a question contemporaries raised (Nicol 1992).
- **The Kerkoporta postern.** Whether it was deliberately left open, accidentally unbarred, or breached separately is contested in the contemporary accounts; the practical effect was the same.
- **Death and body.** Sphrantzes and Doukas give the death-fighting account but neither saw the body. Ottoman accounts variously claim Mehmed identified Constantine's corpse by the imperial purple buskins. The lack of a positively-identified body is the historical seed of the marble-emperor legend.
- **Historical evaluation.** Older historiography (Gibbon) viewed Constantine as a tragic-noble figure; modern (Nicol 1992, Harris 2010) is more cautious — he was a brave but politically isolated emperor of a state already structurally beyond salvation, whose Union policy alienated his own population in exchange for inadequate western aid.
