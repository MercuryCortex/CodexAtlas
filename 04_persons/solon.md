# Solon

## Identity
Solon (~-630 to ~-560) was the Athenian poet, lawgiver, and statesman whose 594 BCE constitutional reforms — the *seisachtheia* (cancellation of debt-bondage), reorganization of the citizenry into property classes, and codification of laws — are the foundational documents of the Athenian political tradition that culminated in classical democracy. Traditionally counted among the Seven Sages of archaic Greece. His own poetry survives in fragments and is the principal contemporary evidence for his program. For the Atlas's purposes, he is load-bearing as the narrative frame of Plato's Atlantis story: in *Timaeus* 20d–25d, Critias (the speaker) reports that the Atlantis tale was originally heard by Solon from an aged priest at Saïs in the Egyptian Delta during his post-archonship travels.

## Historical evidence
Solon is among the better-documented archaic Greek figures. His own surviving poetic fragments (preserved by later anthologists, especially Plutarch and the pseudo-Aristotelian *Constitution of the Athenians*) provide first-person attestation of his political program — a rarity for archaic Greece. Herodotus (~-440) tells the famous Croesus-Solon dialogue (1.29–33) and presumes Solonic chronology. Aristotle's *Constitution of the Athenians* (~-330) gives the most detailed constitutional history. Plutarch's *Life of Solon* (~+100) is the principal narrative biography.

The historicity of the Egyptian journey is *probable but contested*. The journey is asserted by Herodotus 1.30 (where Solon visits Egypt under Amasis and Lydia under Croesus, with chronological problems that classicists have long noted — Amasis reigned -570 to -526 and Croesus to -547, so a 594-BCE-archonship Solon's life span barely accommodates the visits as Herodotus reports). Plato's Saïtic-priest frame (*Timaeus*) is the principal evidence for the specific Saïs visit; classical scholarship (Vidal-Naquet 2007, Gill 1980) reads this frame as Plato's literary device exploiting the existing Solon-in-Egypt tradition rather than as independent attestation. Whether the historical Solon visited Saïs is undetermined; whether he heard an Atlantis tale there is, on every mainstream contemporary reading, Plato's invention.

## Works
- **Poems.** Elegiac and iambic verse on political themes — fragments in West's *Iambi et Elegi Graeci* and Gerber's Loeb *Greek Elegiac Poetry*. Frag. 4 (the "Eunomia" elegy) is the longest surviving piece and a major archaic-political-poetic source.
- **Law code (594 BCE).** Inscribed on rotating wooden *axones* and stone *kyrbeis* in the agora; texts survive only as quotations and paraphrases in later authors (especially Demosthenes and the Atthidographers).

## Role in the tradition
Solon is the *named ancestor* of every later Athenian appeal to political moderation, lawful constitutionalism, and aristocratic-but-not-tyrannical reform. He is one of the Seven Sages (Diogenes Laertius 1.13). For Atlas purposes, his role is doubled: (a) historical Athenian lawgiver of foundational importance, (b) the narrative *frame* through which Plato grants antiquity-and-authority to the Atlantis story. The "Solon heard it from the Egyptian priests" claim is the most influential single source-attribution in the entire later Atlantis literature — every modern Atlantist from Donnelly forward implicitly relies on it.

## Disputes
- **Saïs visit.** Mainstream classical scholarship treats the Saïs encounter as Plato's invented authority-anchor, not as independent transmission. The pseudoarchaeological tradition (Donnelly 1882, Hancock) treats it as transmitted history. See [[phase-3-022-plato-timaeus-critias-atlantis]] Disputes for the full treatment.
- **Chronological problems.** Herodotus's chronology of Solon's travels has internal difficulties that classicists have repeatedly addressed; this is a routine archaic-chronology issue and does not impugn Solon's overall historicity.
- **Authenticity of poems.** Some Solonic fragments are securely his; others are imitative-archaicizing; the corpus is sorted with reasonable confidence in modern editions (West 1972, Gerber 1999).
