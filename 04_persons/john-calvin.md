# Jean Calvin

## Identity
French-Genevan reformer, theologian, and the principal architect of Reformed Protestantism (1509–1564). Born at Noyon in Picardy; educated at Paris, Orléans, and Bourges (humanist law); converted to evangelical Protestantism ~1533; fled France 1534; published the first edition of the *Institutes* at Basel 1536; recruited by Guillaume Farel to Geneva that same year. Two periods in Geneva (1536–1538, 1541–1564) separated by exile in Strasbourg (where he served the French-refugee congregation under Bucer's tutelage). Under Calvin's leadership Geneva became the European center of Reformed Protestantism — the "Protestant Rome" — with its Academy training preachers and missionaries who spread the Reformed faith to France, the Low Countries, Scotland, Hungary, Poland, and (eventually) England. His theological-pastoral output across 25 years was enormous; his Genevan Consistory enforced strict moral discipline; his role in the 1553 execution of Michael Servetus for Antitrinitarianism remains a permanent stain on Reformed history.

## Historical evidence
Densely documented: Calvin's own enormous corpus (*Corpus Reformatorum*, Brunsvigae 1863–1900, 59 vols.), Genevan civic and ecclesiastical records, voluminous correspondence.

## Works
*Institutes of the Christian Religion* (1536–1559, definitive 1559 Latin / 1560 French). Commentaries on most of the Bible. *Genevan Catechism* (1542 French, 1545 Latin). *Ecclesiastical Ordinances* (1541). *Articles on the Lord's Supper* (1541). *Antidote to the Council of Trent* (1547). *On the Eternal Predestination of God* (1552).

## Role in the tradition
Calvin defines Reformed Protestantism. The Reformed branch — distinct from Lutheran in its Eucharist (spiritual presence), ecclesiology (presbyterian-synodal), discipline (consistorial moral oversight), and predestinarian articulation — became the dominant form of Protestantism in much of Europe and America. Westminster Confession (1646–1647), Heidelberg Catechism (1563), Belgic Confession (1561), Synod of Dort (1618–1619) all descend from Calvin's theology.

## Disputes
- **Servetus.** Calvin's role in the 1553 execution is contested in its details (he requested beheading rather than burning; he was not the chief magistrate) but his concurrence in the execution is firm.
- **Calvin's "double predestination."** The development of Reformed predestinarianism beyond Calvin's own articulation is the subject of the "Calvin against the Calvinists" debate.
