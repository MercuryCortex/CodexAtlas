# Pope Gregory I (the Great)

## Identity
Roman senatorial aristocrat (gens Anicia, the same family as Boethius). City prefect of Rome before turning his ancestral villa into a monastery (~574) and entering monastic life. Reluctantly served as papal *apocrisiarius* (envoy) to Constantinople 579–586. Pope from 590 until his death in 604. Inherited a Rome devastated by plague, Lombard invasion, the breakdown of the late Roman administrative system, and a half-empty city. His pontificate is the pivot from late-antique to medieval Latin Christianity: he reorganized papal estates as a welfare-and-supply system, regulated relations with the Lombards (effectively making the papacy a temporal power in central Italy), commissioned the conversion of the Anglo-Saxons (sending Augustine of Canterbury 596–597), and produced the textbook of medieval Western spirituality.

## Historical evidence
Voluminous and continuous transmission. His own *Registrum* (~850 letters) is one of the richest primary archives of the late 6th c. The 8th-c. *Vita Gregorii* by Paul the Deacon and the slightly earlier Whitby *Life* are hagiographic but informative.

## Works
- ***Moralia in Job*** — 35-book allegorical-moral exposition. The textbook of medieval monastic spirituality.
- ***Dialogues*** (4 books) — Italian saints' lives, including the only "biography" of Benedict of Nursia (Book II). The *Dialogues*' purgatorial eschatology shaped medieval Catholic teaching on the afterlife.
- ***Regula pastoralis*** (*Pastoral Rule*) — the textbook of medieval episcopal practice; Alfred the Great translated it into Old English in the 890s.
- ***Homiliae in Evangelia*** (40 Gospel homilies), ***Homiliae in Hiezechihelem***, ***Expositio in Cantica Canticorum*** (incomplete).
- The Gregorian liturgical tradition (chant, sacramentary) is named for him; the historical attributions are partly retrojected.

## Role in the tradition
Last of the four traditional Latin Doctors of the Church (with Ambrose, Jerome, Augustine). The medieval Latin West reads its scripture and runs its bishops on Gregorian patterns for half a millennium. He effectively makes the papacy a temporal power. He launches the conversion of England.

## Connections — substrate and influence
- His allegorical exegesis is methodologically Origenist-via-Augustine-and-Ambrose — and hence ultimately Philonic and Alexandrian-Platonic.
- The *Dialogues*' picture of demonic intervention, miracle-working monks, and purgative afterlife draws on older Mediterranean spirit-world traditions; Markus 1997 reads the work as a deliberate Christianization of pagan folkloric supernaturalism for a still semi-pagan Italian countryside.
- His Anglo-Saxon mission (596) instructed Augustine of Canterbury (per the famous letter to Mellitus, *Reg.* XI.56) **not to destroy pagan temples but to convert them into churches** — explicit Christian re-use of pre-Christian sacred sites. This is one of the cleanest documentable cases of intentional substrate-Christianization (Markus 1997: 78–82; Mayr-Harting, *The Coming of Christianity to Anglo-Saxon England*, 1991).
- Stoic and Senecan ethics persist as the framework of his ascetic-practical teaching (Straw 1988: 107–46).

## Disputes
- **The "Gregorian" chant.** Modern musicology shows the repertoire was largely Frankish (8th–9th c.) and only retroactively credited to Gregory.
- **The historicity of the *Dialogues* miracles.** A minority position (Clark 1987) once argued for pseudonymous authorship of the *Dialogues*; the majority accepts Gregorian authorship.
