# Ibn Sīnā (Avicenna; Abū ʿAlī al-Ḥusayn ibn ʿAbd Allāh ibn Sīnā)

## Identity
**Abū ʿAlī al-Ḥusayn ibn ʿAbd Allāh ibn Sīnā (980–1037 CE)** — known throughout the Latin Middle Ages as **Avicenna** — is the central figure of the falsafa tradition and **the most influential Islamic philosopher in world-historical terms**. A Persian-language Iranian polymath born in Afshana (near Bukhara, in the Samanid-controlled Persianate east), Avicenna combined an itinerant courtier-physician career — moving between the courts of Bukhara, Gurgānj, Gurgān, Rayy, Hamadān, and Iṣfahān as patrons rose and fell — with the production of an **encyclopedic philosophical-scientific corpus** that became the standard curriculum text in both the Islamic world (where it shapes Suhrawardi, Mulla Sadra, the Persianate philosophical tradition through the 19th century) and the Latin Christian West (where the *Canon of Medicine* is the standard European medical textbook from the 13th to the 17th century, and the *Metaphysics of the Shifāʾ* is the principal source-text for the medieval Latin reception of Aristotle's *Metaphysics* — read by Albert the Great, Aquinas, Duns Scotus, Suarez). Honored with the title **al-Shaykh al-Raʾīs** (the Chief Master). Author of an autobiographical *Sīra* dictated to his disciple al-Jūzjānī — one of the earliest extant Islamic-philosophical autobiographies. (Gutas 1988; Bertolacci 2006.)

## Historical evidence
Documented. The autobiographical *Sīra* (dictated to al-Jūzjānī) is **the foundational source for Avicenna's intellectual biography** — covering his prodigiously early education (mastered Quran by age 10; logic by 14; medicine by 16; metaphysics with difficulty until reading al-Farabi's *Intentions of Aristotle's Metaphysics* aged ~22), his itinerant career, and his philosophical-scientific aims. Continuations by al-Jūzjānī cover the rest of his life. Numerous independent biographical entries in Ibn al-Nadim's later editions, al-Qifṭī, Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿa, Ibn Khallikān, etc. The Latin-Christian biographical tradition (e.g., Roger Bacon, the Toledo-school translator-prefaces) treats him as an authority. His extant manuscripts are extensive; the modern critical-editions program (Cairo, Tehran, Brill) covers most of the corpus.

## Works
- **[[phase-5-044-ibn-sina-kitab-al-shifa|*Kitāb al-Shifāʾ* (Book of Healing)]]** — the encyclopedic philosophical *summa*: logic (9 parts paralleling Aristotle's *Organon*), physics (8 parts), mathematics (4 parts), metaphysics (10 *maqālāt*). The Metaphysics (*al-Ilāhiyyāt*) is **the principal Avicennian text for Latin scholasticism**, translated in the 12th c. at the Toledo School as *Liber de philosophia prima sive scientia divina*. Treats the essence/existence distinction, the modalities (necessary / possible / impossible), the proof of the Necessary Being, the emanational cosmology (Necessary Being → First Intellect → cascade of celestial intellects + souls + spheres → sublunary Active Intellect), psychology (the rational soul's separability from body), and the program of philosophical theology that defines post-Avicennian Islamic philosophy.
- **[[phase-5-044-ibn-sina-kitab-al-shifa|*al-Najāt* (The Salvation)]]** — shorter *summa* version of the *Shifāʾ*; widely circulated.
- ***Kitāb al-Qānūn fī al-Ṭibb* (The Canon of Medicine)** — the medical *summa* in 5 books, integrating Galenic medicine with Aristotelian natural philosophy. **Translated into Latin by Gerard of Cremona in the 12th c.** and used as the **standard European medical textbook from c. 1200 to c. 1700** at Bologna, Padua, Montpellier, Paris. Vesalius's anatomical work in the 16th c. begins the European displacement of the *Canon*. (Siraisi 1987.)
- ***al-Ishārāt wa-l-Tanbīhāt* (Pointers and Reminders)** — Avicenna's late philosophical-spiritual work; more compressed and personal than the *Shifāʾ*, with mystical-philosophical notes that the post-Avicennian Persian tradition (Suhrawardi → Mulla Sadra) extends. The foundational text of the **Eastern (Persian) Avicennian tradition**.
- ***Ḥayy ibn Yaqẓān*** — a short Avicennian philosophical allegory (the original; not the Ibn Tufayl novel). Avicenna's *Ḥayy* is a brief allegorical encounter with the personified Active Intellect ("Living, son of Vigilant"); [[ibn-tufayl|Ibn Tufayl]]'s 12th-c. [[phase-5-046-ibn-tufayl-hayy-ibn-yaqdhan|novel of the same title]] is a deliberate Avicennian-Andalusi extension into the natural-religion / autodidact-on-an-island form.

## Medicine — the *Canon* and clinical method

Alongside being the keystone philosopher of *falsafa*, Ibn Sīnā was **the most influential physician of the post-classical world**. The **[[phase-5-063-avicenna-canon-of-medicine|*Kitāb al-Qānūn fī al-Ṭibb* (Canon of Medicine)]]** (c. 1025) organised the whole of Greek, Persian, and Islamic medicine into a five-book *summa*: (I) general principles, the causes of health and disease, and an early **contagion-and-quarantine** theory; (II) a materia medica of some 800 substances; (III) systematic pathology organ-by-organ with differential diagnosis; (IV) conditions crossing organs — fevers, poisons, contagious diseases, surgery; (V) compound drugs.

Beyond systematisation the *Canon* advances a genuinely **clinical method**: drugs to be tested in pure form before combination and on animals before humans; the cure of a single case proves nothing — a result must be **reproducible** (an early articulation of the experimental-clinical standard); **psychosomatic** causation (his pulse-test diagnosis of lovesickness is a first documented psychosomatic clinical test); and a staging-like account of cancer. Gerard of Cremona's 12th-c. Latin translation made the *Canon* the **standard European medical textbook from c. 1200 to c. 1700**, required at Bologna, Padua, Montpellier, and Paris; [[hippocrates|Hippocratic]] and [[galen-of-pergamon|Galenic]] medicine reached Latin Europe largely through it. He is the culminating figure of [[medicine-islamic-golden-age|Islamic Golden Age medicine]]. (Siraisi 1987; Savage-Smith 1992.)

**MASSIVE WIN — one corpus, two civilisations, ~600 years.** The *Canon* was the dominant medical authority in *both* the Islamic world and Latin Europe **simultaneously**, from c. 1025 to c. 1650 — no other single medical text has held such authority across so broad a cultural and geographic range for so long.

## Role in the tradition
- **The principal Islamic philosopher of all time.** Avicenna's metaphysics, epistemology, psychology, philosophical theology, and natural philosophy structure the rest of Islamic philosophy. Even his principal critic — **[[al-ghazali]] in [[phase-5-045-al-ghazali-tahafut-al-falasifa|*Tahāfut al-Falāsifa*]]** — is fundamentally targeting Avicennian theses (the eternity of the world, God's knowledge of particulars, bodily resurrection) and proceeds by deep engagement with Avicennian arguments. [[ibn-rushd|Averroes]]'s reply ([[phase-5-046-ibn-rushd-tahafut-al-tahafut|*Tahāfut al-Tahāfut*]]) is partly a re-defense of Avicenna and partly a critique of Avicenna in favor of a more strictly Aristotelian reading.
- **The essence/existence distinction.** Avicenna's foundational metaphysical move — distinguishing the essence (*māhiyya*, "what-it-is-ness") of a thing from its existence (*wujūd*, "that-it-is-ness"), with God alone as the being whose essence is identical to existence (the Necessary Being) — becomes the foundation for **Aquinas's metaphysics of *ens commune* and the *actus essendi***. The medieval Latin distinction between *essentia* and *esse* is Avicenna's *māhiyya* / *wujūd* distinction received via the Toledo translations.
- **The *flying-man* thought-experiment.** Avicenna's psychological thought-experiment — imagine a human created in mid-air, blindfolded, with limbs separated so no sensory input — argues that even in such a state the person would affirm her own existence, demonstrating the substantial self-existence of the soul apart from the sensible body. Cited extensively by Aquinas (under Avicenna's name) and a load-bearing source for Descartes's *cogito* via Augustine and the Suarezian-scholastic mediation.
- **The Necessary Being argument.** Avicenna's argument from contingency to a Necessary Being whose essence is identical to its existence — God as *wājib al-wujūd*, the Being-whose-existence-is-necessary-through-itself — becomes **Aquinas's Third Way** (*ex possibili et necessario*) in the *Summa Theologiae*. The cosmological argument from contingency is in its mature scholastic form an Avicennian argument.
- **Latin reception as Avicenna.** The 12th-c. Toledo School (Gerard of Cremona; Dominicus Gundissalinus + Avendauth) translates the *Shifāʾ* Metaphysics, the *Shifāʾ* De Anima (psychology), and the *Canon of Medicine* into Latin. Albert the Great's *Avicennan* references run throughout his commentaries; **Aquinas engages Avicenna more extensively than any other non-Christian philosopher** (over 400 citations in the *Summa Theologiae* and other works; the references are sometimes critical, frequently approving). Duns Scotus's metaphysics is heavily Avicennian; Suarez's *Disputationes Metaphysicae* (1597 — the late-scholastic Avicennian *summa*) is the bridge between medieval Avicennianism and early-modern philosophy.
- **Eastern (Persian) Avicennian tradition.** In the Persianate Islamic world, Avicenna is read continuously from the 11th c. to the 20th, with major reworkings by [[suhrawardi|Suhrawardī]] (Illuminationism, *ḥikmat al-ishrāq*), [[ibn-arabi|Ibn ʿArabī]] (Sufi metaphysics), and [[mulla-sadra|Mullā Ṣadrā]] (*ḥikmat al-mutaʿāliya*, "transcendent wisdom" — the principle of the primacy of existence (*aṣālat al-wujūd*) reverses one Avicennian doctrine while extending the broader Avicennian frame).

## Disputes
- **Theological position.** Avicenna's God-of-the-philosophers (the Necessary Being whose essence is existence and whose knowledge of particulars is *in a universal way*) was a principal target of [[al-ghazali]]'s anathema ([[phase-5-045-al-ghazali-tahafut-al-falasifa|*Tahāfut al-Falāsifa*]]) on three counts: eternity of the world, denial of God's knowledge of particulars, denial of bodily resurrection. Avicenna's defenders ([[ibn-rushd|Averroes]] in *Tahāfut al-Tahāfut*; the later Persian Avicennians) argue that Ghazālī misreads — that Avicenna *does* hold God's knowledge of particulars *qua* universal, that the eternity-of-the-world doctrine is consistent with createdness *atemporally* understood, etc. The debate is alive in contemporary scholarship.
- **Mystical late period.** The *Pointers and Reminders* contains mystical-philosophical material that the later Eastern tradition (Suhrawardi, Mulla Sadra) reads as the seed of Persian Illuminationist mysticism; modern Western scholarship (Gutas 1988) reads this material as more philosophical than mystical in Avicenna's own intent.
- **Originality vs. al-Farabi-dependence.** Avicenna acknowledges al-Farabi's *Intentions of Aristotle's Metaphysics* as the work that finally unlocked Aristotle's metaphysics for him after he had read it 40 times without understanding; how much of Avicenna's metaphysics is novel vs. development of al-Farabian foundations is partly contested.
