# Ali al-Rida

## Identity
Son of [[musa-al-kazim]]; born in Medina ~765 (the same year his grandfather al-Sadiq died). Eighth Imam of the Twelver line, succeeding his father in 799. The most politically unusual of the Imams: in 817, the Abbasid Caliph **al-Ma'mun** (r. 813–833) — having recently won the civil war against his brother al-Amin, ruling from Marv in Khurasan, and pursuing a complex political-religious strategy that included Mu'tazila rationalism and conciliation with Shi'i partisans — *designated al-Rida as heir-apparent* (*walī al-ʿahd*) to the caliphate. Al-Rida was summoned from Medina to Marv, accepted the designation (under what degree of coercion is debated), was publicly proclaimed, and coins were struck in his name. Within two years (818) he was dead at Tus in Khurasan, almost certainly poisoned (Shi'i tradition holds al-Ma'mun himself ordered the poisoning, after the political utility of the designation had been exhausted; Cooperson 2000's analysis is more nuanced but the poisoning is plausible). His shrine at Tus / Mashhad ("the place of martyrdom" — Mashhad takes its name from the event) becomes the major Shi'i pilgrimage site in Iran and one of the holiest places in Twelver Shi'ism.

## Historical evidence
The heir-apparency episode is unusually well-documented in non-Shi'i sources because it was a major event of the Abbasid caliphate, treated extensively in [[al-tabari]]'s *Ta'rikh*, in al-Mas'udi, in al-Ya'qubi, and in numerous later sources. Coinage minted in al-Rida's name as heir-apparent survives. The death at Tus is uncontested across Sunni and Shi'i sources.

## Works
- ***al-Risala al-Dhahabiyya*** ("The Golden Treatise") — a treatise on medicine attributed to al-Rida, reportedly written for al-Ma'mun. Authenticity debated; if authentic, it would be an unusual piece of 9th-c. Imami medical writing.
- ***Sahifa al-Rida*** — devotional / hadith collection.
- Extensive attributed material in the Shi'i hadith corpus, including a notable corpus of *kalām*-theological responses to questions from Christians, Jews, Zoroastrians, and Sabaeans at al-Ma'mun's court (the famous *Munāẓarāt* — inter-religious debates).

## Role in the tradition

### The al-Ma'mun heir-apparency: meaning and consequences
The episode is the *single most politically unusual moment* in the Twelver Imami history. For two years (817–818) the Imami Imam was officially the designated successor of the Abbasid caliph. Multiple readings are available:

- **Al-Ma'mun's political strategy.** Cooperson 2000 reads the designation as al-Ma'mun's deliberate maneuver — co-opt the most respected Imami figure to legitimate his own contested caliphate, neutralize the Imami partisans by absorbing them into the caliphate, exploit Khurasani-Iranian sympathies (Khurasan had been the heartland of the Abbasid Revolution and remained pro-Hashemite). Once the political crisis was resolved, al-Rida became expendable.

- **Al-Rida's stance.** Was the acceptance forced or strategic? Shi'i tradition tends toward the *forced* reading (al-Rida had no real choice) and reads his comportment in Khurasan as exemplary *taqiyya*-quietism within an imposed political role. Some critical readings see al-Rida as a more active participant, hoping the heir-apparency might genuinely transition to an Imami caliphate.

- **The aftermath.** Al-Rida's death in 818 ended the experiment. Al-Ma'mun pursued public mourning, publicly rejected accusations of his own involvement, and shifted policy toward Mu'tazila *kalām* (the Mihna inquisition over the createdness of the Qur'an, 833–848) and away from Shi'i conciliation. The Imami line returned to political marginality under al-Rida's son [[muhammad-al-jawad]].

### Mashhad
The shrine of al-Rida at Mashhad becomes, from the late 9th c. onward, the major Iranian Shi'i pilgrimage center. Under the Safavids (1501+) and especially under the Qajars and the Pahlavi-era developments, Mashhad becomes one of the great religious cities of the Iranian Shi'i world, alongside Qom (the city of Fatima al-Ma'suma, al-Rida's sister, who died in Qom and whose shrine anchors that city). The Iranian devotional landscape, in significant part, is structured around al-Rida's shrine.

## Disputes
- **Whether al-Ma'mun was directly responsible for al-Rida's death.** Shi'i tradition is unambiguous (poisoning by al-Ma'mun's orders). Cooperson 2000 and other modern historians regard the poisoning as plausible but politically not strictly necessary; the death may have been opportune. The fact that al-Ma'mun's public mourning was extensive does not settle the question.

- **Whether al-Rida actually accepted the heir-apparency as authoritative.** Shi'i tradition reads the acceptance as forced taqiyya-quietism with no doctrinal implications for the Imami theology. Critical readings note that al-Rida did accept publicly, accept the political role, conduct inter-religious debates at al-Ma'mun's court, etc. — the *taqiyya* framing is partly later apologetic.

## Cross-tradition connections
- ↔ [[musa-al-kazim]] (father), [[muhammad-al-jawad]] (son, Ninth Imam) — the Twelver line through the heir-apparency episode.
- ↔ Abbasid caliphate / al-Ma'mun — the Imam who briefly came closest to caliphal authority.
- ↔ [[tradition-shia-islam]] — definitive of the Iranian Shi'i sacred landscape via the Mashhad shrine.
- ↔ Inter-religious *kalām* — al-Rida's debates at al-Ma'mun's court with Christians, Jews, Zoroastrians, and Sabaeans are an unusual instance of 9th-c. Imami-Imam engagement with non-Muslim traditions; preserved in ibn Babawayh's *ʿUyun Akhbar al-Rida*.
