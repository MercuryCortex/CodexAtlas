# Naram-Sin

## Identity
Naram-Sin ("Beloved of [the moon god] Sin") was the fourth king of the Akkadian Empire and grandson of [[sargon-of-akkad]], reigning ~-2254 to -2218 BCE. His 37-year reign is the high-water mark of Akkadian imperial power: he suppressed the *Great Revolt* of the Sumerian city-states against Akkadian rule, campaigned successfully into the Zagros (against the Lullubi tribes — commemorated on the famous **Victory Stele of Naram-Sin**, now in the Louvre, captured by Elamites as booty in the 12th century BCE), and extended Akkadian power further than his grandfather. He was the **first Mesopotamian ruler to claim divinity in his own lifetime** — from approximately regnal year 30, his name on inscriptions takes the *dingir* divine-determinative, and he assumes the title "**Naram-Sin, God of Akkad**" and the unprecedented imperial title "**King of the Four Quarters of the World**" (*šar kibrāt arba'i*). After his death, Akkadian power collapsed within a generation under his successors Shar-kali-sharri and the final Sargonic kings; the Gutian invasion followed.

The principal literary monument concerning Naram-Sin is the *Curse of Akkad* — a Sumerian composition (Cooper 1983) which attributes the empire's collapse to Naram-Sin's *sacrilege against Enlil's Ekur* at Nippur. The text accuses him of attacking the Ekur, destroying its sacred precincts, and provoking Enlil to summon the Gutians as instruments of divine vengeance. Whether the Ekur incident is historical is debated — the Akkadian-period building works at the Ekur are archaeologically attested but their character (renovation vs. destruction) is disputed; the *Curse of Akkad* is post-Akkadian literary etiology, plausibly a Ur III composition retroactively explaining the Akkadian collapse.

## Historical evidence
Robust:
1. **Numerous royal inscriptions** preserved in OB copies (Frayne RIME 2).
2. **The Victory Stele of Naram-Sin** (Sb 4, Louvre) — the originally Akkadian monument depicting him in horned crown (the iconography of divinity) trampling the Lullubi king Satuni. The stele itself is an extraordinary primary-source object.
3. **The Bassetki Statue** — inscribed bronze base preserving a Naram-Sin dedication, the earliest known major Mesopotamian bronze cast.
4. **The Great Revolt narrative** — preserved both in his own royal inscriptions and in later legendary tradition.
5. **Year-names** and administrative texts.
6. **The *Curse of Akkad*** — *literary*, not primary-historical, but providing the principal account of his Ekur involvement.

## Works
None directly authored. The royal inscriptions speak in his voice but are court-scribal productions. The Naram-Sin legend cycle (Cuthean Legend; later compositions) is *legendary* — Old Babylonian and Neo-Assyrian pseudo-autobiographical works in which Naram-Sin is portrayed as the cautionary archetype of the *hubristic king who ignores divine warning and brings ruin*. The Cuthean Legend in particular casts Naram-Sin as the king who fails to consult the omens, attacks an enemy without divine sanction, loses his army, and ends as a chastened figure warning his successors against repeating his error.

## Role in the tradition
Naram-Sin became the **paradigmatic hubristic king** in the Mesopotamian historical-literary memory. The trajectory from Sargon (founder, sanctioned) through Naram-Sin (divinized, sacrilegious, brings ruin) is the moral arc Mesopotamian scribal tradition imposed on the Akkadian Empire's rise and fall. This narrative — *the king who claims divinity attacks the gods and brings catastrophe* — is one of the foundational templates of ancient Near Eastern political theology. It will surface in altered form in (a) the Hebrew prophetic critique of Assyrian/Babylonian kings, (b) the Greek tragic theme of hubris, (c) the later Mesopotamian *Erra Epic* meditation on civic destruction.

His self-divinization established the imperial precedent that [[shulgi]] of Ur III would follow ~150 years later — and that Hellenistic monarchs would, distantly, echo.

## Disputes
- **Historicity of the Ekur sacrilege**. Whether Naram-Sin actually attacked the Ekur (as the *Curse of Akkad* claims) or merely renovated it in a manner the post-Akkadian Sumerian scribal class regarded as inappropriate, is unresolved. Archaeology shows Akkadian-period building activity at the Ekur but cannot adjudicate the literary claim.
- **Date of the *Curse of Akkad***. Ur III versus early Old Babylonian composition is debated; Cooper 1983 favors Ur III, in which case the Sumerian-Akkadian political memory of the collapse was still close.
- **Cause of the Akkadian collapse**. Naram-Sin's hubris (the *Curse* account) vs. climate-deterioration arguments (the "4.2 kiloyear event" / Weiss-and-Bradley climate proposals) vs. invasion-by-Gutians vs. internal succession crises — all current candidates. The collapse is multifactorial.
