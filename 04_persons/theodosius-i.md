# Theodosius I

## Identity
Flavius Theodosius (347-395) — Roman emperor 379-395 (Eastern empire from 379; full empire after the suppression of Eugenius and Magnus Maximus, 394-395). **The last emperor to rule the unified Roman Empire** before its permanent administrative-political division. Born in Hispania (Cauca, modern Coca, Segovia); son of the *magister militum* Theodosius the Elder. His sixteen-year reign **made Nicene Christianity the legally-mandated religion of the Roman Empire**, formally closed the legal recognition of Roman paganism, completed the conciliar settlement of the Christological controversies (First Council of Constantinople 381), and inaugurated the Latin-Christian *imperium-with-confessional-state* model that subsequent European Christendom inherited.

## Historical evidence
Documented. Imperial legislation in the *Codex Theodosianus* (compiled 438 by Theodosius II, codifying his grandfather's constitutions) is the primary source for the religious-political legal framework. Ecclesiastical histories of Socrates, Sozomen, Theodoret (5th c., pro-Nicene). Ambrose's surviving letters and orations (the Letter 51 on the Thessalonica massacre is canonical). Zosimus's *Historia Nova* (early 6th c., the main pagan-source hostile counter-narrative). Numismatic and epigraphic evidence is extensive.

## Role in the tradition

### The Edict of Thessalonica (380) — Nicene Christianity as the state religion
On 27 February 380 CE Theodosius issued (jointly with his co-emperors Gratian and Valentinian II) the **Edict of Thessalonica** (*Cunctos populos*) — preserved in the Codex Theodosianus 16.1.2 — declaring that all peoples of the empire should follow the religion "which the divine apostle Peter delivered to the Romans, which the pontiff Damasus and Peter, bishop of Alexandria, a man of apostolic holiness, evidently profess." The edict effectively made the **Nicene-Constantinopolitan formulation of Christian doctrine the legally-mandated religion of the empire** — naming the formula by reference to Pope Damasus and Patriarch Peter of Alexandria as the touchstones of orthodoxy. The edict is the **legal foundation of the Christian-imperial confessional state**: Christianity now is the imperial religion, dissent from Nicaea is "heresy" in the new technical legal-administrative sense. See [[event-edict-of-thessalonica-380]].

### The First Council of Constantinople (381) — completion of the Nicene creed
Theodosius convened the First Council of Constantinople (May-July 381) — the Second Ecumenical Council — which **completed the Nicene-Constantinopolitan creed** as the dogmatic backbone of Christianity. The 381 council affirmed the full divinity of the Holy Spirit (against the Pneumatomachi), expanded the Nicaea-325 creed into the form Christianity recites to the present, and resolved the Constantinopolitan succession-dispute by confirming Gregory of Nazianzus as Patriarch (briefly) and then Nectarius. The council declared Constantinople the second see of the Christian world after Rome (canon 3 — "because [Constantinople] is the New Rome"), planting the seed of the Constantinopolitan-Roman primacy dispute that runs through the medieval schism.

### Anti-pagan legislation 391-393 — the legal end of Roman public paganism
Theodosius's legislation of 391-393 (Codex Theodosianus 16.10.10-12) **banned all blood sacrifice, the visiting of pagan temples, and various forms of household cult**. The Olympic Games — a pan-Hellenic religious festival in Hera-and-Zeus context — were closed in 393 (or possibly 394). The 391 sack of the Serapeum of Alexandria (under the patriarch Theophilus, but under Theodosius's general permission) destroyed the great Greco-Egyptian temple of [[serapis|Sarapis]]. The Vestal Virgins in Rome were disestablished and the fire on the altar of the Roman state was extinguished (391). These laws **mark the legal end of Roman paganism as a recognized public religion**, although household and rural pagan practice continued for generations. See [[event-closure-of-pagan-mysteries-392-393]].

### The massacre of Thessalonica (390) and the penance to Ambrose — load-bearing church-state precedent
In 390 the population of Thessalonica rioted against the local Gothic garrison; the *magister militum* Buthericus was killed. Theodosius ordered massive reprisal — variously reported as ~7,000 civilians killed in the hippodrome over several days. **Bishop [[ambrose-of-milan|Ambrose of Milan]] then excommunicated Theodosius**, refusing him communion until he publicly confessed and did penance. Theodosius eventually submitted, performing public penance in the cathedral of Milan, and was re-admitted to communion only at Christmas 390. The episode is the **canonical late-antique precedent for the bishop's authority over the Christian emperor** — invoked across the next millennium of medieval Latin-Christian church-state struggles (Henry IV at Canossa, the Investiture Controversy, Becket vs Henry II, etc.). See [[ambrose-of-milan]], [[event-massacre-of-thessalonica-390]].

### The Frigidus and the suppression of Eugenius (394)
Theodosius's 394 defeat of the western usurper Eugenius at the Battle of the Frigidus (5-6 September 394) was framed by contemporary Christian sources (Augustine *City of God* 5.26) as a divine victory for Christianity over paganism — the Eugenius regime had been supported by a senatorial-pagan faction in Rome that had briefly attempted to restore some traditional cults. The Frigidus is the political-military closure of the legal-pagan-restoration possibility.

### Unification (briefly) and the final division
Theodosius ruled the entire unified empire from January 394 (after defeating Eugenius) to his death January 395 — the **last emperor to rule the undivided Roman Empire**. At his death the empire passed to his sons Honorius (West, 10 years old) and Arcadius (East, 18 years old), and the de facto separation of Latin West and Greek East became permanent (notwithstanding nominal claims of single legitimate emperorship).

## Disputes
- **The Edict of Thessalonica's actual effective scope.** Whether the 380 edict was a sweeping religious-establishment law or (per McLynn 1994 and other revisionists) a more narrow doctrinal-clarification edict that contemporary sources later elevated to the "Christianity becomes state religion" framing has been debated. The traditional reading (Cameron 2011) remains majority.
- **Theodosius's personal religious commitment.** Some sources (Zosimus) emphasize political pragmatism; the Christian sources (Socrates, Sozomen) emphasize personal Nicene conviction. Both are partially true; the legislation is clear regardless.
- **The Ambrose-Theodosius confrontation.** McLynn 1994 reassessed the older heroizing accounts (Theodoret's narrative of public excommunication was probably literary elaboration; the actual sequence was more diplomatic, with Theodosius submitting before formal excommunication). The political-symbolic effect (the bishop has authority to discipline the emperor) was the same.
- **The 393 Olympic Games closure.** Whether the actual mechanism was a single Theodosian edict or a gradual administrative process is debated; the festival had effectively ceased by ~395.
