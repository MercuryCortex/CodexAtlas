# Pamela Colman Smith

## Identity

**Pamela Colman Smith** (b. Pimlico, London, 16 February 1878; d. Bude, Cornwall, 18 September 1951) — "Pixie" to everyone who knew her — was an artist, illustrator, folklorist, theatre and costume designer, publisher and suffragist. Born in London to American parents, she spent parts of her childhood in Manchester, in **Jamaica**, and in Brooklyn, where she studied at the **Pratt Institute** under Arthur Wesley Dow. She collected and published Jamaican Anansi tales (*Annancy Stories*, 1899), toured with the Lyceum Theatre company around Ellen Terry and Henry Irving, kept company with W.B. Yeats and Bram Stoker, edited her own hand-coloured magazine *The Green Sheaf* (1903–1904), and had her drawings shown at Alfred Stieglitz's **291** gallery in New York in 1907.

In **1901** she was initiated into the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn; at the order's 1903 schism she followed [[arthur-edward-waite]] into his Independent and Rectified Rite. In 1909, on Waite's commission, she drew **all 78 cards** of the deck published that year by William Rider & Son.

## The decision that changed the practice

Waite supplied the doctrine: a Golden Dawn correspondence scheme, a specified iconography for the trumps, the swap of Strength and Justice, the Fool assigned to *aleph*. What Waite did **not** supply, and what no previous deck in five centuries had, was a picture on every pip card.

Every European tarot before 1909 — the Visconti-Sforza decks, the Marseille pattern, Etteilla's *Livre de Thot* — showed the 40 numbered Minor Arcana as repeated suit-signs: five cups, seven swords, arranged decoratively. Reading them required memorising a correspondence table. Smith drew a **scene** on each: a woman weeping in bed (Nine of Swords), a figure walking away from eight cups at dusk, a man carrying ten staves bent double under them, a heart pierced by three blades under rain.

The consequence was structural, not decorative. It made a 78-card reading **narratable by anyone**, from the picture, without the Kabbalistic apparatus that had justified the deck in the first place. That is why the Waite-Smith pack — not the Marseille pattern, and not any Golden Dawn manuscript — became the world's standard tarot, and why the modern practice is largely intuitive-pictorial rather than correspondence-driven. The deck's esoteric scaffolding is Waite's; its reach is Smith's.

Scholars generally hold that Waite gave her close direction on the trumps and comparatively little on the minors — meaning the most influential portion of the deck is substantially her invention (Decker & Dummett 2002; Kaplan et al. 2018).

## Role in the tradition — and the attribution

Smith was paid a **flat fee** for the commission and received no royalty. Rider marketed the pack as Waite's. For most of the twentieth century it was known as the "Rider-Waite" deck and she was, at best, "the artist". She converted to Catholicism in 1911, worked for the suffrage campaign, ran a holiday house for priests in Cornwall on a legacy, and died in Bude in 1951 in debt; her effects were sold to satisfy creditors and her grave is unmarked.

The corrective naming — **Waite-Smith**, or **Rider-Waite-Smith** — is now standard in both scholarship and the trade. This vault uses it. The recovery of her biography is owed principally to Stuart Kaplan's decades of archival work, consolidated with Mary K. Greer, Elizabeth Foley O'Connor and Melinda Boyd Parsons in *Pamela Colman Smith: The Untold Story* (2018).

Her position in the tarot chain is worth stating plainly against the origin-myth this vault documents elsewhere. The deck that carries the "ancient Egyptian" claim into the twentieth century was drawn in **1909**, in London, by a thirty-one-year-old Jamaican-raised American illustrator working from a Golden Dawn table of correspondences and her own theatrical eye. There is no older layer under it.

## Disputes

- **How much of the Minor Arcana is hers.** Waite's own account claims general direction; Smith left no statement. The consensus reads the minors as largely her invention within his scheme, but the division cannot be fixed card by card.
- **Sources for the pictorial minors.** The 15th-century **Sola-Busca** tarocchi — the earliest known pack with illustrated pip cards, photographs of which the British Museum acquired in 1907 — is widely proposed as a source, with several Smith cards (notably the Three of Swords) showing close correspondence. The proposal is well supported but not documented by any statement of Smith's.
- **Her Golden Dawn grade and her level of doctrinal engagement.** Records of her progress in the order are thin; how far she worked the system, as against illustrating it, is not recoverable.
- **Spelling.** "Coleman" is a persistent misspelling; the correct form is **Colman**.
