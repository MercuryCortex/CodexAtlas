# Mengzi (Mencius)

## Identity
Meng Ke, conventionally known as Mengzi ("Master Meng") and Latinized as Mencius, was a Confucian philosopher and political adviser of the mid-Warring States (~-372 to -289). He was born in the small state of Zou, just south of Confucius's Lu, and traveled state to state in his middle years offering political advice to the major rulers of the era — King Hui of Liang, King Xuan of Qi, Duke Wen of Teng. He found none willing to put his program into operation, but his recorded advisories — preserved in the *Mengzi*, seven books in two parts — became the most influential systematization of Confucian thought, and from Zhu Xi's elevation of the Mengzi to the Four Books (~1190) the operational scripture of Chinese state-Confucianism for seven centuries.

## Historical evidence
One of the better-attested figures of the Warring States. The *Mengzi* itself preserves extended dialogues, naming specific rulers, ministers, and disciples. Sima Qian's *Shiji* ch. 74 gives a joint biography with Xunzi. Mengzi's disciple Wan Zhang (whose name attaches to two books of the Mengzi) and his fellow disciple Gongsun Chou are well-attested. The geography and chronology (Hui of Liang's "old age" when Mengzi visited; Mengzi's removal to Qi under King Xuan) are consistent with what is otherwise known of these rulers.

## Works
- The *Mengzi*: seven books, each in two parts. Generally regarded as substantially by Mengzi himself with disciple-editorial contribution.
- Traditional account makes Mengzi a student of Confucius's grandson Zisi (and thence in the line: Confucius → Zengzi → Zisi → Mengzi); modern scholars regard this as an idealized lineage rather than direct transmission.

## Role in the tradition
The second-most-important Confucian (after Confucius) and the philosopher whose particular reading became dominant in late-imperial Chinese state-Confucianism. Zhu Xi (~1130-1200) elevated the Mengzi to one of the Four Books (alongside the Analects, the Great Learning, and the Doctrine of the Mean), placing it at the foundation of the imperial examination system from the Yuan (1313) through the late Qing (1905). The Mengzi's doctrine of innate moral goodness, the four sprouts, and the conditional Mandate of Heaven became the operational anthropological-political theology of late-imperial Chinese governance. Modern Chinese scholarship — and most modern Western Confucian studies — sustains this Mencian centering.

## Disputes
- **Date.** Conventional ~-372 to -289 (a near-contemporary of Aristotle, who died -322). The precision is partly conventional; the period is secure (late mid-Warring States).
- **The Mengzi's authorship.** Generally regarded as more unified than the Analects; probably composed by Mengzi and his immediate disciples. Internal stylistic variation prompts some layered-composition proposals, weaker than for the Analects.
- **"Human nature is good"** — interpretation of *xìng shàn*: all humans have innate moral sprouts that need cultivation (mainstream); or, human nature *tends* toward good in proper environments; or, the moral capacities are universally present but easily corrupted. The 2,500-year-old "Mengzi-Xunzi debate" remains philosophically live.
- **The Confucius-Zisi-Mengzi lineage.** Traditional but historically uncertain; functional rather than directly attested.
