# Mengzi (Mencius)

## Identity
Meng Ke, conventionally known as Mengzi ("Master Meng") and Latinized as Mencius, was a Confucian philosopher and political adviser of the mid-Warring States (~-372 to -289). He was born in the small state of Zou, just south of [[confucius]]'s Lu, and traveled state to state in his middle years offering political advice to the major rulers of the era — King Hui of Liang, King Xuan of Qi, Duke Wen of Teng. He found none willing to put his program into operation, but his recorded advisories — preserved in the *Mengzi*, seven books in two parts — became the most influential systematization of Confucian thought, and from Zhu Xi's elevation of the Mengzi to the Four Books (~1190) the operational scripture of Chinese state-Confucianism for seven centuries.

## Historical evidence
One of the better-attested figures of the Warring States. The [[phase-2-023-mengzi]] itself preserves extended dialogues, naming specific rulers, ministers, and disciples. Sima Qian's *Shiji* ch. 74 gives a joint biography with [[xunzi-person]]. Mengzi's disciple Wan Zhang (whose name attaches to two books of the Mengzi) and his fellow disciple Gongsun Chou are well-attested. The geography and chronology (Hui of Liang's "old age" when Mengzi visited; Mengzi's removal to Qi under King Xuan) are consistent with what is otherwise known of these rulers.

## Works
- The *Mengzi*: seven books, each in two parts. Generally regarded as substantially by Mengzi himself with disciple-editorial contribution.
- Traditional account makes Mengzi a student of Confucius's grandson Zisi (and thence in the line: Confucius → Zengzi → Zisi → Mengzi); modern scholars regard this as an idealized lineage rather than direct transmission.

## Role in the tradition
The second-most-important Confucian (after [[confucius]]) and the philosopher whose particular reading became dominant in late-imperial Chinese state-Confucianism. Zhu Xi (~1130-1200) elevated the Mengzi to one of the Four Books (alongside the Analects, the Great Learning, and the Doctrine of the Mean), placing it at the foundation of the imperial examination system from the Yuan (1313) through the late Qing (1905). The Mengzi's doctrine of innate moral goodness, the four sprouts, and the conditional Mandate of Heaven became the operational anthropological-political theology of late-imperial Chinese governance.

## The four sprouts (siduan 四端)

Mengzi's foundational philosophical argument: every human already has four innate moral "sprouts" (*duān*) — capacities that, if cultivated, grow into full virtues:

| Sprout | Virtue | Mengzi's example |
|---|---|---|
| Compassion / commiseration (*ceyin* 惻隱) | *Ren* 仁 (humaneness) | Instinctive distress seeing a child about to fall into a well |
| Shame / aversion (*xiue* 羞惡) | *Yi* 義 (righteousness) | Instinctive refusal to accept food obtained by humiliation, even when starving |
| Deference / courtesy (*cirang* 辭讓) | *Li* 禮 (ritual propriety) | Instinctive courtesy toward elders |
| Approval / disapproval (*shifei* 是非) | *Zhi* 智 (moral wisdom) | Instinctive sense of what is right and wrong |

The sprouts are universal — every human has them — but they are easily suppressed or corrupted by bad environment, poverty, or habituation to vice. Moral cultivation (*xiū*) is tending the garden, not planting new seeds. This is Mengzi's response to the sceptic: you already know how to be good; you need help not forgetting.

The analogy to [[aristotle]]'s account of moral development is close but not identical: Aristotle argues that virtuous *hexis* (stable disposition) is *formed* through habituation rather than innately present. The philosophical dispute between innate sprouts and habituated character is still live.

## MASSIVE WIN 1 — Innate goodness: Mengzi ↔ Rousseau ↔ Wang Yangming (independent convergence)

Mengzi's doctrine that human nature is fundamentally good (*xìng shàn* 性善, ~300 BCE) has two major structural parallels in entirely separate traditions:

**[[jean-jacques-rousseau]]** (*Discourse on the Origin of Inequality*, 1755 CE): the natural human — the hypothetical pre-social being — is characterized by *amour de soi* (self-love) and *pitié* (natural compassion for suffering). Moral corruption is external and societal. Society, inequality, and property corrupt a naturally good being. The structural logic is identical to Mengzi: goodness is the original condition; corruption is deviation.

**[[wang-yangming]]** (*liangzhi* 良知, "innate moral knowledge", ~1520 CE): building directly on Mengzi, Wang argues that every person already has perfect moral knowledge — it just needs to be actualized through *zhī xíng hé yī* (unity of knowledge and action). Wang Yangming's school became the most important Chinese intellectual movement of the 16th century and spread widely into Korea and Japan.

Three instances of the same philosophical position: one historical chain (Mengzi → Wang Yangming, same tradition), one independent parallel (Rousseau, 2,050 years later, French Enlightenment). The independence of Rousseau makes this a genuine convergence rather than transmission. The vault edge between [[mengzi-person]] and [[jean-jacques-rousseau]] is one of the highest-value cross-tradition edges for the human-nature cluster.

## MASSIVE WIN 2 — The right to rebel: Mandate of Heaven and Locke's social contract

Mengzi's political theory is the earliest systematic formulation of justified popular revolution in the record. *Mengzi* 7B:14: "The people are the most important element; the spirits of the land and grain come next; the sovereign counts for the least." A ruler who fails to provide for the people, oppresses them, or acts immorally loses the Mandate of Heaven (*tiānmìng* 天命) — and can then legitimately be removed, even killed. Mengzi calls the execution of the last Shang king a justified killing of a "mere fellow" (*yī fū*), not a regicide.

The structural parallel with [[john-locke]]'s *Two Treatises of Government* (1689) is striking: government derives legitimacy from its service to the people; when it fails that purpose, the people have a right to resist and replace it. Locke was not influenced by Mengzi (though Jesuit texts on Chinese political theory were in circulation in 17th-century Europe), and the parallel appears to be structural rather than transmitted — two thinkers in very different circumstances arriving at similar conclusions about the conditionality of political authority.

The temporal gap is approximately 1,950 years. Mengzi's version is grounded in a cosmological framework (the Mandate of Heaven as a moral-cosmic order that can be forfeited); Locke's is grounded in natural law and individual rights. The difference in foundation makes the convergence of conclusion even more interesting.

## MASSIVE WIN 3 — The anthropological inversion: Mengzi vs. Xunzi vs. Augustine (is human nature good or bad?)

The three most consequential positions on human moral nature in world intellectual history:

| Thinker | Position | Tradition | Date |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mengzi** | Humans are born with innate moral sprouts (good) | Confucianism | ~300 BCE China |
| **[[xunzi-person]]** | Human nature is crooked/inclined to bad; ritual corrects | Confucianism | ~280–235 BCE China |
| **[[augustine-of-hippo]]** | Original sin: humans are born with a damaged will, inclined to evil | Latin Christianity | ~400 CE North Africa |

Mengzi and [[xunzi-person]] are internal rivals within Confucianism — the same tradition, producing opposite conclusions on the most fundamental question in ethics. Xunzi's position (*xìng è* 性惡, "human nature is bad") is not nihilistic: ritual and education can correct the natural inclination. But he denies Mengzi's claim that the good is already there in seed form.

The [[augustine-of-hippo]] parallel is the deepest cross-tradition edge here: Augustine's doctrine of original sin (developed against Pelagius, who argued that humans retain free will and natural goodness) is structurally identical to the Xunzian position in Chinese terms — and the direct opposite of Mengzi. The debate between Mengzi and Xunzi maps onto the debate between Pelagius and Augustine, in a completely different civilization, 700 years later. The philosophical question — are humans naturally good and corrupted by circumstance, or naturally prone to evil and in need of correction? — is culture-independent. Both traditions produced both answers.

## Disputes
- **Date.** Conventional ~-372 to -289 (a near-contemporary of [[aristotle]], who died -322). The precision is partly conventional; the period is secure (late mid-Warring States).
- **The Mengzi's authorship.** Generally regarded as more unified than the Analects; probably composed by Mengzi and his immediate disciples. Internal stylistic variation prompts some layered-composition proposals, weaker than for the Analects.
- **"Human nature is good"** — interpretation of *xìng shàn*: all humans have innate moral sprouts that need cultivation (mainstream); or, human nature *tends* toward good in proper environments; or, the moral capacities are universally present but easily corrupted. The 2,500-year-old "Mengzi-Xunzi debate" remains philosophically live.
- **The Confucius-Zisi-Mengzi lineage.** Traditional but historically uncertain; functional rather than directly attested.
