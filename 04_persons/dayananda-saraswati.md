# Dayananda Saraswati

## Identity
Gujarati Brahmin renouncer, Vedic restorationist, and founder of the *Ārya Samāj* (1875) — the most ambitious 19th-c. Hindu reform movement and the principal counter-program to both the Christian-influenced Brahmo Samaj and the orthodox Brahmanical mainstream. Born Mūlśaṅkara to a Saurashtra Audich Brahmin family at Tankara (Gujarat); intense early religious experience reading the Śivapurāṇa (~1838); fled an arranged marriage (1846); spent 15 years (1846–1860) wandering as an ascetic seeker, finally finding the teacher Svami Virajananda Sarasvati at Mathura (1860–1863) — a blind Sanskrit grammarian who instructed Dayananda to teach only the four Vedas as the ultimate authority, rejecting Puranas, Tantras, and post-Vedic accretions. From 1863 Dayananda began his public mission of Vedic-restorationist reform; founded the Arya Samaj at Bombay 10 April 1875; the institution's principal subsequent center became Lahore (Punjab) and the Punjab-Delhi-UP belt. Poisoned at Jodhpur in 1883 (per the standard account) by the courtesan of a Maharaja he had publicly criticized.

## Works
- ***Satyārtha Prakāśa*** (The Light of Truth, 1875; rev. 1882): the foundational creedal text of the Arya Samaj. 14 chapters; combines doctrinal exposition of the Arya Samaj's "Vedic" theology (one formless God, no idols, no caste-by-birth, no avatara doctrine, no temple worship) with vigorous polemic against Puranic Hinduism, Vaishnava sect practices, Jainism, Buddhism, Sikhism, Christianity, and Islam. Among the most polemically aggressive religious-reform texts of 19th-c. South Asia.
- ***Ṛgvedādi-bhāṣya-bhūmikā*** (1876): introductory volume to his planned full Vedic commentary, laying out his interpretive principles — including the contested claim that the Vedas are not really polytheistic but speak figuratively of one God under multiple appellations.
- ***Saṃskāra-vidhi*** (Rituals manual): an Arya Samaj household-ritual handbook, simplified from Brahmanical *gṛhya-sūtra* tradition, that allowed laity (including women and the lower castes) to perform Vedic-style rituals.
- ***Veda-bhāṣya***: partial Vedic commentary (Yajurveda, partial Rigveda).

## Role in the tradition
- **Arya Samaj**: founder of the most institutionally durable 19th-c. Hindu reform organization. The Arya Samaj remained particularly strong in Punjab, Haryana, UP, and the global Indian diaspora (Mauritius, Trinidad, Fiji, Suriname, South Africa, East Africa — wherever the late-19th-c. indentured-labor diaspora went). The DAV (Dayanand Anglo-Vedic) school system spread Arya Samaj-style education across the North Indian middle class.
- **Vedic restorationism**: the slogan *"Vedoṅ kī or lauto"* ("Return to the Vedas") makes Dayananda the principal 19th-c. articulator of Hindu *sola-scriptura*-style reform (the structural parallel to Protestant Christian *sola scriptura* is genuine, though Dayananda's "Veda" is the Saṃhitā layer only, not the entire Brahmanical canon).
- **Anti-caste-by-birth, pro-shuddhi**: Dayananda rejected hereditary caste and instituted *śuddhi* (purification / reconversion) ceremonies allowing those who had converted to Christianity or Islam to "return" to Hinduism — a major innovation that re-shaped 20th-c. Indian conversion politics. Hindu nationalist movements drew explicitly on Dayananda's *śuddhi* program.
- **Forerunner of Hindutva**: V.D. Savarkar's *Hindutva* (1923 — see [[phase-7-029-hindutva]]) draws explicit ideological inheritance from Dayananda; the contemporary Sangh Parivar treats Dayananda as one of its founding fathers.

## Cross-tradition connections
- **Hindu Protestant-style scripturalism**: Dayananda's "Vedas alone" maps structurally onto Protestant *sola scriptura* — primary text as exclusive authority, all later tradition as accretion subject to scriptural correction. Jordens 1978 and Jones 1976 trace the structural parallels (without arguing direct influence from Protestantism, though Dayananda was aware of Christian-missionary critiques of Hindu polytheism and constructed his reform partly in response to them).
- **Polemic against Islam, Christianity, Sikhism, and Puranic Hinduism**: chapter 14 of the *Satyārtha Prakāśa* contains the most aggressive Hindu-reform critique of Islam and Christianity in 19th-c. South Asian literature; chapters 11–13 polemicize against Puranic Hinduism, Vaishnavism, and Sikhism. The polemical model — using shared scriptural-rationalist methods to argue against multiple traditions simultaneously — has structural cousins in late-19th-c. comparative-religion polemic (Müller's *Sacred Books of the East* project, the rationalist-orientalist critique of all "popular religion").
- **Theosophical Society initial connection (1879–1881)**: Olcott and Blavatsky's initial 1879 arrival in India produced a short-lived alliance with Dayananda's Arya Samaj, which was dissolved by 1882 over doctrinal incompatibilities — the Theosophists' polytheistic-occult orientation was incompatible with Dayananda's strict Vedic monotheism. The alliance and its failure is a documented moment in the 19th-c. Hindu-Western esotericism entanglement. See [[helena-blavatsky]] and [[henry-steel-olcott]].
- **Hindu diaspora export**: Arya Samaj missions to the Caribbean, Fiji, and East Africa exported Hindu-modernist reform across the global Indian indentured-labor diaspora — comparable in structure to Christian missionary diaspora expansion.

## Disputes
- **Vedic interpretation**: Dayananda's interpretation of the Vedic Saṃhitās as figuratively monotheistic, rather than literally polytheistic, was rejected by 19th-c. Indological mainstream (Max Müller, Whitney) and remains a minority position philologically. The Arya Samaj's "monotheistic Veda" reading is a religious-interpretive choice rather than a philological consensus.
- **Anti-caste vs. caste-functional**: Dayananda rejected caste-by-birth but retained *varṇa* (the four-class system) as a functional-occupational category. Some modern Dalit critics treat this as insufficient reform.
- **Death**: poisoning by the Maharaja of Jodhpur's courtesan is the traditional and most-supported account; some alternative accounts exist.
