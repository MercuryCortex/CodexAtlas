# Rabia of Basra

## Identity
Rābiʿa al-ʿAdawiyya (~717–801) was the foundational female figure of early Sufism — an ascetic and mystic of Basra in the early Abbasid period who transformed Sufi piety from fear-of-hell and hope-of-paradise into pure love of God. Born to poverty in Basra; sold into slavery as a child according to traditional accounts; freed for her piety; lived a life of solitary devotion. Her surviving sayings emphasize disinterested love (*maḥabba*) of God for God's own sake: "O God, if I worship Thee for fear of Hell, burn me in Hell; and if I worship Thee in hope of Paradise, exclude me from Paradise; but if I worship Thee for Thine own sake, withhold not Thy everlasting Beauty." The famous image of Rābiʿa carrying a torch in one hand and a bucket of water in the other — "to burn paradise and quench hell, so people may love God for God alone" — captures the radical reorientation of Sufi piety she effected.

## Historical evidence
Limited contemporary documentation; substantial later hagiographic material (especially in ʿAṭṭār's *Tadhkirat al-Awliyāʾ* ~1220). The historical kernel is widely accepted, though many specific anecdotes are likely embellishment.

## Works
Nothing survives in her own hand; her teachings come down to us through later Sufi anthologies — sayings, prayers, and poetic verses preserved by al-Sulamī, ʿAṭṭār, and others.

## Role in the tradition
The foundational female Sufi; the inaugurator of Sufi love-mysticism (*maḥabba*) that becomes central in later Sufism (Junayd, al-Ḥallāj, al-Ghazālī, Ibn ʿArabī, Rūmī). In modern Muslim feminist Islam, Rābiʿa is the principal historical resource for arguing the deep Islamic legitimacy of women's spiritual authority and public religious teaching.
