# Arganthonius of Tartessos

## Identity
Arganthonius (*Arganthonios* in Greek) is the only named king of Tartessos in surviving ancient sources — and the stories told about him make him one of the most intriguing figures in pre-Roman Iberian history, balancing between attested historical contact and legendary elaboration. His name is almost certainly Tartessian (possibly Celtic): *arganto-* = "silver" (cognate with Latin *argentum*, Gaulish *arganto-*, Welsh *arian*) + *-nio-* = a suffix. "Arganthonius" probably means something like "the Silver One" or "the Silver Lord" — a kingly epithet referencing either Tartessian silver-wealth or a ritual/sacred association with silver.

## The Herodotus Account
Herodotus (*Histories* I.163, written c. 440 BCE) is the main source:

> *"The Phocaeans were the first Greeks to make long sea voyages; it was they who discovered the Adriatic, Tyrrhenian, Iberia, and Tartessos. They did not sail in merchant vessels but in fifty-oared galleys. When they arrived at Tartessos, they became friends with the king of the Tartessians, whose name was Arganthonius, who ruled Tartessos for eighty years and lived a hundred and twenty in all. The Phocaeans became such good friends with this man that he first urged them to quit Ionia and settle wherever they liked in his land; and then, when he could not persuade them to do that, on learning of the Mede [Persian expansion] how it was increasing, he gave them money to build a wall round their city [Phocaea]."*

This passage establishes several historically plausible facts:
1. The Phocaeans (from Phocaea on the Ionian coast, modern Turkey) made early contact with Tartessos — confirmed by the "Huelva Hoard" (bronze artifacts, 10th-9th c. BCE) showing eastern Mediterranean connections
2. Tartessos had a king with a Celtic/Tartessian name
3. The Tartessian king was wealthy enough to fund the construction of city walls in Anatolia
4. The Phocaean-Tartessian relationship was close enough for a relocation offer

## The Legendary Age: 120 Years
Herodotus gives Arganthonius 120 years of life and 80 of reign; Anacreon, writing c. 540 BCE (possibly contemporary with the events), uses "Arganthonius" as a proverbial figure of extreme longevity. This extraordinary age is either:
1. A real generational compression — "Arganthonius" as a dynastic title used by multiple kings, telescoped in Greek memory into one figure
2. A mythological elaboration — the wealthy, remote, hospitable western king given legendary longevity as an attribute of his otherworldly Western position. The world's western edge was the land of eternal life and blessed immortals in Greek mythology (Elysian Fields, Isles of the Blessed) — a king who lived there for 120 years is structurally consistent with the Greek mythological imagination about the West
3. A genuine oral tradition from Tartessos itself — a king whose reign was memorable enough to preserve for three generations before Herodotus wrote it down

**Cross-tradition finding — The 120-Year King and the Western Immortal Archetype**
Arganthonius's legendary age connects him to a cross-tradition archetype: the figure at the world's western edge who has transcended normal mortality.
- The Celtic Otherworld (*Tír na nÓg* = "Land of Youth") is characterized by the absence of aging and death
- The biblical Methuselah (969 years), Enoch (365 years, "taken by God"), and the antediluvian patriarchs embody the same structure: the closer to the sacred origin/edge, the longer the life
- Tithonus (Greek mythology) is granted immortality by Eos — but without eternal youth; he shrinks to a cicada. The immortality of the western blessed requires the proper conditions (the Elysian Fields, not the mortal world)
- Arganthonius at the Atlantic edge of the world, ruling for 80 years and dying at 120, sits exactly in the zone where the Greek mythological imagination expected long-lived kings to dwell

The "Silver King" ruling at the world's western edge, granting hospitality and silver gifts to travelers, is structurally the same figure as the Celtic Otherworld king who greets those who reach the Blessed Isles. See [[finisterre-end-of-world]].

## Historical Significance
Arganthonius's generosity to the Phocaeans — funding their city walls — places him at the hinge moment of Greek westward expansion and Persian eastward pressure (c. 600–540 BCE). The Phocaean Greeks who traded with Tartessos became the founders of Massalia (Marseille), Emporion (Empúries), and other western Mediterranean Greek colonies. The Tartessian-Greek connection was thus a direct conduit for:
1. Tartessian/Phoenician western geographical mythology entering Greek cultural memory (→ Heracles's western labors)
2. Greek scientific geography (Hecataeus, Herodotus) incorporating Atlantic European data
3. The eventual Roman interest in Iberia that led to conquest of the Tartessian zone in the 2nd century BCE

## The Silver Etymology and Lusitanian Connections
*Arganto-* ("silver") is a Celtic/Tartessian root. The same root appears in:
- Latin *argentum* → French *argent* → the currency name for multiple languages
- The country of **Argentina** (Spanish *plata* = silver, but *Argentina* uses the Latin *argentum* root)
- The Lusitanian-Gallaecian nobility, who in Roman-period inscriptions often bear names with *argant-* or similar silver/bright roots

If Tartessian was indeed a Celtic language or proto-Celtic (Koch's hypothesis, contested), then "the Silver King" would be a Celtic-named ruler. If Tartessian was a separate IE branch (mainstream position), the *arganto-* root still demonstrates IE shared vocabulary for silver/precious metal. Either way, Arganthonius's name is the oldest attested evidence of a named individual in the territory that would become Portugal.
