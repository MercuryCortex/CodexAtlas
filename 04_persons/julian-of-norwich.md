# Julian of Norwich

## Identity
A late-14th / early-15th-c. **English anchoress** at the church of St. Julian, Conisford (Norwich, East Anglia), from whose dedication she takes her name (her birth name is unknown). On **8 May 1373**, aged 30, during a near-fatal illness, Julian experienced sixteen **showings** (revelations) over approximately one day. She wrote the experience in two stages: the **Short Text** ~1373–1388, and the much-expanded theologically-developed **Long Text** ~1395–1410. **Julian is the first identifiable woman to have written a book in the English language**, and the *Revelations of Divine Love* is the **first book in English by a woman** to survive.

## Historical evidence
- The Long Text survives in Paris (BnF Fonds anglais 40, ~1580 — the Sloane-tradition mss are later copies of an earlier exemplar; the Westminster Cathedral ms. is the earliest fragment).
- The Short Text survives in the Amherst Manuscript (BL Add. 37790, ~1450).
- Margery Kempe's *Book* records her ~1413 visit to Julian for spiritual counsel — the only external attestation of Julian as a living person.
- Norwich civic / diocesan records confirm a "Dame Julian" anchoress at St. Julian's Conisford 1394, 1404, 1415.

## Works
- ***A Revelation of Love*** (Long Text) — [[phase-5-041-revelations-of-divine-love]]. 86 chapters of theological elaboration.
- ***A Vision Showed to a Devout Woman*** (Short Text) — 25 chapters; the earlier, less theologically-developed account.

## Role in the tradition
- **Vernacular English mystical theologian.** Julian writes Middle English at the height of its 14th-c. literary development (contemporary with Chaucer, the *Cloud of Unknowing*, Walter Hilton, Richard Rolle).
- **Maternal Christology.** Chapters 58–63 of the Long Text develop the **Christ-as-Mother** theology more fully than any other Western medieval source. Bynum 1982 *Christ as Mother* placed Julian in the broader 12th-15th-c. tradition (Anselm, Bernard, Aelred, William of Saint-Thierry had developed maternal-Jesus language; Julian gives it the most sustained theological articulation).
- **Apophatic optimism.** Julian's *'al shal be wel'* is a theological position, not a sentimental one — she sees sin as *behovely* (necessary) within the divine plan, and ultimate restoration as certain. Turner 2011 reads her as a serious *apokatastatic* theologian (universal-restoration position).

## Disputes
- **Was Julian a nun, a Benedictine, or a laywoman who became an anchoress?** Probably the third — there is no evidence she had taken religious vows before her enclosure. Turner 2011 ch. 1.
- **What was her social class?** Probably literate and from a Norwich burgher / clerical-adjacent background. The theological sophistication of the Long Text suggests substantial reading-access.
- **Universal restoration.** How fully Julian embraces apokatastasis is debated; she explicitly says some are saved and some are not, but her *'al shal be wel'* statements push toward the universalist reading. Turner 2011.
