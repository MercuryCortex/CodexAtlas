# Snorri Sturluson

## Identity
Icelandic chieftain, lawspeaker, historian, poet, and mythographer; one of the most consequential medieval Northern European authors. Snorri was born ~1179 into the politically prominent Sturlung clan; raised at Oddi (a center of Icelandic learning); twice held the office of *lögsögumaðr* (lawspeaker of the Alþingi, 1215–1218 and 1222–1232); accumulated extensive property and political power; was assassinated in his bath at Reykholt on September 23, 1241 by enemies acting on the orders of King Hákon IV of Norway, transmitted through the chieftain Gizurr Þorvaldsson.

## Historical evidence
Extensively documented for a medieval figure: *Íslendinga saga* (within the Sturlunga compilation) gives a near-contemporary account of his life and death; Norwegian royal records; his own works' authorial colophons in some manuscripts.

## Works
- *Prose Edda* / *Snorra Edda* (~1220) — the four-part mythographic-poetic handbook (Prologue, Gylfaginning, Skáldskaparmál, Háttatal). The principal systematic source on Norse mythology.
- *Heimskringla* (~1230) — the sagas of the Norwegian kings, from mythic-prehistoric times through the 12th c.; one of the great works of medieval European historiography.
- *Egils saga Skallagrímssonar* — the saga of the Icelandic warrior-poet Egil; modern philological consensus attributes this to Snorri though it carries no authorial colophon.
- Skaldic poetry.

## Role in the tradition
Snorri is *the* central figure of Norse-mythological scholarship: without him, we would have the Eddic poems but no coherent narrative integrating them; we would have skaldic poetry but no systematic poetics. He preserved a pre-Christian religious tradition in writing in Christian Iceland — a paradox that has made him the principal channel through which post-medieval Europe has known Norse religion. Wagner's *Ring of the Nibelung*, Tolkien's mythopoetic works, and modern Heathen religion all depend on Snorri.

## Disputes
- **Euhemerism in the Prologue.** Snorri's framing the Norse gods as ancient deified humans — possibly a clerical fig leaf permitting the writing-down of paganism, possibly sincere medieval mythography. Wanner 2008 treats it as sincere; older readings as fig leaf.
- **Authorship of *Egils saga* and certain skaldic poems.** Some attributions remain contested.
- **Political agenda.** Snorri's *Heimskringla* presented Norwegian royal history with particular interpretive choices that map onto his own political stance toward the Norwegian crown — which itself contributed to his assassination.
