# ʿUmar ibn al-Farrukhān al-Ṭabarī

## Identity

A Persian astrologer from **Ṭabaristān**, active at Baghdad in the first ʿAbbāsid generation and dying around 815 CE. He belonged to the circle of first-rank astrologers who served al-Manṣūr and later the Barmakid viziers under Hārūn al-Rashīd, and he is reported among the astrologers who elected the foundation-moment of Baghdad in 762 alongside [[mashallah-ibn-athari|Māshāʾallāh]] and Nawbakht.

He has a node in this vault for one reason, and it is a decisive one.

## Why this node exists: he is the mechanism

The Codex Atlas requires that a cross-tradition edge name the **mechanism of contact** — a named translation, a named text, a named person — rather than asserting influence in the abstract. ʿUmar ibn al-Farrukhān is what that requirement looks like when it is satisfied completely.

Around **800 CE** he translated the *Pentateuch* of [[dorotheus-of-sidon|Dorotheus of Sidon]] into Arabic. He did not translate it from the Greek, which he had no access to; he translated it from a **Middle Persian (Pahlavi) version** made at the Sasanian court, probably in the 3rd century and revised in the 5th. The Greek hexameters are lost. What survives entire — the [[phase-3-040-dorotheus-carmen-astrologicum|*Carmen Astrologicum*]], the earliest substantially-surviving working manual of [[astrology-horoscope-twelve-places|horoscopic astrology]] — survives **because ʿUmar translated it, and in the form he gave it**.

The Persian stage is not inferred from resemblance. Pingree (1976) identified it *inside the text*: chart data interpolated by the Sasanian redactors, computed for Persian-era dates, sits among the original 1st-century Greek examples. The intermediary left its arithmetic behind.

So the sentence "Hellenistic astrology reached the Islamic world" has, in this case, a subject and a verb and a date. It reached it through this man, from that Pahlavi manuscript, around 800, in Baghdad. Everything downstream — [[abu-mashar-al-balkhi|Abū Maʿshar]], [[phase-5-066-bonatti-liber-astronomiae|Bonatti]], [[phase-6-054-lilly-christian-astrology|Lilly]], and the horary technique still taught today — runs through that act of translation.

## His own work

- ***Kitāb al-Mawālīd*** (*Book of Nativities*), his principal original treatise, was itself translated into Latin in the 12th century as ***De nativitatibus*** and printed repeatedly in the Renaissance — so he travels westward twice over: once as the translator of Dorotheus, once as an author in his own right under the Latin name **Omar Tiberiadis**.
- Ibn al-Nadīm's *Fihrist* credits him with work on Ptolemy's *Tetrabiblos* in Arabic, placing him at the centre of the effort to render the Greek theoretical corpus as well as the practical one.
- He worked within, and helped constitute, the wider translation enterprise at Baghdad ([[event-translation-movement-baghdad]]; [[mathematics-house-of-wisdom]]) — the same enterprise that carried Greek medicine, mathematics and philosophy into Arabic.

## Disputes

- **How much did he change?** The Arabic *Carmen* is a translation of a revision of a translation, and Pingree's edition separates the strata only as far as the evidence permits. Which departures from the Greek are ʿUmar's, which are the Pahlavi redactors', and which are later Arabic copyists' is not fully resolvable. Any reading of the *Carmen* as a transparent window onto 1st-century Greek practice is reading through three layers of transmission.
- **Which ʿUmar?** The Arabic bibliographical tradition is not always careful in distinguishing him from other astrologers of similar name, and the Latin *Omar Tiberiadis* corpus accreted material. Sezgin (1979) is the reference for sorting the attributions.
- **Dating the Pahlavi version.** Pingree argued for a 3rd-century Pahlavi translation with a 5th-century revision, on the basis of the interpolated chart dates. The general shape is accepted; the precise dates depend on his computation of those charts.
