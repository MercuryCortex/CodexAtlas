# Strabo of Amaseia

## Identity
Strabo (~64 BCE – 24 CE) is the most important ancient geographer for the vault's Iberian cluster — and one of the most important ancient writers for understanding the pre-Roman and early-Roman Mediterranean world generally. His *Geographica* (17 books, written between c. 7 BCE and 23 CE, based on a lifetime of travel and reading) is the most comprehensive geographical account of the ancient world to survive in substantial form. For the Iberian Peninsula, Strabo's **Book III** is the single most important literary source.

## Strabo on Iberia (Book III)
Strabo's Book III covers Iberia in remarkable detail, drawing on personal observation (he visited Spain under Augustus) and earlier sources (Polybius, Posidonius, Artemidorus of Ephesus — most of whom are now lost). Key passages:

**On Tartessos** (III.2.11–14): Strabo discusses the identification of Tartessos as the most ancient of Iberian civilizations, its silver wealth, its relationship to Phoenician Gades, and the traditions about king Arganthonius (III.2.11: *"they say that Arganthonius reigned there for 80 years and lived to be 120"*). He identifies the Turdetanians of his own time as the heirs of the Tartessians and praises them as the most cultured Iberian people, noting they had *"written records of their history in verse and prose and even laws in verse form"* six thousand years old (a legendary claim he seems to accept).

**On Gades and the Melqart temple** (III.5.3): *"The temple of Heracles [Melqart] is on the eastern side... It has two brazen pillars, each four fathoms high... The brazen pillars are the 'Pillars of Heracles' according to most people... but the sacred rites are more like those of the Phoenicians than the Greeks."* This is the most detailed literary description of the temple, confirming the perpetual fire, the prohibition of women and swine, the two columns, and the Phoenician rather than Greek character of the cult.

**On the Gallaecians and Lusitanians** (III.3.7–8): Strabo provides the most detailed ancient account of Lusitanian and Gallaecian customs — descriptions that are simultaneously revealing and distorted by Greco-Roman cultural assumptions:
- Gallaecians bathing in urine fermented in cisterns to clean their teeth (a Roman topos for barbarous people)
- Women doing the agricultural labor while men fight (widespread ancient topos for inverted barbarous gender norms)
- Ritual practices including human sacrifice (almost certainly exaggerated Roman propaganda from the conquest wars)
- BUT: the detailed description of the *castro* settlements, the social organization, the type of warfare (mountain guerrilla tactics), and the religious practices at sacred springs and hilltops is consistent with archaeology and represents genuine observation

**On the Gallaecians' Atlantic relationship** (III.3.5): *"Some say that this people [the Gallaecians] go on the boats to carry the dead to the islands in front of the Lusitanian coast... and other stories of this kind."* This passage is the most direct ancient literary reference to the Lusitanian/Gallaecian death-boat tradition — the belief that the dead are ferried westward by sea — and connects directly to [[celtic-otherworld]] and [[finisterre-end-of-world]].

## Strabo as Evidence and as Problem
Strabo is simultaneously our primary evidence for pre-Roman Iberia and one of our greatest methodological problems:

1. **He wrote after the conquest**: Strabo is describing a world that the Romans have already substantially destroyed and transformed. His "Lusitanians" and "Gallaecians" are post-conquest survivors; his Tartessians are a memory. The culture he describes is already a Roman-period residue.

2. **He used earlier sources, now lost**: His best descriptions come from Posidonius (~135–51 BCE), Polybius (~200–118 BCE), and Artemidorus of Ephesus (~100 BCE) — all of whom had better access to the pre-Roman world than Strabo did. When Strabo's account is accurate, it may be accurate because Posidonius was accurate; when it is wrong, the error may go back further.

3. **He applies Roman-Hellenistic interpretive frameworks**: The "barbarian customs" tropes (inverted gender roles, cannibalism, human sacrifice, promiscuity) are standard Roman ethnographic conventions applied to any people described as uncivilized. These topoi cannot be taken at face value; they require archaeological corroboration to carry any weight.

4. **He was occasionally there**: Strabo traveled in Spain and saw things with his own eyes — particularly around Gades, Tartessian territory, and the lower Guadalquivir. His first-hand observations (the temple at Gades, the tidal phenomena of the Atlantic) are more reliable than his second-hand ethnographic reports.

## The Pytheas Question
Strabo's treatment of [[pytheas]] is notorious: he dismissed Pytheas as a liar and fabulist throughout the *Geographica*, yet he preserved (by quoting them to debunk them) the fragments that allow modern reconstruction of Pytheas's voyage. Strabo's skepticism about Pytheas's Arctic and Atlantic observations is a case study in how a well-informed scholar can be systematically wrong when the subject matter exceeds his conceptual framework. Modern analysis vindicates Pytheas on virtually every point Strabo disputes.
