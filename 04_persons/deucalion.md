# Deucalion

## Identity
**Deucalion** (Greek *Δευκαλίων*) is the Greek flood-survivor — the *Hellenic parallel-form* of the Mesopotamian [[atrahasis-flood-hero]] / [[utnapishtim]] / [[ziusudra]] tradition and the Hebrew [[noah]]. In the canonical Greek myth, when **Zeus** decides to destroy the corrupt Bronze-Age humanity with a flood, **Prometheus** (Deucalion's father — the same Titan who gave humans fire) warns Deucalion to build a chest/ark with his wife **[[pyrrha]]**. They survive the nine-day flood, land on **Mount Parnassus**, and repopulate humanity by throwing stones over their shoulders — the stones becoming new men and women (a folk-etymological play on Greek *laos* "people" / *laas* "stone").

Deucalion's son **Hellen** is the eponymous ancestor of the **Hellenes** (Greeks), making the Greek peoples in their own mythological self-presentation a *post-flood* humanity descended from Deucalion-Pyrrha.

## Historical evidence

- **Pindar, *Olympian* 9.41–46** (~-466 BCE) — the earliest surviving Greek attestation, naming Deucalion as restorer of humanity from stones.
- **Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca* 1.7.2** (~1st–2nd c. CE) — the standard mythological-handbook form.
- **[[phase-4-085-ovid-metamorphoses-book-1]] (Ovid *Metamorphoses* 1.260–415)** — the principal Latin literary form (~8 CE); the Mediterranean Renaissance-and-medieval reception of the Greek flood runs through Ovid's Latin verse.
- **Lucian, *De Dea Syria* 12–13** — Hellenistic-period syncretic identification of Deucalion with the flood-hero of Hierapolis (a Phoenician-Hellenistic blending).
- **Plutarch, *De sollertia animalium* 13** — Plutarch on the Deucalion narrative with comparative observations.

Deucalion is **legendary** — no contemporary attestation; the Greek flood-tradition is mythological-canonical.

## Role in the tradition

Deucalion is the **Greek instantiation** of the Mediterranean-flood narrative pattern. The principal scholarly question (West 1997 *The East Face of Helicon*) is whether the Greek Deucalion-tradition is:

1. **An independent parallel-form** of the Mediterranean flood-archetype (Frazer 1918 *Folklore in the Old Testament* — comparative-religion approach),
2. **A direct borrowing from Mesopotamia** via Phoenician or Hittite intermediation (West 1997 — the *East Face of Helicon* thesis of substantial Near-Eastern influence on Greek myth),
3. **A localized memory of a real Mediterranean flood-event** (Black Sea Deluge hypothesis — see [[event-black-sea-deluge-hypothesis-1997]] — Ryan-Pitman 1997, contested).

West 1997 makes the strongest modern case for substantial Mesopotamian → Greek transmission via Bronze Age and Iron Age cultural contact (Hittite intermediation; Phoenician trade-networks; the Theban-cycle vs. Sumerian-King-List-cycle parallels).

The Deucalion myth has a distinctive Greek feature: **the stones-into-people repopulation motif** is absent from Mesopotamian and Hebrew versions, and reflects a *positive* post-flood-creation theme rather than the more pessimistic Mesopotamian post-flood-mortality establishment.

## Disputes

- **Historicity.** Legendary; no contemporary attestation.
- **Date of the underlying tradition.** Pindar ~-466 is the earliest surviving reference; the underlying oral tradition is presumably older — Bronze Age origin is plausible but not directly documented.
- **Relationship to the Mesopotamian flood-tradition.** West 1997 argues substantial influence; Burkert 1992 *The Orientalizing Revolution* supports; Kirk 1970 *Myth* treats as parallel-form. Most current scholarship accepts substantial Mesopotamian influence with localized Greek elaboration.
- **Multiple Greek floods?** Some Hellenistic sources (Plato *Timaeus* 22A — the Egyptian priest to Solon, "you Greeks have had many floods") distinguish *several* Greek floods, with the Deucalion flood as one among them. The Atlantis-Saïs narrative ([[phase-3-022-plato-timaeus-critias-atlantis]]) operates within this framework.
