# Vasugupta

## Identity
Vasugupta (~800–850 CE) is the traditional founder of the Kashmir Shaivism (Trika) philosophical school — the figure who first formulated the non-dual Śaiva metaphysics that Abhinavagupta (~975–1025 CE) would later synthesize into its mature systematic form. According to the tradition, the *Śiva Sūtras* were revealed to Vasugupta on a rock at Mahādeva hill near the village of Harwan in Kashmir — a founding narrative that frames the sūtras as divine revelation (*āgama*) rather than human philosophical construction. His direct student Kallata (9th c.) wrote the first commentary on the *Spanda Kārikās* and established the Spanda sub-school.

## Works
- **[[phase-5-033-shiva-sutras]]** — 77 sūtras in three sections; the founding text of Trika Shaivism. Asserts: consciousness (Śiva) is the ultimate ground; the individual self is identical with this consciousness; the obstacle is contraction (*āṇava-mala*); the path is recognition.
- **Spanda Kārikās** — 52 verses articulating the doctrine of *spanda* (divine vibration/pulsation): ultimate reality is not static being but dynamic pulsation. The world is Śiva's own vibration, not separate from him. This doctrine directly anticipates later Trika metaphysics.

## Significance
Vasugupta is the starting point of the Kashmir Shaivism intellectual lineage:
**Vasugupta** → Kallata (Spanda commentator) → Bhaskara (Śiva Sūtra commentator) → Utpaladeva (*Pratyabhijñā Sūtras*, ~925 CE) → Abhinavagupta (*Tantrāloka*, *Mālinīvijayavārttika*, ~975–1025 CE)

This is one of the most traceable continuous philosophical lineages in the vault — ~200 years from founding revelation to systematic synthesis.

## Cross-tradition position
The *Śiva Sūtras* (~825 CE) and Vasugupta's formulation are contemporaneous with:
- **John Scotus Eriugena** (~810–877 CE, Carolingian Empire): translating Pseudo-Dionysius, articulating the same apophatic move (God unknowable even to himself) from a completely independent position. See [[john-scotus-eriugena]].
- **Al-Kindi** (~801–873 CE): first Islamic philosopher systematically engaging Greek philosophy, at the Baghdad translation movement. Three intellectuals at the outer edges of the known world (~Ireland, Kashmir, Baghdad) articulating related questions about the nature of the absolute within the same generation.

The structural parallel to Eriugena is the most striking: both posit an absolute that is self-luminous but self-concealing (divine darkness = divine contraction into individual minds), both reject the knowability of the ultimate through discursive reason, both affirm that the path of return passes through the ground of consciousness/God rather than through external mediation.
