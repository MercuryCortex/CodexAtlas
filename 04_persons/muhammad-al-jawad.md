# Muhammad al-Jawad

## Identity
Son of [[ali-al-rida]]; born in Medina ~811. **Succeeded his father as Ninth Imam at age 7 or 8** (818) — the first of three child-Imams in the late Twelver line. The Abbasid Caliph al-Ma'mun, after al-Rida's death, brought the young al-Jawad to his court, married him to his own daughter Umm al-Fadl, and treated him (per the historiographical sources) with public respect — likely both as political accommodation of the Imami partisans his policy had cultivated and as ongoing surveillance of the Imami leadership. Al-Jawad lived primarily in Medina, with periods in Baghdad. He died in Baghdad in 835 (under Caliph al-Mu'tasim), aged ~25 — by Shi'i tradition poisoned by his Abbasid-affiliated wife Umm al-Fadl. Buried with his grandfather [[musa-al-kazim]] at Kazimayn (Baghdad), which becomes a major Iraqi Shi'i pilgrimage site.

## Historical evidence
Documented in Shi'i hadith collections and (more briefly) in Sunni biographical literature. The marriage to Umm al-Fadl is uncontested; the precise date and circumstances of death less so.

## Role in the tradition

### The child-Imam problem
Al-Jawad's accession at age 7–8 posed a theological challenge that mature Twelver doctrine had to address: *can a child be Imam with full ʿisma (infallibility) and full ʿilm (divine knowledge)?* The doctrinal solution — that the Imam's *ʿilm* is divinely-bestowed rather than humanly-acquired, and therefore does not depend on adult learning — is articulated firmly in this period and applied retrospectively (and prospectively, since [[ali-al-hadi]] and [[hasan-al-askari]] would also succeed as relatively young figures, and [[muhammad-al-mahdi-person]] famously as a child of ~5).

Modarressi 1993 reads this period as a *crisis* for the Imami community: the rapid turnover of Imams (al-Rida 818, al-Jawad 835, al-Hadi 868, al-Askari 874), the Abbasid surveillance of the Imams' households, and the increasing dispersion of the Imami partisans across the empire (Kufa, Qom, Khurasan, Baghdad) put pressure on the community's institutional cohesion. The *wukala'* deputy network became increasingly central as the substantive institutional structure of Imami religion, with the Imam himself increasingly inaccessible. This trajectory culminates in the Minor Occultation under al-Askari's son.

## Disputes
- **Cause of death.** Standard Shi'i tradition (poisoned by his wife Umm al-Fadl on Abbasid instigation). Historical evidence is suggestive but not conclusive; the *imams-died-as-martyrs* topos applies.

- **The child-Imam doctrine.** When the doctrinal solution to the child-Imam problem was articulated — concurrent with al-Jawad, or in retrospective formalization — is debated.

## Cross-tradition connections
- ↔ [[ali-al-rida]] (father), [[ali-al-hadi]] (son, Tenth Imam) — the Twelver line continued.
- ↔ Abbasid court — uniquely close (marriage alliance) and uniquely strained.
- ↔ [[tradition-shia-islam]] — the child-Imam theology is consequential.
- ↔ The *wukala'* deputy network — increasingly central in this period, foreshadowing the post-occultation institutional pattern.
