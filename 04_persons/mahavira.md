# Mahāvīra

## Identity

**Vardhamāna**, called **Mahāvīra** ("Great Hero"), is the twenty-fourth and last **Tīrthaṅkara** — "ford-maker," one who builds a crossing over the flood of rebirth — of the present half-cycle of Jain cosmic time, and the figure through whom Jainism enters documented history. He is also called **Jina**, "the conqueror," the word from which *Jain* derives.

Two framings of him are both true and must not be collapsed. **In Jain terms** he is not a founder at all: he is the last of a series stretching back through [[parshvanatha]] and [[neminatha]] to [[rishabha]] at the beginning of the age, each teaching the same eternal path. **In historical terms** he is the point at which that tradition becomes visible in evidence — and, on the dominant scholarly reading, a reformer who tightened and re-systematized an order that already existed.

He is also, in one specific respect, better attested than almost any founder-figure of his era: the scriptures of a **rival religion** name him, describe his followers, argue with his doctrines, and record his death. The Buddhist Pali canon calls him **Nigaṇṭha Nātaputta** — "the unbound one, son of the Jñātṛ clan."

## Historical evidence

**1. Buddhist cross-attestation — the strongest single item.** Nigaṇṭha Nātaputta appears in the Pali canon as one of the six named non-Buddhist teachers of the day (*Sāmaññaphala Sutta*, DN 2). The *Upāli Sutta* (MN 56) stages a debate between his followers and the Buddha; the *Cūḷadukkhakkhandha Sutta* (MN 14) describes Nigaṇṭha standing austerities in circumstantial detail; and the *Pāsādika* and *Saṅgīti Suttas* (DN 29, DN 33) report **his death at Pāvā and the immediate schism among his followers**. See [[phase-2-016-early-buddhist-suttas]].

An outside tradition with every motive to ignore him instead names him, dates his death by its own reckoning, and describes the disarray that followed. That is unusually good evidence.

**The caveat matters just as much.** The Buddhist portrayal is **polemical**, and it misrepresents Jain doctrine at points — MN 14's claim that the Nigaṇṭhas hold all present feeling to be caused by past action, exhaustible by austerity alone, is a caricature Jains reject. The Pali canon is strong evidence that Mahāvīra **existed** and weak, hostile evidence for what he **taught**.

**2. The Jain canon.** The [[phase-2-038-acharanga-sutra]] contains, in its *Upadhāna* section, the oldest surviving account of his austerities — a startlingly concrete passage in which he meditates motionless while insects crawl over him and birds settle in his hair, refusing to brush any living thing away. The [[phase-2-039-sutrakritanga]] preserves early doctrinal polemic against rival schools. The *Kalpa Sūtra* gives the full biography and the *Bhagavatī Sūtra* the doctrinal dialogues. All were fixed in writing centuries after the events, in [[language-ardhamagadhi-prakrit]].

**3. Epigraphy and archaeology.** [[asoka-maurya]]'s **Seventh Pillar Edict** (3rd c. BCE) instructs his dhamma-officers to attend to the **Nigaṇṭhas** alongside other communities — imperial confirmation of an established, recognised Jain order within roughly two centuries of Mahāvīra ([[phase-3-030-asokan-edicts]], [[event-asokan-dhamma-missions-c-250-bce]]). The **Hāthīgumphā inscription** of King Khāravela of Kaliṅga (Udayagiri, Odisha, c. 1st c. BCE) records royal Jain patronage and the recovery of a Jina image previously carried off by a Nanda king — pushing Jain image-cult back further still. The **Mathurā** finds at Kaṅkālī Ṭīlā (Kuṣāṇa period) give the earliest substantial Jain art, including *āyāgapaṭas* and Tīrthaṅkara images, with donor inscriptions naming monastic lineages that **match the lineage lists in the canon** — an independent check on the canon's own transmission claims.

## Dating — what tradition holds, what scholarship holds

This is genuinely contested, and the vault carries the range rather than picking silently.

- **Śvetāmbara tradition: -599 to -527.** This is not merely a scholarly opinion within Jainism — the **Vīra Nirvāṇa Saṃvat**, the era still used in the Jain calendar, counts from 527 BCE. The date is load-bearing for a living community's reckoning of time.
- **Digambara tradition: -582 to -510.** The two branches do not agree with each other either.
- **Older Western reckoning:** Jacobi, working from the Buddhist synchronism, arrived at c. -477 for the nirvāṇa.
- **Current academic: c. -497 to -425**, or a death anywhere in roughly **-425 to -400**. This follows mechanically from the redating of the Buddha's death from c. -483 to c. -400 (the "short chronology," consolidated in the Bechert symposia, 1991–97). Because both traditions agree Mahāvīra was the Buddha's slightly older contemporary who **died first**, moving one moves the other.

**What survives every chronology** — and is therefore the fixed point to reason from — is the *relation*: Mahāvīra and the Buddha were contemporaries in the same region; Mahāvīra was the elder; Mahāvīra died first; each tradition knew the other and argued with it.

⚠️ **A vault-internal inconsistency to be aware of.** This node's YAML carries the Śvetāmbara traditional dates, while [[event-buddha-parinirvana-c-400-bce]] carries the short chronology for the Buddha. Read literally across the graph, that has Mahāvīra dying more than a century before a man both traditions call his contemporary. The prose here states the range; harmonizing the YAML across the Buddhist and Jain nodes is a graph-wide decision, not one this node should make alone.

## Life — as the tradition tells it, marked as tradition

Born at **Kuṇḍagrāma** (Kṣatriyakuṇḍa), near **Vaiśālī** in Videha, modern Bihar. Father **Siddhārtha**, a *kṣatriya* of the **Jñātṛ** (Nāya) clan; mother **Triśalā**, sister of the Licchavi chief Ceṭaka. He is born into the republican-oligarchic confederacies of the eastern Gangetic plain — the same social world, the same generation and the same warrior-aristocrat stratum as [[siddhartha-gautama-buddha]].

**The two branches tell two different lives, and the divergences are doctrinally motivated:**

- **The embryo transfer.** The Śvetāmbara *Kalpa Sūtra* has him conceived in a brahmin woman, Devānandā, with the embryo transferred to the kṣatriya Triśalā by the god Hariṇegameṣin — because a Tīrthaṅkara cannot be born into a brahmin household. **Digambaras reject the episode entirely.**
- **Marriage.** Śvetāmbara: he married Yaśodā and had a daughter, Priyadarśanā. **Digambara: celibate from birth, never married.** The stricter branch requires a stricter life.

These are not variant details; they are two incompatible biographies, each shaped by what its branch needs a Tīrthaṅkara to have been.

**Renunciation and austerity.** He renounced at about thirty, after his parents' deaths, performing *keśa-loca* — plucking out his hair by hand rather than having it shaved, still the Jain monastic practice. Within months he discarded his single remaining garment and went naked. Then **twelve years and some months** of wandering austerity: extreme fasting, motionless standing meditation, silence, the refusal to defend himself against assault or to remove any living creature from his body.

**Kevalajñāna.** At forty-two, under a *sāla* tree on the bank of the **Ṛjupālikā** river outside Jṛmbhikagrāma, he attained *kevala-jñāna* — omniscience, the soul's innate knowing once the obscuring karma is burned away. Not an illumination received; an obstruction removed.

**Teaching.** Roughly thirty years. The tradition pictures his preaching in the ***samavasaraṇa***, a tiered circular assembly in which gods, humans and animals all hear him simultaneously, each in their own language. His eleven **gaṇadharas** (chief disciples) were brahmin converts led by **Indrabhūti Gautama**; only Indrabhūti and Sudharman outlived him, and the canon is transmitted through Sudharman to Jambū.

**Death.** At **Pāvā** (Pāvāpurī, Bihar), aged seventy-two, by ritual fasting — his own death the paradigm of [[ritual-sallekhana]]. Jain tradition identifies the night of his liberation with **Dīvālī**: lamps were lit because the light of the world's knowledge had gone out ([[ritual-diwali-festival-light]]).

## The teaching

**An infinite plurality of real souls.** Jainism holds that there are innumerable *jīvas* — individual, eternal, genuinely distinct souls, each intrinsically omniscient and blissful and each currently obscured. Not the one Self of Vedānta; not the no-self of Buddhism. This is the position's distinctive metaphysical location, and it is the reason Jain and Buddhist arguments about rebirth are real disagreements rather than terminological ones. See [[reincarnation]] §2 for the four Indic settlements set side by side, and [[anatman-no-self]] for the Buddhist alternative.

**Karma is matter.** The defining Jain claim, and one of the most striking in world religion: *karma* is not a metaphor for moral bookkeeping but **subtle physical substance** (*karma-pudgala*) that flows into the soul, adheres to it, weighs it down, tints it (*leśyā*, the soul's karmic colour), and determines the form of its next embodiment. The mechanism has a full technical vocabulary — *āsrava* (influx), *bandha* (bondage), *saṃvara* (stoppage of influx), *nirjarā* (shedding, by austerity), *mokṣa* (liberation) — and a fourteen-rung ladder of spiritual states. Liberation is therefore literally a **purification**: burn off what has accumulated and stop taking on more. See [[jain-karma]], [[doctrine-karma-action-fruit]], [[moral-karma-as-moral-law]].

**Ahiṃsā, and why it is stricter here than anywhere else.** Because karma is matter and adheres through the act, **harm binds regardless of intention**. This single premise generates the whole visible texture of Jain life: the mouth-cloth, the soft broom to sweep the path, filtered water, no eating after dark, abstention from root vegetables (uprooting kills the plant and the organisms in the soil), and the historic avoidance of agriculture as a lay occupation — which is a substantial part of why Jain communities concentrated in trade, finance and later law. See [[moral-ahimsa-nonviolence]].

**The five great vows.** *Ahiṃsā* (non-harm), *satya* (truth), *asteya* (non-stealing), *brahmacarya* (celibacy), *aparigraha* (non-possession) — the *mahāvratas* for mendicants, with lay analogues ([[moral-jain-five-mahavratas]]). That there are **five** rather than four is itself a major piece of historical evidence; see MASSIVE WIN 1.

**Many-sidedness — with a dating caveat.** *Anekāntavāda* (no single view captures reality), *syādvāda* (conditional predication, formalized as the sevenfold *saptabhaṅgī*: "in some respect it is," "in some respect it is not," and so on), and *nayavāda* (analysis by standpoint) are Jainism's distinctive epistemology, illustrated by the blind men and the elephant. **At tier:** the seeds are canonical, but the finished doctrinal apparatus is **post-canonical systematization** — Umāsvāti, Siddhasena Divākara, Samantabhadra and Haribhadra, roughly the 2nd–8th centuries CE. Attributing the developed *syādvāda* to Mahāvīra himself is anachronism. See [[anekantavada]], [[philosophy-jain-darshana]].

**No creator, and no help.** The universe is eternal and uncreated; it operates by its own laws; the liberated *siddhas* at the summit do not intervene, and cannot be petitioned to. Jain "gods" are simply beings in more fortunate realms, still inside the cycle and still needing to get out. There is no grace anywhere in the system. See MASSIVE WIN 4.

## After him

**The great schism.** Tradition traces it to a twelve-year famine and a migration south under Bhadrabāhu, with disputes over clothing and canon on the northerners' return. Whatever the trigger, the split hardens across the 1st–5th centuries CE into **Digambara** ("sky-clad" — monks naked; the original canon held to be lost; women cannot attain liberation in a female body) and **Śvetāmbara** ("white-clad" — monks robed; canon preserved; women can be liberated). The **Vallabhī council** under Devarddhigaṇi, c. 453 or 466 CE, fixed the Śvetāmbara canon in writing; Digambaras reject it and work from the *Ṣaṭkhaṇḍāgama* and *Kaṣāyaprābhṛta*. Jaini 1991 documents the female-liberation dispute from both sides' own texts. See [[attire-jain-monastic-svetambara-digambara]], [[attire-ritual-nudity-cross-tradition]].

**Language as a claim.** The canon is in **Ardhamāgadhī Prākrit** — the vernacular, not Sanskrit. Like the Buddha's use of the local speech, this is a deliberate refusal of brahmanical linguistic monopoly, and it is part of the same argument as the rejection of Vedic sacrifice ([[language-ardhamagadhi-prakrit]]).

**Living practice.** [[ritual-paryushana]] and [[paryushan-jain-fast]] (the annual period of fasting, confession and forgiveness), [[ritual-pratikraman]] (ritual repentance), [[ritual-diwali-festival-light]], [[mendicancy-alms-begging]], [[ritual-fasting-cross-tradition]], [[monastic-asceticism]]. Symbols: the [[swastika]] (the four states of existence) and the [[lotus]].

**Continuity.** The order Mahāvīra established has practised without interruption for roughly two and a half millennia — with Buddhism, the oldest surviving *śramaṇa* tradition, and unlike Buddhism it never left India.

## Disputes

- **Dating.** See above. Traditional Śvetāmbara -599/-527, traditional Digambara -582/-510, current academic c. -497/-425.
- **Founder or reformer?** The dominant scholarly view, from Jacobi onward, is that [[parshvanatha]] — the 23rd Tīrthaṅkara, traditionally -877 to -777 — has a **probable historical kernel**, and that Mahāvīra therefore reformed and re-systematized an existing order rather than founding one. The earlier twenty-two Tīrthaṅkaras, including [[rishabha]] and [[neminatha]], are cosmological rather than historical figures. The evidence for Pārśva is better than such claims usually get — see MASSIVE WIN 1.
- **Whose biography?** The Śvetāmbara and Digambara lives differ on conception, marriage and more, and each is shaped by its branch's doctrinal commitments. There is no neutral Jain account.
- **Anekāntavāda's date.** Canonical seeds, post-canonical system. Do not backdate the *saptabhaṅgī* to Mahāvīra.
- **Makkhali Gosāla.** The *Bhagavatī Sūtra* reports that Gosāla, later leader of the **Ājīvikas**, was Mahāvīra's companion for six years before a bitter break, and gives a hostile account of his death. But Jain testimony about a defeated rival is no more neutral than Buddhist testimony about Mahāvīra: the Ājīvikas are known almost entirely through the hostile reports of the two traditions that outlived them (Basham 1951). See [[philosophy-ajivika-determinism]].
- **Two nodes, one figure.** This vault carries Mahāvīra twice — here as a `person`, and at [[mahavira-jain]] as a `deity`, reflecting his cultic status as an object of veneration. The two are the same historical figure viewed through two lenses; keep them cross-linked rather than duplicated.

## Cross-tradition position

### MASSIVE WIN 1 — four vows or five: two rival scriptures corroborating a pre-Mahāvīra order

This is the vault's best example of what independent cross-attestation actually looks like, and it is worth stating precisely.

**From inside the tradition.** The Jain canon's own *Uttarādhyayana* 23 stages a meeting between **Keśi**, a follower of Pārśva's tradition, and **Indrabhūti Gautama**, Mahāvīra's chief disciple. The two negotiate a difference in the rule: Pārśva's community observed a **fourfold** restraint (*cāujjāma*); Mahāvīra's observes **five** vows, having made celibacy explicit as a separate vow rather than leaving it inside non-possession. The text exists to *reconcile* the two communities — which means the Jain tradition itself preserves the memory of an older order with a different rule, and of its absorption.

**From outside the tradition.** The Pali canon independently refers to the Nigaṇṭhas' **fourfold restraint** (*cātuyāma-saṃvara*) — apparently preserving the **older, pre-Mahāvīra formula**, from a source with no interest in Jain internal history and no reason to invent it.

Two scriptures, two rival religions, two languages, converging on the same detail: a Jain-type ascetic order existed **before** Mahāvīra, with a four-vow rule, and Mahāvīra's five-vow formulation is a **reform of it**. Very little about sixth-to-fifth-century BCE Indian religion is this well evidenced. It is also the concrete reason the "founder vs. reformer" question is not merely a matter of taste.

### MASSIVE WIN 2 — Mahāvīra and the Buddha: the closest thing religious history has to a controlled experiment

Same generation, same region, same *kṣatriya* class, same rejected authority (Vedic sacrifice and brahmin priestly monopoly), same problem (rebirth, and how to get out), same competition for the same lay donors and royal patrons — and each tradition's scriptures name the other. Almost every variable is held constant, which makes the divergences genuinely informative rather than picturesque. Bronkhorst 2007 supplies the frame: this eastern *śramaṇa* world was a **distinct culture**, not a heterodox offshoot of the Vedic west, which is why the two look like siblings rather than dissidents.

| Question | Mahāvīra / Jainism | The Buddha / Buddhism |
|---|---|---|
| What is reborn | a permanent, real, individual *jīva* | nothing that persists; *anātman* |
| What karma is | subtle **matter** adhering to the soul | intention (*cetanā*) — functional, not physical |
| How liberation works | burn off accumulated karmic matter by austerity | extinguish craving and ignorance |
| Does intent matter | harm binds **regardless** of intent | intent is decisive; unintended harm does not bind |
| Asceticism | extreme; the paradigm death is a ritual fast | the Middle Way, defined **against** extreme austerity |
| Omniscience | the *kevalin* knows all things simultaneously | knowledge is soteriologically bounded; the unanswerable questions are set aside |
| Creator god | rejected — the universe is eternal and uncreated | rejected — the universe is causally conditioned |
| Vernacular scripture | Ardhamāgadhī Prākrit | Pali / local vernaculars |

**The sharpest point:** the Pali account of the Bodhisatta's years of austerity — near-starvation, breath-suppression — describes practices recognisably of this type, and the "Middle Way" is defined as a middle between courtly indulgence and **this specific rival's regime**. Buddhism's foundational methodological claim is formulated against Jain-type asceticism. That is a genuine Transmission-adjacent relation (formation-by-opposition), not merely a parallel, and it is documented in the Buddhists' own canon.

The shared negatives are as striking as the disagreements: both reject a creator, both reject Vedic sacrificial efficacy, both reject brahmin birth-authority, both preach in the vernacular, both build mendicant orders with lay support. See [[siddhartha-gautama-buddha]], [[tradition-jainism]], [[tradition-buddhism]], [[phase-2-016-early-buddhist-suttas]], [[theme-axial-age]], [[event-axial-age-800-200bce]].

### MASSIVE WIN 3 — the political afterlife of ahiṃsā

The most historically consequential transmission out of Jainism, and a documented chain rather than a resemblance: Jain merchant culture in Gujarat → **[[mahatma-gandhi]]**, who names the Jain layman **Shrimad Rajchandra** (Raychandbhai Mehta) in his own autobiography as the contemporary who most shaped him → *satyagraha* → **Martin Luther King Jr.** and the American civil rights movement → the global repertoire of non-violent civil resistance.

The load-bearing claim is not that Gandhi was a Jain — he was not — but that the **absolute** form of ahiṃsā, in which non-harm is not one virtue among others but the criterion of everything else, is Jain, and that Gandhi encountered it as a lived neighbourhood ethic before he read it into anything else. [[moral-ahimsa-nonviolence]] carries the full chain; this node wires to it rather than duplicating it.

### MASSIVE WIN 4 — a complete soteriology with no god in it

Jainism has a full path to liberation with **no creator, no grace, no divine agency, and no petition that could help** — and it has run continuously for about two and a half millennia with a living monastic order and a lay community. That is a rare and useful data point: it separates "religion" from "theism" **empirically**, by exhibiting a case, rather than by definition.

It is not unique, and the comparison set is the interesting part: Theravāda Buddhism ([[tradition-theravada-buddhism]], [[doctrine-nirvana-buddhist-cessation]]) reaches a comparable position by a different route; classical Sāṃkhya is atheistic in its own idiom; and the Ājīvikas were determinist and godless in a third way ([[philosophy-ajivika-determinism]]). All four emerged in the same Indian ferment. Meanwhile Jainism is intensely *devotional* in practice — image worship, pilgrimage, temple ritual, veneration of the Tīrthaṅkaras who by definition cannot respond ([[mahavira-jain]], [[parshvanatha]], [[rishabha-jain]]). **Devotion without a recipient who acts** is the finding here, and it is one that theories of religion built on Abrahamic assumptions consistently mishandle.

### Rebirth, and where this node stops

Mahāvīra's soteriology presupposes the rebirth cycle and the "ethicization" that made it something to escape (Obeyesekere 2002). The comparative treatment — the four Indic settlements, the Greek material, the contested Greek–Indian question, the independent West African and Arctic cases — is carried in full at [[reincarnation]] and should not be duplicated here. The Jain contribution to that map is the **materialist** account of the binding mechanism: alone among the four, Jainism makes what binds the soul a physical substance, which is why its exit route is physical austerity rather than insight or grace. See also [[doctrine-reincarnation-samsara]], [[doctrine-moksha-hindu-liberation]].
