# Apollonius of Tyana

## Identity
Apollonius of Tyana (~+15 to ~+100 CE) was a Cappadocian Greek **Neo-Pythagorean** philosopher, ascetic, and reputed miracle-worker active across the Mediterranean during the 1st century. He practiced Pythagorean vegetarian asceticism, traveled extensively (per Philostratus's biography, as far as India to consult the Brahmins), advised emperors, and was reputed in his own lifetime as a divine man (*theios anēr*) capable of miracles, prophecy, and bilocation.

## Historical evidence
**Apollonius existed**: Cassius Dio (*Roman History* 67.18) independently mentions a Domitianic-era prophetic incident involving him. The *Letters* (the genuinely Apollonian portion of the surviving collection) and references in Lucian and others confirm a real figure. **The elaborated *Life of Apollonius* by Philostratus** (~+220, commissioned by the Syrian-Roman empress Julia Domna) is a hagiographical romance written nearly 130 years after Apollonius's death; how much represents genuine tradition and how much is Philostratan invention is contested. Dzielska (1986) carefully distinguishes a real Pythagorean philosopher with some prophetic reputation from the elaborated thaumaturgic-divine-man portrayal.

For this vault, Apollonius matters as: (a) a **direct chronological-and-geographical parallel to Jesus** — both first-century itinerant teachers with miracle-traditions, both prosecuted by Roman authorities, both apotheosized by their followers — making the *theios anēr* / divine-man category itself a useful comparative type; (b) the focus of explicit **anti-Christian polemic** in the **Diocletianic persecution** era: the Roman governor Hierocles (~+303) wrote *Lover of Truth (Philalēthēs)* contrasting Apollonius favorably with Christ, prompting Eusebius's *Against Hierocles* response; (c) a continuing presence in *Renaissance and Theosophical* literature as the "pagan Christ"; (d) a Neo-Pythagorean continuity-figure transmitting Pythagorean theurgy and asceticism into late antiquity, with influence on the later Iamblichus and theurgic Neoplatonism.
