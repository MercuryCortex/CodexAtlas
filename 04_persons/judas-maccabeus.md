# Judas Maccabeus

## Identity
Judas Maccabeus (Judah the Maccabee, "the Hammer," d. 160 BCE) was the **military leader of the Maccabean Revolt** and the central hero of the Hasmonean rising against the Seleucid king [[antiochus-iv-epiphanes]]. The third son of the priest Mattathias of the Hasmonean family, he took command of the Jewish guerrilla forces after his father's death (~166 BCE), won a string of victories against numerically superior Seleucid armies, and in 164 BCE recaptured Jerusalem and **rededicated the Temple** that Antiochus had defiled — the event commemorated annually by the festival of Hanukkah.

## Historical evidence
Documented chiefly in the two Septuagint deuterocanonical histories — [[document-1-maccabees|1 Maccabees]] (a sober dynastic chronicle, originally Hebrew, ~100 BCE) and [[document-2-maccabees|2 Maccabees]] (a Greek epitome with a more theological, martyrological framing). The two accounts diverge in detail and emphasis but corroborate the core sequence of revolt, Temple rededication, and Judas's death in battle at Elasa.

## Context
The revolt was triggered by [[antiochus-iv-epiphanes|Antiochus IV]]'s ban on Jewish practice (circumcision, Sabbath, dietary law), the desecration of the Temple with a pagan altar (the "abomination of desolation"), and the imposition of Hellenistic cult. Judas's victories preserved Jewish religious autonomy and established the Hasmonean dynasty, which would rule a semi-independent Judea until the Roman conquest of 63 BCE. The Maccabean episode shaped Second Temple Judaism's self-understanding and its theology of righteous resistance and martyrdom.

## Cross-tradition findings
- **Maccabean martyrdom → Christian martyr theology.** The martyrdom narratives of [[document-2-maccabees|2 Maccabees]] (the mother and seven sons; Eleazar the scribe) and the related 4 Maccabees provided an influential template of voluntary death-for-the-Law that early Christianity drew on for its own martyr-cult and theology of redemptive suffering (Goldstein 1976; van Henten). This is a documented intra-Jewish-to-Christian transmission of a martyrological pattern; it is carried by the Maccabean books themselves rather than by a deity edge.
