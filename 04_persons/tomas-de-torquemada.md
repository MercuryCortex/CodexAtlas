# Tomás de Torquemada (1420–1498)

## Identity
Tomás de Torquemada was a Spanish Dominican friar who served as the **first Grand Inquisitor of the Spanish Inquisition** (appointed 1483 by Pope Sixtus IV at the request of Ferdinand and Isabella) and as personal confessor to Queen Isabella I of Castile. His name became the European symbol of fanatical religious persecution.

## Life and office
Born in Valladolid (~1420); nephew of the theologian Juan de Torquemada (cardinal and defender of papal supremacy). Entered the Dominican order; became prior of the Monastery of Santa Cruz, Segovia. Appointed confessor to Isabella (then princess) before 1474. In 1483, appointed Inquisitor General with jurisdiction over Castile, Aragon, Valencia, and Catalonia — unified under a single national inquisitorial structure, which was the institutional innovation: previous inquisitions had been regional and episcopal.

Key acts during his tenure (1483–1498):
- Issued the **Alhambra Decree** (March 31, 1492) — the edict expelling all Jews from Spain who refused baptism; an estimated 40,000–100,000 people expelled (scholarly estimates vary widely). The decree was issued the same year as Columbus's first voyage.
- Established standardized inquisitorial **procedures** (*instrucciones*) — interrogation protocols, use of torture, auto-da-fé structure, appeals processes. He codified the institution he led.
- Oversaw approximately **2,000 executions** by burning during his tenure (Kamen's revised count is lower than earlier estimates; Lea's older figures are higher; the scholarly consensus falls in the 2,000 range for Torquemada specifically).
- Targeted primarily **conversos** — Jewish and Muslim converts to Christianity suspected of secretly maintaining their former religious practices (*Judaizers*, *moriscos*). The Inquisition had no formal jurisdiction over Jews or Muslims who had not been baptized.

## The "Black Legend" problem
Torquemada's reputation has been shaped by the "Black Legend" (*Leyenda Negra*) — the tradition of exaggerating Spanish Catholic cruelty, often promoted by Protestant England and the Netherlands for political purposes. Henry Kamen (1997) is the most important revisionist: the Spanish Inquisition tried ~150,000 people over its 356-year existence (1478–1834); about 3,000–5,000 executions total (not the hundreds of thousands in some popular accounts). Torquemada's personal tenure accounts for roughly 2,000 of these. The institution was less uniformly brutal than its legend, but Torquemada specifically was among its most aggressive Inquisitors General.

## Converso context
Torquemada himself may have had **converso ancestry** — his uncle Juan de Torquemada is often cited as having Jewish grandparents. If accurate, this is a profound irony: the Grand Inquisitor persecuting conversos for insufficient Christian commitment may himself have descended from Jews. This biographical detail remains debated (Netanyahu 1995 disputes it).

## Institutional legacy
The Spanish Inquisition outlasted Torquemada by 336 years (abolished 1834). His 1484/1485/1488 *instrucciones* remained the procedural foundation. He resigned as Inquisitor General in 1494 due to ill health; died at Ávila, 1498.
