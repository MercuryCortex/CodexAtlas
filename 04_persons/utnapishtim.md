# Utnapishtim

## Identity
**Utnapishtim** (Akkadian *Ūta-napišti(m)*, "he found life" / "he found long days") is the **Standard Babylonian recension** of the Mesopotamian flood-hero — the same legendary figure as [[ziusudra]] (Sumerian) and [[atrahasis-flood-hero]] (Akkadian Old Babylonian), but appearing under his Standard Babylonian name in **Tablet XI of the Standard Babylonian Gilgamesh Epic** (~-1300 BCE, Sin-leqi-unninni recension).

Utnapishtim is the **immortal sage** of the Mesopotamian tradition: after surviving the flood and sacrificing to the gods, he is granted immortality by Enlil and dwells thereafter at "the mouth of the rivers" (Sumerian *Dilmun*, often identified with Bahrain), beyond mortal reach. His role in the Gilgamesh Epic is as the *destination* of Gilgamesh's quest for immortality: Gilgamesh travels to Utnapishtim seeking the secret of eternal life, and Utnapishtim narrates the flood-story to him as part of the answer — explaining that immortality was a *one-time* divine grant tied to the flood-event and is not available to ordinary mortals.

This narrative structure makes Tablet XI of Gilgamesh the **most theologically-developed Mesopotamian flood-presentation** — embedded within a larger meditation on mortality, friendship (Enkidu), kingship, and the limits of human aspiration.

## Historical evidence

- **Gilgamesh Tablet XI** — from Ashurbanipal's Library at Nineveh (Layard / Rassam excavations 1849–1854); the tablet [[george-smith-cuneiformist]] read in 1872 ([[event-george-smith-flood-tablet-1872]]).
- **The Sumerian Death of Bilgames / Gilgamesh-and-Huwawa tradition** does not include the flood-narrative; the Atrahasis-Utnapishtim flood material was added to the Gilgamesh epic during the Standard Babylonian recension (George 2003).

## Role in the tradition

Utnapishtim is the **Standard Babylonian terminus** of the Mesopotamian flood-hero name-chain. The three Mesopotamian recensions:

1. [[ziusudra]] (Sumerian)
2. [[atrahasis-flood-hero]] (Old Babylonian Akkadian)
3. **Utnapishtim** (Standard Babylonian — within Gilgamesh XI)

…all describe the same legendary figure. The Standard Babylonian recension's *embedding* of the flood-narrative within the Gilgamesh-quest-for-immortality frame is what makes it theologically distinctive: the flood is not just a cosmic event but the *origin of mortality* as the normal human condition.

The 1872 discovery of Tablet XI ([[event-george-smith-flood-tablet-1872]]) is the single most consequential moment in the modern critical reception of the Mesopotamian-Hebrew flood-relationship — for the first time the Mesopotamian narrative was demonstrably documented to *pre-date* the Hebrew Genesis 6–9 by at least a millennium.

## Disputes

- **Identity with Ziusudra and Atrahasis.** Scholarly consensus: same legendary figure across three Mesopotamian recensions (George 2003).
- **Date of the Standard Babylonian Gilgamesh.** The Sin-leqi-unninni redaction is conventionally dated ~-1300 to -1100; Tablet XI was added during this redaction, drawing on the Old Babylonian Atrahasis material (George 2003).
- **Etymology of the name.** *Ūta-napišti* is generally parsed "he found life" or "he found long days" — semantically equivalent to the Sumerian *Zi-ud-sura* ("life of long days"). The name-translation across language preserves the meaning.
