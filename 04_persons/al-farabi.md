# al-Farabi (Abū Naṣr Muḥammad ibn Muḥammad al-Fārābī)

## Identity
**Abū Naṣr Muḥammad ibn Muḥammad al-Fārābī (~872–950 CE)** — known in Latin as **Alfarabius** or **Avennasar** — is the most consequential philosopher of the Arabic 10th century and the central architect of the Aristotelian-Platonic-Islamic synthesis that defines the falsafa tradition. Honored with the title **al-muʿallim al-thānī** ("the Second Teacher" — second after Aristotle himself) by the subsequent Arabic philosophical tradition. Born in Farab (modern Otrar, Kazakhstan), trained in logic at Baghdad under the Christian-Aramaic philosopher Yūḥannā ibn Ḥaylān and the Syriac-Christian logician Mattā ibn Yūnus (both transmitters of the late-antique Alexandrian Aristotelian curriculum); long residence in Baghdad; later years under the Hamdanid prince Sayf al-Dawla at Aleppo; died in Damascus. His authentic corpus is substantial (~100 attributed treatises, many extant), covering **logic, metaphysics, epistemology, political philosophy, music theory, and the philosophy of language**. He is the principal Arabic philosopher to make **Platonic political philosophy** central — his *Excellent City* (*al-Madīna al-Fāḍila*) is a deliberate Islamic reframing of Plato's *Republic* with the imam-philosopher-king at the top of the politico-religious hierarchy. (Mahdi 2001; Walzer 1985; Gutas 1988.)

## Historical evidence
Documented. Biographical entries in **Ibn al-Nadim *Fihrist*** (~988), Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿa, Ibn Khallikān, and the Latin-Christian bio-bibliographical sources. His extant manuscripts are extensively catalogued; his intellectual lineage (the Baghdad School of Aristotelians) is reconstructible from his works' references and the broader 10th-c. Baghdad-philosophical milieu. Birth-date is approximate; some sources give 870 or 875.

## Works
- **Political philosophy.** *Mabādiʾ ārāʾ ahl al-madīna al-fāḍila* (*The Principles of the Opinions of the Inhabitants of the Excellent City*) — al-Farabi's principal Platonist-Islamic political-philosophical treatise; **the foundational text of Islamic political philosophy**, deliberately reframing Plato's *Republic* with the imam-philosopher-prophet at the top, the Active Intellect (*al-ʿaql al-faʿʿāl*) descending into the philosopher-imam, and the "virtuous city" (*al-madīna al-fāḍila*) modeled on the Medina-of-Muhammad ideal-state. *Kitāb al-Siyāsa al-Madaniyya* (*Book of Political Regime*) and *Taḥṣīl al-saʿāda* (*Attainment of Happiness*) extend the political-philosophical program.
- **Metaphysics + epistemology.** *Risāla fī al-ʿaql* (*Epistle on the Intellect*) lays out the four-fold intellect distinction (potential / actual / acquired / Active Intellect) that becomes the foundation for **[[ibn-sina|Avicenna]]'s** and **[[ibn-rushd|Averroes]]'s** intellect-theory. The Active Intellect doctrine — a separate, eternal cosmic intellect that brings human potential intellect into act — is the keystone of falsafa epistemology and metaphysics; it will be the principal point of Aquinas's engagement with the falsafa tradition. *Kitāb al-Ḥurūf* (*Book of Letters*) is al-Farabi's philosophy of language.
- **Logic + science classification.** *Iḥṣāʾ al-ʿulūm* (*Enumeration of the Sciences*) — encyclopedic classification of the sciences; **translated into Latin by Gerard of Cremona (12th c.) as *De scientiis*** and influential on the medieval Latin curriculum. Extensive commentaries on Aristotelian *Organon*.
- **Plato-Aristotle harmonization.** *Falsafat Arisṭūṭālīs* + *Falsafat Aflāṭūn* + *Kitāb al-Jamʿ bayna raʾyay al-ḥakīmayn* (*Book of Harmonizing the Opinions of the Two Sages*) — arguments for the substantial unity of Plato and Aristotle. (The harmonization partly depends on the **[[phase-5-048-theology-of-aristotle-arabic|pseudo-Aristotelian *Theology of Aristotle*]]**, which is actually Plotinus *Enneads* IV-VI — the misattribution-induced harmonization is a foundational falsafa motif, corrected in Christian Europe only by Casaubon and Ficino.)
- **Music theory.** *Kitāb al-Mūsīqā al-kabīr* (*Great Book of Music*) — foundational Arabic music theory; al-Farabi was also a celebrated practicing musician.

## Role in the tradition
- **The Second Teacher (*al-muʿallim al-thānī*).** The honorific title in subsequent Arabic philosophical literature signals al-Farabi's status as the principal post-Aristotelian Aristotelian; the title was deployed to give al-Farabi an Aristotelian-canonical authority that subsequent falsafa would build on.
- **Architect of Islamic political philosophy.** Al-Farabi's reframing of Plato's *Republic* in Islamic-prophetic terms is the **foundational move of Islamic political philosophy** — read forward into Avicenna's *Shifāʾ* book on political philosophy, Ibn Bājja, [[ibn-tufayl|Ibn Tufayl]], [[ibn-rushd|Averroes]]'s *Commentary on Plato's Republic*, and ultimately Maimonides's *Guide for the Perplexed*. The figure of the **philosopher-prophet** as the apex of human possibility is al-Farabi's signature contribution.
- **Active Intellect doctrine into Latin scholasticism.** Al-Farabi's intellect-theory passes to [[ibn-sina|Avicenna]] then [[ibn-rushd|Averroes]] and enters Latin scholasticism via the Toledo School translations; Aquinas engages al-Farabi directly (under the name *Alfarabius*) on the intellect-doctrine. The medieval Latin discussion of *intellectus agens* / Active Intellect is al-Farabi's discussion received through Avicennian and Averroist intermediaries.
- **Maimonides on al-Farabi.** Maimonides (Letter to Samuel ibn Tibbon, ~1199) named al-Farabi as the only Arabic philosopher worth reading without reservation — *"What al-Farabi has written is, with no exception, finer than fine flour."* The *Guide for the Perplexed* ([[phase-5-019-maimonides-guide-for-the-perplexed]]) is substantially al-Farabi-formed; **al-Farabi is the keystone Islamic-philosophical source for medieval Jewish philosophical theology.**
- **Latin reception as Alfarabius.** Gerard of Cremona's 12th-c. Latin translation of *Iḥṣāʾ al-ʿulūm* (*De scientiis*) is widely read in medieval Latin schools; al-Farabi is cited by Albert the Great, Aquinas (under the name *Alfarabius* or *Abunazar*), Dominicus Gundissalinus, and later commentators. His Latin reception is narrower than Avicenna's and Averroes's but real.

## Disputes
- **Theological alignment.** Some scholars (older comparative-Islam literature) suggest Ismāʿīlī / Shīʿī sympathies (the philosopher-imam structure of the *Excellent City* reads Ismāʿīlī-friendly); Mahdi 2001 argues against this — al-Farabi's political theory is philosophically generic, not narrowly Ismāʿīlī.
- **Religion-vs-philosophy on prophecy.** Al-Farabi's account of prophecy as the imaginative faculty's reception of intelligibles from the Active Intellect (*intellectus agens*) implies that the philosopher's intellect-reception is *epistemically prior* to the prophet's imaginative-reception; this is theologically delicate (the Quran would seem to imply prophecy's superiority over philosophy). Modern scholarship divides: Strauss-Mahdi reading sees al-Farabi as covertly subordinating revealed religion to philosophy; mainstream contemporary reading (Adamson, Druart) sees a more straightforward complementarity.
- **The harmonization-of-Plato-and-Aristotle thesis.** Al-Farabi's *Jamʿ* depends on attributing the *Theology of Aristotle* (actually Plotinus) to Aristotle; the harmonization is partly an artifact of this misattribution.
