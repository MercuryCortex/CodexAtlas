# Varāhamihira

## Identity

The great **astronomer-astrologer-encyclopaedist of Ujjain**, working in the sixth century CE (conventionally c. 505–587) at the Gupta-era centre of Indian astral science. He is the figure through whom the Greek-derived horoscopic material that entered Sanskrit in the [[phase-3-041-yavanajataka|*Yavanajātaka*]] becomes a **settled classical system** rather than a foreign import — and, in the *Pañcasiddhāntikā*, the figure who documents the state of Indian astronomy at exactly the moment of that absorption.

His range is unusual even for an encyclopaedist. The *Bṛhat Saṃhitā* is not an astrology book; it is a compendium of everything a court scholar was expected to be able to read as a sign — planetary risings, comets, halos, earthquakes, the behaviour of animals, the quality of gemstones, the proportions of temple images, the siting of wells, the making of perfumes. It is one of the richest single windows onto sixth-century Indian material culture, and it happens also to contain the passage that makes this node load-bearing for the Atlas.

## The receiving culture names its source

The [[phase-3-041-yavanajataka|*Yavanajātaka*]] announces the transmission in its **title** — "the Greeks' book of nativities". Varāhamihira supplies the other half of the evidence: the transmission acknowledged, defensively and explicitly, from **inside the receiving tradition**, by its most authoritative practitioner.

*Bṛhat Saṃhitā* 2.15 concedes the awkwardness directly. The Yavanas — the Greeks — are *mleccha*, barbarians, outside the ritual order; and yet, Varāhamihira says, this science is properly established among them, and for that reason they are honoured like *ṛṣis* (seers) — so how much more should a twice-born man who knows the science be honoured (Kern 1875).

That is a remarkable sentence to find in a Sanskrit *śāstra*. It is an admission, made in order to defend the astrologer's own status, that the science he practises came from foreigners of low ritual standing and is nevertheless authoritative. In the Atlas's terms it is a **self-attesting transmission edge**: the receiving tradition, in its own canonical text, naming the transmitting tradition and conceding the debt.

Combined with the loanwords (*horā* < *hōra*, *kendra* < *kentron*, *dṛkāṇa* < *dekanos*) and the one-to-one translation of the sign-names (*Meṣa* = *Krios* = Aries), the Greek origin of the Indian horoscopic layer is about as thoroughly evidenced as any cultural transmission of antiquity.

## The double frame he fixes in place

What Varāhamihira systematizes is not a replacement but a **layering**, and it is the layering that defines [[tradition-jyotisha-indian-astrology|jyotiṣa]] to this day:

- The **indigenous stratum** — the 27 (or 28) *nakṣatras*, the lunar mansions ([[astronomy-indian-nakshatras]]; [[astronomy-lunar-mansions]]), attested in India from the Vedic period, governing the lunar-natal layer, the *muhūrta* timing of ritual, and the [[hindu-panchang-calendar|pañcāṅga]] almanac.
- The **imported stratum** — the twelve *rāśis* (signs), the twelve *bhāvas* (places), the planetary aspects and rulerships: [[astrology-horoscope-twelve-places|the Hellenistic horoscopic apparatus]], arriving via the *Yavanajātaka* material.

Neither displaces the other. Indian astrology runs both simultaneously — which is why it is unusually *legible* as a transmission case: the seam is still visible, and the imported layer still speaks Greek in its technical vocabulary.

The *Pañcasiddhāntikā* documents the same seam on the astronomical side. Of the five schools it digests, the **Romaka** ("Roman", i.e. Byzantine-Greek) and **Pauliśa** siddhāntas are Greek-derived; the others are indigenous. Varāhamihira sets them side by side and compares their parameters, which is precisely what makes the work usable as evidence (Neugebauer & Pingree 1971).

## A third stratum, seven centuries later

The Indian case is doubly stratified, and it is worth stating because it complicates any simple "one transmission" story. From roughly the 13th century a **second wave** of the same western material returned — the **Tājika** school, importing Perso-Arabic horary and annual-revolution doctrine into jyotiṣa. So Indian astrology carries Greek material absorbed in late antiquity *and* Perso-Arabic material absorbed in the medieval period, both ultimately from the same Hellenistic source, arriving five centuries apart by different routes (Pingree 1981).

## Disputes

- **Dates.** The conventional 505–587 rests on the *Pañcasiddhāntikā*'s stated epoch (Śaka 427 = 505 CE) and on the tradition of his death date. Both are reasonable; neither is documentary in the modern sense.
- **How much is "Greek"?** The Greek derivation of the *rāśi*/horā layer is mainstream and is not seriously contested in the scholarly literature. A nationalist counter-position asserting indigenous Indian origin of the zodiacal horoscope — sometimes with a claimed reversal of the direction of transmission — circulates in popular Indian writing on jyotiṣa; it is not accepted by the specialist literature (Pingree 1981, 1997; Plofker 2009), which rests its case on the loanwords, the sign-name translations, and the receiving tradition's own attributions, including Varāhamihira's. The vault records the counter-position's existence and does not carry it as a claim.
- **Was he a Zoroastrian, a sun-worshipper, or a Brahmin?** Later tradition variously associates him with Maga (Iranian-derived) sun-priest lineages at Ujjain. The evidence is thin and the question is genuinely open; it is raised here only because it would, if resolved, bear on how the Iranian and Greek material reached him.
- **What the vault asserts.** That the horoscopic layer of jyotiṣa is Greek-derived, and that Varāhamihira systematized it, is Tier-1 history. That the resulting system yields true predictions is a claim internal to the tradition; the vault reports that practitioners have held it continuously for fifteen centuries and does not adjudicate it.
