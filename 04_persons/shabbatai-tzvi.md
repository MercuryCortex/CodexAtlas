# Shabbatai Tzvi

## Identity
Sephardic Jew of Smyrna (1626–1676), proclaimed Messiah in May 1665 by Nathan of Gaza and accepted as such by a massive movement that swept across the Jewish communities of the Ottoman Empire, Italy, the Netherlands, Germany, Poland, and Lithuania. Charismatic-mystical of clinically unstable temperament — long-attested cycles of ecstatic illumination alternating with despondency (the modern psychiatric description manic-depressive is sometimes applied) — Shabbatai had performed *ma'asim zarim* ("strange acts," antinomian violations) for years before his 1665 encounter with Nathan; Nathan provided the Lurianic-Kabbalistic theological framework that read these acts as messianic descents into the realm of the *kelippot*. The movement reached its peak in 1665–1666; on his 1666 summons to Constantinople by Sultan Mehmed IV, Shabbatai chose conversion to Islam over execution, becoming Aziz Mehmed Efendi. The apostasy shattered the public movement but did not end Sabbateanism, which continued in the Donmeh communities and was theologically defended by Nathan as itself a redemptive descent. Shabbatai died in Albanian exile 1676.

## Historical evidence
Densely documented: contemporary letters and pamphlets from across the European-Mediterranean Jewish world (the Sabbatean controversy generated one of the largest pamphlet exchanges of 17th-c. Jewish history); Ottoman court records; the anti-Sabbatean *Zizath Novel Zvi* of Jacob Sasportas (1666–1667); Nathan's writings.

## Works
None of substance. The Sabbatean doctrinal apparatus is Nathan of Gaza's; Shabbatai himself acted out the messianic role.

## Role in the tradition
Shabbatai is the central figure of the most consequential messianic crisis in post-classical Jewish history. The Sabbatean theology of "redemption through sin" (Scholem's phrase) had long covert effects, particularly through the Donmeh, the Frankist movement, and (in Scholem's contested thesis) early Hasidism.
