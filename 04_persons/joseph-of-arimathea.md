# Joseph of Arimathea

## Identity
A wealthy member of the Jerusalem Sanhedrin who, after Jesus's crucifixion, petitioned Pontius Pilate for the body and arranged a hasty burial in his own new rock-cut tomb before sundown. All four canonical Gospels narrate the episode with broadly convergent detail — among the more strongly multiply-attested events of the Passion sequence.

## Historical evidence
Strong multiple attestation. The historical existence of a Sanhedrin member who arranged Jesus's burial is widely accepted by critical scholarship (e.g., Crossan, Brown). Whether the burial was *honorable* (in a personal tomb) per the Gospels, or a *dishonorable* mass-grave (per Crossan's reconstruction extrapolating Roman crucifixion-burial customs), is contested. The Markan version's restraint — Joseph as a sympathetic Sanhedrin member, not yet called a disciple — is generally read as historically prior to Matthew/Luke/John's increasingly explicit *discipleship* framing.

## Works
None genuine. The 4th-century-and-later *Acts of Pilate / Gospel of Nicodemus* gives him expanded narrative (imprisonment, rescue, evangelism). Medieval British tradition (12th c. onward, Robert de Boron's *Joseph d'Arimathie* and Cistercian Grail literature) connects him to Glastonbury and the Holy Grail — entirely late-medieval legend.

## Role in the tradition
**Burial-narrator**: foundational role in all four Passion narratives.

**Medieval British/Grail tradition**: legendary first evangelist of Britain, custodian of the Holy Grail; the Glastonbury Thorn legend has him planting his staff at Glastonbury. None of this has early attestation.

## Disputes
- **Honorable vs. dishonorable burial.** Crossan argues Roman crucifixion victims were typically left unburied or thrown into common pits; the Gospel burial scene is theological. Brown and the mainstream defend a plausible honorable burial.
- **Glastonbury/Grail legends.** Medieval invention; no early evidence.
