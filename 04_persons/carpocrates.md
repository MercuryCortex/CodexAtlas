# Carpocrates

## Identity
Alexandrian Christian Gnostic teacher in the first half of the second century. Irenaeus (Adv. Haer. 1.25) attributes to the Carpocratians a libertine ethic (deliberately committing all acts to free the soul from the archons' debts), reverence for icons of Jesus alongside Pythagoras and Plato, and a doctrine of metempsychosis. The sect's antinomian reputation should be evaluated with awareness of heresiological exaggeration. Clement of Alexandria's *Letter to Theodore* (Mar Saba letter, controversial in its own right) mentions a "secret Gospel of Mark" used by the Carpocratians — but the authenticity of the Mar Saba letter is contested.

## Historical evidence
- **Irenaeus** (*Adv. Haer.* 1.25, ~180) is the principal source. He reports the Carpocratian doctrine of universal metempsychosis (souls must transmigrate until they have committed *every* possible act), the *icon-veneration* practice (images of Jesus alongside Pythagoras, Plato, Aristotle), and the antinomian ethical extreme.
- **Clement of Alexandria** (*Stromata* III.5–9, ~200) is more valuable methodologically because he quotes the Carpocratian text *On Justice* by Epiphanes (Carpocrates's son) directly, allowing us to read at least one Carpocratian voice rather than only the heresiological caricature. Epiphanes's text argues for communal property and sexual sharing as the *natural justice* of divine providence (an argument with Cynic-Stoic philosophical substrate, not random antinomianism).
- **Hippolytus** (*Refutatio* 7.32) and **Tertullian** (*De anima* 23, 35) depend on Irenaeus.
- **Epiphanius** (*Panarion* 27) adds details about Epiphanes's cult on Cephallenia and 17-year death — typically embellished hagiographic-heresiological material.

## Role in the tradition
- **Antinomian-libertine reputation.** The Carpocratians are heresiology's *type specimen* of Gnostic libertinism; whether their actual practice matched the heresiological description is precisely the contested point. Epiphanes's *On Justice* (preserved by Clement) reads as serious philosophical argument for *natural communism*, not as the orgiastic license Irenaeus describes.
- **Image-veneration practice.** The Carpocratian use of icons of Jesus alongside Pythagoras and Plato is one of the earliest documented Christian icon-veneration practices, and it occurs in a *Gnostic* community. This complicates the standard narrative of icon-veneration as a later Byzantine development.
- **Metempsychosis.** The Carpocratian doctrine of soul-transmigration aligns the group with Pythagorean and Platonist soul-doctrines and distinguishes them from the more typical Gnostic *single-fall, single-redemption* schema. This is one channel through which Pythagorean-Platonist metempsychosis enters 2nd-c. Christian-related thought.
- **Epiphanes's cult on Cephallenia.** Per Clement and Epiphanius, Epiphanes died young (~17 years old) and was deified by his Cephallenian followers — a startling case of a 2nd-c. Greek hero-cult organized around a Christian-Gnostic theologian-child. The historicity is disputed but the report itself is striking evidence of the Greek-Mediterranean substrate of Carpocratian piety.

## Connections — substrate and influence
- **Platonist and Pythagorean substrate.** Carpocratian icon-veneration of Pythagoras, Plato, and Aristotle alongside Jesus, plus the metempsychosis doctrine, locates the group firmly in the Middle Platonist / Neopythagorean milieu of 2nd-c. Alexandria. The school operates as a hybrid Christian-Platonist movement — what later orthodoxy will reject but what Clement himself partly continues in less radical form.
- **Cynic-Stoic antinomian substrate.** Epiphanes's *On Justice* (in Clement *Strom.* III.6–9) is structurally a Cynic-Stoic argument from *natural law* against conventional law — that property and sexual exclusivity are *human* artifacts that conflict with the *natural* divine economy of shared use. The argument has direct philosophical predecessors in Diogenes the Cynic and the early Stoic *Republic* of Zeno (preserved in Diogenes Laertius VII). Goulet-Cazé 1996, Dudley 1937 *A History of Cynicism* trace the Cynic-Stoic philosophical substrate. The Carpocratian use of these arguments shows how Hellenistic philosophical *libertinism* (more properly: anti-conventionalism) provided categories that 2nd-c. Christian Gnostics could deploy.
- **Egyptian-Hellenistic religious substrate.** Alexandria is the Carpocratian setting; the Carpocratian fusion of Christian, Platonic, Pythagorean, and (per Epiphanius) Cephallenian-Greek elements is recognizable as Alexandrian religious cosmopolitanism. Pearson 1990.
- **Heresiological reliability problem.** King 2003 reads the libertine reports as polemical caricature without independent attestation: Irenaeus does not claim to have met Carpocratians or to have seen their practice; he is reporting a theological *position* (every act must be done to escape the archons) and inferring practice. The Epiphanes *On Justice* text Clement quotes is theoretical-philosophical, not a confession of practice. The heresiological libertine picture should be evaluated as polemical until corroborated.
- **The *Secret Gospel of Mark* / Mar Saba letter.** A letter ascribed to Clement of Alexandria, discovered by Morton Smith at Mar Saba 1958, reports that the Carpocratians used a longer ("secret") version of Mark. The authenticity of the Mar Saba letter itself is heavily contested (Carlson 2005 vs. Brown 2005); if genuine, it would be an important data point on Carpocratian Markan usage. Treat as *claim: contested*.

## Disputes
- **Historicity of the libertine practice.** King 2003 and recent Gnosticism scholarship treat the heresiological libertine description with strong skepticism. Pearson 1990 is somewhat more willing to accept a kernel of historical antinomian practice. Whether the Carpocratian *theology* of universal experience (the metempsychosis-debt argument) produced corresponding *practice* remains genuinely unclear.
- **The Mar Saba letter / Secret Gospel of Mark.** Authenticity of the alleged Clement letter is heavily contested. If forged (likely on stylometric and provenance grounds per Carlson 2005), then no early-3rd-c. evidence of Carpocratian Markan usage exists.
- **Epiphanes's existence.** Some scholars (notably Kraft 1949) argued *epiphanes* in Clement is the adjective ("the illustrious one") referring to Carpocrates rather than to a son named Epiphanes; the proposal has been mostly rejected but is occasionally revived.
