# Marcus Aurelius

## Identity
The last of the "Five Good Emperors" (161–180), Stoic philosopher, author of the ***Meditations*** — a Greek-language Stoic self-examination journal not intended for publication. Reigned during the Antonine plague and the wars on the Danube against the Marcomanni and Quadi. His reign saw two notable persecution events: the **martyrdom of [[justin-martyr|Justin]] and his companions at Rome** (~+165) and the **Lyon and Vienne martyrdoms** (~+177).

## Historical evidence
The *Meditations* — extant Greek text, Marcus's own composition. His public career and imperial actions are well-documented in the *Historia Augusta*, Dio Cassius, Herodian, and inscriptions. Christian sources (Justin's *2 Apology*, the *Letter of the Churches of Lyons and Vienne* in Eusebius HE 5.1, Athenagoras's *Embassy*) provide perspective from the targets.

## Works
- ***Meditations*** — one of the most-read Stoic texts of all time; a personal handbook of Stoic discipline, not a systematic treatise. Famously contains a single passage that may be a reference to Christians: "what a soul is one that is ready to be released from the body — provided this readiness arises from a personal judgment, not from mere obstinacy, as with the Christians" (Med. 11.3) — though many scholars consider this a later interpolation.

## Role in the tradition
- **Persecutor in Christian memory**: Christian tradition lists him as one of the bad-emperor persecutors, though his personal disposition was philosophical rather than malicious.
- **Lyon martyrdoms (177)**: the principal Christian persecution event of his reign — graphically narrated in the *Letter of the Churches of Lyons and Vienne* preserved by Eusebius (HE 5.1).
- **Apologetic addressee**: Justin's *2 Apology*, Athenagoras's *Embassy*, and Melito's lost *Apology* were all addressed to him — Christians thought of him as the kind of educated philosophical emperor who *might* be persuaded.
- **Stoic-Christian comparison**: the *Meditations*' moral seriousness has been a recurring touchstone for comparison with early Christian ethics; the parallels are real, the differences (Marcus's pagan-cosmic-providence vs. Christian personal-providence) genuine.

## Disputes
- **Personal role in the persecutions**: probably allowed local governors to apply Trajan's rescript without imperial intervention rather than launching directed persecution.
- **Authenticity of *Med.* 11.3 reference to Christians**: contested.
- **Stoic-Christian connection**: a perennial scholarly comparison, with extensive literature on whether Marcus *knew* Christian texts or merely shared cultural milieu.
