# Ezekiel

## Identity
Ezekiel son of Buzi, a priest deported with King Jehoiachin in the first Babylonian deportation of 597 BCE, who received his prophetic call while resettled at Tel-Abib by the Chebar canal in Babylonia (Ezek 1:1–3, dated to the fifth year of the exile = 593 BCE). His ministry continued through and beyond the destruction of Jerusalem (586 BCE). His prophecies are dated with unusual precision — the book contains the most detailed dated chronology of any prophetic work — and his visions (the chariot-throne of YHWH in ch. 1, the valley of dry bones in ch. 37, the future temple in chs. 40–48) are among the most influential in later Jewish and Christian tradition.

## Historical evidence
The book of Ezekiel itself with its dated oracles. The general historical setting — first Babylonian deportation, exile in Babylonia, destruction of Jerusalem — is confirmed by Babylonian sources (the Babylonian Chronicle, Jehoiachin ration tablets from Babylon naming Yaukin king of Yahud).

## Works
The book of Ezekiel — 48 chapters — has unusual literary unity for a prophetic book (Greenberg argued the book is substantially the work of the prophet himself; others see more redaction).

## Role in the tradition
The throne-chariot vision of Ezekiel 1 (the *merkavah*, with its four faces, wheels-within-wheels, and *ḥayyot* living creatures) is the seed of *merkavah* mysticism — the earliest documented Jewish mystical tradition, which flowed into the *hekhalot* literature of late antique Judaism and ultimately into kabbalah. The valley of dry bones (ch. 37) shaped resurrection theology. The new-temple vision (chs. 40–48) is the model for the heavenly temple in Revelation and other apocalyptic literature.

## Disputes
- **Authorial unity.** Greenberg (1983) argued substantial single-authored composition; many scholars see more redactional layering (Zimmerli's classic two-volume commentary distinguishes "Ezekiel core" from later "school" elaborations).
- **Reliability of dated prophecies.** Dating system is internally consistent and broadly compatible with Babylonian chronology.
