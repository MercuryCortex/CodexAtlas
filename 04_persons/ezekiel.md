# Ezekiel

## Identity

**Ezekiel ben Buzi** (c. 622–570 BCE) — a Jerusalem priest deported in the first Babylonian deportation of 597 BCE — is the founding figure of the **[[merkabah-miraj-ascent-chain|heavenly ascent tradition]]** in Western religion. His single vision beside the Chebar canal in Babylonia (Ezekiel 1, dated precisely to 593 BCE) generated 2,500 years of mystical tradition: Jewish Merkabah mysticism, the Hekhalot literature, Muhammad's Mi'raj, Christian apocalyptic iconography, and Dante's *Paradiso* all trace their structural DNA to this one text.

He is one of the few major prophets for whom we have unusually precise external corroboration: the Babylonian Chronicle confirms the 597 BCE deportation, and Babylonian administrative tablets from Nippur name "Yaukin king of Yahud" (*Jehoiachin*) and his rations — placing Ezekiel's community of exiled Judahites in exactly the region where the prophet says he received his call.

## The Chariot Vision (Ezekiel 1, -593 BCE)

The vision arrives during the year 593 BCE: *"a stormy wind came out of the north, a great cloud with brightness around it and fire flashing forth continually, and in the midst of the fire, as it were gleaming metal"* (Ezekiel 1:4). What follows is one of the most psychedelically intense and precise visionary descriptions in biblical literature:

**The four *ḥayyot*** (living creatures, later identified as **[[tetramorph]]**):
- Four faces each: human (front), lion (right), ox (left), eagle (rear)
- Straight legs ending in calves' feet with the gleam of burnished bronze
- Four wings each — two spread upward, two covering their bodies
- They move without turning, straight ahead in whichever direction the spirit drives
- Between them: burning coals of fire, torches moving among them, lightning

**The wheels** (*ʾofannim*):
- One wheel beside each creature, gleaming like topaz
- Their construction: a wheel within a wheel
- Their rims were "high and terrible, and the four rims were full of eyes all around"
- The wheels move with the creatures; when the creatures rise, the wheels rise

**The firmament** (dome) above the creatures: the color of crystal, awesome

**Above the firmament**: a sapphire throne, and **on the throne: a figure with the appearance of a human being**, surrounded above the waist by gleaming metal in fire, and below the waist by fire, and all around: a rainbow.

Ezekiel's response: *"Such was the appearance of the likeness of the glory of YHWH. And when I saw it, I fell on my face, and I heard the voice of one speaking."* (1:28)

This is his prophetic call.

## Why Ezekiel 1 Is the Most Influential Single Vision in Western Mysticism

**1. The Chariot as the gateway to mysticism.** The *merkavah* (Hebrew: chariot/vehicle) becomes the technical term for the highest mystical object in Jewish thought. The Talmud (*b. Ḥagiga* 11b–16a) forbids public teaching of *maʿaseh merkavah* (the Work of the Chariot) — which itself testifies that a secret tradition of chariot-interpretation already existed. This prohibition gave Merkabah mysticism its prestige and drove it underground, generating the Hekhalot literature.

**2. The four living creatures generate the [[tetramorph]]** — in Revelation 4:6–8, they become the four celestial beings around the divine throne; in Jerome's Vulgate preface, they become the symbols of the four evangelists (Matthew/human, Mark/lion, Luke/ox, John/eagle); in medieval art, they appear on every major Gothic cathedral tympanum as the four beasts of the Last Judgment.

**3. The vision establishes the "heavenly throne" as the supreme mystical object** — which Hekhalot mystics will spend centuries attempting to reach through ascent through the seven heavens, which Muhammad will reach via the Mi'raj, which Dante will reach in the Empyrean.

**4. The wheels-within-wheels become the archetype of divine incomprehensibility** — the mechanism of divine action that operates beyond human conceptual categories.

## Disputes

- **Authorial unity.** Greenberg (1983) argued for substantial single-authored composition; Zimmerli (1979) distinguished an Ezekiel core from later school elaborations. The chariot vision of ch. 1 is generally accepted as early and authentic.
- **The *maʿaseh merkavah* tradition.** Scholem argued for actual circles of Merkabah mystics practicing ascent-techniques in late antiquity; Halperin (1988) argues the Hekhalot texts are literary compositions by unknown post-rabbinic authors, not records of actual visionary practice. The scholarly debate is unresolved.
- **Babylonian influence.** Zimmerli and others identify Babylonian iconographic parallels for the chariot imagery (composite creatures, cosmic wheels in Babylonian astral religion). The vision may consciously deploy Babylonian divine-throne iconography to assert YHWH's sovereignty in the geographic and cultural context of Babylonian exile.
