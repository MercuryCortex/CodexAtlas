# Gershom Scholem

## Identity
German-born Israeli historian (1897–1982); emigrated from Berlin to Jerusalem 1923; founding-faculty member of the Hebrew University (Jewish mysticism chair, 1933 onward). Effectively founded the academic study of Kabbalah and Jewish mysticism as a modern discipline. Close friend of Walter Benjamin (1915–1940); brother of the German Communist Werner Scholem (murdered at Buchenwald 1940).

## Works
See texts-authored. *Major Trends* (1941, English) is the most-cited; *Sabbatai Sevi* (1957) the most monumental.

## Role in the tradition
Scholem's *Major Trends* (1941) opened academic Kabbalah studies; the field he built has continued through students and successors (Liebes, Idel, Wolfson). Together with Hans Jonas (Gnosticism), Frances Yates (Hermeticism), and Henry Corbin (Islamic esotericism), Scholem is one of the mid-20th-century scholars whose modern academic recovery made Phase-4-through-Phase-6 esoteric traditions visible as serious history.

## Intellectual biography

Scholem grew up in a thoroughly assimilated Berlin Jewish family; his break with assimilation was intellectual and political simultaneously — he became a Zionist through encountering Kabbalistic texts, not through political argument. He taught himself Hebrew as a teenager, corresponded with Martin Buber (initially supportive, later sharply critical of Buber's romanticization of Hasidism), and formed a friendship with Walter Benjamin that lasted until Benjamin's suicide at the Spanish border in 1940. Scholem's later memoir *Walter Benjamin: The Story of a Friendship* (1975) is one of the finest intellectual portraits in the 20th-century record.

He arrived in Jerusalem in 1923 with two suitcases and a library of Kabbalistic manuscripts. He was appointed to the Hebrew University in 1925 and spent the next 57 years building Kabbalah studies from nothing. His method was rigorously philological-historical: he read the texts in their original languages, traced manuscript traditions, established the dating of key documents (his 1941 proof that the Zohar was a 13th-c. Castilian pseudepigraph, not ancient, was initially controversial), and placed the texts in their political-historical contexts. His central controversial thesis — that Kabbalah shows strong Gnostic influence and that medieval Jewish mysticism was not simply a product of rabbinic tradition but a revolution within it — shaped a generation of scholarship before Moshe Idel's *Kabbalah: New Perspectives* (1988) substantially pushed back.

## Key contributions to the vault's framework

**The Gnostic-origins thesis:** Scholem argued that the imagery of Kabbalistic cosmogony — primordial catastrophe, divine sparks scattered in matter, salvific return — showed structural parallels to Gnostic thought close enough to suggest historical transmission. Idel argued the parallels were too general and too much could be explained from inner-Jewish development. This debate is the foundational methodological question in Kabbalah scholarship and runs through multiple vault nodes. See [[tradition-kabbalah]], [[tradition-gnosticism]], [[shevirat-hakelim]].

**The Proclus-sefirot transmission channel:** Scholem (1962) documented the intellectual chain from Neoplatonism (via ibn Gabirol's *Fons Vitae*) to the Provençal Kabbalists, arguing that the sefirot as divine emanations is a Judaized and emotionalized form of Proclean henadic theology. This is the vault's key documented cross-tradition channel linking [[proclus]] → [[ibn-gabirol]] → [[tradition-kabbalah]]. See [[sefirot]].

**Sabbatai Zevi as the defining moment of Jewish mysticism:** Scholem's *Sabbatai Sevi* (1957 Hebrew; 1973 English) argued that the 17th-century Sabbatean messianic movement — which ended in the messianic pretender's conversion to Islam — was not an aberration but the *logical consequence* of Lurianic Kabbalah. Luria's *tikkun* doctrine required messianic redemption; Sabbatai Zevi mobilized that expectation; his apostasy forced the tradition into the most psychologically intense rationalization in Jewish religious history. This reading — that heresy could be the fulfillment of mysticism rather than its betrayal — was, and remains, Scholem's most provocative claim.

**The secularization thesis:** Scholem argued that modern secular Jewish nationalism (Zionism) and even secular Jewish thought (Benjamin's messianism; the Frankfurt School's negative theology of history) are displaced forms of the Kabbalistic tradition — that the mystical energy of Lurianic tikkun was "secularized" into political messianism. This thesis connects Kabbalah to the broader problem of disenchantment and places the vault's Jewish mysticism cluster into modern intellectual history.

## The scholarly legacy
Scholem's students at Hebrew University — Yehuda Liebes, Rivka Schatz Uffenheimer, Moshe Idel — constitute the second generation of modern Kabbalah scholarship. Idel's *Kabbalah: New Perspectives* (1988) represents the most systematic revision of Scholem's framework; Wolfson's later work pushes further into phenomenology and gender studies. The field Scholem built is now the discipline through which the vault's entire Jewish mysticism cluster is understood.
