  - target: plato
    type: student-of
    note: Aristotle studied at Plato's Academy for 20 years (-367 to -347). He was Plato's most accomplished student and most persistent critic — systematically refuting Platonic Forms while absorbing the entire Platonic philosophical vocabulary. His critique of the Forms (the "Third Man" argument, the rejection of separate mathematical entities) shaped every subsequent Platonic defense. Aristotle is Plato's most important interlocutor.
  - target: mathematics-pythagorean-sacred
    type: contrast
    note: Aristotle is the first systematic critic of Pythagorean number-mysticism. In *Metaphysics* Book I he gives a detailed account of Pythagorean doctrines and refutes them — numbers cannot BE things; mathematical entities are abstractions from perceptible objects, not their causes. This critique defines the Platonic-Aristotelian split: Plato accepts Pythagorean mathematical realism; Aristotle rejects it. Every subsequent debate about the ontological status of mathematics (Platonism vs. nominalism) returns to this split.

    note: Aristotle's corpus was the single most important Greek intellectual export to the Islamic world. Al-Mansur's 8th-century translation project and the House of Wisdom's 9th-century expansion were explicitly organized around translating Aristotle — the Organon, Physics, Metaphysics, De Anima, Nicomachean Ethics. Al-Farabi was called "the Second Teacher" after Aristotle; Avicenna rewrote the entire Aristotelian corpus in the Islamic context; Averroes wrote three levels of commentary on every Aristotelian text. The House of Wisdom is largely a project of Aristotle transmission and extension.
  - target: tradition-neoplatonism

The "harmony of Plato and Aristotle" was a central Neoplatonic project. Aristotle's Unmoved Mover (pure thought thinking itself) influenced Plotinus's conception of the One.
  - target: thomas-aquinas
    type: ancestor-of
    note: Aquinas's synthesis is precisely the Christian reception of Aristotle — the *Summa Theologiae* systematically uses Aristotelian categories (substance, accident, act, potency, the four causes) to express Christian doctrine. Aquinas's Five Ways for the existence of God all derive from Aristotle's physics and cosmology, particularly the Unmoved Mover. Medieval epithet: Aristotle is simply "the Philosopher"; Aquinas is his interpreter within the faith.
  - target: galileo-galilei
    type: contrast
    note: Galileo's physics directly refuted Aristotelian natural philosophy. Aristotle held that heavier objects fall faster (refuted by Galileo's inclined-plane experiments); that terrestrial and celestial physics obey different laws (refuted by Newton building on Galileo's work); that motion requires a continuous cause (refuted by the law of inertia). The Scientific Revolution is in large part the replacement of Aristotelian physics with mathematical-experimental physics.
  - target: euclid

    note: Aristotle (-384 to -322) and Euclid (-325 to -265) are near-contemporaries at the peak of Athenian intellectual life. Both were at or connected to the Platonic Academy. Aristotle's *Organon* (logic) and Euclid's *Elements* (geometry) are the two great Greek systematizations of reasoning — one for valid argument in general, one for valid geometric proof. Together they define the formal-deductive tradition that runs through all subsequent Western mathematics and philosophy.
  - target: unmoved-mover

This is the philosophical "God" that Aquinas's Five Ways identify with the Christian God, that Avicenna's Necessary Being develops in Islamic context, and that Averroes's commentary tradition transmitted throughout medieval Europe and the Islamic world. Aristotle's theology is the deep structure of much medieval theism.

# Aristotle

## Identity
Aristotle of Stagira (-384 to -322) was Plato's most accomplished student, Alexander the Great's tutor, and founder of the Peripatetic school at the Lyceum in Athens. His corpus — surviving as "esoteric" school-internal lectures recovered from a damp cellar and edited by Andronicus of Rhodes in the 1st century BCE — covers logic, physics, biology, metaphysics, ethics, politics, rhetoric, poetics. His influence on medieval Islamic philosophy (where he was *al-mu'allim al-awwal*, "the First Teacher") and Christian Scholasticism (where Aquinas calls him simply "the Philosopher") is matched by his influence on the natural sciences through the 17th century.

## Historical evidence
Excellent documentation: continuous philosophical reception, named author with biography, named teacher (Plato), named pupil (Alexander), well-attested institutional foundation (the Lyceum). His connection to the Macedonian royal house and his Stagirite (Macedonian) origin made him politically vulnerable in Athens; after Alexander's death (-323) he fled to Chalcis "lest the Athenians sin twice against philosophy" (referencing Socrates), dying there in -322.

For this vault: Aristotle is the *philosophical alternative track* to Plato — his **unmoved mover** is the philosophical "God of the philosophers" who reaches into Christian and Islamic theology (Aquinas's Five Ways, Avicenna's Necessary Being). His *De Anima* on the soul, and the active-intellect doctrine, shape both Christian and Islamic anthropology. His role as Plato's principal critic shaped *all* subsequent Platonist apologetics, including the Neoplatonic synthesis that ultimately fed Christian and Gnostic theology.

## MASSIVE WIN — The Aristotle Transmission Chain

The most consequential intellectual transmission in world history runs through Aristotle:

**Aristotle's Organon** (logic) + **Physics/Metaphysics** + **De Anima** →
Arabic translation (c. 800–900 CE, House of Wisdom) →
**al-Farabi** (The Second Teacher, c. 870–950) — systematized Aristotelian-Platonic synthesis →
**Avicenna** (ibn Sina, 980–1037) — Aristotelian psychology, *Flying Man* argument →
**Averroes** (ibn Rushd, 1126–1198) — three levels of commentary on every Aristotelian text →
Latin translation through Toledo (c. 1150–1250) →
**Albertus Magnus** (c. 1200–1280) — introduced Aristotle to Scholasticism →
**Thomas Aquinas** (1225–1274) — Christian-Aristotelian synthesis: *Summa Theologiae*

This chain is why every medieval European university taught Aristotelian logic as the foundation of all knowledge, why Catholic theology uses Aristotelian categories of substance and accident to describe the Eucharist (transubstantiation), and why the Scientific Revolution was largely a project of *replacing* Aristotle — which took until the 17th century to complete.

**The Unmoved Mover → Five Ways connection**: Aristotle's *Physics* VIII argues that all motion requires a chain of causation that must ultimately terminate in something that causes motion without itself being moved — pure actuality, pure thought. Aquinas's First, Second, and Third Ways are explicitly Aristotelian arguments. The "God of the philosophers" — the God that Einstein and Newton debate, the God that Spinoza identifies with Nature, the God that Descartes proves *a priori* — is Aristotle's Unmoved Mover dressed in successive philosophical vocabularies.
