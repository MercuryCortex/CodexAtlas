# Abū Maʿshar al-Balkhī

## Identity

**Abū Maʿshar Jaʿfar ibn Muḥammad al-Balkhī** (787–886), from Balkh in Khurāsān, working at Baghdad and dying at Wāsiṭ at nearly a hundred. Latin Europe knew him as **Albumasar**, and for four centuries he was, quite simply, *the* authority on the stars — the most-copied, most-translated, most-argued-with astrological writer in the medieval West.

He arrives in the tradition one generation after the translators. [[umar-ibn-al-farrukhan-al-tabari|ʿUmar ibn al-Farrukhān]] and [[mashallah-ibn-athari|Māshāʾallāh]] had made the Greek and Persian material available in Arabic; Abū Maʿshar's contribution was to **weld it into a single system with a physics behind it**.

## What he did to the tradition

### 1. He gave astrology a natural philosophy

The ***Great Introduction*** (848) does not open with technique. It opens with an **argument that astrology is a science** — that celestial motion is the efficient cause of generation and corruption in the sublunary world, so that reading the heavens is reading the causes of terrestrial change. The argument is Aristotelian, drawn chiefly from the *De generatione et corruptione* and the *Meteorologica*, and it converts astrology from a craft with an omen ancestry into a claimed branch of natural philosophy.

This is the move that makes astrology **arguable in a university**. Everything in the Latin scholastic engagement — Albertus Magnus's qualified acceptance, Aquinas's careful division between bodily and volitional effects, and eventually [[giovanni-pico-della-mirandola|Pico]]'s attempt to dismantle the whole structure in 1496 — is a response to a position Abū Maʿshar formulated.

### 2. He systematized historical astrology

The Sasanian doctrine that [[mashallah-ibn-athari|Māshāʾallāh]] transmitted — Jupiter–Saturn conjunctions as the clock of religious and dynastic change — Abū Maʿshar worked out in full in the *Kitāb al-Qirānāt* and the lost *Kitāb al-Ulūf*. Pingree's 1968 reconstruction of the latter showed how much Persian world-chronology stands behind it.

The downstream is remarkable and is documented at every step: the conjunction doctrine passes into Latin, and **Pierre d'Ailly** (1350–1420) uses it to compute the date of Antichrist — a Sasanian technique for dating dynastic turnover, transmitted through an Arabic systematization, applied to Christian eschatology (Smoller 1994). Columbus read d'Ailly.

### 3. He is the conduit into Latin Europe

The *Great Introduction* was translated into Latin **twice within seven years** — by **John of Seville** in 1133 and by **Hermann of Carinthia** in 1140, the latter as the *Introductorium maius*. Two independent translations of the same long technical book in a single decade is a measure of the demand. Through them, the full [[astrology-horoscope-twelve-places|horoscopic apparatus]] — Greek in origin, Persian in transit, Arabic in its developed form — became available in Latin, and astrology entered the medieval university curriculum. See [[event-toledo-translations-1085-1150]] and [[event-toledo-school-of-translators]].

## The Lemay thesis and its afterlife

Richard **Lemay** (1962) argued something stronger and more provocative: that the *Great Introduction*, precisely because it was translated early and read widely, was a **principal channel by which Aristotle's natural philosophy itself re-entered the Latin West** — that Latin Europe met Aristotelian physics inside an astrological book before it met it in Aristotle.

The thesis is influential and is cited constantly. Its strong form is contested: later scholarship (Burnett and others) treats Abū Maʿshar as one important early vehicle among several, alongside the direct Greek-Latin and Arabic-Latin Aristotle translations that followed quickly. It is carried here at **T2** — a serious, published, engaged-with position that is not the settled consensus — with the qualification stated rather than buried.

## Disputes

- **The Lemay thesis** (above): how much of Latin Aristotelianism actually came through Abū Maʿshar. Strong form contested; weak form (he was *an* important early vehicle) is uncontroversial.
- **The conversion anecdote.** The Arabic biographical tradition reports that Abū Maʿshar began as a scholar of *ḥadīth*, hostile to the mathematical sciences, and was turned to them in middle age — in some versions by [[al-kindi|al-Kindī]], who is said to have set him on geometry to get him out of the way. It is a good story and it may be true; it is a biographical topos of the kind Arabic literary sources produce readily, and it is reported here as tradition rather than as fact.
- **Astrology's standing in Islam.** Abū Maʿshar's naturalistic defence did not settle the question. The counter-tradition — certain *ḥadīth*, the Muʿtazilite objection to astral determinism, and later Ibn Taymiyya and Ibn Khaldūn — remained live throughout. Ibn Khaldūn's *Muqaddima* attacks astrology's epistemology directly and names Abū Maʿshar. The Islamic dispute runs parallel to the Christian one and reaches the same shape: institutional criticism alongside sustained practice.
- **What the vault is and is not asserting.** That Abū Maʿshar argued astrology was a natural science, and that his argument shaped Latin scholastic natural philosophy, is Tier-1 documented history. Whether the argument is *sound* is not a historical question and the vault takes no position on it; the argument is reported as what he held, and the tradition of objection to it is reported alongside.
