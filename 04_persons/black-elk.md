# Heȟáka Sápa (Black Elk)

## Identity
Oglala Lakota *wičháša wakȟáŋ* (holy man), Ghost Dancer, survivor of Wounded Knee, later Catholic catechist. Black Elk was born ~1863, was present as a child at the Battle of the Greasy Grass (1876), received a powerful vision at age nine (recounted in *Black Elk Speaks*), participated in the Ghost Dance in 1890, narrowly survived the Wounded Knee Massacre (December 29, 1890), converted to Catholicism in 1904, became a successful catechist (helping convert several hundred Lakota to Catholicism), and in his later years collaborated with the poet John Neihardt (1930–31) on *Black Elk Speaks* and with the religious-studies scholar Joseph Epes Brown (1947–48) on *The Sacred Pipe*. He died in 1950.

## Historical evidence
Extensive contemporary documentation: Neihardt's stenographic notes (transcribed and published as *The Sixth Grandfather* by DeMallie 1984); Brown's notes for *The Sacred Pipe*; family and reservation records; photographs throughout his life; his children's recollections.

## Works
- *Black Elk Speaks* (Neihardt, 1932) — autobiographical narrative shaped substantially by Neihardt's editorial framing.
- *The Sacred Pipe* (Brown, 1953) — Black Elk's account of the seven sacred rites of the Oglala Sioux.
- *When the Tree Flowered* (Neihardt, 1951) — derived from later Black Elk material.
- *The Sixth Grandfather* (DeMallie, 1984) — Neihardt's stenographic notes published as primary source.

## Role in the tradition
Black Elk's significance is multi-layered:
1. *Lakota religious figure* — he was a recognized *wičháša wakȟáŋ* of his generation; his great vision at age nine and his subsequent ceremonial practice constituted him a legitimate Lakota holy man.
2. *Catholic catechist* — for over 40 years he was a successful Catholic missionary among the Lakota. The Catholic Church opened a beatification process for him in 2017.
3. *Comparative-religion source* — *Black Elk Speaks* (1932) became enormously influential in 20th-c. comparative religion and depth psychology. **Carl Jung explicitly read Black Elk Speaks** and incorporated material from the great vision into his discussions of archetypal vision; *Mysterium Coniunctionis* and Jung's correspondence reference Lakota material substantially derived from Neihardt. Joseph Campbell drew on Black Elk for *The Hero with a Thousand Faces* and *The Power of Myth*. The "Indigenous wisdom" reading of Black Elk has been both productive and problematic — productive in legitimizing Indigenous religion in non-Indigenous discourse, problematic in the projection of New-Age and Jungian frames onto Lakota material.

## Disputes
- **Neihardt's editorial role.** How much of *Black Elk Speaks* is Black Elk's voice and how much is Neihardt's literary mediation has been the subject of decades of debate. DeMallie's 1984 publication of the stenographic notes (*The Sixth Grandfather*) clarified the question — Neihardt's literary shaping is substantial, but the underlying Lakota material is genuinely Black Elk's. Vine Deloria Jr.'s 1979 introduction to the reprint defended the book's authenticity while acknowledging its constructed character.
- **Black Elk's Catholicism.** Whether his Catholic practice is consistent with his Lakota religious authority, whether one displaced the other, or whether he held both authentically simultaneously, has been variously argued. Holler 1995 (*Black Elk's Religion*) argues for genuine syncretic synthesis; Steltenkamp 1993 (*Black Elk: Holy Man of the Oglala*) emphasizes the Catholic catechist years that *Black Elk Speaks* downplayed.
