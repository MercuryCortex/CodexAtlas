# Calvert Watkins

## Identity

**Calvert Watkins** (1933–2013) was an American historical linguist and philologist — Victor S. Thomas Professor of Linguistics and the Classics at Harvard, and after his 2003 retirement a professor-in-residence at UCLA (Classics and the Program in Indo-European Studies), where he worked until his death in Los Angeles in March 2013. He was among the leading Indo-Europeanists of the twentieth century: his doctoral work rebuilt the prehistory of the Celtic verb (*Indo-European Origins of the Celtic Verb I*, 1962, source of the paradigm-levelling generalization still cited as "Watkins's Law"), his *Indogermanische Grammatik* fascicle (1969) treated the history of Indo-European verbal inflection, and his edition of *The American Heritage Dictionary of Indo-European Roots* (1985; 3rd ed. 2011) put the reconstructed PIE lexicon in front of a general readership. He was married to the Vedic philologist Stephanie Jamison, his UCLA colleague.

In this Atlas he is a principal modern terminus of the storm-god/dragon branch: the scholar whose philology turned the [[chaoskampf]] pattern's Indo-European wing from a structural resemblance into a demonstrated genealogical inheritance.

## In the discipline: *How to Kill a Dragon* (1995)

Watkins's masterwork, *How to Kill a Dragon: Aspects of Indo-European Poetics* (Oxford, 1995), argues that the daughter traditions inherited from Proto-Indo-European not only words and grammar but a **poetic grammar** — formulas, the fixed verbal expressions that carry themes. Its centerpiece is the **dragon-slaying formula**, reconstructed as *\*gʷhent h₃égʷhim* — "(he) SLEW the SERPENT" (cited in the [[chaoskampf]] hub as *\*gʷhen- h₃gʷhim-*), built on the root *\*gʷhen-* "slay, smite" and the serpent-word *\*h₃égʷhis*. The formula surfaces in nearly identical inherited wording across the family:

- **Vedic**: *áhann áhim* — "he slew the serpent" (Rig Veda 1.32.1, of [[indra]] slaying [[vritra]]);
- **Avestan**: *yō janat̰ ažīm dahākəm* — "who slew the serpent Dahāka" (Yasna 9.8, of [[thraetaona]] and [[azi-dahaka]]);
- **Hittite**: *MUŠilluyankan kuenta* — "he slew the serpent" (CTH 321, the storm-god [[teshub]]/Tarhunna and [[illuyanka]]);
- **Greek**: both formula constituents survive (πεφνέμεν "slay" < *gʷhen-; ὄφις < *h₃egʷhis) and the verb appears in dragon-slaying contexts, though the noun slot is usually filled by δράκων — Apollo and [[python]], with further reflexes in the Herakles dossier;
- **Germanic**: the serpent-slayings of [[thor]] and the Beowulf dragon-fight, where the formula surfaces in inherited poetic diction.

Because the correspondences are systematic — the same verbal root throughout, the full noun+verb collocation preserved in Indo-Iranian, with lexical substitution in the noun slot elsewhere (Hittite illuyanka-, Greek drakōn, Germanic wurm-) — Watkins argued these are **genuine cognates**, a single formula persisting through four millennia of language divergence, and therefore that the myth it encodes (the hero or storm-god who slays the serpent to release waters or restore order) was already told among the speakers of Proto-Indo-European. The book also traces the formula's "bidirectional" life (in the Hittite tale the serpent first defeats the god, in the same wording reversed) and its extensions into healing charms and hero-narrative.

## Cross-tradition significance

Watkins is the vault's clearest case of the distinction the rigor rules require between **linguistic cognates and structural parallels**. Within the Indo-European family his formula upgrades the Indra–Vṛtra / Tarhunna–Illuyanka / Apollo–Python / Thor–serpent resemblances from typological likeness to **demonstrated common descent**: among the strongest philological evidence that the [[perkwunos|*Perkʷunos]]-type storm-god-versus-serpent mytheme is inherited, not converged. Outside the family, Watkins himself kept the boundary sharp: the Mesopotamian, Canaanite, and Egyptian combat-myths ([[tiamat]], [[yam]], [[apophis]]) are **not** Indo-European and share no cognate wording; their resemblance to the IE material is a matter of areal diffusion and independent convergence, plausibly through Bronze-Age contact networks. Of the chaoskampf hub's summary judgment — partly inherited (within IE), partly convergent or diffused (across families) — the IE-inheritance half is in large measure Watkins's finding; the diffusion/convergence reading of the Mesopotamian–Canaanite–Egyptian material descends from Gunkel and the areal-contact literature. He belongs beside [[georges-dumezil]] (trifunctional sociology of the pantheon) and [[marija-gimbutas]] / the Mallory–Anthony archaeology as one of the principal evidence-streams by which [[tradition-proto-indo-european-religion]] was reconstructed: Dumézil recovered the inherited *structure*, Watkins the inherited *words*.

## Disputes

- **Formula versus myth.** Watkins's own claim is deliberately narrow: what is reconstructed is a poetic formula and the theme it expresses. M.L. West (*Indo-European Poetry and Myth*, 2007) builds a much fuller reconstructed mythology on this and parallel evidence; skeptics within the discipline have cautioned that a two-word collocation ("slay" + "serpent") carries limited narrative information by itself, and that the fuller PIE "dragon myth" is an inference stacked on the formula rather than a datum. The vault's [[dragon-western]] node records this as the West-versus-Watkins framing: mythological descent read maximally, versus formulaic poetics read strictly.
- **Inheritance versus areal contact.** For the Anatolian–Greek parallels in particular, Mary Bachvarova (*From Hittite to Homer*, 2016) documents Late-Bronze-Age textual and performance channels by which Hurro-Hittite narrative reached Greek epic — a transmission route that complements, and for some episodes rivals, common PIE inheritance as the explanation of the Illuyanka–Typhon resemblances.
- Neither dispute touches the core philological result, which remains standard: the formulaic reconstruction of the Vedic, Iranian, Hittite, and Greek serpent-slaying phrases is the standard view among Indo-Europeanists — with the caveat that the Hittite and Greek instances substitute the noun slot lexically.
