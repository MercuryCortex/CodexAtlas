# Yared (the Deacon)

## Identity
**Saint Yared** (c. 505–571 CE), conventionally placed at the court of King Gabra Masqal of Aksum (mid-6th century), is the foundational figure of Ethiopian liturgical music. Ethiopian tradition credits him with the entire system of *zema* (sacred chant) and the five principal liturgical-musical books that organize the Ethiopian Orthodox liturgical year:

1. **Degua** — the great antiphonary, year-round festal chants.
2. **Mawasə'ət** — the responses.
3. **Zəmmare** — communion hymns.
4. **Mə'eraf** — common office.
5. **Sə'atat (Horologion)** — the Hours.

Yared's three modes — *Ge'ez*, *Ezel*, and *Araray* — function as melodic-modal categories under which all chants are classified by liturgical season and theological character. He is also traditionally credited with the *meleket* notation system, a notation using individual Ge'ez letters as melodic-formula cues — making Ethiopian chant one of the world's earliest notated musical traditions (the medieval surviving meleket-notated manuscripts are 16th-c. and later, but the formula-letter notation system is much older).

## Historical evidence
Yared is a *legendary-historical* figure: there is no doubt that the indigenous Ethiopian chant tradition exists in deep continuity from at least the 6th century, and the *literary attribution* of the system to Yared is medieval-canonical; but contemporary 6th-c. external attestation is lacking. The reasonable historical reading:

- **A real 6th-c. liturgical reformer at Aksum** is plausible and indeed likely — the Christianization of the previous centuries required a vernacular-Ge'ez liturgical-musical adaptation, and the timing fits the post-Nine-Saints monastic-liturgical consolidation.
- **The Yared name and attribution** crystallizes in the medieval *gadl* literature (Lives of Yared). The historical core — that a saint *named* Yared was the principal architect of Ethiopian chant in the 6th c. — is consistent with the institutional memory but not externally documentable.
- **The body of chant** itself is the primary evidence: a systematic, modally-structured, notationally-developed tradition that has continued in oral-and-written transmission for ~1500 years.

The famous hagiographic legend — Yared in a moment of monastic discouragement was given a celestial vision in which the Three Heavenly Birds taught him the three modes of *zema* — encodes the tradition's self-understanding that its chant is a *received* sacred system rather than a humanly invented one.

## Works
- **The Mazgaba Degua** (the *Degua* corpus) — survives in many Ge'ez manuscripts, the textual core dating in identifiable strata back to the 6th century and earlier. The Shelemay-Jeffery edition (1993, 3 vols) is the modern scholarly anthology.
- **The Aksumite hagiographic-musical-pedagogical tradition** — Ethiopian *debteras* (cantors) train for 10+ years in the Yaredian system; the four "Gondarine houses of chant" (founded under the Gondar emperors, ~17th–18th c.) preserve and elaborate distinct schools.

## Role in the tradition
Yared is to Ethiopian liturgical music what Romanos the Melodist is to Byzantine: the founding hymnographer whose name and system organize the entire tradition. Beyond music, Yared is a major saint, honored on 11 Genbot (May 19 Julian); his hagiography places him at the center of the Aksumite-monastic theological-spiritual flowering of the post-Nine-Saints period. The Yaredian musical tradition is one of the most distinct and continuously-attested liturgical-musical systems in the world; Shelemay and Jeffery 1993 emphasize that it is *not* derived from Byzantine, Coptic, or Syriac chant traditions despite the broader liturgical-ecclesiastical Coptic-Syriac connections — the indigenous Ge'ez-Aksumite musical-stratum is distinct.

## Disputes
- **Historicity of Yared as an individual.** Probable but not externally verifiable; the consensus among Ethiopianists is "legendary-with-historical-core" — there is no need to deny that a real 6th-c. liturgical reformer existed, but the hagiography is post-hoc.
- **Date of the meleket notation.** Probably medieval-Ethiopian (12th–15th c.) rather than 6th-c.; the surviving notated manuscripts are 16th-c.+; but the notation system may build on much earlier mnemonic-formula traditions.
- **Continuity of the chant tradition itself.** The *transmission* of Yaredian chant is via direct teacher-to-student oral apprenticeship, with manuscripts as memory aids; chant scholars (Shelemay, Powne) accept the chant's overall continuity with substantial accumulated medieval elaboration.
