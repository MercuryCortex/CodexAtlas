Hippocrates of Kos (c. 460–370 BCE) is the most famous physician in Western history and the founding figure of rational medicine — the tradition that attempts to explain and treat disease through natural causes rather than divine intervention or supernatural forces. He likely did not write most of the texts attributed to him, but the tradition he founded and named transformed medical practice across the Mediterranean and ultimately the world.

## The Historical Problem

The "historical Hippocrates" is one of antiquity's most famous ghosts. Plato mentions him twice, Aristotle once — confirming he existed and was famous. But the 60+ texts of the *Hippocratic Corpus* were written over approximately 400 years (c. 420 BCE–50 CE) by many different authors with often contradictory views. The debate over which texts Hippocrates actually wrote has occupied scholars for 2,000 years without resolution.

What we can say: a physician named Hippocrates from Kos was so influential that his name became the banner under which the entire tradition of naturalist medicine organized itself.

## The Kos School — Method Above All

The Kos school's distinctive contribution was **methodological**, not primarily theoretical:
- **Systematic observation**: take the patient's history; observe all symptoms
- **Record what you see**: the *Epidemics* (Books I and III) are the oldest surviving clinical case records — diseases observed across seasons and populations
- **Prognosis**: predict what will happen; a physician who cannot predict is not truly diagnosing
- **Restraint**: when uncertain, do nothing harmful rather than intervening blindly

The **Epidemics** case records are remarkable documents. They describe patients by name, location, day of illness, and systematic daily observation of symptoms. Many patients die. The physician does not claim to have saved them — he records what happened. This is the founding gesture of evidence-based medicine: honest documentation of outcomes, including failures.

## The Hippocratic Oath — Medical Ethics as Sacred Contract

The **Hippocratic Oath** is the foundational document of medical ethics. Its commitments include:
- **Teacher-student obligation**: treat one's teacher as a parent; teach medicine freely to the teacher's children; teach only those who have taken the oath
- **Do no harm** (*primum non nocere* — though this exact phrase is not in the original): do not give poison, even if asked
- **No abortion**: do not give a pessary to cause abortion (this clause's interpretation and application has generated 2,400 years of ethical debate)
- **No surgery**: leave surgery to the craftsmen who practice it (the Hippocratics were internists, not surgeons)
- **Confidentiality**: "What I may see or hear in the course of treatment, which ought not to be spoken of outside, I will keep to myself, holding such things shameful to be spoken about"
- **No sexual exploitation**: "I will not abuse my position to indulge in sexual contacts with the bodies of women or of men"

Modern medicine has modified all of these except confidentiality and the anti-exploitation clause. The structure — physician as oath-bound professional rather than merely skilled craftsman — is 2,400 years old.

## On the Sacred Disease — The First Anti-Supernatural Medical Argument

The Hippocratic text *On the Sacred Disease* (c. 400 BCE) is the first systematic argument against supernatural causation of disease. It opens:

*"I do not believe that the Sacred Disease [epilepsy] is any more divine or sacred than any other disease but, on the contrary, has specific characteristics and a definite cause."*

The argument is methodological: if epilepsy were divine, it would affect everyone equally. But it has systematic triggers (wind, temperature, emotional states), systematic occurrence patterns, and systematic treatments. Its causes are natural; its divine reputation is a fabrication by charlatans who profit from mystery.

This is the founding text of **secular medicine**: the argument that divine mystery is not an explanation but an evasion.
