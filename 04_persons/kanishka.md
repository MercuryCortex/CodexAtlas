# Kaniṣka I

## Identity
Kaniṣka I (r. ~127–150 CE) was the third major emperor of the Kushan dynasty (Yuezhi-origin Central-Asian-Iranian rulers of Bactria, Gandhāra, and northern India). His reign is the high-water mark of the Kushan empire, of Greco-Buddhist Gandhāran art, and of the political-patronage moment that scholars associate with the consolidation of Mahāyāna Buddhism. His coinage — gold *dīnāra*s with Bactrian-Greek script — depicts a syncretic pantheon of Iranian (Mithra / Miiro, Mao the moon, Mithra / Mithro, Verethraghna / Orlagno, Anāhita / Nana), Greek (Helios, Selene), Hindu (Śiva / Oesho), and Buddhist deities. On one famous gold type the reverse reads "BODDO" with a standing Buddha figure — **the earliest dated coinage explicitly representing the Buddha** (Cribb 1980 dating). Kaniṣka is traditionally identified as the patron of the **Fourth Buddhist Council** (Kashmir, ~100-150 CE), which produced the Sarvāstivāda Sanskrit canon and the *Mahāvibhāṣā* commentary — the institutional consolidation that scholars now associate with the late Mahāyāna emergence.

## Historical evidence
- **Numismatic**: extensive Kushan gold and copper coinage in his name with Bactrian-Greek inscriptions ("ϷAONANOϷAO KANHϷKI KOϷANO" = "King of Kings, Kaniṣka, Kushan"). His coinage *abandons* the Greek-language convention of the Indo-Greek kings and uses Bactrian-in-Greek-script (a Middle Iranian language written in adapted Greek alphabet), reflecting the Iranian-Central-Asian rather than Hellenic identity of the dynasty.
- **Epigraphic**: the **Rabatak inscription** (discovered 1993 at Rabatak, Baghlan province, Afghanistan; edited Sims-Williams & Cribb 1995) is the foundational Kushan dynastic text, declaring Kaniṣka's lineage (Kujula Kadphises → Vima Taktu → Vima Kadphises → Kaniṣka) and his pantheon of patronage. The text inaugurates "year one" of a new era ("the Kaniṣka era") starting in his accession year — Falk 2001 places this at 127 CE on astronomical grounds.
- **Textual (Buddhist)**: Chinese pilgrim Faxian (~5th c.) and Xuanzang (~7th c.) record Kaniṣka's patronage of Buddhism at length; Xuanzang describes the great Kaniṣka-stūpa at Puruṣapura (Peshawar) and the Fourth Council. The Sarvāstivāda *Vibhāṣā* and its summary the *Mahāvibhāṣā* are traditionally credited to the Fourth Council under Kaniṣka.
- **Archaeological**: the Kaniṣka *stūpa* at Shah-ji-ki-Dheri (Peshawar), excavated by D.B. Spooner 1908-1911, yielded a reliquary inscribed with Kaniṣka's name — direct physical attestation of his Buddhist patronage at the imperial center.

## Works
None — he is a patron, not an author. His "works" are: (a) the Kushan imperial visual program; (b) the Kaniṣka stūpa and reliquary; (c) the Fourth Buddhist Council; (d) the syncretic Kushan pantheon expressed on coinage.

## Role in the tradition
- **In Buddhism**: Kaniṣka is the second great cakravartin-king after [[asoka-maurya]], with a structurally similar Buddhist-patronage hagiography — Sanskrit and Chinese sources construct him as a violent conqueror turned Buddhist patron. The Fourth Council under his sponsorship is the institutional event that the Sarvāstivāda school associates with their canonical consolidation. Mahāyāna emergence is closely linked to the Gandhāran milieu of his reign: the earliest extant Mahāyāna manuscripts are the Gāndhārī Mahāyāna texts of the Schøyen Collection and the British Library Kharoṣṭhī manuscripts (~1st-3rd c. CE), produced precisely in the Kushan-Gandhāran cultural sphere.
- **In Greco-Buddhist art**: Kaniṣka's reign is the classical period of Gandhāran sculpture. The standing Buddha figures of Hadda, Begram, and Taxila — drawing on Hellenistic Apollo and ruler-portrait conventions — date stylistically to this period. **The first dated Buddha-image coinage (the "BODDO" type) is from Kaniṣka's mint.**
- **In modern reception**: Kaniṣka was a Yuezhi-origin Central Asian Iranian-speaking emperor who patronized Greco-Buddhist art and Mahāyāna Buddhism. He embodies the cross-cultural-religious-linguistic synthesis at the heart of [[tradition-greco-buddhism]].

## Disputes
- **Accession date**: the Kaniṣka era's year-one was uncertain for over a century; Falk 2001 ("The Yuga of Sphujiddhvaja and the Era of the Kuṣāṇas") established 127 CE on astronomical grounds, now consensus.
- **Religious identity**: was Kaniṣka personally a Buddhist? The conservative reading (Cribb): he was a Bactrian-Iranian polytheist whose state-religion pantheon included the Buddha alongside Iranian and Hindu deities, with strong patronage of Buddhism as one favored cult. The maximalist reading (Buddhist hagiographic tradition): he was a Buddhist convert and *cakravartin*. The numismatic record clearly shows polytheist devotion; the *imperial* patronage of Buddhism is uncontested.
- **The Fourth Buddhist Council**: Theravāda (Pali) tradition does not recognize this council and counts the Aśokan Third Council as the last orthodox council. Sarvāstivāda + later Mahāyāna tradition treat the Kushan Fourth Council as authoritative.
- **Relationship to Mahāyāna**: was Kaniṣka a Mahāyāna patron specifically, or a Sarvāstivāda patron whom later Mahāyāna tradition retroactively claimed? The institutional evidence points to Sarvāstivāda; the Gāndhārī Mahāyāna manuscripts of the same milieu suggest doctrinal currents that became Mahāyāna were already present.
