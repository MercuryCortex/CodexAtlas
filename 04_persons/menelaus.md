# Menelaus

## Identity

Menelaus (Greek **Μενέλαος**, *Menelaos*, the "fair-haired" — *xanthos* — of the fixed Homeric epithet) is the legendary king of **Sparta** (Lacedaemon): son of Atreus, younger brother of Agamemnon, husband of **Helen**, and father of Hermione. In the epic tradition the abduction of Helen by the Trojan prince Paris — committed while Paris was a **guest in Menelaus's own house** — is the cause of the Trojan War: the war is, at the mythic scale, the punishment of a single violated guest-friendship. There is no historical evidence for an individual person Menelaus; he is a heroic-legendary figure of the oral epic tradition, stabilised in the *Iliad* and *Odyssey* in roughly the 8th-7th centuries BCE (see Disputes on the Therapne cult and the Bronze Age site).

In the Atlas Menelaus is the missing **person-anchor for two existing deity nodes' defining episodes**: the seal-ambush and wrestling of [[proteus-greek|Proteus]] is *his* exploit (Od. 4.384-570), and the earliest attestation of Elysium — the earliest text to place [[rhadamanthus|Rhadamanthus]] there (the name itself already appears at Il. 14.322) — is *his* promised destiny (Od. 4.561-569). He is also the double exemplar — model host and wronged host — of Greek *xenia* in the sacred-hospitality hub. He is typed as a **person** on the model of [[odysseus]] and [[theseus]]: a legendary mortal hero whose importance is literary, holding a localized Laconian cult rather than a pan-Hellenic divine one (the typing is discussed honestly in Disputes).

## In the Homeric epics

**In the *Iliad*** Menelaus is the wronged husband on whose behalf the war is fought, and a brave but deliberately second-rank fighter. He duels Paris in single combat to settle the war and wins — but Aphrodite snatches Paris away in a mist before the kill (Il. 3.340-382). The truce is then broken by Pandarus's arrow, which wounds Menelaus (Il. 4.104-147). His finest hour is his *aristeia* in Book 17, standing over the body of Patroclus "like a cow over her first calf"; yet the poem also lets Apollo call him a "soft spearman" (μαλθακὸς αἰχμητής, Il. 17.587-588) — the epic is precise that his claim on our sympathy is his cause and his decency, not his prowess. In Book 13 he kills Peisandros and delivers one of the poem's most explicit theological readings of the war, rebuking the Trojans for the guest-crime and invoking **Zeus Xenios**, god of the violated guest-right (Il. 13.620-627).

**In the *Odyssey*** (Book 4, the close of the Telemachy) Menelaus is the poem's **model host**. Telemachus and Peisistratus arrive at Sparta unannounced, mid-wedding-feast; when the squire Eteoneus asks whether to receive the strangers or send them on, Menelaus rebukes him sharply — they two have eaten the hospitality of others often enough on the road home — and has the guests bathed, fed, and seated in honour *before* asking who they are (Od. 4.20-36). The panel is the positive pole against which the poem's monstrous and parasitic hosts (Polyphemus, the suitors) are measured. Helen, recognising Odysseus's son, drugs the wine with Egyptian *nepenthes* so the sad stories can be told without weeping (4.219-232), and the visit closes with the canonical gift-exchange — Menelaus offers horses and a chariot; Telemachus declines them (rocky Ithaca pastures no horses) and receives instead the silver mixing-bowl wrought by Hephaestus (4.589-619).

**The Egyptian logos and the seal-ambush** (Od. 4.351-586). Menelaus's own *nostos* took eight years; becalmed on the island of Pharos off Egypt and starving, he is pitied by the sea-nymph Eidothea, daughter of [[proteus-greek|Proteus]], who betrays her father's habits: the Old Man of the Sea comes ashore at noon to count and sleep among his seals. Disguised under fresh-flayed sealskins (Eidothea supplies ambrosia against the stench), Menelaus and three companions **seize the god** as he sleeps and hold him fast through every transformation — lion, serpent, leopard, boar, running water, towering leafy tree — until Proteus, mastered, resumes his own form and answers truly (4.435-570). He tells Menelaus the sacrifice owed to the gods, the fates of Ajax and of Agamemnon, and that Odysseus lives, weeping on Calypso's island.

**The Elysium prophecy** (Od. 4.561-569). Proteus ends with Menelaus's own destiny — the passage that is the **earliest attestation of Elysium in Greek literature**: it is not fated for him to die in horse-pasturing Argos; the immortals will convey him to the **Elysian plain** at the ends of the earth, "where fair-haired Rhadamanthus is," where there is no snow nor storm nor rain but always the singing west wind off Ocean — "because you have Helen, and are in their eyes the son-in-law (*gambros*) of Zeus" (4.569). Menelaus is thus the earliest named mortal in the extant record to be promised the heroes' paradise — and the ground of the promise is **divine kinship, not merit**.

## The cult at Therapne (the Menelaion)

From the late 8th century BCE a hill-shrine at **Therapne**, overlooking the Eurotas across from Sparta, received cult later known as the **Menelaion**. Excavated dedications from the 7th century onward name the recipients: the inscribed bronze aryballos "to Helen, wife of Menelaus" and its companion piece (Catling and Cavanagh 1976), with later dedications to Menelaus himself. Isocrates (*Helen* 63) says the Spartans at Therapne sacrifice to Helen and Menelaus "**not as heroes but as gods**." The shrine stands beside a substantial Mycenaean mansion complex (Late Helladic II-IIIB), which gave the site its excavators' name — but the Bronze Age building and the Iron Age cult are separated by centuries, and the cult is best understood as the 8th-century heroizing appropriation of an impressive ruin, not as continuous memory of a historical king (see Disputes).

## Cross-tradition significance

Menelaus's comparative importance is that of an **anchor-mortal**: three motifs the Atlas tracks across traditions enter the Greek record through his person.

**The mastered shape-shifter.** The rule of the Proteus episode — hidden truth can be wrung from a transforming power only by one who holds on through every change — is a widespread folkloric type (it recurs in Thetis's wrestling by Peleus, and the Atlas's proteus-greek node notes the structural echo in Jacob's night-wrestling at the Jabbok, who likewise must be held until he yields a blessing). These are **typological parallels, not transmissions**: no documented line connects the Pharos episode to the Genesis one, and the honest classification is independent convergence on a common narrative logic. What Menelaus contributes is the canonical Greek instance — the grip that the motif's later Western history (down to the English word "protean") descends from.

**The two poles of sacred hospitality.** Menelaus is unique in the Homeric corpus in embodying *xenia* from both sides: the exemplary host of Odyssey 4 and the violated host whose wronged guest-right is the war's cause and Zeus Xenios's case (Il. 13.620-627). The [[moral-sacred-hospitality-cross-tradition|hospitality hub]] sets Greek *xenia* beside the Torah's *ger*-command, Islamic *ḍiyāfa*, and the Vedic *atithi* ideal as parallel institutions — a **structural cross-tradition comparison** (Herman 1987 for the Greek institution), not a genealogical claim, though the eastern-Mediterranean instances at least share a contact zone (Arterbury 2005, cited at the hub, treats Greco-Roman and Near Eastern hospitality as one broadly shared regional custom).

**The exempted favourite and the paradise plain.** Odyssey 4.561-569 opens the Greek heroes'-paradise tradition: a named mortal exempted from death and translated to a plain of ease at the world's end. Within Greek tradition the development is traceable and secure — Hesiod generalises the destination to the race of heroes on the **Isles of the Blessed** (*Works and Days* 167-173), Pindar moralises the entrance (*Olympian* 2, where [[rhadamanthus]] sits as assessor and three blameless lives are the ticket), and Plato inherits the geography. *Upstream* of Homer the picture is debated, and the debate should be stated honestly: the **origin of Elysium** is unsettled (see Disputes), with an Indo-European, a pre-Greek/Minoan, and a Near Eastern-comparative account all in the field. What is *not* debated is the shape of the earliest datum: admission by **divine kinship** — Menelaus enters as Zeus's son-in-law, exactly the "heroic lineage or divine favor" criterion the [[theme-heroes-paradise-island|heroes'-paradise hub]] tabulates for the Greek row, and the baseline against which the later ethical-entrance versions (Hesiod, Pindar) are innovations.

## Disputes

- **Person or heroized god?** The Therapne cult, and Isocrates' explicit "not as heroes but as gods" (*Helen* 63), could support typing Menelaus as a divinized figure on the [[heracles]] model. The Atlas types him **person**: his divine honours are localized to one Laconian sanctuary and are the standard archaic heroization of an epic figure (Burkert 1985 on hero-cult), not a pan-Hellenic cult with an independent divine mythology; this matches the treatment of [[theseus]] (Athenian cult, typed person) against [[heracles]] (pan-Hellenic apotheosis, typed deity). The call is flagged rather than buried.
- **Historicity.** None demonstrable. The Mycenaean mansion at Therapne shows only that the 8th-century cult founders chose an impressive Bronze Age ruin for their shrine; continuity of memory from a historical Spartan king to the epic figure cannot be shown and is not the mainstream view.
- **The origin of Elysium.** Since the word Ēlysion has no agreed Greek etymology, three accounts compete: (i) an **Indo-European inheritance** — Puhvel (1969) sets it beside Hittite *wellu-*, the otherworld "meadow" of the dead; (ii) a **pre-Greek/Minoan survival** — Nilsson's old proposal that the effortless paradise for the king's kin is Minoan afterlife belief inherited by the Greeks; (iii) an inner-Greek derivation from *enēlysios*, "struck by lightning" (the lightning-struck as god-touched), discussed by Burkert. West (1997) adds the Near Eastern comparanda — exempted favourites of the gods translated to a distant land of ease (Utnapishtim at the rivers' mouth) — as a possible shaping influence on the Homeric picture. None of the three is secure; the Atlas records the debate rather than adjudicating it.
- **A different Menelaus.** The Hellenistic **high priest Menelaus** (2 Maccabees 4, the purchaser of the Jerusalem high-priesthood, d. 162 BCE), discussed in this vault at [[onias-iii]] and the Aaronide-continuity hub, is an unrelated person who merely bears the same Greek name; nothing in this node concerns him.
