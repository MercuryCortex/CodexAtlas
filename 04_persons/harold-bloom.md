# Harold Bloom

## Identity
American literary critic (1930–2019); Sterling Professor of Humanities at Yale University; among the most prolific late-20th-century American literary critics (over 40 books). Best known in literary scholarship for *The Anxiety of Influence* (1973). In the last 25 years of his life Bloom wrote increasingly on religion as a literary-visionary phenomenon, developing a controversial "American Religion" thesis.

## Works
*Kabbalah and Criticism* (1975) and the early Bloom-trilogy used Lurianic Kabbalah ([[isaac-luria]], the doctrine of [[tzimtzum]] and *shevirat hakelim*) as a model for literary influence. *The American Religion* (1992) argued that American religion — Southern Baptists, Mormons ([[joseph-smith]], [[brigham-young]]), Christian Scientists ([[mary-baker-eddy]]) — is fundamentally Gnostic: an inward, knowing, solitary, self-as-divine spirituality rather than orthodox Christianity. *Omens of Millennium* (1996) develops the Gnostic-angelological theme. *Jesus and Yahweh* (2005) reads "Yahweh" and "Jesus" as distinct literary characters of two different bibles.

## Role in the field
A high-profile non-specialist intervenor in religious studies, working from a literary-critical and Kabbalistic-Gnostic perspective. His "American Religion = Gnosticism" thesis has been widely discussed but only partially adopted by religious-studies specialists — the historical claim is contested, but the descriptive observation about American religious individualism has more traction. Bloom's Kabbalah-as-criticism work helped popularize academic awareness of Kabbalah for the general literary public.

## Connections
- Reads modern American religion as continuation of ancient Gnostic spirituality — explicit lineage from the Nag Hammadi corpus (especially [[phase-4-001-gospel-of-thomas]]) into modern American religious imagination.
- Uses [[isaac-luria]]'s Kabbalah ([[phase-6-015-luria-vital-etz-chayyim]]) as a model for literary influence, importing the ancient kabbalistic vocabulary into late-20th-century literary theory.

## Disputes
- **"American Religion" as Gnosticism.** Academic religious-studies historians (Albanese 1993, Hatch, Noll) have generally judged Bloom's identification of American evangelicalism with ancient Gnosticism as a literary-suggestive overreach: the historical-genetic claim is unsupported; the structural-analogy claim is weak. Bloom is best read as a *literary* critic of American religion, not a historian of it.
- **Specialist standing in religious studies.** Bloom worked outside the academic discipline of religious studies; his religious writing is Tier 3 essayistic rather than Tier 2 disciplinary scholarship. The vault flags this distinction.
