# Epicurus

## Identity
Epicurus of Samos (-341 to -270) founded one of the four great Hellenistic philosophical schools (alongside Stoicism, Platonism, Skepticism), the **Garden** (*Kepos*) in Athens. His philosophy combines (a) **Democritean atomism**: the cosmos consists of atoms moving in the void, no providence, gods exist but in the intermundia and do not concern themselves with human affairs; (b) **hedonism**: pleasure (especially mental tranquility — *ataraxia*) is the goal of life; (c) **death-is-nothing-to-us**: since soul dissolves with body, death is not an experience and not to be feared; (d) **friendship**: practical happiness depends on a circle of philosophical friends.

## Historical evidence
Well-documented despite the near-total loss of his ~300 books. Three Letters (to Herodotus on physics, Pythocles on celestial phenomena, Menoeceus on ethics), the Principal Doctrines (40 maxims), and the Vatican Sayings survive complete via Diogenes Laertius and the medieval manuscript tradition. The Herculaneum papyri (carbonized in the Vesuvius eruption of +79 and slowly being recovered with modern imaging) contain fragments of his lost *On Nature*. Lucretius's *De Rerum Natura* (~-50) is a full Latin poetic exposition of his physics.

For this vault: Epicurus matters as the *anti-providential* alternative within Hellenistic philosophy — explicitly rejecting the Platonic-Stoic-providentialist view that grounded most later religious philosophy. **Christianity's hostility to Epicureanism** is one of its defining philosophical orientations; "Epicurean" became a term of abuse in patristic literature for any denial of divine providence. The Talmud's *apikoros* (heretic) etymologically derives from Epicurus's name. Modern Western secular philosophy (Gassendi, Hobbes, the Enlightenment) recovers Epicureanism as a serious alternative.
