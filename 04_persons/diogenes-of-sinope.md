# Diogenes of Sinope

## Identity

Diogenes of Sinope (c. 412–323 BCE) is the archetypal Cynic and, after Socrates, among the most famous of the "philosophers who lived their philosophy." Born in the Black Sea colony of Sinope, where his father Hicesias was a banker or mint-official, he was exiled after a scandal over *defacing the currency* (*parachattein to nomisma*) — a phrase the tradition turned into his philosophical motto: to *deface the common coin* of convention (*nomos*) and revalue life according to nature (*physis*). He settled in Athens and later Corinth, owned nothing, slept in a large ceramic storage-jar (*pithos* — the "barrel" of legend), begged for food, and performed every bodily function in public as a deliberate assault on *aidōs* (shame). He wrote nothing that securely survives; he is known entirely through anecdote, above all Diogenes Laertius *Lives* VI.20-81.

He embraced the insult *kyōn* ("dog") — the source of the name *Cynic* (*kynikos*, "dog-like") — because the dog lives shamelessly by nature, guards what is good, and bites what is false.

## In the Cynic tradition

Diogenes survives as a cycle of *chreiai* — pointed sayings and deeds — rather than as a body of doctrine. The load-bearing episodes each dramatize a Cynic commitment:

- **The jar, the cloak, the begging-bowl** — reducing possessions to nothing (*autarkeia*, self-sufficiency); throwing away his last cup on seeing a child drink from cupped hands.
- **The lamp in daylight** — walking the Agora with a lit lamp "looking for a human being" (*anthrōpon zētō*), an indictment of a city full of people but empty of the genuinely human.
- **"Stand out of my sunlight"** — his reply to Alexander the Great (see [[alexander-the-great]]), who stood over him offering any favor (Diog. Laert. VI.38; Plutarch, *Alexander* 14). Alexander is said to have answered, "If I were not Alexander, I would be Diogenes."
- **Defacing the currency** — the biographical scandal reread as the philosophical program: revalue the counterfeit values of convention.
- **"A citizen of the world" (*kosmopolitēs*)** — his answer to where he was from (Diog. Laert. VI.63), the earliest attested use of the word.
- **Sold into slavery** — captured by pirates and sold at Corinth to Xeniades, he reportedly told the auctioneer to announce "a master for sale," and became tutor to Xeniades' sons.

The line ran on through his pupil Crates of Thebes and Crates' wife Hipparchia of Maroneia, and thereafter as a recurring itinerant-ascetic type across the Greco-Roman world. Menippus of Gadara gave the movement its literary form (Menippean satire), and Dio Chrysostom's *Diogenes* orations and Lucian's dialogues kept the figure alive as a literary mask into the Roman imperial period.

## Cross-tradition significance

**The seed the Stoa grew from (secure transmission).** Diogenes stands at the head of one of the best-attested filiations in Greek philosophy: his pupil Crates taught [[zeno-of-citium]], who founded [[philosophy-stoicism]]; ancient doxography places Cynicism at the head of the Stoic "succession" (Diog. Laert. VI.15, VII.2-4). The Stoics inherited and domesticated the Cynic core — *live according to nature*, virtue as the only good, the invulnerable sage, *askēsis*, cosmopolitanism — reclassifying rejected conventional goods as "preferred indifferents." Later Stoics called Cynicism "a short road to virtue," and Epictetus' *Discourses* III.22 idealizes the true Cynic as "God's scout and messenger." This is documented descent, not conjectured parallel (Goulet-Cazé in Branham & Goulet-Cazé 1996). See the hub [[philosophy-cynicism]].

**Cosmopolitanism.** Diogenes' *kosmopolitēs* (Diog. Laert. VI.63) is the documented origin of the Hellenistic idea that the wise belong to the cosmos rather than to a single *polis* — an idea the Stoa systematized and that fed, via Philo and Paul, universalizing strands of later Mediterranean thought. Developed at [[cosmopolitanism]].

**Socratic descent (contested pedigree).** The tradition makes Diogenes the philosophical grandson of [[socrates]] through Antisthenes, and Plato's reported epithet "Socrates gone mad" fixes the relation: the Socratic project of self-examination and truth-telling pushed past irony into shameless public provocation. But the Antisthenes link is doxographic convention, and Dudley (1937) and others suspect it was retrofitted to give Cynicism a Socratic ancestry — Diogenes may be the real origin. Record the descent as tradition, not certain biography.

**Diogenes and Zhuangzi (independent convergence).** The antinomian sage of philosophical Daoism who mocks rank, refuses office, and lives by nature against Confucian ritual is a standing comparandum: both deploy shocking anecdote and reductio-by-lifestyle against social convention, both prize spontaneity and self-sufficiency over status. Zhuangzi and Diogenes are exact contemporaries (4th c. BCE) with no possible contact — a textbook case of typological convergence on the "natural sage versus artificial convention" pattern, valuable precisely because transmission is excluded (developed at [[philosophy-cynicism]] and [[philosophy-daoism-philosophical]]).

**Ascetic renunciation (structural parallel, contested transmission).** Diogenes' *askēsis* — voluntary poverty, itinerancy, the begging-bowl, indifference to the body — is a clear Greek philosophical anticipation of the trans-traditional renunciant ideal organized at [[monastic-asceticism]]. A genealogical link to the wandering early-Christian preacher and thence the desert fathers (the "Cynic Jesus" hypothesis) has been proposed but is not established; the mainstream reads the resemblance as typological, the holy-poor-itinerant pattern arising independently. Treat as parallel with possible-but-unproven contact, not descent.

## Disputes

- **The historical Diogenes is largely irrecoverable.** A large share of the tradition is later invention — *chreiai* accreted over centuries and retold to make a point. Claims about "what Diogenes did or taught" are reports of a legend as much as of a life; even the storage-jar and the synchronized death-with-Alexander are literary as much as biographical.
- **Founder or exemplar?** Whether Cynicism's true founder is Antisthenes or Diogenes is unresolved (Dudley 1937; Goulet-Cazé 1996). The vault types Diogenes as the *founding exemplar*: the figure the movement organized itself around, whatever the doxographic pedigree.
- **Did he write anything?** A *Republic*, tragedies, and letters are ascribed to him (Diog. Laert. VI.80), but their authenticity was doubted in antiquity and none survives; he is best treated as an author of deeds, not texts.
