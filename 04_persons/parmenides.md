# Parmenides of Elea

## Identity
Parmenides of Elea (~-515 to ~-440) was the founder of the Eleatic school of Presocratic philosophy and the most important Greek metaphysician before Plato. Elea was a Greek colony on the Tyrrhenian coast of southern Italy (modern Velia). His one work, the philosophical poem *On Nature*, presents itself as a divine revelation to the philosopher by an unnamed goddess: the goddess sets out the *Way of Truth* (*alētheia*) — that what-is *is*, and that what-is-not is unthinkable and unsayable — and contrasts it with the *Way of Opinion* (*doxa*) — the merely apparent world of generation, change, and plurality, which mortals foolishly take to be real. The doctrine is the most radical statement of monistic-eternal-Being in ancient philosophy, and provoked the elaborate developments of his followers (Zeno's paradoxes, Melissus's articulation) and of his major opponents (the atomists Leucippus and Democritus; Empedocles's plurality; Plato's analysis in the *Sophist*).

## Historical evidence
Sources: the poem itself (~160 lines preserved as DK28 fragments, principally in Sextus Empiricus and Simplicius); Plato's *Parmenides* dialogue (which features a fictional meeting between the young Socrates and an elderly Parmenides at Athens, datable in the dramatic frame to ~-450); Aristotle's references in *Metaphysics* I; Diogenes Laertius IX; later doxographical sources. Plato's *Parmenides* says Parmenides visited Athens with Zeno around -450; the historicity of this visit has been variously assessed. Diogenes reports Parmenides legislated for Elea (cited the legal tradition there as the *Parmenidean laws*); the report is plausible but not independently attested.

## Works
*On Nature* (a single hexameter philosophical poem, of which ~160 lines survive). The structure: (a) the *Proem* — the goddess receives the philosopher and announces the program; (b) the *Way of Truth* — what-is *is*, what-is-not *is-not*, fundamental ontology; (c) the *Way of Opinion* — a cosmology of the manifest world organized around the two principles of Light and Night, of which Parmenides says explicitly that he gives it not as true but as "the seeming opinions of mortals." The interpretation of the *Way of Opinion* is one of the central puzzles of Parmenides studies. Modern editions: Coxon 2009 (definitive textual edition); Palmer 2009; Mansfeld 1964.

## Role in the tradition
Parmenides is the single most consequential ancient Greek metaphysician before Plato. His thesis that *what-is is and what-is-not is not* makes generation, perishing, change, and plurality philosophically problematic; the subsequent history of pre-Socratic philosophy is largely an attempt to vindicate or to escape Parmenidean monism. Zeno's paradoxes defend Parmenides against the absurdities of motion-and-plurality; Melissus generalizes Parmenidean Being to infinity; the atomists, Empedocles, and Anaxagoras devise pluralistic ontologies that can accommodate change without violating Parmenides's prohibition on what-is-not. Plato's *Parmenides* and *Sophist* present a sustained engagement: the *Sophist*'s analysis of *not-being as difference* is the major ancient philosophical solution to the Parmenidean puzzle. Aristotle's *Physics* I.8 explicitly responds.

Through Plato and the Neoplatonic tradition, Parmenides becomes the philosophical ancestor of all monistic and apophatic ontology in Western philosophy. The *eternal-being beyond change* schema is genealogically Parmenidean.

## Disputes
- **The status of the *Way of Opinion*.** Why does Parmenides present an elaborate cosmology if it is explicitly *not true*? Major modern interpretations: (a) the *Doxa* is the best possible cosmology of mortal seeming, given as instruction so the philosopher can recognize and dismiss it (traditional); (b) it is a *modal-distinction* exercise — what would have to be true if reality *did* include plurality and change (Owen 1960); (c) it sets out two ways of thinking about the world, both philosophical, both partially correct (Mourelatos 1970); (d) it is genuinely cosmologically informative but applies to the appearance-realm only, not to ultimate Being (Palmer 2009).
- **Strict monism vs. modal monism.** Whether Parmenides denies plurality and change *outright* (strict monism: only one thing exists, immutable) or *predicatively* (modal monism: any genuine reality must be eternal and immutable). Palmer 2009 defends the modal reading; older readings (Burnet, Kirk-Raven) press the strict.
- **Kingsley's shamanic-mystical reading.** Peter Kingsley (1999 *In the Dark Places of Wisdom*; 2002 *Reality*) reframes Parmenides as a mystical-shamanic *iatromantis* figure whose poem is initiatory religious literature, not analytical philosophy. The reading is rejected by mainstream Parmenides scholarship: A.A. Long's review (*Common Knowledge* 13 (2007): 478–480) raises serious philological objections; Palmer 2009 treats Kingsley's reading as ungrounded. Kingsley's work has wide non-academic readership but is Tier 4 in source-integrity terms; cite alongside Long for balance.
- **Dating.** The Olympiad-69 floruit (-504/-501) in Diogenes is sometimes preferred over Plato's *Parmenides* dramatic dating (-450 for the Athens meeting); the disagreement determines whether Parmenides could have been a contemporary of the young Socrates.
