# Jean-François Champollion

## Identity
Jean-François Champollion (1790–1832) was a French linguist and historian who achieved the decipherment of Egyptian hieroglyphics — the act of scholarship that unlocked 3,000 years of silent Egyptian civilization and founded Egyptology as a modern discipline. Born in Figeac, France, he was a prodigy: by age 16 he had presented a paper to the Grenoble Academy arguing that Coptic was the direct descendant of the ancient Egyptian language, a methodological insight that would prove essential to the decipherment. He died in Paris at 41, probably of a stroke, having spent only a few years working in Egypt itself (1828–1829).

## The Decipherment (September 14, 1822)

On September 14, 1822, Champollion burst into his brother Jacque-Joseph's Paris office, threw his notes on the table, declared "Je tiens mon affaire!" ("I've got it!"), and fainted. He had recognized that Egyptian hieroglyphic writing was neither purely symbolic (as Horapollo and Athanasius Kircher had claimed) nor purely phonetic, but a mixed phonetic-ideographic system: some signs represent sounds (phonograms), others represent concepts (ideograms/logograms), and some serve as determinatives. Crucially, the phonetic signs could spell out foreign royal names in cartouches — a principle Thomas Young had partially grasped for demotic script — but Champollion extended this to the entire hieroglyphic system and demonstrated it worked for Egyptian names as well as foreign ones.

He announced the decipherment formally on September 27, 1822, in a letter to Bon-Joseph Dacier (*Lettre à M. Dacier*) read before the Académie des Inscriptions et Belles-Lettres. The systematic exposition followed in his *Précis du système hiéroglyphique* (1824).

## The Coptic Key

Champollion's decisive methodological advantage over all competitors was his mastery of Coptic — the final stage of the Egyptian language, written in a modified Greek alphabet and still used in the Coptic Christian liturgy. Coptic preserved the phonetic structure of ancient Egyptian with vowels visible (unlike the earlier hieroglyphic orthography, which largely suppressed vowels). By using Coptic to reconstruct how ancient Egyptian words might have sounded, Champollion could match phonograms to phonetic values with precision that Young — who did not know Coptic — could not achieve.

This means the Coptic Christian liturgical tradition — the survival of an ancient religious language in a late-antique Christian church — was the instrument that unlocked 3,000 years of pre-Christian Egyptian religion. ## MASSIVE WIN: Recovery of Lost Worlds

Champollion's decipherment is the vault's most dramatic "recovery of lost knowledge" event. Before September 1822, Egyptian religion was known only through the accounts of foreign observers: Herodotus (Book II — "Euterpe"), Diodorus Siculus (Bibliotheca Book I), Plutarch (*De Iside et Osiride*), and later Horapollo (*Hieroglyphica*) — all Greek or Roman, all filtered through foreign categories, none able to read the primary sources.

After 1822, the Egyptians could speak for themselves. Within a generation of the decipherment:
- The Pyramid Texts (c. 2400–2300 BCE) — the oldest religious corpus in the world — became readable: [[phase-1-002-pyramid-texts]]
- The Coffin Texts and Book of the Dead entered systematic philological edition: [[phase-1-010-book-of-the-dead]]
- The Great Hymn to the Aten (Akhenaten's composition) was first read in its original hieroglyphic form: [[phase-1-011-great-hymn-to-aten]]
- The Amarna Letters were identifiable as a diplomatic archive: [[phase-1-012-amarna-letters]]
- The Amduat and Books of the Underworld were opened to scholarship: [[phase-1-036-amduat]]

Every Egyptian-language node in the vault — every Phase 1 Egyptian document — exists in modern accessible form because of one scholar who taught himself to read a dead script using a living liturgical language as his key.

**The key structural finding:** the Coptic Christian church — a tradition that survived from late antiquity precisely because it was embedded in Christian liturgical practice — was the archive that made the pre-Christian Egyptian religious world legible again. Christianity preserved the key that unlocked its own predecessor religion.

## Young vs. Champollion

A long-running Anglo-French priority dispute. Thomas Young (1773–1829), the English polymath, made critical contributions between 1814 and 1819: he recognized the Rosetta Stone's demotic section as related to the hieroglyphic; he identified that cartouches enclosed royal names; he decoded several sound-values for Ptolemy's and Berenice's names. But Young thought hieroglyphic was essentially symbolic-ideographic with only a limited phonetic element for foreign names — he could not generalize the phonetic principle to the full system, partly because he lacked Coptic.

Champollion generalized the phonetic principle to all hieroglyphic writing and cracked the system. Modern consensus: both contributed, the decisive systematic breakthrough was Champollion's. The British-French rivalry gave the dispute a nationalist flavor that persists in historiography.
