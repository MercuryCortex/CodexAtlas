# Jacob Frank

## Identity
Polish-Podolian Jewish messianic claimant and antinomian sectarian (1726–1791), self-proclaimed continuation of Shabbatai Tzvi and the most extreme post-Sabbatean heterodox leader. Born Yaakov Leibowicz in Podolia; spent his early adulthood in Ottoman territories (Smyrna, Salonica) absorbing Donmeh-Sabbatean teaching; returned to Podolia 1755 to lead a sectarian group that the Polish rabbinic establishment denounced as Sabbatean. Following the 1759 Lwów Disputation (in which Frank's followers debated Polish rabbis under Catholic-Church auspices), Frank led a mass conversion to Catholicism. He was nonetheless imprisoned by Polish-Catholic authorities at Częstochowa monastery for thirteen years (1760–1773) on suspicion of insincerity; freed by Russian intervention; spent his final years at the courts of Brünn (Brno) and Offenbach as a self-styled aristocrat with a substantial movement of titled European Jewish-converso followers. The Frankist movement gradually dissolved through the 19th century, but elements of its antinomian theology percolated through Central European Jewish culture (Adam Mickiewicz's mother was reportedly Frankist; the movement intersects with the early Polish Enlightenment).

## Historical evidence
Substantial: Frankist and anti-Frankist Polish-Hebrew sources, Catholic ecclesiastical records (the Lwów Disputation, the Częstochowa imprisonment), the *Słowa Pańskie* manuscript collection, Maciejko's exhaustive reconstruction.

## Works
*Słowa Pańskie* (The Words of the Lord) — a sayings/teachings collection compiled by Frank's secretaries from his oral teaching, surviving in multiple manuscript versions. Modern critical edition / translation work by Paweł Maciejko.

## Role in the tradition
Frank is the most extreme antinomian descendant of Sabbateanism. The theology of redemption through transgression, the rejection of normative halakhic life, the mass conversion as messianic strategy, and the Holy Trinity reinterpreted in Kabbalistic-Sabbatean terms together make Frankism the doctrinal endpoint of the Lurianic-Sabbatean line. Scholem's "Redemption Through Sin" essay places Frankism on the trajectory from Lurianic Kabbalah to Jewish modernity.
