# Jakob Böhme

## Identity
Lutheran cobbler-mystic and Christian theosopher of Görlitz in Upper Lusatia (1575–1624); founder of the Boehmian theosophical tradition that runs through Pietism, English Boehmenism, German Idealism (Hegel called him "the first German philosopher"), and Russian religious philosophy. Born to a Lutheran peasant family near Görlitz; apprenticed and established as a master shoemaker; experienced a foundational 1600 vision (the gestalt of the cosmos revealed in a polished pewter dish); composed *Aurora* in 1612 (manuscript circulation only, confiscated by Görlitz pastor Gregor Richter, writing-prohibition imposed); observed the prohibition for seven years; broke it in 1619 with *De tribus principiis*; wrote prolifically over the remaining five years of his life. Died at Görlitz 1624; the funeral was contested by Lutheran authorities. His circle of patrons and disciples preserved his manuscripts; his works were printed posthumously beginning Amsterdam 1634 and were widely disseminated in 17th-c. Protestant Europe, especially England (where the Philadelphian Society of John Pordage and Jane Lead carried the tradition).

## Historical evidence
Densely documented: his own substantial corpus (~30 works), the Görlitz town council records and Pastor Richter's denunciations, contemporary correspondence with his patrons (Karl von Endern, Abraham von Sommerfeld), the early Amsterdam printings.

## Works
*Aurora* (1612), *De tribus principiis* (1619), *De triplici vita hominis* (1620), *De incarnatione Verbi* (1620), *De signatura rerum* (1622), *Mysterium Magnum* (1623), *De electione gratiae* (1623), *Theoscopia* (1624). Three editions of his collected works: Henrik Beets (Amsterdam 1675), Johann Georg Gichtel (1682, 1730).

## Role in the tradition
Böhme is the central early-modern figure of Christian theosophy. His doctrines of *Ungrund* (the divine groundless), divine self-realization through opposition, Sophia, and the seven *Eigenschaften* shaped Pietism, German Idealism (Hegel, Schelling), English Romanticism (William Law, Coleridge, Blake's possible indirect engagement), Russian religious philosophy (Solovyov, Berdyaev, Bulgakov), and 20th-century Jungian psychology.
