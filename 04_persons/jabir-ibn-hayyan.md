# Jabir ibn Hayyan (Geber)

## Identity
Traditionally identified as Abū Mūsā Jābir ibn Ḥayyān al-Azdī, an 8th-century Arabic-language alchemist and natural philosopher associated with Kufa and the Abbasid court. **Modern philological scholarship (Kraus 1942) established that the vast 'Jabirian corpus' is largely the work of a 9th-10th-century circle of authors writing under his name, with Ismaili and proto-Ismaili intellectual connections** — Jabir himself as a single 8th-c. historical individual is reduced to a probable nucleus around which the corpus accreted. The corpus is, however, historically real and consequential: it is the **principal vehicle of Hermetic-alchemical material from the late-antique Greek world into the medieval Arabic world**, and from there into the medieval Latin world (via 12th-13th-c. Arabic-Latin translation) and the Renaissance Hermetic-alchemical synthesis.

Jabir / the Jabirian corpus is one of the two **load-bearing Arabic transmission nodes** for the entire Western Hermetic-alchemical tradition (the other being the Pseudo-Apollonian *Sirr al-Khalīqa*, the Arabic vehicle for the [[phase-4-073-tabula-smaragdina|Tabula Smaragdina]]).

## Historical evidence
Substantial Arabic documentation:
- **Ibn al-Nadim's *Fihrist*** (~987 CE) — the foundational bibliographic survey of the Jabirian corpus. Ibn al-Nadim lists hundreds of works attributed to Jabir and treats him as historical.
- **Numerous manuscript witnesses** of Jabirian works in Arabic libraries from the 10th c. onward; major holdings at the British Library, BNF Paris, Süleymaniye Istanbul, Cairo libraries.
- **Latin translations** beginning in the 12th c. — *Liber Geberis*, *Summa Perfectionis*, etc. — established 'Geber' as the foremost Arabic authority in medieval Latin alchemy.
- **Kraus 1942** is the foundational modern philological treatment; established that the corpus is plural-authored and 9th-10th-c. in its developed form.

## Works
The Jabirian corpus is conventionally divided (per Kraus's analysis) into thematic groups:
- The ***112 Books*** — early or core texts.
- The ***70 Books*** — *Kitāb al-Sabʿīn* — including the *Book of the Balance* (*Kitāb al-Mīzān*) elaborating the quantitative-qualities theory.
- The ***10 Books of Rectification***.
- The ***Books of the Balance*** (~144 attributed texts).
- The ***Books of the 500*** (the most speculative-philosophical layer).
- The ***Latin Geber corpus*** — including the 13th-century *Summa Perfectionis Magisterii* by Paul of Taranto (Newman 1991 established the actual authorship).

## Role in the tradition

- **Foundational Arabic alchemical synthesis.** The Jabirian corpus integrates: Hellenistic Greek alchemy (Zosimos, Maria the Jewess, the Greek Hermetic tradition), Indian alchemical material, Iranian / Zoroastrian-medical material, and Aristotelian natural philosophy (the four qualities / four elements substrate). This is *the* synthesis-event that creates "medieval alchemy" as a continuous scientific-philosophical tradition.
- **Quantitative proto-chemistry.** The *mīzān* theory — that substances can be characterized by precise numerical ratios of the four Aristotelian qualities (hot, cold, wet, dry) — is an *unsuccessful but conceptually important* proto-quantitative chemistry. It anticipates the later quantitative shift in chemistry by ~1,000 years.
- **Sulphur-mercury theory of metals.** Becomes canonical: metals are composed of mercury (representing metallic quality) and sulphur (representing combustibility), in different proportions. This theory dominates Latin alchemy through Newton.
- **Technical-chemical vocabulary.** Most of the basic chemical and equipment vocabulary of Western Europe — *alembic, alkali, alcohol, elixir, retort, alembic, naphtha, borax, antimony, soda* — comes through Latin translation of Arabic alchemical Arabic, with the Jabirian corpus a principal source.
- **Ismaili-adjacent intellectual milieu.** Kraus 1942 demonstrated that the Jabirian corpus is intellectually adjacent to the **Ismaili / Brethren of Purity** (*Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ*) circles of the 9th-10th c. — meaning the corpus has Shia-esoteric philosophical resonances, which fed back into European reception of "Arabic alchemy" as an esoteric science.

## Disputes
- **Did Jabir exist as a single historical person?** Kraus 1942 reduces the historical Jabir to a probable 8th-c. nucleus; the corpus is largely 9th-10th c. and plural-authored. Some modern Arabic scholarship (Ahmad Y. al-Hassan) defends a more substantial historical Jabir; the Kraus position remains consensus.
- **Latin 'Geber' = Arabic Jabir?** Newman 1991 demonstrated they are different: the *Summa Perfectionis Magisterii* (the principal "Latin Geber" text) is by 13th-c. Italian Franciscan Paul of Taranto, who took the name "Geber" to capitalize on the authority of the Arabic tradition.
- **Ismaili affiliation.** Kraus's identification of the corpus with Ismaili-adjacent intellectual circles is largely accepted but the specifics of the affiliation remain debated.
