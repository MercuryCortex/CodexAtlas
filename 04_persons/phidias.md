# Phidias

## Identity
**Phidias** (~490–430 BCE) was the greatest sculptor of Classical Greece and the artistic director of the **Periclean building program** on the Athenian Acropolis. He is credited with designing and executing two works described by ancient sources as among the supreme achievements of human art, both counted among the **Seven Wonders of the World**:

1. **Athena Parthenos** — a colossal gold-and-ivory (*chryselephantine*) statue, approximately 12 meters tall, housed inside the Parthenon (~447–432 BCE). Athena was depicted standing, helmeted, holding Nike (Victory) in one hand and a spear in the other, with her shield at her side. No original survives; only Roman copies and ancient descriptions.

2. **Zeus at Olympia** — a seated chryselephantine statue approximately 13 meters tall in the Temple of Zeus at Olympia (~435 BCE). Ancient sources regarded this as Phidias' supreme achievement. Quintilian (*Institutio Oratoria* 12.10.9) wrote that the Zeus "added something to traditional religion" — meaning the statue was so powerful that it appeared to make Zeus more real, more present, more worshippable than before. The statue was eventually transferred to Constantinople, where it is believed to have been destroyed in a fire, ~475 CE.

## Trial and death
Phidias was prosecuted in Athens on two charges: (1) **embezzlement** — allegedly stealing gold intended for the Athena Parthenos (Pericles' political enemies used the trial as a proxy attack on Pericles); (2) **impiety** — allegedly including his own portrait and Pericles' portrait on the shield of Athena Parthenos. According to some ancient sources, Phidias died in prison (~430 BCE); others say he was exiled and died at Olympia.

## Context
Phidias worked within the commission of **Pericles**, the Athenian statesman who oversaw the Acropolis building program. The political and religious dimensions were inseparable: the Parthenon and its statues were simultaneously an act of Athenian imperial self-assertion and a genuine act of religious devotion to Athena as protector of the city. The chryselephantine technique (gold + ivory) was not arbitrary aesthetics — gold and ivory were the materials of maximum purity and luminosity available to the ancient world, indexing divine light and transcendence.

## MASSIVE-wins connections
- **Phidias' Zeus at Olympia → Constantine's Constantinople**: The Zeus statue was reportedly transferred to Constantinople in the 4th–5th century CE; some historians have proposed (with limited documentation) that its iconographic influence — the seated, majestic divine figure — fed into the development of the **Christ Pantokrator** iconography, which replaced Zeus as the seated divine king. This remains a scholarly hypothesis.
- **Chryselephantine technique as sacral art**: Gold (solar, divine light) + ivory (purity, white light) = materials that index transcendence. The same logic recurs in later Christian use of gold mosaic in Byzantine church decoration and gold in icon painting — the sacral meaning of the material, not just the image.
- **"Added something to traditional religion" (Quintilian)**: The claim that great art can intensify religious experience, make a deity more *present* and *real*, is a cross-tradition claim. The same was said of Michelangelo's Sistine Chapel ceiling and of icons in Byzantine Christianity — art as theophanic technology.
