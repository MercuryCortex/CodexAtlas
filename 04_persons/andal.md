# Andal

## Identity
The only female among the twelve Tamil *Āḻvārs* — the foundational devotional poets of South Indian Vaishnavism (~6th–9th centuries) — and the most beloved woman saint of pre-modern Tamil Hindu tradition. Per traditional account, found as an infant in a *tulasī* (sacred basil) garden by Periyāḻvār (Viṣṇucitta), the temple priest at Srivilliputhur; raised as his daughter; secretly wore the garlands intended for Viṣṇu before they were offered (whence her epithet *Sūḍikkodutta Nāccīyār*); refused human marriage, taking Viṣṇu as her sole husband; finally received by Viṣṇu at Srirangam in her bridal form per the canonical narrative. Composed her two works in her early teens per tradition; the work itself displays a fully developed Tamil literary sophistication.

## Historical evidence
- The Tamil Vaishnava community's continuous transmission from the 8th–9th c.
- Inscriptional evidence at Srivilliputhur attests temple worship of Andal from at least the 11th c.
- The *Nālāyira Divya Prabandham* — the canonical four-thousand-verse anthology of the Alvar canon — was compiled by Nāthamuni (~9th c.) and includes both of Andal's works in their canonical form.
- Andal is the only Alvar who is also a *paratattva* (ultimate-reality) figure in the Sri Vaishnava theology — a unique status reflecting her early-medieval cult.

## Works
- ***Tiruppāvai*** — 30 Tamil verses; the cosmic-erotic *pāvai* (girls' vow) ritual cycle in which the speaker, with her companions, observes a month-long Mārkaḻi-vow to win Krishna as bridegroom. Among the most-recited Hindu liturgical texts globally; the principal devotional text of Tamil Vaishnavism. The *Tiruppāvai-Tiruvembāvai* festival pairs it with the Saiva *Tiruvempāvai* of [[manikkavacakar]] each Margazhi.
- ***Nācciyār Tirumoḻi*** — 143 verses in 14 sections; more elaborate, more erotic, more virtuosic. Includes the famous "Vāraṇam Āyiram" — Andal's dream of marriage to Viṣṇu, recited at Tamil Vaishnava weddings.

## Role in the tradition
- **Tamil Vaishnava devotional canon**: Andal's works are part of the *Divya Prabandham*, the Tamil "Veda" of Sri Vaishnavism, treated by Ramanuja and his school as on a par with the Sanskrit Veda in soteriological authority — a major early-medieval claim about the legitimacy of vernacular scripture.
- **Sri Vaishnavism**: Andal is identified by the school as an incarnation of Bhūdevī (Vishnu's Earth-consort) and is a *paratattva* — an ultimate-reality figure, not merely a saint.
- **Tamil literature**: among the great vernacular voices of pre-modern India. Modern Tamil feminist and Dalit literary traditions have repeatedly reclaimed Andal as a model.

## Cross-tradition connections
- **Female bhakti voice across Hindu/Sufi/Christian devotional traditions**: Andal's poetics — the female devotee yearning for the divine Bridegroom — runs parallel to the Sufi *‘ishq-i ḥaqīqī* literature (Rumi, the Indo-Persian Sufis), to the Christian *Song of Songs* commentary tradition (Bernard of Clairvaux, Mechthild of Magdeburg, Teresa of Avila), and to later North Indian female bhakti (Mirabai). Hawley and Wulff (1996, *Devī*) and Dehejia (1990) place Andal in the comparative-religion category of "female mystic bridal poets." The structural pattern crosses traditions — a genuine cross-tradition motif rather than direct borrowing.
- **Tamil Bhakti as the historical seed of the pan-Indian Bhakti movement**: the 9th-c. compilation of the Tamil Alvars and Nayanars is the source of the South-to-North bhakti diffusion thesis (Hawley 2015, *A Storm of Songs*) — the Tamil bhakti tradition is the deep root from which medieval and early-modern North Indian bhakti (Kabir, Mirabai, Tulsidas, Chaitanya) descends.

## Disputes
- **Dates**: traditional 8th c.; some scholarship places her closer to the 9th c.
- **Historicity vs. cult-construction**: the Bhūdevī-incarnation identification is theological, not historical; the historical kernel — a Tamil woman composing two surviving works in the 8th–9th c. — is firm.
