# Caesar Augustus

## Identity
Gaius Julius Caesar Octavianus — born Gaius Octavius in 63 BCE, died 14 CE — was the great-nephew and adopted son of [[julius-caesar]]. After Caesar's assassination in 44 BCE he won the civil wars of the Second Triumvirate, defeated Antony and Cleopatra at [[event-battle-of-actium-31-bce|Actium]] (31 BCE), and on 16 January 27 BCE accepted from the Senate the title *Augustus* ("revered"), inaugurating what later historiography calls the Principate. He reigned forty-one years, restructured every level of Roman political-religious life, fixed the legions at the frontiers (creating the *Pax Romana*), and bequeathed an imperial system that lasted (in continuous variant forms East and West) until 1453.

## Historical evidence
Saturated. Augustus's own [[phase-3-097-res-gestae-divi-augusti|Res Gestae Divi Augusti]] (the deeds of the divine Augustus) — his autobiographical inscription, inscribed on bronze pillars at his Mausoleum in Rome and copied throughout the eastern provinces; the most complete surviving copy (the Monumentum Ancyranum) is incised on the walls of the Temple of Augustus and Rome at Ancyra (modern Ankara). Suetonius's *Divus Augustus* (early 2nd c.) is the canonical biography. Tacitus's *Annals* I covers the succession to Tiberius. Cassius Dio devotes twelve books (45–56). Numismatic and epigraphic evidence is enormous; the Ara Pacis (dedicated 9 BCE) is the surviving canonical visual statement of the Augustan religious-political program.

## Role in the tradition

### The imperial cult — beginning of the *divi filius* ideology
Augustus's adoptive father [[julius-caesar]] was deified by senatorial decree in 42 BCE (*Divus Iulius*). Augustus consequently ruled as **Divi filius** — "Son of the Divine" — a title struck on coinage from the 30s BCE onward. He did not (in Rome itself, during his life) accept worship as a living god; in the Greek-speaking East provinces, however, the cult of *Roma et Augustus* was established with his approval from 29 BCE (first at Pergamum and Nicomedia for the Greek non-Roman population). After his death the Senate formally deified him (*Divus Augustus*) and the imperial cult became the religious-political backbone of the empire (Price 1984; Galinsky 2005). This is the political-theological substrate against which early Christianity will articulate its own theology of a *divi filius*.

### "Son of the Divine" — substrate for Christian "Son of God" titulature
Christian titulature of Jesus as *huios theou* (Son of God) emerges in a Greek-Roman world in which *divi filius* (Greek: *theou huios*) had been an imperial honorific for seventy years. New Testament scholarship has documented the deliberate counter-imperial register of phrases like *euangelion* ("good news" — Roman usage: imperial decree / accession-news; Christian usage: the news of Jesus), *kyrios* (lord — imperial usage and Christian usage), and *soter* (savior — imperial usage and Christian usage). See [[doctrine-incarnation]], [[julius-caesar]], [[phase-3-097-res-gestae-divi-augusti]]; Crossan & Reed 2004 (*In Search of Paul*); Wright 2013 (*Paul and the Faithfulness of God*).

### Luke 2:1 — the "decree from Augustus"
[[phase-3-018-luke-acts|Luke 2:1]] frames the Christian Nativity by Augustus's decree: "a decree went out from Caesar Augustus that all the world should be enrolled." The historicity of the specific census-at-the-time-of-Quirinius is contested (Quirinius's actual Syrian governorship is dated to 6 CE per Josephus *Ant.* 18.1–2, ten years after Herod's 4 BCE death). Whether or not the chronology can be harmonized (Pearson 1999; Brown 1993 *Birth of the Messiah* takes the discrepancy as decisive), the Lucan framing remains historically load-bearing: Christianity's foundational text places the Nativity inside Augustan imperial chronology, anchoring the new dispensation in the *Pax Romana* it will eventually transform.

### Reorganization of the Roman religious establishment
Augustus was *pontifex maximus* from 12 BCE (assuming the office after Lepidus's death). He restored 82 temples in Rome (per *Res Gestae* 20), rebuilt the office of the Vestal Virgins, revived archaic priesthoods (the Arval Brethren, the Lupercalia), and undertook the moral-legislative reform of the Lex Iulia / Lex Papia Poppaea (marriage and adultery legislation). The combined effect was the deliberate fusion of religious restoration, moral reform, and imperial legitimacy — what Galinsky 1996 calls the Augustan cultural program.

### The *Pax Romana*
The Augustan settlement closes the doors of the Temple of Janus three times (peace), brings the legions to the frontiers, codifies provincial administration, and establishes a single legal-monetary-postal infrastructure across the Mediterranean basin. This is the political-economic substrate on which Christian missionary movement runs in the 1st century (Paul's letters travel imperial post-roads; the Mediterranean is one military-economic zone).

## Disputes
- **The Luke 2 census.** Whether the empire-wide enrollment Luke describes can be historically reconciled with Quirinius's known 6 CE census remains contested (Brown 1993 holds the divergence; some apologetic harmonizations propose two-census models).
- **Was the Principate a 'restoration of the Republic' or its disguised monarchy?** Tacitus and Cassius Dio frame the Principate as monarchy-with-Republican-vocabulary; Augustus's own *Res Gestae* frames it as restoration. Modern scholarship (Syme 1939 *Roman Revolution*; Galinsky 1996) sees the Augustan settlement as deliberate ambiguity — monarchy dressed in Republican forms.
- **Imperial-cult divinity claims.** Whether the Greek-Eastern *Sebastos*-cult during Augustus's life implied actual divine status or remained a civic honor is debated (Price 1984; Gradel 2002 *Emperor Worship and Roman Religion*); the *post mortem* deification is uncontested.
