# John Milton

## Biography

John Milton was born in London in 1608 into a Puritan household of comfortable means — his father was a scrivener and amateur composer. He was educated at St Paul's School and Christ's College Cambridge, where his scholarly brilliance was evident but his temperament contentious. A Grand Tour of Italy in 1638–1639 brought him into contact with the cultural centres of Catholic Europe; he met Galileo Galilei in Florence, a meeting he later recorded in *Areopagitica* (1644) as a symbol of intellectual liberty suppressed by inquisitorial censorship. Returning to England, Milton spent the 1640s and 1650s as a polemical prose writer — his *Areopagitica* remains the foundational English argument for freedom of the press. During the Commonwealth he served as Secretary for Foreign Tongues to the Council of State under Oliver Cromwell, writing Latin state correspondence and official defences of the regicide. Total blindness overtook him by 1652 — the date of his sonnet "When I consider how my light is spent" (Sonnet 19), one of the great lyric responses to physical catastrophe in English literature. From 1658 onward he dictated *Paradise Lost* to daughters and amanuenses, completing the greatest English epic blind. The Restoration of Charles II in 1660 briefly endangered his life; he was imprisoned, then placed under home arrest. He died in 1674.

## Works and intellectual formation

Milton was one of the most extensively read men of the seventeenth century. His formation drew on classical literature in the original languages — Homer, Virgil, Hesiod, Ovid, Plato — on the Hebrew Bible in Hebrew, on the Greek New Testament, on the Church Fathers, on rabbinic commentary, on Dante, on the Italian Renaissance, and on contemporary natural philosophy including Copernican astronomy, which is present in *Paradise Lost* through the ambiguous cosmological dialogue of Book 8 (Raphael declines to arbitrate between heliocentric and geocentric systems, preserving a deliberate ambiguity). His Puritanism was heterodox to a degree not publicly known in his lifetime: *De Doctrina Christiana*, discovered in the Public Record Office in 1823 and published in 1825, revealed that Milton denied the Trinity (the Son is subordinate to and derived from the Father — an Arian position), held mortalist views (the soul does not survive independently after death but is resurrected with the body), and defended a form of polygamy. These positions shape *Paradise Lost* in ways still debated: the poem's God is not the God of Calvinist double-predestination but a being who grants genuine free will because love requires freedom — a move that distances Milton from Calvinist orthodoxy and brings his theology closer to Arminianism and ultimately to a kind of voluntarist-libertarian theology with no close confessional parallel.

## Political theology

Milton's Satan in *Paradise Lost* is partly a portrait of Stuart tyranny read through the lens of Puritan republican theology: monarchy as the rebellion against God's proper republic of virtuous governance. The War in Heaven (Books 5–6) is simultaneously a cosmic event and a political allegory. God's abdication of direct rule over creation — governing through covenant and law rather than direct compulsion — mirrors the Cromwellian republican ideal of governance by consent and law. *Paradise Lost* (1667) was written and published in defeat: the Restoration had reversed the Puritan republican experiment, executed or driven into exile its principal defenders, and reimposed the monarchy Milton had spent two decades opposing in print. The poem's exploration of how paradise is lost through disobedience and can be rebuilt "within" (the final lines of Book 12 — "a paradise within thee, happier far") is Milton's political theology of defeat: the republic is not externally recoverable, but the virtuous soul can constitute its own interior republic. Christopher Hill (*Milton and the English Revolution*, 1977) reads *Paradise Lost* as Puritan revolutionary politics in epic form — not allegory but a transformation of political crisis into cosmic narrative.

## Influence

Milton's influence across the subsequent three centuries is structural, not incidental. William Blake (*The Marriage of Heaven and Hell*, 1793) announced that Milton "was of the Devil's party without knowing it" — Satan's energy is creative, God's law is repressive, and the poem's moral poles require inversion. Shelley (*Defence of Poetry*, 1821) declared Milton's Satan "the type of the highest perfection of moral and intellectual nature, and the most heroic subject that ever was afforded to the contemplation of the poet" — and *Prometheus Unbound* (1820) rewrites Milton's Satan as the defiant Prometheus freed from Zeus's tyranny. Mary Shelley (*Frankenstein, or the Modern Prometheus*, 1818) epigraphed from *Paradise Lost* ("Did I request thee, Maker, from my clay / To mould me Man?") and her creature reads *Paradise Lost* as an account of his own condition. The Romantic rehabilitation of Milton's Satan is complete: what Milton wrote as a warning became the Romantics' model of heroic defiance. The twentieth-century debate reprises the dispute at a higher scholarly level: C.S. Lewis (*A Preface to Paradise Lost*, 1942) — Satan is pathetically self-deceived and the poem condemns him; William Empson (*Milton's God*, 1961) — God is the villain and Milton's theology is morally incoherent. The poem continues to refuse resolution.
