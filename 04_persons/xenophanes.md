# Xenophanes of Colophon

## Identity
Xenophanes of Colophon (~-570 to ~-475) was a Presocratic philosopher, itinerant poet, and the earliest known Greek critic of Homeric and Hesiodic anthropomorphic theology. Born in Colophon (Ionian Asia Minor); fled west around -545 after the Persian conquest under Cyrus and Harpagus; spent the bulk of his long life as an itinerant rhapsode in Magna Graecia, including time at Elea (Velia in southern Italy) — Diogenes Laertius and others report he was Parmenides's teacher, a tradition many modern scholars find chronologically plausible but unproveable. His fragments include both philosophical theology (the critique of anthropomorphism, the one-god doctrine) and natural-philosophical speculation (proto-geological observations of marine fossils inland, evidence of past sea levels). The Diels-Kranz collection (DK21) preserves ~120 lines.

## Historical evidence
Sources: the fragments themselves (preserved chiefly in Clement of Alexandria, Sextus Empiricus, Diogenes Laertius, Hippolytus, and various scholia); Plato's *Sophist* 242d; Aristotle's *Metaphysics* I.5 (Xenophanes "gazed at the whole heaven and declared the One to be God"); Diogenes Laertius IX.18–20. Xenophanes himself dates his career in fragment B8: "Already there are seven and sixty years tossing my care about the land of Greece; from my birth there were twenty-five in addition to these" — establishing both his approximate age (92) and his long itinerant career.

## Works
The fragments are conventionally divided into *Elegies* (sympotic-philosophical poetry on right conduct, the gods, civic life) and *Silloi* (satirical hexameter verses attacking Homer, Hesiod, and other Greek religious-poetic figures); a *Foundation of Colophon* and a *Colonization of Elea* are reported but unattested. Whether Xenophanes wrote a distinct philosophical poem *On Nature* (analogous to Parmenides's) is contested: Aristotle's testimony and the natural-philosophical content of some fragments suggest yes; modern interpreters (Lesher 1992) are mixed.

## Role in the tradition
Xenophanes is the earliest known Greek thinker to articulate the *critique of anthropomorphism* in religious representation. Fragments B14–B16: "Mortals think the gods are born and have clothes, voices, and bodies of their own. But if oxen and horses and lions had hands and could draw with them and produce works of art as men do, horses would draw the forms of gods like horses, oxen like oxen, and each would make the gods' bodies like their own. The Ethiopians make their gods snub-nosed and black; the Thracians say theirs have grey eyes and red hair." B23–B24 articulate the positive doctrine: "One god, greatest among gods and men, not like mortals in body or in thought." The critique of anthropomorphism founds a tradition that runs through Plato's critique of poetic theology (*Republic* II), the Stoic allegorical reading of myth, Philo's allegorical exegesis of the Hebrew Bible, and the entire patristic-Jewish-Islamic tradition of negative theology (see [[via-negativa]], [[critique-of-idolatry]]).

He also makes substantial natural-philosophical contributions: the observation that marine fossils found inland (B33 implicit; reported by Hippolytus *Ref.* I.14) demonstrate past sea-level changes is among the earliest documented empirical-geological inferences; his epistemological fragment B34 — "no one knows the truth about the gods and all the things I speak of; even if one happened to say what was perfectly the case, he himself would not know it" — is sometimes called the first articulation of philosophical fallibilism.

## Disputes
- **Was Xenophanes a philosopher or a poet?** The 19th-c. tradition often classed Xenophanes as a primarily religious poet with philosophical asides; modern scholarship (Lesher 1992, Mourelatos, Graham) treats him as a substantial philosophical theologian and proto-natural-philosopher, founder of philosophical theology.
- **Eleatic founder?** Plato's *Sophist* 242d treats Xenophanes as the "founder of the Eleatic clan" that culminates in Parmenides. Aristotle's *Metaphysics* I.5 reports the same affiliation. Whether the affiliation is genealogical-textual (Parmenides studied with Xenophanes) or merely doxographical (later traditions grouped them as both monotheist-monists) is contested. The chronology *permits* the affiliation; the philosophical content of Xenophanes's *one god* is significantly different from Parmenides's *what-is*.
- **Monotheism, henotheism, or pantheism?** B23–B24 speak of "one god, greatest among gods and men" — the "greatest among gods" phrasing leaves room for henotheist rather than strict monotheist interpretation. Modern interpreters are divided.
- **Epistemological fragment B34.** Whether Xenophanes is a skeptic, a fallibilist, or a non-skeptic theological agnostic is debated (Hussey 1990, Lesher 1992).
