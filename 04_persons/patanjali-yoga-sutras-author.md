# Patanjali (compiler of the Yoga Sutras)

## Identity
The figure (or composite figure / school) to whom is attributed the *Yoga-Sūtra* — the four-chapter, 195-aphorism text that became the foundational scripture of the classical Yoga *darśana* and is the most influential single text in the entire global yoga tradition. The Yoga-Sūtra's date is contested across a range from ~2nd century BCE to ~4th–5th century CE; the recent text-critical scholarship (Maas, Wujastyk, Larson-Bhattacharya 2008) converges on ~325–425 CE — i.e., this Patañjali is a late-classical figure, considerably later than the grammarian Patañjali (~150 BCE). Traditional Hindu reception conflated the two Patañjalis — the grammarian, the yoga-sūtra author, and (in some traditions) a medical-Ayurvedic Patañjali — but the philological evidence makes them three distinct authors.

## Historical evidence
- The text itself.
- The *Yoga-Sūtra-Bhāṣya* commentary attributed to Vyāsa (~5th–6th c. CE) — the earliest extant continuous commentary; tightly bound to the base text in transmission. Maas 2013 has argued, on the basis of the colophon and manuscript structure, that the Yoga-Sūtra and the Vyāsa-Bhāṣya are in fact a single composite work (the *Pātañjala-Yoga-Śāstra*) by a single author, "Patañjali" being the name for the compositional pair. This is the current strongest text-critical position.
- Subsequent commentary tradition: Vācaspati Miśra (~9th c.), Bhoja (~11th c.), Vijñānabhikṣu (~16th c.).
- Inscriptional and epigraphic evidence is absent — Patañjali's biographical reality is entirely text-internal.

## Works
- ***Yoga-Sūtra*** (or, per Maas, the *Pātañjala-Yoga-Śāstra* including the Vyāsa-Bhāṣya): 4 chapters (*pādas*) — *Samādhi-pāda* (on meditative absorption), *Sādhana-pāda* (on practice and the eight limbs), *Vibhūti-pāda* (on yogic powers), *Kaivalya-pāda* (on isolation/liberation) — 195 *sūtras* (or 196 in some manuscripts). Synthesizes Sāṃkhya metaphysics (the dualist scheme of *puruṣa* / *prakṛti*) with a systematic meditative-yogic discipline organized around the *aṣṭāṅga* (eight-limb) progression: *yama*, *niyama*, *āsana*, *prāṇāyāma*, *pratyāhāra*, *dhāraṇā*, *dhyāna*, *samādhi*.

## Role in the tradition
- **Yoga as one of the six classical *darśanas***: classical Hindu philosophy organizes itself into six āstika (Veda-affirming) schools; the Yoga *darśana* is one of the six, traditionally paired with Sāṃkhya (Yoga = applied Sāṃkhya). The Yoga-Sūtra is the textual foundation.
- **Cardinal text of the global yoga tradition**: through Vivekananda's 1896 *Raja Yoga* (a popularized reading of the Yoga-Sūtra for the Anglophone audience), the Yoga-Sūtra became, by the 20th c., the single most-cited classical text in modern global yoga — far more cited in the 20th–21st-c. global yoga discourse than the medieval Hatha Yoga texts that actually shape modern *āsana* practice (Singleton 2010).

## Cross-tradition connections
- **Yoga-Sūtra and Hellenistic philosophy (contested)**: a long-standing comparative-philosophical literature, most fully developed in McEvilley 2002 *The Shape of Ancient Thought*, has proposed structural parallels and possible historical-influence connections between the Yoga-Sūtra's introspective-meditative discipline and: (a) Stoic *prosochē* (attention to one's interior states) and *prokopē* (progress in virtue); (b) Pyrrhonian *epochē* (suspension of judgment) and *ataraxia* (untroubled mind); (c) Neoplatonic *henōsis* (mystical union). The contact hypothesis points to Pyrrho of Elis (~4th c. BCE), who is reported (Diogenes Laertius 9.61) to have accompanied Alexander's army to India and met "the gymnosophists" — Indian renouncers. McEvilley argues for substantial mutual influence across the post-Alexandrian Bactrian / Gandharan Hellenistic-Indian contact zone (~300 BCE – 200 CE). Halbfass 1988 is more cautious; the mainstream Indological position (Larson, Whicher) treats the parallels as structurally suggestive but historically unproven. Worth treating as a live contested hypothesis, not as an established borrowing.
- **Aṣṭāṅga-yoga and Buddhist meditative-eightfold-path parallels**: the *aṣṭāṅga-yoga* (eight-limb) progression of the Yoga-Sūtra is structurally adjacent to the Buddha's *āryāṣṭāṅgika-mārga* (Noble Eightfold Path, ~5th c. BCE). The directionality is contested (much of the Yoga-Sūtra material is detectably absorbed from Buddhist *dhyāna* / meditative-absorption discourse; Larson-Bhattacharya 2008 treats the Buddhist influence as substantial). The integration of Buddhist meditative technology into a Sāṃkhya-Vedic framework is one of the Yoga-Sūtra's distinctive synthesizing moves.
- **Modern global yoga's complicated relationship to Patañjali**: Singleton 2010 has shown that modern postural yoga (Iyengar, Pattabhi Jois, Bikram, the global gym-yoga industry) is much more indebted to 19th–20th-c. transformations of the medieval Hatha Yoga tradition (Krishnamacharya, the Mysore Palace yoga revival) than to the Yoga-Sūtra — but is conventionally legitimated through the Yoga-Sūtra. The Patañjalian *aṣṭāṅga* (which contains *āsana* as one of eight limbs, with no detailed posture instruction) bears only family-resemblance to modern "Ashtanga Yoga" (which is principally a system of postures).

## Disputes
- **Historicity of Patañjali**: the existence of a compiler is widely accepted; the unitary authorship of the Yoga-Sūtra has been variously contested (some scholars have argued for multiple-author / school redaction). Maas 2013's argument for the unitary Pātañjala-Yoga-Śāstra (Sūtra + Bhāṣya as a single work) is the strongest current position.
- **Dates**: 2nd c. BCE (early), 2nd c. CE (a common middle), 4th–5th c. CE (Maas, recent consensus). The range is constrained by Buddhist citations and by stylistic-philological features.
- **The "three Patañjalis" issue**: the traditional Hindu conflation of grammarian, yoga-sūtra author, and Ayurvedic author has been undone by modern philology; tradition continues the conflation in liturgical practice.
- **Influence-direction (Yoga ↔ Buddhism, Yoga ↔ Hellenistic philosophy)**: contested.
