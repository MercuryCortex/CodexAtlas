# Sir Edward Burnett Tylor

## Identity

English anthropologist (2 October 1832 – 2 January 1917); the principal architect of anthropology as an academic discipline in Britain and, with [[max-muller|Max Müller]], conventionally paired with Max Müller as a founding figure of academic comparative religion in the English-speaking world. Born into a London Quaker family, he was excluded as a nonconformist from the Anglican universities and never took a degree; threatened tuberculosis sent him travelling, and a chance meeting with the ethnologist-collector Henry Christy in Havana (1856) took him through Mexico — the journey behind his first book, *Anahuac* (1861), and his only sustained first-hand ethnographic experience. The rest of the career was built at Oxford: Keeper of the University Museum (1883), first Reader in Anthropology (1884), and first Professor of Anthropology (1896) — the discipline's first chair in Britain; he retired in 1909 and was knighted in 1912. Elected FRS in 1871, the year *Primitive Culture* appeared. Max Müller's quip that anthropology was "Mr Tylor's science" stuck to the discipline (via Marett 1936; Stocking 1987).

## Historical evidence

Extensively documented through Oxford University records, the biography by his successor in the Oxford readership R. R. Marett (*Tylor*, 1936), and George Stocking's standard histories (*Victorian Anthropology*, 1987; *After Tylor*, 1995). Like [[james-frazer|Frazer]] after him, Tylor worked from his armchair — after Mexico his data came from missionary reports, colonial administrators, travellers, and folklore collections, a dependence whose colonial conditions Chidester (1996) has made part of the discipline's reckoning with itself.

## Works

- *Anahuac: or Mexico and the Mexicans, Ancient and Modern* (1861) — the Mexican travel ethnography.
- *Researches into the Early History of Mankind and the Development of Civilization* (1865) — first statement of the developmental method.
- *Primitive Culture* (1871, 2 vols) — the central work: the culture definition, the doctrine of survivals, and animism as the minimum definition of religion.
- *Anthropology: An Introduction to the Study of Man and Civilization* (1881) — often called the discipline's first general textbook (an Anglophone framing — continental surveys like Waitz's Anthropologie der Naturvölker predate it).
- "On a Method of Investigating the Development of Institutions" (*Journal of the Anthropological Institute* 18, 1889; read November 1888) — the founding paper of statistical cross-cultural comparison.

## Role in the tradition

Four doctrines define Tylor's position at the head of the discipline:

1. **The culture concept.** *Primitive Culture* opens with what is conventionally credited as the first anthropological definition of culture (though Stocking 1963 argued Tylor's 'culture' remained singular and evolutionary, not the plural Boasian concept) — "that complex whole which includes knowledge, belief, art, morals, law, custom, and any other capabilities and habits acquired by man as a member of society" (vol. 1, ch. I). Later anthropology of religion conventionally treats it as the discipline's founding definition.
2. **Animism.** Tylor proposed "the belief in Spiritual Beings" as the **minimum definition of religion** (vol. 1, ch. XI) and coined the discipline's use of *animism* for it. His account is intellectualist: the "primitive philosopher," reasoning from dreams, visions, and death, infers a separable soul; souls generalize to spirits, spirits to gods — an evolutionary sequence running toward polytheism and monotheism.
3. **Survivals.** Rites, sayings, and customs "carried on by force of habit into a new state of society" persist as fossils of earlier cultural stages (chs. III–IV) — the warrant for reading peasant folklore and children's games as evidence for archaic religion. Frazer's *Golden Bough* is built on this warrant.
4. **The comparative method.** The 1889 "adhesions" paper tabulated correlations among institutions (marriage residence, descent, avoidance customs) across some 350 societies — conventionally counted the first statistical cross-cultural comparison and an ancestor of the Human Relations Area Files tradition.

His direct heirs were Frazer, whose *Golden Bough* ([[phase-7-007-golden-bough]]) extends the survivals-plus-comparison machinery to its Victorian maximum, and Andrew Lang, first a Tylorian against Müller's solar mythology and later Tylor's sharpest critic; R. R. Marett succeeded him at Oxford.

## Cross-tradition significance

Tylor is in this vault not as a member of any mythic tradition but as a builder of the machinery by which traditions are compared — the [[comparative-religion-method]] itself. Three of his problems are the Atlas's problems:

- **Transmission versus convergence, in its original form.** Tylor adopted the doctrine of the psychic unity of mankind (after Adolf Bastian), which reads cross-cultural parallels as **independent invention** by like minds — the convergence default. Yet he also argued celebrated **diffusion** cases: his 1879 paper on the Aztec game *patolli* claimed an Asiatic origin via its resemblance to Indian *pachisi*, and he entertained Old-World comparisons for Mexican symbols — the [[ollin]]–swastika comparison associated with his school is now rejected as independent convergence on the rotating cross-form. When his 1888 adhesions paper was read, Francis Galton objected from the chair that correlated traits might reflect common descent or borrowing rather than independent recurrence — **"Galton's problem,"** still the name for the non-independence of comparative cases. It is the transmission-versus-convergence question this vault's every wire must answer, stated at the discipline's founding moment.
- **The minimum-definition move.** Defining religion by its least common denominator ("belief in Spiritual Beings") made a single field of data out of Australian, Amerindian, Vedic, and biblical material — the enabling gesture of comparative religion, and the one the late-20th-century critique (J. Z. Smith, Asad) would put in question.
- **The high-god dispute.** Lang's *The Making of Religion* (1898) turned the southeastern-Australian sky-father [[baiame]] against Tylor, arguing "primitive monotheism" against the animism-first sequence; Schmidt's *Urmonotheismus* school ran with it. Current scholarship (Swain 1993) rejects both sides' use of Baiame — a standing lesson in how comparative slots distort local figures.

## Disputes

- **The evolutionary framework is rejected.** Tylor's unilineal ladder — savagery → barbarism → civilization, with living "savages" as windows on the archaic past — was dismantled by Boasian historical particularism and is now abandoned across the discipline; Stocking (1987) is the standard account of both the framework and its collapse. The node documents the scheme as history of the discipline, not as usable method.
- **Intellectualism.** Durkheim (*Elementary Forms*, 1912 — [[phase-7-010-elementary-forms-religious-life]]) argued religion is a social fact, not a primitive philosopher's inference; Marett (*The Threshold of Religion*, 1909) posited a "pre-animistic" stage of impersonal power (*mana*) beneath Tylor's spirits; Evans-Pritchard (*Theories of Primitive Religion*, 1965) condemned the whole introspective method as the "if I were a horse" fallacy.
- **Survivals.** Hodgen (*The Doctrine of Survivals*, 1936) showed the concept's circularity: items are dated archaic by the theory they are then used to prove.
- **Colonial context.** Chidester (*Savage Systems*, 1996) demonstrates that the "raw data" of Victorian comparativism was produced on colonial frontiers, where declaring peoples to "have no religion" or "mere animism" tracked the politics of dispossession — animism's career as a category is inseparable from that history.
- **Animism's afterlife.** The term survived its theory: the 21st-century "new animism" (Bird-David, "'Animism' Revisited," *Current Anthropology* 40, 1999; Harvey, *Animism: Respecting the Living World*, 2005) rehabilitates animism as a relational ontology precisely by rejecting Tylor's framing of it as a childlike epistemic mistake.
