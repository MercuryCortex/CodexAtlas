# Tsongkhapa

## Identity

**Tsongkhapa Losang Drakpa** (1357–1419) is the last of the great systematizers of Tibetan Buddhism's later diffusion and the founder of the school that became **Gelug** (*dge lugs*, "the virtuous way") — today the largest and institutionally dominant of the Tibetan orders. "Tsongkhapa" is not a name but a place-tag: *the man from Tsongkha*, the district of Amdo where he was born. His personal ordination name was Losang Drakpa.

His achievement is a synthesis of three things Tibetan Buddhism had been holding apart: **strict Vinaya monasticism**, **rigorous Indian philosophical training** in Madhyamaka and *pramāṇa*, and **the full tantric path** — ordered so that each is the prerequisite of the next. Almost everything distinctive about the Gelug — the celibate scholastic monastery, the twenty-year debate curriculum, the insistence that Madhyamaka view must be established before tantric practice means anything — follows from that ordering.

Two things he did **not** do, and the vault should be exact about both. He did not found the Dalai Lama institution: the title was conferred in 1578, 159 years after his death. And he did not make the Gelug the government of Tibet: that happened in 1642, under the Fifth Dalai Lama with Khoshut Mongol military backing. Tsongkhapa founded a monastery and a curriculum.

## Historical evidence

By the standards of fifteenth-century Tibet he is exceptionally well documented. **Roughly eighteen volumes of his own writing survive**, many with dated colophons; his teachers, his monastic examinations, his patrons, his foundations and his succession are named and datable; and the institutions he established have run continuously since. There is no historicity dispute here — the person, the corpus and the chronology are secure.

What must be held separately is the ***namthar***. The Tibetan "liberation-story" is a devotional genre with its own conventions, and Khedrup Je's *Haven of Faith* and its successors supply the material most people associate with Tsongkhapa: prophecy of his coming by the Buddha, direct visionary conversation with **[[manjushri]]**, prostrations performed until his forearm was raw, the sandalwood tree at Kumbum that grew leaves marked with Buddhist syllables at his birthplace. These are the tradition's account of his significance, not evidence about his week. Reporting them as biography is the error; deleting them is also an error, because they are what the school has actually believed and they shaped its self-understanding. This node states them as tradition and marks them as such.

Even the visions have a documentable social layer: the *namthar* itself says that for years the Mañjuśrī communications were **mediated by another man**, the yogin **Lama Umapa Pawo Dorje**, who acted as medium — Tsongkhapa put questions, Umapa relayed answers. That is a specific, unglamorous, and rather more interesting claim than direct vision, and it is in the tradition's own record.

## Life

**Formation (1357 – c. 1392).** Born in Tsongkha, Amdo. Took novice vows young under Döndrup Rinchen. Around the age of sixteen he travelled to central Tibet and never went back — a detail the tradition itself treats as costly. There he did what no Tibetan of his generation did quite as thoroughly: **studied under teachers of every available lineage** — Kadam (the tradition descending from [[atisa]]), Sakya, Kagyu, Nyingma, and the Jonang — and submitted to the full public-debate examination circuit, travelling between monastic colleges to be tested. His decisive teacher was **Rendawa Zhönnu Lodrö**, a Sakya master and Madhyamaka specialist; the two exchanged verses of mutual homage, and Rendawa's own reading of Candrakīrti is the foundation Tsongkhapa built on.

**Retreat (1392–1398).** Following the instruction transmitted through Umapa — purify, accumulate merit, practise deity yoga, and withdraw from teaching — he retreated to **Wölkha** with eight companions for roughly four years. The mature Madhyamaka position dates from this period. Whatever one makes of the visionary framing, the intellectual outcome is documented in what he wrote immediately afterwards.

**Composition and foundation (1402–1419).** In **1402**, at **Reting**, the old Kadam seat, he completed the ***Lamrim Chenmo***. In **1405** the tantric counterpart, the ***Ngagrim Chenmo***. In **1409** he instituted the **Mönlam Chenmo**, the Great Prayer Festival at Lhasa, offering ornaments to the Jowo image — an act of enormous public and political visibility — and founded **Ganden Monastery**. His disciples founded **Drepung** in 1416 and **Sera** in 1419; **Tashilhunpo** followed in 1447, founded by Gendün Drup.

He was summoned by the **Yongle Emperor** of Ming China and declined, sending his disciple Shakya Yeshe in his place — one of the clearest signals of what his project was and was not about.

He died at Ganden in 1419. The abbatial seat, the **Ganden Tripa**, passed to Gyaltsab Je (Darma Rinchen) and then to Khedrup Je (Gelek Palsang); the three together are the *je yabsé sum*, "the master and his two spiritual sons," the standard iconographic triad.

## The thought

### 1. The object of negation

Tsongkhapa's signature methodological demand: **before you can meditate on emptiness you must identify precisely what is being negated** (*dgag bya*). Get this wrong in one direction and you negate too much — conventional existence, causality, karma, ethics, the path itself — and land in nihilism. Get it wrong in the other and you leave a residue of intrinsic nature untouched and have not reached emptiness at all.

What is negated is **establishment by way of intrinsic nature** (*rang bzhin gyis grub pa*) — **not existence as such**. Things exist; they do not exist *from their own side*. The whole architecture of his Madhyamaka hangs on holding that distinction steady, and the entire subsequent Tibetan argument is about whether it can be held.

### 2. Emptiness and dependent arising are the same insight

Not merely compatible — **mutually entailing**. Because things arise dependently, they cannot have intrinsic nature; because they lack intrinsic nature, they are free to arise dependently. Emptiness *means* dependent arising ([[doctrine-pratitya-samutpada-dependent-origination]], [[emptiness-sunyata]]).

This is why his short devotional masterpiece, the ***Tendrel Töpa***, praises the Buddha not for omniscience or compassion but specifically **for having taught dependent origination**. For Tsongkhapa that teaching *is* the teaching of emptiness, and any account of emptiness as an ineffable something standing behind appearances has already gone wrong.

### 3. Conventional truth is robust; valid cognition works

He defends **[[philosophy-pramana-buddhist-epistemology]]** — [[dharmakirti]]'s epistemology — at the conventional level. Ordinary perception and inference genuinely establish conventional truths. Emptiness does not dissolve epistemology, does not suspend ethics, and does not license the claim that the Mādhyamika has "no position." This puts him against a reading of Nāgārjuna, live in Tibet then and now, on which the Mādhyamika asserts nothing at all.

### 4. Prāsaṅgika as the final view

Following [[candrakirti]] and Buddhapālita against Bhāvaviveka's Svātantrika, he takes Prāsaṅgika to be the definitive Madhyamaka, and takes its distinguishing mark to be the denial of intrinsic existence **even conventionally**. His *Gongpa Rabsel* on Candrakīrti's *Madhyamakāvatāra* and *Rigpé Gyatso* on the [[phase-4-075-mulamadhyamakakarika]] are the systematic statements. He also sorts the Indian schools doxographically in the *Legshé Nyingpo*, assigning [[philosophy-yogacara]] — [[asanga]] and [[vasubandhu]] — to interpretable rather than definitive meaning.

### 5. Insight is reached by analysis

The *Lamrim Chenmo*'s final and most demanding sections treat **śamatha** (calm abiding) and **vipaśyanā** (insight), and Tsongkhapa insists that insight is generated by **reasoned analysis**, not merely by resting the mind in non-conceptuality. Simply withdrawing attention from concepts does not produce the realization of emptiness; you have to *establish* it. This positions him squarely against quietist and "mental non-engagement" approaches — the argument that runs back to the eighth-century Samye debate and forward into his disputes with the Kagyu Mahāmudrā and Nyingma Dzogchen traditions ([[consciousness-mahamudra-luminosity]], [[consciousness-dzogchen-rigpa-recognition]]).

### 6. Sūtra before tantra — and both

Tantra is the swifter vehicle, not an alternative one. Vinaya discipline and Madhyamaka view are prerequisites, not optional groundwork, and a tantric practitioner without them is doing something else. His tantric corpus is large and technically dense — the *Ngagrim Chenmo* on the four tantra classes, and substantial commentary on the [[guhyasamaja]] cycle (which he treated as the paradigm case), on [[kalachakra]] and on [[yamantaka]], the Gelug's principal yidam. The Western picture of Tsongkhapa as a dry scholastic is a distortion produced by which of his works got translated first. See [[ritual-vajrayana-empowerment-abhisheka]], [[ritual-tantra-initiation]], [[tradition-tantra]].

### 7. The graded path

The ***Lamrim Chenmo*** is built directly on [[atisa]]'s eleventh-century *Bodhipathapradīpa* and the Kadam tradition that grew from it. Its structure is the "three scopes": the practitioner concerned with a good rebirth, the practitioner seeking personal liberation, and the practitioner who takes on the [[bodhisattva-vow]] for all beings — each stage with its own meditations, its own prerequisites, and no skipping. The Mahāyāna sections rest on Śāntideva's *Bodhicaryāvatāra* ([[phase-5-049-bodhicaryavatara]]) and the Prajñāpāramitā literature ([[phase-3-031-asthasahasrika-prajnaparamita]], [[moral-paramitas-six-bodhisattva]]). The *Lamtso Namsum* compresses the whole thing to three points: renunciation, bodhicitta, correct view.

On rebirth: the lamrim's lower scopes take the cycle of rebirth as the fact that makes the whole path urgent, and the Gelug position on what transmigrates is the standard Buddhist one — the cycle without a transmigrating self. See [[reincarnation]] for the full comparative treatment and [[anatman-no-self]] for the doctrine; [[doctrine-bardo-tibetan-intermediate-state]] carries the specifically Tibetan intermediate-state material.

## Disputes

**He was contested immediately, and the argument has never closed.** This is the single most important thing a reader should know about him, and Gelug-derived summaries tend to omit it.

- **Rongtön Sheja Kunrig** and **Taktsang Lotsawa Sherab Rinchen** attacked his Madhyamaka in his own century and shortly after; Taktsang compiled a famous list of "eighteen great contradictions" in Tsongkhapa's position.
- **Gorampa Sönam Senge** (1429–1489), the Sakya scholar, wrote the systematic critique. His charge is the mirror image of the one Tsongkhapa feared: that Tsongkhapa negates **too little**. If conventional existence survives the analysis intact, the object of negation was drawn too narrowly, and what remains is a *conceptually determined* emptiness rather than genuine freedom from all four extremes. Cabezón & Dargyay 2007 translate the text; Thakchöe 2007 sets the two positions side by side.
- **Mikyö Dorje**, the Eighth Karmapa, attacked from the Kagyu side.
- **Against Jonang *zhentong*.** Dolpopa Sherab Gyaltsen's "other-emptiness" — the ultimate is empty of adventitious defilement but *not* of its own nature — is the position Tsongkhapa's *rangtong* ("self-emptiness") is set most sharply against. The Jonang was later suppressed under the Fifth Dalai Lama, which is what a philosophical dispute looks like once one side holds the state.

**Reformer, or innovator claiming to be a restorer?** The Gelug account is restoration: Tsongkhapa recovered the pure Indian tradition of Nāgārjuna, Candrakīrti and Atiśa against Tibetan drift. Critics then and now reply that several of his signature positions — the exact drawing of the object of negation, the Prāsaṅgika/Svātantrika distinction as he draws it, the defence of conventional *pramāṇa* — have no clear Indian precedent and are **Tibetan innovations presented as recovery**. Both readings are defensible and both are carried here.

**Two standing anachronisms.**
- **The Dalai Lama lineage.** Gendün Drup (1391–1474) is the retrospective First Dalai Lama, but the title was conferred by Altan Khan on Sönam Gyatso — the Third — in **1578** and applied backwards. Gendün Drup studied within Tsongkhapa's circle late in the master's life; "Tsongkhapa's direct disciple founded the Dalai Lama lineage" compounds two separate overstatements.
- **Gelug political power.** The Gelug government of Tibet dates from **1642**, under the Fifth Dalai Lama with Güshi Khan's Khoshut Mongol backing — 223 years after Tsongkhapa died. Reading the theocratic state back onto him is anachronism. What *is* traceable to him is the Mongol-facing orientation of the school, which begins with the 1409 Mönlam and the Ming refusal and matures long after him (see [[mongol-empire]], [[mongols]], [[event-mongol-conquests-1206-1294]] for the earlier Mongol-Tibetan patronage pattern the Sakya had established).

**"Yellow Hat."** A later and largely external identifier, standard in Chinese and Western sources and of limited use for the fifteenth century.

## Cross-tradition position

### MASSIVE WIN 1 — the object of negation as a universal problem

Every tradition that claims the ultimate exceeds ordinary predication faces the same question, and it is a genuinely hard one: **once you have negated the predicates, is there anything left?** Negate too little and you have merely relocated the idol; negate too much and there is nothing to be liberated by, encountered, or united with.

Tsongkhapa's answer is the most technically explicit that any tradition has produced — negate *intrinsic nature*, retain *conventional function* — and it is precisely calibrated to the failure mode on each side. Set beside:

- **Christian apophaticism.** [[pseudo-dionysius]] negates the divine names and then negates the negations, and the *Mystical Theology* leaves genuinely open what remains ([[theme-apophatic-theology]], [[doctrine-apophatic-via-negativa]]). [[meister-eckhart]] pushes to a *Gottheit* that is *nichts*, beyond even God-as-named ([[the-godhead-eckhart]]) — and was condemned partly for negating too much.
- **Advaita Vedānta.** [[shankara]]'s *neti neti* negates every determination of brahman; his opponents accused him of being a crypto-Buddhist for exactly the reason Tsongkhapa feared — that the negation had swallowed the world.
- **Madhyamaka's own internal split.** The *rangtong* / *zhentong* dispute **is** this question, in the one tradition that named it and argued it formally for six centuries.

This is a **Parallel** wire: no transmission between the Tibetan and Christian cases, and the Advaita and Madhyamaka cases are in real polemical contact but reach opposite verdicts. The value is that the *problem* is shared and the *solutions* are distinct and comparable — see [[nagarjuna]], [[apophatic-mysticism]], [[philosophy-apophatic-mysticism]].

### MASSIVE WIN 2 — the graded path as a genre: contemplation made into a curriculum

The *Lamrim Chenmo* belongs to a rare and identifiable genre: the **staged contemplative manual**, which claims that inner development can be sequenced, that stages have prerequisites, and that skipping is not merely inadvisable but incoherent. Independent instances:

- **[[buddhaghosa]]**'s *Visuddhimagga* ([[phase-4-076-visuddhimagga]]), 5th c. Theravāda — sīla, samādhi, paññā, with the jhānas graded and their access conditions specified. The closest formal analogue, and a genuine common ancestor in Indian Buddhist path literature rather than a coincidence.
- **Sufi *maqāmāt***, the stations of the path, distinguished from the transient *aḥwāl* (states) precisely by being *earned in order* ([[moral-sufi-spiritual-stations-maqamat]]).
- **Ignatius of Loyola's *Spiritual Exercises*** (1548) — four "weeks," timed, sequenced, with a director controlling progression; the Jesuit order institutionalized it as a curriculum ([[tradition-jesuits]]). Fully independent of anything Tibetan.
- **The purgative / illuminative / unitive scheme** of Latin mystical theology, and the Cistercian formation programme ([[tradition-cistercian]]).

The convergence is on a structural claim, not a doctrine: that contemplative attainment is **cumulative and orderable**, and therefore teachable to institutions rather than only to individuals. Every tradition that built large training establishments produced one of these. That is the finding.

### MASSIVE WIN 3 — analysis or quiet: the recurring fight over whether reasoning helps

Tsongkhapa's insistence that insight is reached **through analysis** puts him inside an argument that recurs wherever contemplative traditions get large enough to standardize training:

- **Tibet's own founding instance.** The Samye debate of the late eighth century, traditionally between the Indian Kamalaśīla and the Chinese Chan master Moheyan, over whether awakening is gradual and cultivated or sudden and unconstructed. The traditional Tibetan account has the Indian side win; modern scholarship (Kapstein 2000) treats that account as itself a construction of later Tibetan historiography. Tsongkhapa's position is the mature form of the "cultivated" side.
- **Within Tibet, after him.** His disputes with Mahāmudrā and Dzogchen approaches ([[consciousness-mahamudra-luminosity]], [[consciousness-dzogchen-rigpa-recognition]], [[tradition-nyingma]]) are the same argument continued.
- **In Chan/Zen.** The sudden/gradual dispute, and the later Rinzai kōan-versus-Sōtō-just-sitting divergence — [[dogen]] arrives at a *third* answer that dissolves the means-end frame Tsongkhapa keeps ([[philosophy-chan-zen]]).
- **In Christian prayer.** Discursive meditation versus imageless contemplation, the recurring suspicion of quietism, and the disciplinary consequences when the suspicion hardened.

Tsongkhapa is the strongest available spokesman for the analytical side in any tradition, because he argues it rather than merely asserting it.

### The Indian inheritance, and what Tibet did with it

Tsongkhapa is the endpoint of a specific transmission chain the vault should be able to draw whole: [[nagarjuna]] → [[candrakirti]] → the Indian monastic universities → [[atisa]] (arriving in Tibet 1042) → the Kadam lamrim → Tsongkhapa. Alongside it, [[dignaga]] → [[dharmakirti]] → the Tibetan *pramāṇa* curriculum. What Tibet contributed was not new doctrine so much as **institutional form**: a debate-based, decades-long, text-anchored monastic education that preserved and extended a body of Indian philosophy after its home institutions were destroyed. Tibetan translation preserved Indian Buddhist texts that survive in no Sanskrit original at all ([[language-classical-tibetan]], [[alphabet-tibetan-tantric]]).
