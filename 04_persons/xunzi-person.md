# Xunzi (Xun Kuang)

## Identity
Xun Kuang, conventionally known as Xunzi ("Master Xun"), was a Confucian philosopher of the late Warring States — among the most philosophically systematic thinkers in pre-imperial China. Born in the state of Zhao (~-310), he taught for many years at the famous Jixia Academy in Qi (the great Warring States center of intellectual exchange), where he served as the senior figure ("libationer") for three terms. After difficulties in Qi he moved to Chu, where he was appointed magistrate of Lanling and where he probably remained until his death (~-235). Two of his most famous students became the architects of the Qin's Legalist unification: Han Fei (the Legalist synthesizer) and Li Si (the Qin chancellor and engineer of Qin Shi Huangdi's imperial program). The Xunzi-Han-Fei-Li-Si lineage is the great contested inheritance of classical Chinese thought.

## Historical evidence
Sima Qian's *Shiji* ch. 74 (joint biography with Mengzi) is the principal narrative source. The Xunzi text itself preserves Xunzi's name in its title and discusses contemporaries directly. The Jixia Academy is well-attested; Xunzi's tenures as "libationer" are consistent with other sources. Han Fei's and Li Si's discipleship under Xunzi is attested across multiple early-Han sources.

## Works
- The *Xunzi*: 32 essay-chapters, the first work of sustained essay-prose in Chinese philosophy. Most chapters by Xunzi himself; some chapters disputed. Redacted by Liu Xiang (~-25 BCE) from a larger collection of 322 *pian* into the received 32-chapter text. Key essays: "Encouraging Learning" (ch. 1); "Human Nature Is Bad" (ch. 23); "Discourse on Heaven" (ch. 17); "Dispelling Obsession" (ch. 21); "Discourse on Ritual" (ch. 19); "Discourse on Music" (ch. 20); "Correct Naming" (ch. 22).

## Role in the tradition
The third great classical Confucian, after Confucius and Mengzi — and the most philosophically systematic of the three. Han- and Tang-era state-Confucianism owed much to Xunzi (Dong Zhongshu's Han synthesis; the Tang ritual codification). The Song-onward Neo-Confucian movement, however, privileged Mengzi and explicitly excluded Xunzi from the Four Books (Zhu Xi treated Xunzi as a heterodox Confucian who opened the door to Legalism). The result: Xunzi's reputation was eclipsed for ~800 years before substantial modern rehabilitation. Twentieth-century Chinese scholarship (Hu Shih, Liang Qichao) and Western scholarship (Knoblock, Hutton, Eno, Goldin) have reconstructed Xunzi as the unsung systematizer of Confucian thought, especially of its ritual-naturalist dimension.

## Disputes
- **Date.** Conventional ~-310 to -235. The Shiji's reference to his "advanced age" when he met King Xiang of Qi (r. -283 to -265) is consistent with a long life (perhaps to ~75 or longer).
- **"Human nature is bad"** — translation: *xìng è* doesn't quite mean "evil" in the Christian-Augustinian sense. Closer: "unruly," "uncultivated," "prone to conflict." This affects the cross-tradition parallel.
- **Was Xunzi an orthodox Confucian or a transitional figure to Legalism?** A 2,300-year debate. Han Fei and Li Si as students complicate Confucian self-presentation. Modern scholarship (Knoblock, Hutton, Goldin) increasingly treats Xunzi as a legitimate, just-more-austere, Confucian.
- **Liu Xiang's selection.** What was cut from the original 322 *pian* is unrecoverable.
