# Māshāʾallāh ibn Atharī

## Identity

A **Persian Jew** of Basra, active from the 760s to about 815, and the senior astrologer of the first ʿAbbāsid generation. His name as it comes down to us — *Māshāʾallāh*, "what God has willed", an Arabic pious formula — is almost certainly an Arabicization; the tradition reports an underlying Hebrew name (*Mānashē* / Manasseh). He is one of the clearest single illustrations of the Islamicate scientific culture's actual composition: a Jewish astrologer, working in Arabic, transmitting Greek and Sanskrit material through Persian intermediaries, at a Muslim court.

By the vault's membership-and-wires principle his **home tradition is Judaism** — that is his community of origin and identity — while his position inside ʿAbbāsid court science, and his standing in the Latin Christian tradition as *Messahala*, are **appearances carried by wires**, not second memberships. ## The Baghdad horoscope, 762 CE

In 762 the caliph **al-Manṣūr** commissioned an astrological election for the moment at which construction of his new round city — Baghdad — should begin. Māshāʾallāh cast it together with **Nawbakht the Persian**; some reports add [[umar-ibn-al-farrukhan-al-tabari|ʿUmar ibn al-Farrukhān al-Ṭabarī]] and Ibrāhīm al-Fazārī.

This is the single most consequential documented use of **electional astrology** in history, and it is worth stating exactly what it shows and what it does not. It does not show that the election worked. It shows that the founding of the capital of the largest polity of its age was, as a matter of institutional fact, timed by [[astrology-horoscope-twelve-places|the Hellenistic horoscopic apparatus]] — the same ascendant, the same twelve places, the same katarchic doctrine set out by [[dorotheus-of-sidon|Dorotheus]] seven centuries earlier in Greek hexameters. The apparatus was not a curiosity at the ʿAbbāsid court. It was **statecraft**.

## The great conjunctions: history as an astrological object

Māshāʾallāh's most influential work is the ***Book of Conjunctions, Religions and Sects*** — an astrological history of the world, from the Flood through the Persian empires to the ʿAbbāsid revolution, organized around the twenty-year **Jupiter–Saturn conjunctions** and their long cycles of triplicity-shift and grand return. Major conjunctions mark the rise of prophets, religions and dynasties; the doctrine treats **religious change itself** as a celestial period-phenomenon.

The doctrine is **Sasanian-Persian in origin**, not Greek: Pingree (1968) established that this historical astrology comes from the Persian material translated in the same generation, and that Māshāʾallāh is its principal Arabic transmitter. It then travels:

- **→ [[abu-mashar-al-balkhi|Abū Maʿshar]]**, who systematizes it in the *Kitāb al-Qirānāt* and gives it a fuller theoretical apparatus.
- **→ Latin Europe**, via the 12th-century translations, where it becomes the standard framework of mundane astrology.
- **→ Christian apocalyptic chronology**, where **Pierre d'Ailly** (1350–1420) used conjunction cycles to calculate the date of Antichrist, and where the same literature was read by Columbus. A Sasanian technique for dating dynastic change, transmitted by a Persian Jew in Arabic, ends up being used to date the end of the Christian world (Smoller 1994).

That arc — Sasanian court → Jewish astrologer → Arabic text → Latin translation → Christian eschatology — is the kind of transmission the Atlas exists to make visible, and every step of it has a name and a text attached.

## Reception in the Latin West

Māshāʾallāh was among the most-translated Arabic astrologers in the 12th century, chiefly through **John of Seville**; *De receptione* and the *Epistola de rebus eclipsium* circulated widely under the name **Messahala**. His Latin afterlife extends to the astrolabe: the *Compositio et Operatio Astrolabii* ascribed to "Messahalla" was the most-copied Latin astrolabe treatise of the Middle Ages and stands behind Chaucer's *Treatise on the Astrolabe* — though the ascription itself is now doubted (see Disputes). ## Disputes

- **The astrolabe treatise.** The Latin *Compositio et Operatio Astrolabii* under the name Messahalla is now generally held by specialists NOT to be a translation of a work by Māshāʾallāh; the leading candidate for its source is the Andalusī astronomer Ibn al-Ṣaffār, with the Messahalla name attached in transmission. The treatise's importance — including its descent to Chaucer — is unaffected; the authorship is. Kept here because the misattribution is itself a well-documented piece of transmission history, not because the vault endorses it.
- **His name and religion.** That he was Jewish is the consistent report of the Arabic bibliographical tradition and is accepted; the reconstruction of the underlying Hebrew name (*Mānashē* / Manasseh) is less secure. Some scholarship prefers to leave the original name open.
- **Who was at the 762 election.** Māshāʾallāh and Nawbakht are constant in the reports; the further names vary by source. The event is well attested; its precise roster is not.
- **What survives is quotation.** The *Book of Conjunctions* exists because Ibn Hibintā quoted it at length a century and a half later. Kennedy & Pingree's reconstruction is authoritative but is a reconstruction, and the boundary between Māshāʾallāh's text and Ibn Hibintā's framing is not everywhere sharp.
