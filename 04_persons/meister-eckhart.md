# Meister Eckhart

## Biography

Johannes Eckhart von Hochheim was born around 1260 in Hochheim, Thuringia (central Germany). He entered the Dominican order, studied theology, and rose to become one of the most distinguished Dominican intellectuals of the high medieval period. He studied and taught at Paris (holding the *Magister* chair twice — a distinction almost without precedent, earned first in 1302–03 and again in 1311–13), served as Dominican Provincial for Saxony (1303–1311), as Vicar-General for Bohemia (1307), and as leading vernacular preacher in Strasbourg (~1313) and Cologne (~1314–1326).

His final years were legally fraught. The Archbishop of Cologne, Heinrich von Virneburg, opened an inquisitorial investigation around 1326. Eckhart appealed to Avignon, submitted his work to the Church's judgment, explicitly disavowed any heretical intent, and died in Avignon in early 1328 before the case concluded. Pope John XXII issued the bull *In Agro Dominico* on March 27, 1329 — **one year after Eckhart's death** — condemning 17 propositions from his work as heretical and 11 more as "evil-sounding, rash, and suspect of heresy." The condemnation was posthumous: Eckhart had already submitted and died, yet was condemned as a heretic he could not contest. The German Dominican Province formally affirmed his place in its tradition in 1992; the condemnation has never been officially lifted.

## The Radical Claims

Eckhart's German sermons contain some of the most extreme theological statements in the history of Christian thought. These are not figures of speech — Eckhart deployed them as philosophical propositions grounded in Neoplatonic emanation theory:

- **"The eye through which I see God is the same eye through which God sees me: my eye and God's eye are one eye, one seeing, one knowing, one love."** (Sermon 57 / DW 12) — The subject-object structure of devotion collapses; there is no separate God over against a separate soul.
- **"I am the cause of God."** — Not that Eckhart creates God, but that the eternal generation of the Son in the soul is where the divine life occurs; the soul's ground is not the effect of God but the site of God.
- **"God is nothing [*niht*]."** — Not atheism, but apophatic theology at its most austere: God transcends every category including "being," and therefore the most adequate statement is a negation.
- **"There is something in the soul that is uncreated and uncreatable; if the whole soul were this, it would be uncreated and uncreatable; and this is the Intellect [*Vernünftigkeit*]."** — The soul possesses a depth that has never been separated from God and cannot be.

These propositions were the condemned ones. They are also the ones that made Eckhart one of the most theologically important thinkers in Western Christian history — and the primary instance of Christian non-dualism.

## The Neoplatonic Framework

Eckhart's key philosophical move: the soul has a "spark" (*Fünklein*) or "ground" (*Grunt*) which is uncreated — it is continuous with God's own ground (*Gottheit*, Godhead). The *Gottheit* is Eckhart's technical term for the divine ground prior to and beyond the Trinity — the aspect of God that precedes all distinctions, including the distinction of Father, Son, and Spirit. This is structurally identical to what [[plotinus]] calls "the One" — beyond all predication, beyond being itself.

The spiritual practice Eckhart prescribes is *Abgeschiedenheit* (detachment, releasement) — the stripping away of all created images, desires, and even representations of God from the soul, leaving the bare ground where the divine birth (*Geburt des Wortes*, birth of the Word) occurs. This is not an act but a non-act: the soul does not achieve union, it allows the union that was always already the case to be recognized. Eckhart's *Abgeschiedenheit* is formally equivalent to what [[pseudo-dionysius-the-areopagite]] calls the apophatic ascent through divine darkness, and to what Heidegger (drawing on Eckhart directly) calls *Gelassenheit* (releasement).

The transmission chain: [[plotinus]] → [[proclus]] → [[pseudo-dionysius-the-areopagite]] → Eriugena → [[albertus-magnus]] → Eckhart. Eckhart taught [[thomas-aquinas]]'s Dominican Aristotelianism, but his mystical theology is far closer to Neoplatonic emanationism than to Thomistic participation doctrine.

## Cross-Tradition Connections — MASSIVE WINS

**D.T. Suzuki and the Zen parallel.** Suzuki (*Mysticism: Christian and Buddhist*, 1957) devoted detailed analysis to Eckhart-Zen structural parallels — the most rigorous early cross-tradition comparison in this cluster:

| Eckhart | Zen |
|---|---|
| "Desert of the Godhead" (*Gottheit* beyond all attributes) | *Sunyata* (emptiness) or *mushin* (no-mind) |
| "Spark of the soul" (*Fünklein*) — uncreated, never separate from God | Buddha-nature (*tathāgatagarbha*) — inherent awakening in every being |
| *Abgeschiedenheit* (detachment — stripping away all images of self and God) | *Wu wei* in its contemplative form; *mu* practice |
| "God is nothing" (*niht*) | "Form is emptiness, emptiness is form" (*Prajnaparamita*) |
| The eternal now (*nunc stans*) in which the divine birth occurs | The "gateless gate" — liberation is not an event in time |

These are structural convergences, not transmission relationships. Eckhart did not know Buddhism; Zen did not know Eckhart. The parallel is independent and therefore evidence of a universal mystical grammar.

**The Sufi parallel.** [[farid-ud-din-attar]]'s *Manṭiq al-Ṭayr* (~1177 CE) encodes the identical non-dual disclosure in the Simorgh pun: the thirty birds who seek the divine king discover they ARE the king (*sī murgh* = thirty birds = Simorgh). Eckhart's formulation — "the eye through which I see God is the eye through which God sees me" — states the same identity in first-person theological proposition. No transmission; the convergence is independent, within a century.

**The Upanishadic parallel.** *Tat tvam asi* ("That art thou," *Chandogya Upanishad* 6.8.7) and the Advaita Vedanta framework: the individual self (*jīva*) is identical with Brahman; the apparent separation is epistemological error (*avidyā*), not ontological fact. Eckhart's uncreated spark of the soul that is identical with God's ground is the Christian-Trinitarian reformulation of the same ontological claim, independently derived.

**The apophatic convergence.** [[maimonides]] (negative attributes: God has no positive attributes — all divine descriptions are negations), [[ibn-arabi]] (*tanzih* — divine incomparability; nothing is like God), the *Daodejing* ("The Tao that can be named is not the eternal Tao"), Buddhist *sunyata* — all arrive at the same place as Eckhart's "God is nothing." The [[theme-apophatic-theology]] node documents this convergence. Five traditions arrive at the via negativa independently; Eckhart is the most radical Christian instance.

**The transmission to modern thought.** Eckhart did influence traditions the vault has already documented:
- [[johannes-tauler]] and [[henry-suso]] (his Dominican students) continued his work with terminological adjustments that kept them clear of the 1329 condemnation.
- The anonymous *Theologia Germanica* (15th c.) — praised by Martin Luther — is saturated with Eckhart's vocabulary.
- Hegel's dialectic of the Absolute coming to know itself through negation is structurally Eckhartian.
- Heidegger's *Gelassenheit* is explicitly derived from Eckhart's *Abgeschiedenheit*.

## The Beguine Antecedents

McGinn (*Flowering of Mysticism*, 1998) demonstrates that Eckhart's vernacular mystical theology has substantial Beguine precedents:
- [[mechthild-of-magdeburg]] (*Das fliessende Licht der Gottheit*, ~1250–1280) — already uses *Gottheit* for the divine essence-beyond-distinction in vernacular Middle Low German.
- [[hadewijch-of-antwerp]] — vernacular Middle Dutch mystical-erotic register.
- [[marguerite-porete]] (*Mirror of Simple Souls*, ~1290–1300, burned 1310) — *annihilation* of the soul into God, vernacular Old French. **Eckhart was in Paris 1311–1313, concurrent with the posthumous Vienne 1311–1312 condemnation of Porete's positions**; the conceptual continuity is striking even without a smoking-gun citation (Hollywood 1995, McGinn 2001 ch. 7).

## The Posthumous Condemnation

Pope John XXII's bull *In Agro Dominico* (March 27, 1329) is a structurally anomalous document in the history of heresy proceedings: it condemns propositions from a man who had already submitted to the Church's authority before dying. The 28 condemned propositions include the soul's uncreated spark, the identity of the soul's ground with God's ground, the formula "I am the cause of God," and the birth of the Son in the soul as identical with the eternal Trinitarian generation.

The condemnation's incompleteness — it condemned propositions, not the man's intention — allowed the Rhineland mystical tradition to continue his work. This is the [[executed-divine-claimant]] structure in its posthumous form: the condemnation arrives after the claimant has already submitted and died. Compare: al-Ḥallāj (922 CE — refused to recant, executed for "Ana'l-Ḥaqq"); Giordano Bruno (1600 CE — refused to recant, burned); Eckhart (1329 CE — submitted, died before condemnation). The pattern of execution-by-condemnation for claiming radical divine identity runs through all three.
