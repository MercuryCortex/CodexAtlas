# Enheduanna

## Identity
Enheduanna was the daughter of Sargon of Akkad (~2334–2279 BCE), the founder of the first multi-ethnic empire in Mesopotamia, and was installed by her father as **en-priestess of Nanna** (Sumerian moon god) at the Ekishnugal temple at Ur — the premier religious office in the Sumerian south. She served in this role for approximately fifty years, under her father, her brothers Rimush and Manishtushu, and her nephew Naram-Sin. She composed (or at least compiled and is credited with) the earliest body of authored religious poetry in human history, including the 42 Sumerian Temple Hymns, the *Exaltation of Inanna* (Nin-me-šara), and *In-nin ša-gur-ra*. The Temple Hymns close with her colophon: "The compiler of the tablets was Enheduanna. My king, something has been created that no one has created before."

## Historical evidence
Multiple lines: (1) her own self-attestation in the colophons and the first-person narrative of the *Exaltation*; (2) seal impressions naming her as Sargon's daughter and *en*-priestess from Ur; (3) a calcite disc (the "Enheduanna Disc," excavated by Leonard Woolley at Ur in the 1920s, now in the Penn Museum) bearing her name and image, showing her overseeing a libation ritual; (4) Old Babylonian scribal-curriculum copies of her works (the primary textual source). She is the **earliest historical author known by name** in any tradition — preceding the Egyptian Ptahhotep, Indian Vyasa, Greek Homer/Hesiod, and Hebrew Moses by centuries.

## Works
- *Temple Hymns* — 42 hymns to the principal sanctuaries of Sumer and Akkad; closing colophon names her as compiler.
- *Exaltation of Inanna* (*Nin-me-šara*) — 153-line first-person plea to Inanna recounting Enheduanna's exile from the temple by the usurper Lugalanne and praying for her restoration.
- *In-nin ša-gur-ra* (*Inanna and Ebih*) — longer hymn celebrating Inanna's cosmic supremacy.
- Several additional shorter hymns attributed to her by some scholars (the corpus's exact boundary is contested).

## Role in the tradition
For nearly two millennia after her lifetime, Enheduanna's compositions were standard scribal-school curriculum at Nippur and Ur — students learned Sumerian by copying her hymns. The 20th-century Western rediscovery of Sumerian literature, especially the editorial work of Hallo, van Dijk, and Sjöberg in the 1960s, recovered her historical figure for modern scholarship. Recent feminist and literary-historical interest (Meador 2009, Helle 2023) has brought her to wider attention; she now anchors most general accounts of the history of authorship.

## Disputes
- **Authenticity of authorship.** A majority of Assyriologists accept her as a real historical author. A minority (Black, Civil) has argued the attributions are an Old Babylonian scribal projection. The texts we have are all Old Babylonian copies from ~500 years after her lifetime; the question is whether the Sargonic original was substantively the same as the OB copies. Helle (2020, 2023) defends the historical authorship.
- **Autobiographical reading of the *Exaltation*.** Whether the first-person exile narrative is autobiography or stylized literary persona is debated. Helle (2020) is skeptical of strong autobiographical readings.
- **Date of death.** Approximate; she is generally taken to have served fifty years and died ~2225 BCE.
