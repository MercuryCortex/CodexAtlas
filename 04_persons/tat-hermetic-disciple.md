# Tat (Hermetic disciple)

## Identity
The "son" or principal disciple of Hermes Trismegistus in the dialogue frame of much of Hermetic literature. Tat is a legendary literary character, not a historical person — but the name is significant: **"Tat" is the Greek transliteration of the Egyptian "Thoth"**, so the Hermetic dialogue frame is structurally **Hermes-Thoth-Trismegistus the Father instructing a doubled Thoth-figure as Son**. The Egyptian temple-tradition has multiple Thoths (Thoth as scribe, Thoth as moon-god, Thoth as cosmocrator), and the Hermetic literature exploits this multiplicity by treating Hermes Trismegistus and Tat as a divine teacher / divine disciple pair within the same theological lineage.

Tat is the addressee of more individual Hermetic treatises than any other figure: in the Greek Corpus Hermeticum he addresses CH IV, V, VIII, X, XII, and XIII (six of the seventeen treatises); in the Coptic Hermetica he is the disciple in NHC VI,6 (the *Discourse on the Eighth and Ninth*). Asclepius is addressed in CH II, VI, IX, XIV, XVI–XVII (six treatises) and in the Latin Asclepius; Ammon in CH XVI–XVII; Hermes himself receives revelation from Poimandres in CH I and from his own Nous in CH XI. Together Tat and Asclepius are the two principal Hermetic addressees.

## Historical evidence
None — Tat is a literary character, the addressee of dialogues. The name's Egyptian etymology (Tat = Thoth) is the historical evidence for the **Egyptian temple-tradition substrate** of Hermetic literature: the dialogue-frame's addressees are Egyptian gods (Thoth → Tat, Imhotep → Asklepios → Hermetic Asclepius, Amun → Hermetic Ammon) Hellenized into philosophical-dialogue characters. Mahé 1978 reads the Tat dialogues as preserving Egyptian temple-instruction (sebayet) genre conventions in Greek translation.

## Works
None authored — Tat receives instruction from Hermes within the dialogues. CH XIII (the rebirth dialogue) ends with Tat being instructed to *not* write down what he has learned ("the secret discourse"), and the closing scribal-note tradition of NHC VI,6 has Tat inscribing the revelation on turquoise stelae — both conventions are adapted from Egyptian temple-inscription practice.

## Role in the tradition
- The structural-doubled Thoth-disciple alongside Hermes-Thoth-Trismegistus.
- The principal addressee of the most explicitly *initiatory* and *mystical* Hermetic treatises (CH XIII rebirth dialogue; NHC VI,6 visionary ascent).
- Renaissance Hermeticism (Ficino, Pico) read the Hermes-Tat father-son relationship as analogous to the Plotinian Master-disciple chain or to the prophetic-school chain in patristic Christianity.

## Disputes
- **Single Tat or multiple Tats?** The Hermetic literature is internally inconsistent. Sometimes Tat is *one* specific Hermes-Trismegistus's-son who progresses through initiation across multiple dialogues; sometimes Tat is a generic disciple-figure for *any* candidate. Fowden 1986 reads this as an Egyptian-priestly-school convention rather than a literary inconsistency.
