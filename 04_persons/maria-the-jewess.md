# Maria the Jewess (Maria Prophetissa)

## Identity
A Hellenistic-era Jewish alchemist active in Alexandria (~50 BCE – 100 CE, dates approximate), **the earliest named woman alchemist of the Western tradition** and one of the foundational figures of Greco-Egyptian alchemy. Her own writings are lost, but substantial technical and aphoristic material survives through direct citation by [[zosimos-of-panopolis|Zosimos of Panopolis]] (~290–310 CE), who treats Maria as a major alchemical authority, and through subsequent Byzantine, Syriac, and Arabic transmission. The technical inventory attributed to her — the **bain-marie / *balneum Mariae*** (water-bath, still named after her in French and English culinary and chemical usage), the **kerotakis** (sealed reflux apparatus), and the **tribikos** (three-armed distillation still) — represents foundational chemical-apparatus engineering that remained standard in Western chemistry from the 1st century to the 18th.

## Historical evidence
The principal evidence is **Zosimos's direct citation** in the Byzantine alchemical Greek corpus (Marcianus graecus 299 and companion manuscripts; Mertens 1995 critical edition). Zosimos treats Maria as a teacher-authority and quotes her aphoristic axioms and apparatus-descriptions; this is the kind of citation that establishes a real historical predecessor rather than a pseudonymous figure. Patai 1994 ch. 3 reviews the totality of the evidence and concludes Maria is *likely historical* — distinct from the *Maria Prophetissa* later medieval pseudonymous attribution.

The historical Maria has often been confused or conflated with (a) Miriam the Prophetess of Exodus (deliberate medieval alchemical conflation), (b) Mary the mother of Jesus, and (c) Mary Magdalene. These conflations are part of the *reception-history* of Maria but not part of her historical identity. Patai distinguishes carefully.

## Works
The historical Maria is reconstructable through:
- **Quoted axioms** preserved in Zosimos and the Byzantine compilers — most famously the **Axiom of Maria** (see below).
- **Apparatus descriptions** for the *balneum Mariae*, *kerotakis*, *tribikos*.
- **Recipes and procedures** for metallurgical operations.

## Role in the tradition

- **Foundational chemical-apparatus engineer.** The *bain-marie* / *balneum Mariae* — a gentle controlled-temperature water-bath — remains in continuous use as standard chemical and culinary equipment. The continuity of the eponym from Zosimos (~300 CE) through medieval Arabic and Latin alchemy through early-modern chemistry to modern French and English usage is unbroken — one of the longest-continuous technical-historical attributions in Western science.
- **Originator of the Axiom of Maria.** The aphorism *"ἓν τὸ πᾶν"* — "One becomes Two, Two becomes Three, and out of the Third comes the One as the Fourth" — quoted by Zosimos as Maria's, becomes a canonical Western alchemical aphorism. In the 20th century [[carl-gustav-jung|Jung]] treats this axiom as the prototype of his quaternity-theory of psychological wholeness (*Psychology and Alchemy* and *Aion*); Jung's interpretation gave Maria's axiom enormous modern visibility in psychological-spiritual circles.
- **Inaugurator of the documented Jewish-alchemical lineage.** Patai 1994 traces a continuous Jewish-alchemical tradition from Maria through medieval Arabic-Jewish alchemists (the Khalid ibn Yazid alchemical circle) through the medieval Hebrew alchemical literature through Renaissance Christian Kabbalah (Reuchlin, Pico) to Isaac Luria's metallurgical-Kabbalistic imagery to modern psychological reception.
- **Foundational woman in the Western scientific tradition.** Along with Hypatia of Alexandria (Neoplatonic philosopher, ~370–415 CE), Maria is one of the two foundational named women of the Greco-Roman Egyptian intellectual tradition.

- **→ [[tradition-judaism]]** *cultural-origin*: Maria's specifically-Jewish-Hellenistic identity is reconstructable (Patai 1994 traces the linguistic and onomastic indicators) and matters for understanding the genuinely-multi-cultural late-Hellenistic Alexandrian intellectual milieu.

## Disputes
- **Historical vs. legendary.** Patai 1994 argues historical (with reservations on specific attributions); some modern scholarship (e.g., Forbes 1948) treated Maria as fully legendary; Mertens 1995 takes the historical-but-with-pseudonymous-overlay position that is now consensus.
- **Date.** Patai 1994 places Maria 1st c. BCE – 1st c. CE; some place her as late as 200 CE. The principal anchor is Zosimos's c. 300 CE citation, which requires Maria to have lived multiple generations earlier as a recognized authority.
- **Identity confusion.** The historical Maria is repeatedly conflated in medieval and early-modern alchemical literature with Miriam the Prophetess, Mary the mother of Jesus, and (less frequently) Mary Magdalene. The conflations are reception-history rather than evidence.
