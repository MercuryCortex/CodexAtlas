# Quadratus the Apologist

## Identity
Among the **two earliest known Christian apologists** (with [[aristides-of-athens|Aristides]]). Per Eusebius, he addressed an apology to Emperor Hadrian (~+124/+125), arguing for the legality and reasonableness of Christian practice. The work is lost except for one striking passage preserved by Eusebius (HE 4.3.2):

> "But the works of our Savior were always present, for they were genuine: those who had been healed and those who had risen from the dead, who were not only seen at the moment of their being healed and raised, but were always present, not merely while the Savior was on earth, but also after his departure; they remained for a considerable time, so that some of them survived even to our own day."

The fragment is striking because it implies that **eyewitnesses to Jesus's miracles were still alive at the time of writing** (~+125) — a chronologically remarkable but unverifiable claim.

## Historical evidence
Eusebius alone. Jerome's notice (*De viris* 19) recycles Eusebius.

## Works
*Apology* — lost except for the Eusebian fragment.

## Role in the tradition
Among the earliest Christian apologists. Frequently confused in later tradition with **Quadratus of Athens** (a 2nd-c. bishop) and with a Quadratus mentioned as a prophet by Eusebius — the three may or may not be the same person.

## Disputes
- **Identity** with Quadratus of Athens and/or Quadratus the prophet: unsettled.
- **Date.** ~+124/+125 standard; Hadrian's reign secures the broad window.
- **Reliability of the eyewitness claim**: hagiographically optimistic but not impossible if the apologist's "considerable time" stretches into the 70s.
