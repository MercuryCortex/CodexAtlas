# Perpetua and Felicity

## Identity
**Vibia Perpetua**, a 22-year-old well-educated Carthaginian noblewoman, nursing infant, and recent catechumen; **Felicity** (Felicitas), her enslaved companion, eight months pregnant when arrested. Together with four male companions (Saturus, Saturninus, Revocatus, Secundulus) they were arrested at Carthage ~+202/+203 under [[septimius-severus|Severus]], imprisoned, refused to recant, and were killed in the arena on the birthday of Geta (March 7, 203) — mauled by wild beasts and then decapitated.

## Historical evidence
The ***Passio Perpetuae et Felicitatis*** (~+203) is **uniquely valuable** among early Christian texts: it embeds **Perpetua's own prison diary in the first person** (chs 3–10), making it the **earliest extant Christian text known to be written by a woman**, and one of the very earliest Christian autobiographical compositions of any kind. The text also includes the vision of Saturus and a framing narrative by an editor (anonymous; possibly [[tertullian]], or a community redactor — debated). The historical core is so detailed and idiosyncratic that nearly all scholarship accepts Perpetua's diary as authentic.

## Works
**Perpetua's prison diary** — extraordinary first-person Christian women's writing from ~+203, containing four visions (Jacob's ladder, the dead brother Dinocrates, the second Dinocrates vision, and the agonal vision of fighting an Egyptian in the arena).

## Role in the tradition
- **Earliest Christian women's autobiographical writing**: foundational document for feminist NT and early-Christian scholarship.
- **Patron saints of Carthage / North Africa**: their cult is one of the strongest and earliest in Latin Christianity. Augustine preached on them annually.
- **Visionary theology**: Perpetua's four visions are remarkable for their independence — she does not defer to her catechist or to scripture but receives direct revelation.
- **Possible Montanist connection**: the New Prophecy's affirmation of women's revelatory authority and the *Passio*'s presentation of Perpetua's visions as authoritative point in this direction; some scholars (Heine 1989; against Trevett 1996's more cautious view) read the *Passio* as a North African Montanist text.

## Disputes
- **Authorship of the framing narrative**: Tertullian or anonymous? Mainstream view: probably not Tertullian (style differences), possibly someone in his circle.
- **Montanist character of the text**: the New-Prophecy-friendly elements are real but the text was preserved by proto-orthodox North African Christianity and used liturgically.
- **Felicity's daughter**: born in prison two days before the execution and given to fellow Christians to raise — striking historical-pastoral detail.
