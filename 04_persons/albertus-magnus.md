# Albertus Magnus (Albert the Great)

## Identity
13th-c. German Dominican friar, theologian, Aristotelian commentator, natural philosopher, and **alchemist** (~1200–1280). **The teacher of [[thomas-aquinas]]**, and the figure who initiated the Latin Christian reception of the full Aristotelian corpus that Aquinas would systematize. Albertus produced a 38-volume commentary corpus on Aristotle in Latin paraphrase, performed substantial empirical observation in mineralogy, zoology, and botany, and was treated by medieval and Renaissance tradition as **the foremost Latin Christian authority on alchemy and natural magic**.

**Two distinct historical Alberti must be distinguished:**
1. The **historical Albertus Magnus** — Dominican theologian-scholar with limited but real engagement with alchemical and natural-philosophical material (esp. in *De mineralibus*).
2. The **pseudonymous 'Albertus Magnus' of the 14th-15th-c. alchemical and magical tradition** — to whom most of the *Libellus de Alchimia* and the *De Secretis Mulierum* and related "secrets" literature is attributed. **Pereira 1995 demonstrated that most Albertian alchemy is pseudo-Albertine.**

The Ritman / Beinecke-Mellon **reception**-canonical Albertus is the second figure as much as the first.

## Works
- **Latin Aristotelian commentary corpus** — systematic paraphrase-commentary on essentially every available Aristotelian work + several pseudo-Aristotelian works. The Cologne edition (Geyer 1951+) is the standard.
- ***De mineralibus*** — substantial treatment of stones and metals; combines Aristotelian theoretical framework with personal observation; references the alchemical tradition without fully endorsing it.
- ***De animalibus*** — major Aristotelian zoology + observational additions.
- **Theological works**: *Summa theologiae* (incomplete), *De Causis et processu universitatis*, scriptural commentaries.
- **Pseudo-Albertine alchemical and magical works**: *Libellus de Alchimia*, *Compositum de Compositis*, *Liber Mineralium* (in its alchemical recensions), *De Secretis Mulierum* — all variously 14th-15th-c. compilations under the prestige-name.

## Role in the tradition

- **Initiator of the Latin Aristotelian project.** Without Albertus's systematic vernacular-Latin reception of Aristotle, the Aquinian synthesis would not have been possible. Aquinas, his pupil, completes the project; Albertus initiates and conducts it.
- **Foremost medieval Latin natural philosopher.** Combines text-commentary with empirical observation. *De mineralibus* and *De animalibus* are landmark works of Latin observational natural philosophy.
- **Alchemy: cautious engagement.** The historical Albertus was familiar with alchemical literature (Geber / Jabir corpus, Hermes, the Latin Aristotle's *Meteorologica* and *De generatione et corruptione*) and treats alchemy as a legitimate natural-philosophical inquiry while declining to vouch for transmutation. The pseudo-Albertine corpus then attributes much more confident alchemical material to him.
- **Reception as magus.** From the 14th c. onward Albertus is canonized in the Western imagination as a foremost authority on **natural magic** (manipulation of natural sympathies and antipathies) and alchemy. This reception explains his prominence in Ritman / Beinecke-Mellon and similar collections.

## Disputes
- **What fraction of the "alchemical Albertus" is historical?** Pereira 1995, Weisheipl 1980 established that most is pseudo-Albertine. Some real Albertine engagement in *De mineralibus* and adjacent works exists.
- **Astrology / *Speculum astronomiae* authorship.** The *Speculum astronomiae* (defending judicial astrology) is sometimes attributed to Albertus; the authentic-attribution is debated.
