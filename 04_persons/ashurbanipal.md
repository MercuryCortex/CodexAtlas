# Ashurbanipal

## Identity
Neo-Assyrian king (reigned 668–c.631 BCE), penultimate ruler of the Neo-Assyrian Empire before its collapse and the fall of Nineveh in 612 BCE. Son of Esarhaddon, grandson of Sennacherib. **The most consequential single figure in the entire chain of Mesopotamian textual preservation**: his royal library at Nineveh — assembled deliberately, by his explicit order, from older temple and scribal collections across Assyria and Babylonia — is the physical reason that Standard Babylonian Gilgamesh ([[phase-1-004-gilgamesh-old-babylonian]] is the older Old Babylonian recension; the SB version is Ashurbanipal-corpus), Enuma Elish ([[phase-1-008-enuma-elish]]), Atrahasis ([[phase-1-006-atrahasis]]), Descent of Inanna/Ishtar ([[phase-1-017-descent-of-inanna]]), the *Enuma Anu Enlil* astrological omen series ([[phase-1-019-enuma-anu-enlil]]), the *Šumma ālu* and *Šumma izbu* terrestrial-and-teratological omen series ([[phase-1-020-shumma-alu]], [[phase-1-021-shumma-izbu]]), the *šuʾila* prayers, lexical lists, hemerologies, and thousands of other Mesopotamian compositions survived. He was uniquely **a literate king** — his royal inscriptions repeatedly emphasize that he could read Akkadian and Sumerian (the latter, by his time, a long-dead liturgical-learned language); the colophons of tablets in his library record his command that they be copied for "the palace of Ashurbanipal, king of the world, king of Assyria, on whom Nabu and Tashmetu have bestowed broad understanding." Without his library, the post-Akkadian-period Mesopotamian literary corpus would have been lost three times over — once to the fall of Nineveh in 612, once to the gradual abandonment of cuneiform c. 100 BCE – c. 75 CE, and once to the eighteen subsequent centuries of buried-tell oblivion.

## Historical evidence
Exceptionally well-attested by ancient Near Eastern standards. Primary sources:
- **His own royal inscriptions** — hundreds of cuneiform texts, the standard collection now in Novotny & Jeffers 2018 (*RINAP* 5/1). They include extensive annalistic accounts of his Egyptian, Elamite, Babylonian (against his brother Šamaš-šum-ukīn), and Arabian campaigns.
- **The Nineveh reliefs** — the famous Lion-Hunt reliefs and the Til-Tuba (Elamite) battle reliefs from his Southwest Palace and North Palace at Nineveh, the most celebrated surviving Assyrian royal-art programs. The North Palace lion-hunt sequence (now British Museum) is the most-reproduced single Assyrian visual artifact.
- **Babylonian Chronicles** — Mesopotamian historiographic notices from his and successor reigns triangulate dates.
- **The library itself** — its colophons name him as collector / patron.
- **Greek classical tradition** — Ashurbanipal appears semi-legendarily as "Sardanapalus" in Diodorus Siculus (drawing on Ctesias of Cnidus, 5th c. BCE). The Greek tradition's Sardanapalus is a luxurious decadent who burns himself on a pyre at the end — the historical Ashurbanipal almost certainly did not die that way (the death is poorly attested) but the legendary luxury-king image was influential in Western reception (Byron's *Sardanapalus*, Delacroix's painting *The Death of Sardanapalus* 1827).
- **Hebrew Bible** — Ezra 4:10 references "Asnappar the great and noble" (= Ashurbanipal) in connection with the Assyrian deportations to Samaria. This is the principal biblical mention of him by name.

## Works
- **Library of Nineveh** — Ashurbanipal did not "author" the library's c. 10,000 compositions, but he programmatically *collected* them. The tablet colophons in his collection are explicit: many are marked as copies made from older Babylonian originals during his reign, brought to Nineveh by his agents. This is the world's first systematic textual collection-program in the sense modern librarians would recognize — comparable in concept (if not in technology) to the much later Alexandria, Pergamum, or imperial Chinese collection efforts.
- **Royal inscriptions** — annals, building inscriptions, prayers. These are formally first-person texts under his name, almost certainly composed by court scribes but representing his programmatic self-presentation.

## Role in the tradition

### Within Mesopotamian tradition
He was the last great Neo-Assyrian king before the dynasty's collapse; his death c. 631 BCE was followed by twenty years of crisis culminating in the fall of Nineveh to the Medes and Neo-Babylonians in 612 BCE. The Library of Nineveh was deliberately destroyed in the sack (tablets broken, the building burned — the cuneiform tablets were actually *better preserved* by the burning, which baked them harder, a happy accident from the modern perspective).

### As the OLDEST→MODERN bridge
This is the load-bearing reception trajectory for Codex Atlas. Sketched as a chain:

1. **Sumerian temple-school originals** (c. 2500 BCE onward — *Eridu Genesis*, the Sumerian Flood Story, the Death-of-Bilgames cycle, the Sumerian wisdom literature, *Lugalbanda* and *Enmerkar* and *Gilgamesh-and-Huwawa* in Sumerian).
2. **Old Babylonian Akkadian translations and re-redactions** (c. 1900–1600 BCE — the Old Babylonian Gilgamesh fragments, Atrahasis in its OB redaction, the OB *Enuma Elish* prototype).
3. **Middle Babylonian / Middle Assyrian scribal transmission** (c. 1600–1100 BCE).
4. **Neo-Assyrian "Standard Babylonian" recensions** (c. 1100–650 BCE — including the canonical 12-tablet SB Gilgamesh attributed to Sin-leqi-unninni; the canonical SB Enuma Elish; the canonical Atrahasis SB recension).
5. **Ashurbanipal's collection program** (c. 668–631 BCE) — the SB canonical recensions, plus older OB and Sumerian copies, gathered into one library at Nineveh.
6. **Fall of Nineveh** 612 BCE — the library is destroyed/buried under collapsed palace walls; the cuneiform tablets are preserved by the destruction itself.
7. **Twenty-five centuries of complete oblivion** — cuneiform reading dies out c. 75 CE (the last datable cuneiform tablet is from 75 CE, Babylonian astronomical); Mesopotamian religious literature is entirely lost to the West and Near East alike until the 19th c.
8. **Layard & Rassam excavations 1849–1854** — Austen Henry Layard, an English diplomat-archaeologist, excavates Nineveh's tell at Kuyunjik (north of modern Mosul) and ships ~30,000 tablets and fragments to the British Museum. Hormuzd Rassam continues 1852–1854.
9. **Cuneiform decipherment 1857** — [[event-decipherment-of-cuneiform-1857]] — the Royal Asiatic Society's controlled experiment (Rawlinson, Talbot, Hincks, Oppert each independently translating a fresh Assyrian inscription) demonstrates the decipherment is real.
10. **George Smith reads the Babylonian Flood Tablet, 3 December 1872** — [[event-george-smith-flood-tablet-1872]] — Smith, working through the Kuyunjik (= Ashurbanipal-library) tablets, identifies a Babylonian flood narrative parallel to Genesis. Publishes *The Chaldean Account of Genesis* (1876). 19th-c. Western religious culture is reorganized around the discovery that Genesis has older Mesopotamian precursors.
11. **20th–21st-c. critical editions** — Lambert & Millard 1969 (*Atrahasis*); Lambert 2013 (*Babylonian Creation Myths*); George 2003 (*The Babylonian Gilgamesh Epic*); the ongoing *State Archives of Assyria* series. The corpus is now the textual basis for both Assyriology as a discipline and for Mesopotamian–Hebrew comparative-religion scholarship.

**Without Ashurbanipal, this chain has no step 5.** Without step 5, the corpus does not survive into a single concentrated archive accessible to Layard's spade. The Old Babylonian and Sumerian texts that *did* survive in other tells (Sippar, Ur, Nippur, Uruk) are an order of magnitude less concentrated and were recovered slowly, piecemeal, across the 19th and 20th centuries; the *fast* recovery of Mesopotamian literature in the 1850s–70s — the recovery that *triggered the* Babel und Bibel *controversy and reorganized 19th-c. biblical scholarship* — is overwhelmingly a Library-of-Nineveh phenomenon.

### In the biblical / Hebrew tradition
Ashurbanipal is the Assyrian king during whose reign Judah lived as a vassal-state. His Egyptian campaigns (sack of Thebes 663 BCE) are referenced in Nahum 3:8–10. The fall of Nineveh that followed his death is the subject of the prophetic book Nahum and a marker in the Deuteronomistic narrative ([[phase-2-019-deuteronomistic-history]]). His Babylonian campaign against his brother Šamaš-šum-ukīn (652–648 BCE) shaped the Mesopotamian political-religious geography that the Judean monarchy navigated in [[josiah-king]]'s era. The Deuteronomistic-reform window in Judah (c. 622 BCE under Josiah) opens precisely in the post-Ashurbanipal Assyrian collapse.

### In Greek and Western reception
"Sardanapalus" — Greek classical tradition's legendary Assyrian decadent — is a literary distortion of Ashurbanipal, mediated through Ctesias. The trope of the luxurious Eastern king burning himself on a pyre with all his treasures and concubines becomes a stock Western image of Eastern despotism, replayed by Byron (*Sardanapalus*, 1821) and Delacroix (*The Death of Sardanapalus*, 1827). This Western-Oriental reception is interestingly *separate* from the post-Layard scholarly reception — the same king as legendary decadent in Romantic art and as world-historical preservation-figure in Assyriology.

## Disputes
- **Death and successor.** Ashurbanipal's date of death is conventionally c. 631 BCE (some scholars argue 627), and the succession is murky — Aššur-etel-ilāni and then Sin-šar-iškun rule briefly before Nineveh falls in 612. No reliable evidence for the "self-immolation" tradition; Sardanapalus's pyre is a Ctesias-derived literary invention.
- **Was the library Ashurbanipal's own program, or a continuation / culmination of earlier Assyrian royal collection efforts?** Recent scholarship (Frame & George 2005) emphasizes that Sargonid predecessors (Sargon II, Sennacherib, Esarhaddon) had also built royal collections; Ashurbanipal's program was *exceptional in scale and intentionality* but not unprecedented in kind. The popular narrative of Ashurbanipal as a unique scholarly visionary is somewhat romanticized.
- **Literacy claim.** Ashurbanipal's royal inscriptions claim he could read both Akkadian and Sumerian — extraordinary for a king of his period. The claim is generally taken seriously (a literate king is unusual but not impossible, especially given his upbringing as a younger son who only became crown prince after his elder brother's death and may have been trained for a scribal-administrative career). Some scholars (e.g., Frahm) are more skeptical of how much of the claim is genuine literacy vs royal-ideological boasting.
- **Ezra 4:10 / "Asnappar".** The identification with Ashurbanipal is the standard reading but is not universally certain — some have proposed Esarhaddon or Šamaš-šum-ukīn. The dominant view (Williamson 1985 *Ezra-Nehemiah*) is Ashurbanipal.
