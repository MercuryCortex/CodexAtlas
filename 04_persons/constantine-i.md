# Constantine I

Roman emperor 306–337 (sole emperor from 324). The Edict of Milan (313) legalized Christianity throughout the empire. Constantine's military victory at the Milvian Bridge (312), preceded by the vision later interpreted as Christian, became the founding event of imperial Christianity. He convened and presided at the Council of Nicaea (325), which produced the Nicene Creed and condemned Arianism. He founded Constantinople (dedicated 330) as a new Christian capital. The "Constantinian shift" — Christianity's move from persecuted minority to imperially favored religion to (under Theodosius, 380) state religion — is the single most consequential political-religious transition of late antiquity.

## Substrate connections — solar / Mithraic / monotheizing pagan substrate
- Constantine continued [[sol-invictus]] iconography on coinage (*SOLI INVICTO COMITI*) through ~325, well after the supposed Christian conversion of 312. The Arch of Constantine (315) shows Sol the unconquered sun, no Christian iconography. Wallraff 2001 (*Christus verus Sol*), Bardill 2012 (*Constantine, Divine Emperor*).
- The Milvian Bridge vision is reported in two divergent forms: Eusebius (*VC* I.28) a cross of light in the sky; Lactantius (*De mort. pers.* 44) the chi-rho monogram seen in a dream. The historical figure underneath these accounts is most parsimoniously read as a Sol-worshipping monotheizing emperor who *shifted* — by stages, through political calculation as much as religious commitment — toward exclusive Christian commitment over the next two decades. The connection between Constantinian early-imperial iconography and the [[mithras-roman]] / Sol cult is **substrate-continuity**, not direct cultic conversion: Mithraism was the dominant religion of the late-3rd-c. Roman military, the world Constantine grew up in. Lieu & Montserrat 1998, Wallraff 2001.
- The Constantinian preference for Christianity rides on a longer late-Roman trend of **monotheizing paganism** (Stoic, Platonist, Sol-cultic, Mithraic, Hermetic) that prepared the cultural ground for Christianity's reception (Athanassiadi & Frede 1999, *Pagan Monotheism in Late Antiquity*).
