# Theodore of Mopsuestia

## Identity
Antiochene biblical exegete and theologian; bishop of Mopsuestia in Cilicia from 392 until his death in 428. Known to later tradition as simply "the Interpreter" (*ho Exegetes*). Fellow-student of John Chrysostom under Diodore of Tarsus. Teacher of Nestorius. The most rigorous exponent of Antiochene historical-grammatical exegesis against the allegorical method of the Alexandrians.

## Historical evidence
Voluminous Greek corpus, much of which survives only in Syriac translation (Eastern Christianity preserved him after the Greek tradition anathematized him). Major rediscoveries: the *Catechetical Homilies* recovered in Syriac (Mingana 1932–33), the *Commentary on John* in Syriac, and Latin fragments of the *Commentary on the Minor Prophets*.

## Works
- Commentaries on most books of the Bible — preserved fragmentarily in Greek, more fully in Syriac.
- *Catechetical Homilies* — 16 sermons preparing catechumens for baptism (the fullest single surviving exposition of Theodore's thought).
- *On the Incarnation* — only fragments survive (anathemas at Constantinople 553 included excerpts).

## Role in the tradition
Esteemed as orthodox in his own lifetime. Posthumously anathematized at the Second Council of Constantinople (553) as one of the "Three Chapters" — the others being his commentaries and the writings of Theodoret of Cyrrhus against Cyril, and the letter of Ibas of Edessa to Mari. The Three Chapters condemnation was Justinian's attempt to conciliate the non-Chalcedonian East; it caused a major schism in the Latin West (the Three Chapters Schism). The Church of the East venerates Theodore as their preeminent theologian.

## Connections — substrate and influence
- Antiochene exegesis (Theodore, Diodore, Chrysostom) is methodologically Aristotelian in its insistence on the *historia* (the literal-historical sense) — a contrast with Alexandrian (Origen, Cyril) Platonic-allegorical reading. Schäublin 1974 traces the school's roots to the rhetorical-grammatical tradition of Hellenistic literary criticism (Aristarchus, Asclepiades).
- His "two natures" Christology became the framework for both Nestorius (his student) and, indirectly, Chalcedonian dyophysitism — though Theodore himself was condemned at 553.
- His emphasis on human free will and rejection of inherited Adamic guilt put him in sympathy with [[pelagius]] (though no direct contact is documented); McLeod 2005 explores this.

## Disputes
- **Was Theodore "Nestorian"?** The 553 condemnation says yes; modern Antiochene-friendly scholarship (McLeod 2005, 2009) argues his Christology is more careful than Nestorius's and arguably compatible with Chalcedon.
- **The Three Chapters and the conciliar status of 553.** Latin opposition (the schism of Aquileia / Milan, lasting into the 7th c.) shows the condemnation was contested even at the time.
