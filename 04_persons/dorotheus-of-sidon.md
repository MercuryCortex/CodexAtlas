# Dorotheus of Sidon

## Identity

A Greek-writing astrologer of the **1st century CE**, from Sidon on the Phoenician coast, who composed a five-book didactic poem in hexameters — the *Pentateuch*, known in modern scholarship by the Latin title of its Arabic version, the ***Carmen Astrologicum***. Almost nothing is known of the man. He is dated by the example horoscopes embedded in his own text, whose computed positions fall in the middle decades of the 1st century CE (Neugebauer & Van Hoesen 1959; Pingree 1976), and he refers to travelling in Egypt, which is why later citation sometimes calls him "the Egyptian".

His importance is not biographical. It is that the *Pentateuch* is the **earliest substantially-surviving working manual** of [[astrology-horoscope-twelve-places|the horoscopic system]] — a practitioner's book of rules and worked cases, written a century before Ptolemy's theoretical codification and covering the branches Ptolemy largely omits.

## Why this node exists: survival as evidence

Dorotheus is the vault's cleanest **transmission exhibit**, because the *route* by which his book reached us is itself the documentation of the route by which astrology reached the medieval world.

The Greek hexameters are **lost**, apart from fragments and the substantial quotations preserved by Hephaistio of Thebes in the 4th century. What survives complete is an **Arabic prose translation**, made around 800 CE by [[umar-ibn-al-farrukhan-al-tabari|ʿUmar ibn al-Farrukhān al-Ṭabarī]] — and made not from the Greek but from a **Middle Persian (Pahlavi) version** produced at the Sasanian court, probably in the 3rd century and revised in the 5th. Pingree (1976) identified the Pahlavi stage from inside the Arabic text: chart data interpolated by the Persian redactors, computed for Sasanian-era dates, sits alongside the original 1st-century Greek examples.

So the line reads: **Greek (1st c.) → Pahlavi (3rd–5th c.) → Arabic (c. 800) → Latin (12th c.) → English (17th c.)**, and each arrow has a name attached to it. This is what the Atlas means by a *mechanism of contact*: not a resemblance between two traditions, but a named translator working from a named intermediate version, with the intermediary's fingerprints still in the text.

## What he contributed to the apparatus

- **Electional (katarchic) astrology.** Book V of the *Pentateuch* is the foundational treatment of choosing an auspicious moment to begin an action — a voyage, a marriage, a building, a lawsuit. This is the branch that Ptolemy's [[phase-3-038-ptolemy-tetrabiblos|*Tetrabiblos*]] does not develop, and it is the branch with the longest institutional afterlife: the ʿAbbāsid court used it to elect the foundation-moment of Baghdad ([[mashallah-ibn-athari|Māshāʾallāh]], 762 CE).
- **Interrogational (horary) doctrine.** The rules by which a chart cast for the moment a question is asked is read for its answer — perfection, prohibition, translation of light — descend substantially from Dorotheus through the Arabic relay into [[phase-5-066-bonatti-liber-astronomiae|Bonatti]] and [[phase-6-054-lilly-christian-astrology|Lilly]].
- **Triplicity rulership.** His method of judging the overall shape of a life from the triplicity lords of the sect light is one of the most durable single techniques in the tradition, carried without substantive change into Perso-Arabic and Latin practice.

## Disputes

- **Date.** The 1st-century dating rests on the internal chart data. A minority has argued for a later date on the grounds that some of the doctrine looks developed; Pingree's identification of the *later* material as Persian interpolation is the standard answer, and the 1st-century date is mainstream.
- **How much of the Arabic is Dorotheus?** Not all of it. Pingree's edition separates, as far as the evidence allows, the Greek original from the Sasanian additions and the Arabic translator's own contributions. Any use of the *Carmen* as a witness to 1st-century Greek practice has to work with that stratification rather than through it — a caution frequently ignored in the modern practitioner revival, which sometimes reads the whole Arabic text as first-century Greek.
- **Was there a single "Dorotheus"?** The name attracted later pseudepigraphic excerpts, as astrological authority-names generally did. The *Pentateuch* itself is coherent enough to be treated as one author's work; material circulating under his name elsewhere is not.
