# Hatshepsut

## Identity
Hatshepsut (~1507–1458 BCE) was the fifth ruler of the 18th Dynasty and one of ancient Egypt's most successful pharaohs. Daughter of Thutmose I, she first served as queen consort to Thutmose II; when he died (~1479 BCE), she became regent for the young stepson Thutmose III. Within a few years she assumed full pharaonic titles and regalia, ruling as pharaoh in her own right for approximately 21 years. After her death, Thutmose III — who had co-existed with her rule for decades — had her images systematically defaced, her name chiseled from monuments, and her cartouches replaced with those of earlier kings.

## Female Pharaoh: Theological Self-Presentation

Hatshepsut is the most thoroughly documented female pharaoh in Egyptian history. In official visual representations she adopted male pharaonic regalia — the double crown, false beard, kilt — while not hiding her female identity in texts: inscriptions regularly use feminine grammatical forms alongside masculine titles. This is not disguise or deception; it is theological. The pharaoh is not a man but the *office* of the solar king — the earthly embodiment of Horus, the divine son, and the posthumous identity of Osiris. The biological body is secondary to the divine office.

Her most elaborate theological self-presentation is in her Deir el-Bahri mortuary temple reliefs: the divine birth narrative. Amun-Ra, taking the form of Thutmose I, visits Hatshepsut's mother and conceives Hatshepsut. The god then announces: "I will make her the ruler of the Two Lands... she shall exercise the excellent kingship in this whole land." This is the most explicit divine election formula in Egyptian history for a female ruler — the daughter of Amun-Ra, personally selected before birth for divine kingship. ## Major Achievements

**The Punt Expedition (~year 9 of her reign, ~1470 BCE):** Hatshepsut dispatched a trading expedition to the land of Punt (location debated: probably modern Eritrea/Somalia coast or inland Ethiopia), returning with incense trees, myrrh, ebony, gold, and exotic animals. The expedition is documented in remarkable detail on the Deir el-Bahri temple reliefs — one of the most complete records of any ancient Egyptian trading expedition. The incense trees were planted in the temple forecourt. Wire: [theme: divine economy, trade as sacred act].

**Construction at Karnak:** Hatshepsut built more extensively at Karnak than any predecessor. Her two obelisks — the tallest ever erected in Egypt at the time — still stand (one complete, one broken). She enclosed them in electrum-sheathed tips that caught the first light of dawn, the solar theology made architectural. Later, Thutmose III built walls around them, obscuring the lower sections but preserving the upper portions — a paradox: the *damnatio memoriae* inadvertently protected what it tried to hide. Wire: [[event-karnak-temple-complex-construction-c-2055-bce-100-ce]].

**Deir el-Bahri mortuary temple:** Hatshepsut's *Djeser-Djeseru* ("Holy of Holies") at Deir el-Bahri — three colonnaded terraces built against the Theban cliff face — is one of the finest surviving examples of New Kingdom temple architecture and the primary source for Hatshepsut's ideology.

## The Damnatio Memoriae

After Hatshepsut's death (~1458 BCE), Thutmose III — who had co-ruled with her for two decades without incident — began a systematic erasure that accelerated later in his reign (~20 years after her death). Her images were chiseled from walls, her cartouches replaced, her statues smashed and buried in a pit (where they were discovered in 1920 by the Metropolitan Museum of Art). The reasons remain debated: personal animosity is unlikely given the long co-rule; dynastic politics (erasing a female ruler precedent) and theological normalization (restoring the male-only solar kingship formula) are the most plausible explanations.

The erasure was sufficiently thorough that Hatshepsut was essentially unknown to modern historians until the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Her identity was reconstructed from the buried statues, the Deir el-Bahri reliefs (where faces were chiseled but cartouches sometimes missed), and Karnak inscriptions that survived by accident. ## MASSIVE WIN: Female Divine Kingship

Hatshepsut's theological self-presentation is the most explicit articulation of the female divine kingship formula in Egyptian history. The pattern:
- Daughter of Amun-Ra → selected before birth for divine rulership → female pharaoh → erected the tallest obelisks → conducted Egypt's most celebrated trade expedition → systematically erased after death.

The convergence of female divine kingship, divine election, monumental building, and posthumous erasure makes Hatshepsut a nexus node for: [[divine-kingship-solar]] (the solar king formula applied to a woman), [[amun-ra]] (as her divine father), [[event-karnak-temple-complex-construction-c-2055-bce-100-ce]] (the temple complex she helped build), [[executed-divine-claimant]] (the erasure pattern recurring within 80 years in the same dynasty).
