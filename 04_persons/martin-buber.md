# Martin Buber

## Identity
Martin Buber (1878–1965) was a German-Jewish philosopher, theologian, Hebrew Bible translator, scholar of Hasidism, and Zionist intellectual. Born in Vienna; raised from age three by his grandfather Solomon Buber, a notable Galician Jewish scholar of midrashic literature; studied philosophy and art history in Vienna, Berlin, Leipzig, and Zürich. His early Hasidic-recovery work (*Die Geschichten des Rabbi Nachman* 1906, *Die Legende des Baal-Schem* 1908) and his early religious-mystical phase gave way after about 1916 to the mature *dialogical* philosophy articulated in *Ich und Du* (1923). His collaboration with Franz Rosenzweig (until Rosenzweig's death 1929) on the German translation of the Hebrew Bible — *Die Schrift*, beginning 1925 and completed by Buber alone in 1962 — was one of the major translation projects of 20th-century German culture. Buber held the chair in Jewish religious history at the University of Frankfurt 1924–1933 (created for him; the first such chair in Germany), was dismissed in 1933 under Nazi racial laws, conducted resistance education in the German-Jewish community throughout 1933–1938, emigrated to Mandate Palestine in 1938, and held the chair in social philosophy at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem 1938–1951. He was a co-founder of *Brit Shalom* (1925) and *Ihud* (1942), the binationalist Zionist circles that advocated for a single Jewish-Arab state in Palestine — a position that made him controversial in mainstream Zionism. After 1948 he was an outspoken critic of certain Israeli state policies (especially toward the Arab population); he advocated dialogue and reconciliation throughout the rest of his life.

## Historical evidence
Densely documented: Buber's voluminous correspondence (the *Briefwechsel* edition, 3 vols. 1972–1975, runs to thousands of letters), his published works (~700 items collected in the Werkausgabe), the archives at the Hebrew University in Jerusalem, contemporary memoirs (Rosenzweig, Gershom Scholem, Hannah Arendt, many others). Mendes-Flohr 2019 is the definitive modern biography.

## Works
*Ich und Du* (I and Thou, 1923) — the philosophical-religious masterpiece. *Die Schrift* (the German Hebrew Bible translation, 1925–1962). *Die Erzählungen der Chassidim* (Tales of the Hasidim, 1947–1948) — the encyclopedic Hasidic-narrative anthology; preceded by earlier Hasidic collections from 1906 onward. *Königtum Gottes* (Kingship of God, 1932), *Der Glaube der Propheten* (Prophetic Faith, 1942), *Moses* (1944) — biblical-theological monographs. *Gottesfinsternis* (Eclipse of God, 1952) — his major mid-century theological-philosophical work, including critiques of Sartre and Heidegger. *Zwischen Mensch und Mensch* (Between Man and Man, 1947 English) — major collected essays. *Pfade in Utopia* (Paths in Utopia, 1947) — political-philosophical work on socialism. The Werkausgabe (Gütersloher Verlagshaus, 1996–) is the standard critical edition.

## Role in the tradition
Buber is the principal articulator of *dialogical philosophy* — the philosophical-theological orientation that grounds human existence in the I-Thou relation (see [[i-thou]]). His work has been foundational for: (a) 20th-c. Jewish religious thought (the dialogical principle reshapes liberal and reconstructionist Judaism; influences Heschel, Soloveitchik in dialogue); (b) Christian-Jewish theological dialogue (Reinhold Niebuhr, Tillich, Karl Heim, Hans Urs von Balthasar); (c) existentialist philosophy of religion (Marcel, Berdyaev, parts of mid-century Heideggerianism); (d) pastoral psychology (Tournier, Rollo May); (e) Emmanuel Levinas's ethical philosophy of the Other; (f) educational philosophy (the I-Thou as pedagogical model, especially the Buberian-derived literature on dialogical pedagogy). His Hasidic recovery work, while criticized for over-spiritualization by Gershom Scholem, brought Hasidic narrative to a vast non-Jewish reading public in the 20th century.

In Zionist intellectual history, Buber represents the *cultural-spiritual* and *binationalist* strand against the dominant political-statist Zionism — a position that made him marginal in mainstream Israeli politics but enormously influential in the religious-cultural and post-1967 critical Zionist intellectual tradition.

## Disputes
- **Buber's Hasidism interpretation.** Gershom Scholem's polemic — *The Messianic Idea in Judaism* (1971) and earlier essays — argues Buber misrepresented Hasidism as a primarily existential-personalist religious movement, suppressing its substantive kabbalistic-doctrinal content and its mystical-theurgic structure. Buber's defense: he was retrieving Hasidism's spiritual essence for contemporary application, not writing technical scholarly history. The Scholem-Buber debate is foundational for modern Jewish studies; both positions are defensible and both have substantial subsequent advocates (Idel 1995 mediates, partly favoring Scholem's complaint).
- **Dialogical philosophy and mysticism.** Buber's mature thought is explicitly anti-mystical-monist (he wrote against his own earlier mystical period). Whether this is a true overcoming of mysticism or a relocation of mystical themes inside the I-Thou framework is debated.
- **Political-Zionist positions.** Buber's binational-state advocacy was controversial in mainstream Zionism in his lifetime and remains controversial in contemporary Israeli political discourse. His relevance to contemporary one-state / two-state debates is intensively discussed.
- **Influence on Levinas.** Buber and Levinas disagree about the reciprocity of the I-Thou. Whether Levinas's *asymmetry* corrects or misreads Buber is debated; their published exchange (Levinas's "Martin Buber and the Theory of Knowledge" 1958; Buber's response) is the principal text.
- **Translation choices in *Die Schrift*.** Buber's idiosyncratic German Bible translation (with neologisms, hyphenated compound words, attention to Hebrew root-recurrences) is admired as literary-theological achievement and criticized as obscurantist. The standard reference is Rosenzweig and Buber's joint *Die Schrift und ihre Verdeutschung* (1936).
