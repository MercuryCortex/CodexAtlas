# Marie Laveau

## Identity
The most famous New Orleans Voodoo priestess of the 19th century; *manbo* (priestess) and *Voodoo Queen*; free woman of color; practicing Catholic; hairdresser to wealthy New Orleans households (a profession that gave her wide social access). Her career spanned the 1830s–1870s; she was succeeded in some capacities by her daughter Marie Laveau II (Marie Heloise Laveau Glapion, 1827–1862, often conflated with her mother in popular memory).

## Historical evidence
Documented: civil records (births, marriages, baptisms, property); contemporary newspaper accounts; St. Louis Cathedral records (she remained a practicing Catholic); the family's St. Louis Cemetery No. 1 tomb is one of the most-visited graves in the United States. The detailed reconstruction is in Long 2006 (the standard biography), which separates historical Marie Laveau from the considerable folkloric tradition that has accreted around her.

## Works
- No written works.
- Her religious practice was oral-ritual; her ritual style is partly reconstructable through Federal Writers' Project interviews with elderly informants in the 1930s and through later New Orleans tradition.

## Role in the tradition
Marie Laveau represents the New Orleans line of Caribbean African-religious tradition: Haitian Vodou brought by refugees from the Haitian Revolution (~1809–1810), substantially modified by the Louisiana environment (Catholic syncretism, Mississippi-Valley folk magic, African-American spiritualism, French and Spanish Catholic Marian devotion). New Orleans Voodoo is a sister-tradition to Haitian Vodou and Cuban Santería but distinct from both. Marie Laveau presided over its 19th-c. florescence.

She was famously simultaneously a practicing Catholic (regular Mass attendance at St. Louis Cathedral) and a Voodoo priestess — embodying a syncretism that some scholars (Long 2006) read as theologically genuine, not as covert paganism under Catholic camouflage.

## Disputes
- **Mother-daughter conflation.** Many "Marie Laveau" anecdotes from the 1860s–1880s actually concern her daughter Marie II (or have no historical basis at all). Long 2006 is the principal disambiguating work.
- **Religious practice details.** Folkloric accretions (the Wishing Stump of the bayou, the snake "Zombi," elaborate "Voodoo dances") are partly genuine ritual practice, partly journalistic-sensationalist 19th-c. invention.
- **"Voodoo Queen" designation.** Whether there was a single recognized Queen, a competing several, or this was a journalistic-invented title is debated.
