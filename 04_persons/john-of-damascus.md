# John of Damascus

## Identity
Last of the Greek Fathers, first of the systematic theologians. Born into the powerful Christian Arab Mansur family of Damascus around 675; his grandfather (also Mansur ibn Sarjun) had been the Byzantine official who surrendered Damascus to the Muslim conquest in 635. John served the Umayyad court in some financial-administrative capacity before retiring (date uncertain) to the Mar Saba monastery in the Judaean desert, where he wrote his major works under Muslim rather than Byzantine jurisdiction — a position that gave him relative freedom to oppose the iconoclast policy of Emperor Leo III (begun 726).

## Historical evidence
Standard biographical sources are late (the 10th-c. *Life* by John of Jerusalem is hagiographic). Documented through his preserved corpus, citations by contemporaries and successors, and the actions of Iconoclast councils that anathematized him (Hieria 754, posthumously).

## Works
- ***Fount of Knowledge*** (*Pege Gnoseos*) — the systematic summa, in three parts:
  - *Dialectica* — Aristotelian logic and metaphysical vocabulary (categories, predicables, *ousia*, *hypostasis*, etc.) prefacing theological work.
  - *On Heresies* — taxonomic catalogue, mostly compiled from Epiphanius but with original chapter 100/101 on Islam (the earliest substantial Christian theological engagement with Islam).
  - ***Exposition of the Orthodox Faith*** (*Ekdosis akribes tes orthodoxou pisteos*, 100 chapters) — the systematic synthesis. Translated into Latin by Burgundio of Pisa (~1153) and influential on Lombard, Aquinas, and the medieval West.
- ***Three Treatises on the Divine Images*** (~726–730) — foundational iconophile defense. Arguments: the Incarnation has made matter capable of carrying the divine; veneration (*proskynesis*) of an image is relative, terminating on the prototype (a distinction from worship, *latreia*, given only to God).
- Hymnography — major contributions to the Byzantine liturgical cycle.

## Role in the tradition
Synthesizer rather than innovator (he says so himself in the prologue to the *Exposition*: he gathers "honey from a meadow of various flowers"). His *Exposition* became the textbook of Byzantine theology and, via Burgundio's translation, fed directly into Aquinas's *Summa* (Aquinas cites *Damascenus* hundreds of times). His iconophile arguments were vindicated at Nicaea II (787) and definitively at the 843 Triumph of Orthodoxy.

## Connections — substrate and influence
- His *Dialectica* is the most systematic Christian deployment of Aristotelian categories in the patristic age — and through Burgundio's Latin translation became a major channel by which **Aristotle re-entered the medieval West** (alongside Boethius's earlier translations). Louth 2002: 31–58.
- His engagement with Islam (*On Heresies* 100/101) is the first major Christian theological characterization of Islam (which he treats as a Christian heresy of a Nestorian-Arian-Gnostic type). The historical importance: written by an Arabic-speaking Christian who lived under the Caliphate.
- The iconophile theology of matter-bearing-divinity has substrate connections to:
  - Egyptian and Greco-Roman religious practice of divine images (the *cult image* tradition that Christian iconoclasts attacked precisely as pagan-style idolatry).
  - The Jewish-Christian aniconic tradition (Second Commandment) that the Iconoclasts invoked.
  - John's defense crystallizes a long Christian rapprochement with image-veneration that goes back at least to the Dura-Europos house-church (mid-3rd c.).

## Disputes
- **Authorship of the Octoechos.** The compilation of the eight-mode liturgical cycle is traditionally credited to John but is almost certainly later — Louth 2002: 252–82.
- **Date of retirement to Mar Saba.** Sources are vague.
