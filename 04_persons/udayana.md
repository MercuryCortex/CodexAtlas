# Udayana

## Identity

**Udayana** (Udayanācārya, fl. **984 CE**, Mithilā) is the last major master of "**Old Nyāya**" (*prācīna-nyāya*) and the philosopher who consolidated the once-separate schools of **[[philosophy-nyaya-vaisheshika-realism|Nyāya]] and Vaiśeṣika** into the single realist system that his successors inherited. He stands at the end of the classical Nyāya commentarial chain — Gautama Akṣapāda → Vātsyāyana → Uddyotakara → Vācaspati Miśra → **Udayana** — and directly before the "New Logic" of Gaṅgeśa: Udayana is thus the **immediate ancestor of [[philosophy-navya-nyaya|Navya-Nyāya]]**, whose founding text presupposes his work at every turn.

He is remembered for two very different achievements. As a **natural theologian** he wrote the *Nyāyakusumāñjali* ("A Handful of Flowers of Reasoning"), the classic Indian rational demonstration of the existence of **Īśvara** (God). As a **polemicist** he wrote the *Ātmatattvaviveka*, the definitive Nyāya refutation of the Buddhist denial of a permanent self. His date is one of the few firm points in classical Indian philosophy: the colophon of his short manual *Lakṣaṇāvalī* records its composition in **Śaka 906 (= 984 CE)**.

## Works and thought

Udayana's corpus spans commentary, manual, and independent treatise:

- **Nyāyakusumāñjali** — five chapters (*stabaka*, "cluster/bouquet") of argument for a supreme intelligent cause. Against the atheism of Pūrva [[philosophy-mimamsa-purva-ritual-metaphysics|Mīmāṃsā]] and the Cārvāka, and against Buddhist objections, Udayana argues that the world, being an **effect** (*kārya*), requires an intelligent maker; that the ordered **combination of eternal atoms** at the start of a cosmic cycle needs a directing will; that **moral desert** (*adṛṣṭa*, the residue of karma) is inert and needs a supervisor to allot its fruits; and that the authority of the **Veda** presupposes a trustworthy author. It is widely regarded (Chemparathy 1972; Matilal 1985) as the classic and among the most influential theistic-proof texts of the Indian tradition.
- **Ātmatattvaviveka** ("Discrimination of the Truth about the Self"), pointedly nicknamed ***Bauddhadhikkāra*** ("Rebuke of the Buddhists") — a sustained refutation of the late Buddhist epistemologists of the **[[philosophy-pramana-buddhist-epistemology|Dignāga–Dharmakīrti]]** school on four fronts: momentariness (*kṣaṇikatva*), the no-self doctrine (*anātman*), the *apoha* ("exclusion") theory of word-meaning, and external-world scepticism.
- **Kiraṇāvalī** — his commentary on Praśastapāda's *Padārthadharmasaṃgraha*, the classic Vaiśeṣika categorial text.
- **Nyāyavārttikatātparyapariśuddhi** — a super-commentary defending Vācaspati Miśra's *Tātparyaṭīkā* (and behind it Uddyotakara's *Nyāyavārttika*) against Buddhist criticism.
- **Lakṣaṇāvalī** and **Lakṣaṇamālā** — compact manuals of Vaiśeṣika definitions; the former carries the dated colophon.

A well-known traditional anecdote has Udayana arrive at the Jagannātha temple of Purī to find the doors shut, and taunt the god: *"Intoxicated with your own existence, you slight me now — but when the Buddhists come, your very existence hangs on me."* Whether or not historical, it captures how the later tradition cast him: the realist theist whose arguments the gods themselves depended on.

## Cross-tradition significance (transmission vs. convergence)

Udayana carries two quite different kinds of cross-tradition wire, and they must be kept apart.

**Within the Sanskrit world — documented transmission.** His relation to [[philosophy-navya-nyaya|Navya-Nyāya]] is genuine intellectual descent: Gaṅgeśa's *Tattvacintāmaṇi* (c. 1325) engages Udayana constantly, and the standard dating of the New Logic is triangulated precisely as "after Udayana, before the Navadvīpa masters." His duel with the Buddhist logicians is equally documented: the *Ātmatattvaviveka* names and answers positions of the Dharmakīrtian school, and his Buddhist near-contemporaries **Jñānaśrīmitra** and **Ratnakīrti** argued the opposite side of the same questions. These are not analogies but actual historical contact.

**Across traditions — convergence, not contact.** The *Nyāyakusumāñjali* is the standard comparandum for Latin natural theology. Its causal, cosmological, and design arguments for *Īśvara* arrive at conclusions structurally close to **[[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]]'s Five Ways** ([[philosophy-thomism|Thomism]]) roughly three centuries later, with **no documented transmission in either direction** — a textbook case of independent convergence on the same argument-forms (Chemparathy 1972; Matilal 1985). The honest qualification is doctrinal: Nyāya's Īśvara is an **efficient orderer of eternal atoms and eternal souls**, not a creator *ex nihilo*, so the shared structure of the proofs sits atop genuinely different metaphysics. The parallel is one of argument-form and rational-theological ambition, not of shared theology.

## Disputes

- **How firm is 984 CE?** The *Lakṣaṇāvalī* Śaka-906 colophon is widely accepted, but a minority has questioned whether the dated verse is Udayana's own or a later insertion; the surrounding estimates (born c. 950, died c. 1050) are inferential, not attested.
- **Nyāya theism: original or defensive?** Scholars debate whether the developed *Īśvara*-arguments are intrinsic to early Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika or a later, largely **anti-Buddhist** apologetic that Udayana brought to its sharpest form; Vaiśeṣika in its oldest stratum (Kaṇāda) is not obviously theistic.
- **The Aquinas comparison.** The parallel is real but is sometimes overstated into influence; the mainstream position (Chemparathy, Matilal) is careful convergence, and stresses the *ex nihilo* vs. atom-ordering difference that keeps the two theologies distinct.
