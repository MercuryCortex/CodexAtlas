# Onomacritus

## Identity
Onomacritus (fl. ~530–485 BCE) was an Athenian **collector, editor, and reputed forger of oracular and Orphic poetry** active at the court of the tyrant Peisistratus and his sons. Ancient tradition makes him the figure most responsible for assembling and arranging the corpus of poems attributed to [[orpheus]] and Musaeus — and, notoriously, for tampering with them. He is a key (if shadowy) name in the early history of [[tradition-orphic|Orphism]] and in the broader phenomenon of pseudepigraphic religious authorship in archaic Greece.

## Historical evidence
The principal source is [[herodotus]] (7.6), who reports that Onomacritus was caught by the poet Lasus of Hermione interpolating a forged oracle into the verses of Musaeus, was expelled from Athens by Hipparchus for it, but was later reconciled with the exiled Peisistratids and accompanied them to the Persian court, where he selectively recited oracles to encourage Xerxes' invasion of Greece. Pausanias and Aristotle also transmit traditions making him an arranger or even partial author of "Orphic" verse.

## Context
Onomacritus stands at the intersection of two archaic-Greek developments: the **Peisistratid program of editing and canonizing poetry** (also associated with the fixing of the Homeric text) and the formation of the **Orphic-Bacchic literature** that would feed the mystery cults. M.L. West (1983) treats him as a real historical compiler whose activity helps explain why so much theogonic poetry circulated under the prestige-name of the mythical singer Orpheus.

## Cross-tradition findings
- **Pseudepigraphy as a religious-literary technology.** Onomacritus is an early, named instance of attributing new religious texts to a venerable ancient authority — the same mechanism that produced the Hermetic Corpus (under Hermes Trismegistus), much apocalyptic literature (under Enoch, Ezra, etc.), and the broad category of pseudepigrapha across Mediterranean traditions. His case shows the practice operating self-consciously in 6th-century Athens, within the [[tradition-orphic|Orphic]] sphere that later transmitted theogonic ideas into Pythagorean and Platonic currents.
