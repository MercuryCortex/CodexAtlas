# Charlemagne

## Identity
King of the Franks from 768, king of the Lombards from 774, Emperor of the Romans from Christmas Day 800 — when Pope Leo III crowned him in Old St Peter's, Rome. By the end of his reign in 814 he had welded most of western continental Europe (the Frankish heartland, conquered Lombard Italy, conquered Saxony, parts of Spain, Bavaria, the Avar territories) into a single Christian polity, the first since the western Roman empire's collapse three centuries before. The Carolingian *renovatio*, with its educational, liturgical, manuscript, and legal reforms, is the foundation of medieval Latin Christendom.

## Historical evidence
- **Einhard, *Vita Karoli*** (~830) — by Charlemagne's friend and courtier; the primary biographical source, modeled on Suetonius's *Lives of the Caesars*.
- **Notker the Stammerer, *Gesta Karoli Magni***  (~887) — later, more legendary.
- **Royal Frankish Annals**, **Capitularies** — extensive administrative documentation.
- His own letters, especially the correspondence with Alcuin.

## Works
Charlemagne did not personally compose theological works, but he commissioned and ratified:
- ***Admonitio generalis*** (789) — comprehensive ordinance on religious education, monastic discipline, liturgical practice, the standardization of the Vulgate text, the schools.
- The ***Libri Carolini*** (792, composed by Theodulf of Orleans under royal direction) — Frankish theological riposte to the iconophile Council of Nicaea II (787); shows the Carolingian court positioned between Greek iconophiles and Greek iconoclasts.
- **Numerous capitularies** on legal, liturgical, monetary, and military matters.

## Role in the tradition
Founder of the "Holy Roman Empire" tradition that runs to 1806. His coronation in 800 establishes the medieval pattern of imperial authority *received from* the Pope — a fact that has consequences for centuries of medieval church-state struggle (Investiture Controversy etc.). His educational reforms standardize liturgical practice (the Roman Rite spreads through his empire), the biblical text (the Alcuinian Tours Vulgate becomes dominant), the script (Carolingian minuscule), and the curriculum (the seven liberal arts). The vast majority of classical Latin literature that survives does so in Carolingian copies made during this campaign.

## Connections — substrate and influence
- Charlemagne's coronation (Christmas 800) deliberately Christianizes the **Roman imperial title** — Constantine's heritage, mediated by the Byzantine emperor in Constantinople (whose authority Charlemagne now displaces in the West), is the substrate that legitimates the new arrangement. The "Holy Roman Empire" is **Christianized Roman imperial ideology**.
- The Saxon Wars (772–804) — over 30 years of intermittent campaigns — culminated in the **forced conversion of the pagan Saxons**, the destruction of the Irminsul (a major Saxon sacred pillar / world-tree cult site, destroyed 772), the Massacre of Verden (4,500 Saxons executed in 782 for apostasy), and the *Capitulatio de partibus Saxoniae* (~785) imposing the death penalty for refusing baptism, eating meat in Lent, cremating the dead, and various other pagan practices. Alcuin objected to the forced-baptism policy (Letter 111 of his corpus). This is **one of the most explicit and brutal substrate-Christianization campaigns in European history** — and it is documented as such. Barbero 2004: 36–55.
- The Carolingian liturgical reform replaces Gallican / regional Latin liturgical traditions with Roman / Gregorian usage — a project of Roman-Christian standardization over local substrate practice.
- The Carolingian Renaissance preserves classical Latin learning by integrating it into Christian monastic-and-court culture; the same operation that Cassiodorus had begun at Vivarium and that Alcuin codifies for Aachen.

## Disputes
- **Date of birth.** Recently revised from the traditional 742 to 747 / 748 on the basis of Einhard's age statements (Becher 2003).
- **The 800 coronation.** Whether Charlemagne knew in advance, was surprised, or stage-managed his own surprise — Einhard's claim that he was "surprised" (and would not have come to Rome had he known) is generally considered diplomatic literary fiction.
- **The Massacre of Verden (782).** Whether 4,500 is literal or a textual error for *delocavit* / "deported" (a famous philological proposal by Lintzel etc.) has been debated; mainstream scholarship now accepts the execution figure.
