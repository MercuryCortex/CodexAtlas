Ibn Sina (Avicenna, 980–1037 CE) is the most influential physician after Hippocrates and Galen — and arguably more influential than either in terms of the duration and geographic scope of his impact. His *Canon of Medicine* was the standard medical textbook in Islamic schools from its composition until the 19th century and in **European universities until the late 17th century** — a dominance of 600+ years over two civilizations simultaneously.

## The Canon of Medicine — Five Books, One Synthesis

The *Qānūn fī al-Ṭibb* (Canon of Medicine, c. 1025 CE) organized all known medical knowledge into a systematic whole:

**Book I** — General principles: definition of medicine, causes of health and disease, preservation of health, general treatment principles. Contains Avicenna's argument for **quarantine** (the first rigorous quarantine theory) and his analysis of environmental causes of disease.

**Book II** — Materia Medica: 800 substances organized alphabetically with their properties, dosages, and contraindications. This is the most comprehensive pharmacopoeia produced before modern pharmacy.

**Book III** — Diseases by organ system: head to foot, systematic pathology of every organ with differential diagnosis. The logical organization is essentially modern.

**Book IV** — Conditions crossing multiple organs: fevers, poisons, cosmetics, contagious diseases, surgery.

**Book V** — Drug compounds: pharmaceutical preparations; combination medicines.

## Clinical Innovations

Avicenna's original clinical contributions (beyond systematization):

**Quarantine theory**: *On Contagion* (part of Book 1) proposes that some diseases spread through contaminated air and water, that infected patients must be isolated, and that soil and water can serve as disease vectors. Conceptually 800 years ahead of Germ Theory.

**Clinical trials**: Insisted that drugs be tested in pure form before combination; that testing on animals must precede human use; that the cure of a single case proves nothing — the result must be reproducible. The earliest articulation of the experimental clinical standard.

**Psychosomatic medicine**: Avicenna proposed that **mental and emotional states cause physical disease** — that love, depression, anxiety, and obsessive thought can manifest as somatic illness. His diagnosis of "lovesickness" as a clinical condition (verified by observing pulse elevation when the patient hears the beloved's name) is the first documented psychosomatic clinical test.

**Cancer theory**: Proposed surgical removal of cancer with wide margins; argued that advanced cancer could not be cured; identified ulcerating masses as a distinct dangerous category. His oncological categories map closely onto modern staging.

## The European Transmission

**Gerard of Cremona** (c. 1114–1187 CE) translated the Canon from Arabic into Latin at Toledo — one of the most consequential acts of cultural transmission in European intellectual history. The Canon became the primary medical textbook at:
- University of Bologna (founded 1088)
- University of Montpellier (founded 1220)
- University of Paris (founded c. 1200)
- Oxford and Cambridge

It was required reading in European medical schools for **600 years after its composition**. Vesalius (1543), Harvey (1628), and Sydenham (1680s) had all studied under the Canon before breaking from it.

## Philosophy — The Second Life

Avicenna was simultaneously the leading Islamic philosopher of his era. His philosophical synthesis of Aristotelian metaphysics with Islamic theology influenced:
- Thomas Aquinas (the "floating man" thought experiment → Descartes's cogito)
- Duns Scotus
- Roger Bacon
- The entire scholastic tradition

His "floating man" argument (if a person floats in air with no sensory input, they still know they exist) anticipates Descartes's *cogito ergo sum* by 600 years.

## MASSIVE WIN: One Man, Two Civilizations, 600 Years

Avicenna's Canon was the dominant medical authority in **both** the Islamic world and Europe simultaneously, from c. 1025 CE through c. 1650 CE — approximately 600 years. No other single text has dominated medical education across such a broad geographic and cultural range for such a sustained period. The Canon structured how physicians in Morocco, Persia, Italy, France, and England understood disease — simultaneously, in the same era. This is the most successful act of medical systematization in history.
