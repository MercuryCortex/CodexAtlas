# Pyrrho of Elis

## Identity
Pyrrho of Elis (~-360 to -270) was a Greek philosopher who accompanied Alexander the Great's eastern campaign to India (~-326), met the "gymnosophists" (naked-philosophers — almost certainly Jain ascetics, possibly Buddhist *sramaṇas*, and possibly Ājīvikas) in the Punjab, and returned to found what became the **Pyrrhonist school of Greek skepticism**: a philosophical-practical method built on *epoché* (suspension of judgment about non-evident matters), *adiaphora* (treating all phenomena as not-different-in-themselves), and *ataraxia* (the freedom-from-disturbance that follows). Pyrrho left no writings; his teachings are reconstructed from his pupil Timon of Phlius (also lost, except via fragments) and the systematic late-Pyrrhonist tradition codified by Sextus Empiricus (~2nd c. CE). The claim that Pyrrho's philosophy is **Buddhist in origin** — that he picked up early-Buddhist view-skepticism (Aṭṭhakavagga-style) from his Indian encounter and Hellenized it — is the central thesis of [[beckwith-2015|Beckwith 2015]] *Greek Buddha*: controversial, partially defensible, and the single most consequential modern hypothesis about the [[tradition-greco-buddhism|Greco-Buddhist contact zone]]'s philosophical traffic.

## Historical evidence
- **Diogenes Laertius *Lives of Eminent Philosophers* 9.61-108** — the principal ancient biographical source; reports Pyrrho's Indian travel + the gymnosophist meeting + his teachings + his pupils.
- **Aristocles of Messene** (1st c. CE), preserved in Eusebius *Praeparatio Evangelica* 14.18 — preserves the closest thing to a Pyrrhonist *propositional* statement: the four-fold formula on phenomena (*adiaphora*, *astathmēta*, *anepikrita* — "without difference, not-distinguishable, not-decidable"). Beckwith 2015 argues this triad has the structural form of the Buddhist [[anatman-no-self|*tri-lakṣaṇa*]] (impermanence / suffering / no-self).
- **Sextus Empiricus *Outlines of Pyrrhonism*** (~2nd c. CE) — the systematic late-Pyrrhonist corpus; Sextus is the principal extant Pyrrhonist author. Loeb edition standard.
- **Cicero, Plutarch, Eusebius** — additional ancient mentions.

## Works
None survive (Pyrrho wrote nothing). His school was transmitted via his pupil Timon of Phlius (lost), then through Aenesidemus's revival (~-80) and the late-Pyrrhonist crystallization in Sextus Empiricus.

## Role in the tradition
- **In Greek philosophy**: founder of Pyrrhonism; one of the four main Hellenistic schools (alongside Epicureanism, Stoicism, Aristotelian Lyceum). The Pyrrhonist method (*epoché*) became foundational for skeptical traditions across the ancient and early-modern world (Sextus's *Outlines* rediscovered ~1562 by Henri Estienne triggered a Pyrrhonist revival central to early-modern philosophy — Montaigne, Descartes responding-to, Hume on natural belief).
- **In the Greco-Buddhist hypothesis**: the load-bearing figure. If Buddhist śūnyatā / view-skepticism did transmit to the Greek world via personal philosophical contact, it would have done so through Pyrrho's Indian encounter and his subsequent teaching at Elis. Beckwith 2015 makes the strongest extant case; Halkias 2014 and Kuzminski 2008 are sympathetic; Stoneman 2019 and Bett 2000 are skeptical (Bett pre-Beckwith and independent).
- **In the broader history of philosophy**: Pyrrhonism's modern reception via Sextus's rediscovery (Estienne 1562 → Montaigne *Essais* → Descartes's methodological doubt → Hume's natural-belief response) is one of the central transmission edges of European intellectual history.

## Disputes
- **The Buddhist-origin hypothesis (Beckwith 2015)**: did Pyrrhonist *epoché* derive from Buddhist view-skepticism, or develop independently from Democritus + Cyrenaic + Megarian Greek predecessors? Modern consensus: the *opportunity* for transmission is real (Pyrrho was demonstrably in India for ~18 months with Alexander's army); the *substance* of transmission is debated. Bett 2000 (pre-Beckwith) argues for Democritean + Cyrenaic Greek antecedents as primary; Beckwith 2015 argues these are insufficient and that the four-fold *adiaphora-astathmēta-anepikrita* formula has Buddhist structure. The truth is probably some genuine Indian contact + already-present Greek skeptical materials. Kuzminski 2008 develops the philosophical-comparative case from a sympathetic philosopher's angle.
- **Which Indian school did he meet?** Almost certainly Jain ascetics (the most prominent "naked philosophers" of -326 Punjab); possibly Buddhist *sramaṇas*; possibly Ājīvikas. Identifying the gymnosophists is consequential for the Beckwith hypothesis — if Buddhist, the case is stronger.
- **Authenticity of attributed teachings**: Pyrrho left nothing in writing; the four-fold Aristocles formula is the closest source. All else is school-reconstruction.
