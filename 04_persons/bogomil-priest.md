# Bogomil (the priest)

## Identity
Pop Bogomil ("the priest Bogomil") is the named founder of Bogomilism, a dualist Christian heresy that emerged in Bulgaria in the mid-10th century during the reign of Tsar Peter I (r. 927–969). The name *Bogomil* (Slavonic *bog-* "God" + *mil-* "beloved") may be a personal name or a programmatic designation ("Beloved-of-God"). The earliest source — Presbyter Cosmas's *Sermon against the Bogomils* (~970) — refers to "the priest Bogomil who first taught this heresy in the land of Bulgaria"; later Byzantine sources (Euthymius Zigabenus, Anna Komnene) elaborate on Bogomil doctrine without adding to biographical detail. The Bogomil movement spread through the Balkans, Byzantium, Northern Italy, and Languedoc (where it became Catharism); was the principal medieval dualist religion of Europe; was suppressed only by the Ottoman conquest (in Bosnia, where Bogomilism may have persisted until conversion to Islam).

## Historical evidence
Limited; the name is documented in Cosmas (~970) but no contemporary direct evidence beyond polemical accounts. Some scholars (notably Obolensky 1948) accept the historicity confidently; others treat the named figure as possibly composite or symbolic.

## Works
None surviving; teaching reconstructed from anti-Bogomil polemical literature.

## Role in the tradition
Founder of medieval European Christian dualism. Bogomil teaching probably had roots in earlier Paulician dualism (a movement of Armenian / Anatolian Christian dualists, ~7th–9th c., relocated to Thrace by Byzantine emperors; Stoyanov argues for direct Paulician → Bogomil transmission). Bogomil teaching spread to give the Cathars and other medieval Western European dualists their core doctrine.

## Disputes
Historicity is the main question; scholarship is divided between confident affirmation (Obolensky, Stoyanov, Hamilton, Lambert) and more cautious framing (Moore 2012, who treats medieval dualist movements as substantially independent of each other and as more constructed by inquisitorial labeling than continuous).
