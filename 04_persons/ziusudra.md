# Ziusudra

## Identity
**Ziusudra** (Sumerian *Zi-ud-sura*, "life of long days"; Greek Hellenistic *Xisuthros*) is the earliest named **flood-hero** in any human textual tradition. He appears in the Sumerian **Eridu Genesis** ([[phase-1-016-eridu-genesis-flood]], ~-1600 surviving manuscript reflecting older traditions), in the **Sumerian King List** ([[phase-1-015-sumerian-king-list]]) as the last antediluvian king of Shuruppak, and in **Berossus's *Babyloniaca*** Book II (~-280 BCE) as *Xisuthros* — the Hellenistic transliteration that introduced him to the Greco-Roman world.

The Eridu Genesis narrative core: the gods (Enki, Enlil, and the assembly) decide to destroy humanity with a flood; [[enki-ea]] (the wise god) secretly warns Ziusudra, king of Shuruppak; Ziusudra builds a large boat and survives the flood with his family and animal-pairs; after the flood subsides he sacrifices to the gods and is granted immortality, dwelling thereafter in **Dilmun** (the paradise-land). The narrative structure is the *original* of the Mesopotamian → Hebrew → Christian flood-tradition.

## Historical evidence

- **Sumerian Eridu Genesis fragments** (Nippur, ~-1600 manuscript witness; underlying tradition older) — the Civil 1969 critical edition.
- **Sumerian King List** — names Ziusudra as last antediluvian king of Shuruppak with a reign of 36,000 years (the inflated antediluvian-reign-length is itself a Mesopotamian-canonical literary feature; Lambert and Millard 1969).
- **Berossus's *Babyloniaca*** (~-280 BCE, lost original; preserved in fragments via Eusebius, Syncellus, and Polyhistor) — Hellenistic Greek preservation of the Ziusudra-Xisuthros narrative; the bridge between the Mesopotamian flood-tradition and the Greco-Roman literary world.

Ziusudra is **legendary** — no contemporary epigraphic attestation of him as a historical king; the Shuruppak-flood tradition is mythological-canonical.

## Works
None directly. The **Instructions of Shuruppak** are traditionally addressed *to* Ziusudra by his father Shuruppak — a royal wisdom-text in the Mesopotamian *wisdom*-literature genre.

## Role in the tradition

Ziusudra is the **foundational template** for the entire Mesopotamian → Hebrew → Christian flood-hero tradition:

1. **Ziusudra (Sumerian, ~-2100 oral / -1600 manuscript)** — Eridu Genesis.
2. **Atrahasis (Akkadian, ~-1800)** — see [[atrahasis-flood-hero]]; the same narrative with the hero renamed.
3. **Utnapishtim (Standard Babylonian, ~-1300)** — see [[utnapishtim]]; Gilgamesh XI flood-tablet incorporates the narrative.
4. **Noah (Hebrew, J ~-950, P ~-550)** — Genesis 6–9; the Israelite recension.
5. **Xisuthros (Hellenistic Greek, Berossus ~-280)** — the Greek rendering, transmitted to patristic Christianity.
6. **Deucalion (Greek)** — see [[deucalion]]; the Hellenic parallel-form.

George Smith's 1872 rediscovery ([[event-george-smith-flood-tablet-1872]]) demonstrated the Ziusudra-Atrahasis-Utnapishtim chain *predates* the Hebrew Noah by over a millennium — one of the most consequential single discoveries in the history of religion.

## Disputes

- **Historicity.** No contemporary attestation. The Shuruppak Early Dynastic II–III archaeological strata show *some* evidence of regional flooding events (Mallowan 1947), but no specific Ziusudra-king is independently documented.
- **Relationship to Atrahasis and Utnapishtim.** Modern scholarship (George 2003; Lambert and Millard 1969) treats Ziusudra, Atrahasis, and Utnapishtim as three names of the same legendary figure across three successive Mesopotamian literary traditions, with the same narrative function.
- **Berossus's Xisuthros = Ziusudra identity.** Established by Lambert and other Assyriologists; the Hellenistic Greek transliteration preserves the Sumerian form recognizably.
