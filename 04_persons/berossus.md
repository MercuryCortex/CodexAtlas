# Berossus of Babylon

## Identity
**Berossus** (Greek *Βήρωσσος / Berossos*; from Akkadian *Bēl-rēʾušunu*, "Bel is their shepherd") was a Babylonian priest of Bel-Marduk (~340–280 BCE) who composed the ***Babyloniaca*** (Babylonian History) in Greek — a three-book history of Babylon from creation through the contemporary Seleucid period, **dedicated to Antiochus I Soter** (~-281 BCE). The work is **lost in its original form** but preserved fragmentarily through Eusebius, Syncellus, Josephus, Polyhistor, and other Greek-and-Latin Christian historians who used it as their principal Babylonian source.

Berossus's *Babyloniaca* is **the single most consequential bridge between Mesopotamian cuneiform tradition and the Hellenistic-Greek-language world** before the modern Assyriological recovery (post-1872). Through Berossus:

1. **The Mesopotamian flood-tradition (Ziusudra → Xisuthros) entered the Greek-speaking world** — Eusebius preserves Berossus's flood-narrative naming the hero as *Xisuthros* (the Hellenized form of Ziusudra), with the full pattern (divine warning → ark-construction → flood → mountain-landing → sacrifice → immortalization).
2. **The Mesopotamian *apkallu* (antediluvian-sage) tradition was transmitted** — Oannes the fish-man-sage who emerged from the sea to teach humanity, the ten antediluvian kings paralleling the Hebrew ten antediluvian patriarchs.
3. **Babylonian astrology entered Hellenistic intellectual culture** — Pliny *Natural History* 7.123 names Berossus among the founders of Hellenistic astrology; the *Babyloniaca* established the cuneiform astronomical tradition's Greek reception.

## Historical evidence

- **Berossus's Babyloniaca fragments** preserved in:
  - **Eusebius, *Chronicle*** — the principal source for the flood narrative (preserved via Armenian and Syncellus's Greek).
  - **George Syncellus, *Ecloga Chronographica*** (early 9th c. CE Byzantine) — major fragments preserved.
  - **Josephus, *Antiquities* 1.93** — uses Berossus for the flood narrative.
  - **Alexander Polyhistor (1st c. BCE)** — extensive citation, surviving through Eusebius.
- **Pliny, *Natural History* 7.123** — Berossus as Hellenistic astrologer-priest; the Athenians erected his statue in their gymnasium with a gilded tongue for his prophetic accuracy.
- **Vitruvius, *De Architectura* 9.6.2** — Berossus on lunar phases (half-light-half-dark moon-ball theory).
- **Verbrugghe and Wickersham 1996** — standard modern English translation of all surviving Berossus fragments.

Berossus is **documented**: extensively cited by named Hellenistic-Roman and Byzantine authors across multiple traditions; his statue on Athens is attested; his work's existence is undisputed.

## Works

- **Babyloniaca** (in 3 books, Greek; lost in original):
  - Book 1: Creation, cosmogony, Oannes the fish-sage, antediluvian kings.
  - Book 2: From the flood (Xisuthros narrative) through the kings of Babylonia to Nabonassar (-748).
  - Book 3: Nabonassar through Berossus's contemporary Seleucid period.
- **Astrological writings** (lost; mentioned in Hellenistic and Roman sources).

## Role in the tradition

Berossus's role is uniquely consequential as the **single Hellenistic-Greek-language preserver of the Mesopotamian tradition** between Alexander's conquest (-331) and the modern cuneiform decipherment (Grotefend 1802; Rawlinson 1837–1857). For over **two thousand years**, the *only* documented access Western intellectual culture had to Mesopotamian flood, cosmogony, and antediluvian-king traditions was via Berossus's *Babyloniaca* fragments — making him the load-bearing transmission node from cuneiform Mesopotamian → Hellenistic Greek → patristic Christian → medieval European reception.

The 1872 [[event-george-smith-flood-tablet-1872]] discovery of the cuneiform Gilgamesh XI flood-tablet is the moment when *direct* access to the Mesopotamian flood-tradition is recovered, displacing Berossus's mediated preservation as the sole channel. Subsequent Assyriological work (Lambert + Millard 1969 *Atrahasis*; George 2003 *Babylonian Gilgamesh Epic*) confirms that Berossus's *Babyloniaca* preserved the Mesopotamian tradition with substantial fidelity — Berossus's Xisuthros is recognizably Ziusudra, the ark-narrative pattern is preserved, the antediluvian-king list parallels the Sumerian King List.

## Disputes

- **Date of composition.** *Babyloniaca* dedicated to Antiochus I Soter (-281 to -261); composed ~-290 to -281 is most likely.
- **Berossus's identity.** Generally accepted as historical; one Hellenistic source (Vitruvius) names a Berossus on Kos (the Hellenistic medical-astrological center); whether this is the same Babylonian Berossus is debated.
- **Reliability of preserved fragments.** Eusebius and Syncellus are *secondary* preservers; the fragments may include Christian-redactional or Hellenistic-Greek modifications. Verbrugghe and Wickersham 1996 attempt to reconstruct the original Berossan voice; substantial uncertainties remain.
- **Berossus's astrological vs. historiographical primary identity.** Pliny and Vitruvius emphasize astrology; the surviving fragments emphasize historiography. Both probably authentic; the *Babyloniaca* itself drew on Mesopotamian astrological-cosmological materials.
