Johannes Kepler (1571–1630) is the scientist who discovered the laws of planetary motion — and simultaneously the last major Western thinker who pursued the Pythagorean music-of-the-spheres program as his primary cosmological framework. His third law of planetary motion, that T²∝a³ (the square of orbital period is proportional to the cube of orbital radius), emerged directly from his search for harmonic ratios among planetary velocities.

The *Harmonices Mundi* (1619) is the most detailed post-antique statement of the Pythagorean doctrine that the cosmos is a musical instrument. Kepler computes the angular velocities of each planet at perihelion and aphelion and shows that the ratios between planetary velocity extremes correspond to musical intervals. Saturn and Jupiter sing a great bass; Mars sings a nearly monotone major third; Earth sings a near-unison of minor second, the "mi-fa-mi" Kepler reads as *miseria* and *fames* — misery and famine — in the tonal language of musica mundana.

The third law falls out of these computations as a mathematical byproduct. Kepler was looking for Pythagoras; he found Newton's law of gravitation's mathematical precondition.
