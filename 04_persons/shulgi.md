# Shulgi

## Identity
Shulgi (r. ~-2094 to -2047 BCE) was the second king of the Third Dynasty of Ur and the longest-reigning Ur III ruler — 48 years on the throne. He was the son of [[ur-nammu]] and inherited the empire his father had founded; under his reign the Ur III state reached its administrative-bureaucratic apogee, producing what Sallaberger has called "the best-documented century in pre-modern history" — over 120,000 cuneiform administrative tablets survive from his reign alone. Shulgi is unique among Sumerian kings for **self-divinization in his own lifetime** (from ~regnal year 21, the *dingir* divine-determinative is prefixed to his name on inscriptions) — a step taken before him only by [[naram-sin]] of Akkad. His extensive royal hymn cycle (Shulgi A through X) celebrates him as scholar, warrior, runner, musician, lover of the goddess Inanna, builder, and administrator — a 360-degree royal panegyric without rival in Sumerian literature.

## Historical evidence
Among the most thoroughly documented monarchs of the ancient world:
1. **The Ur III administrative archives** — over 120,000 cuneiform tablets from the *Bala* (provincial tax) and *Drehem* (royal livestock administration) archives, dated by year-name to his reign. The economic, administrative, and bureaucratic life of his state is documented to a level not equaled until the medieval European chanceries.
2. **The Shulgi hymn cycle** (Klein 1981; ETCSL 2.4.2.01–24) — 24 royal hymns in Sumerian.
3. **Royal inscriptions** on bricks, votive objects, and stelae (Frayne RIME 3/2).
4. **48 named regnal year-names**, each commemorating a major event.
5. **Cylinder seals** of his officials and family members.
6. **Sumerian King List** — entry attributing him a 46-year reign (close to the documented 48).

## Works
- **Shulgi hymns A–X**: an extraordinary cycle of royal self-praise. Highlights:
  - *Shulgi A* — the king's run from Nippur to Ur and back in one day (~200 km), claiming athletic divinity.
  - *Shulgi B* — Shulgi's scribal education and intellectual accomplishments; he claims literacy in Sumerian, Akkadian, Elamite, Amorite, and other languages.
  - *Shulgi C, D, E, X* — military campaigns.
  - *Shulgi P* — the sacred marriage with Inanna.
- **Royal inscriptions** including the famous "Letter of Shulgi" administrative correspondence and the construction inscriptions of the Drehem cult-livestock center, the Bala provincial system, and the standardization of weights and measures.

## Role in the tradition
Shulgi is the archetype of the Sumerian *literate warrior-king*. The Old Babylonian scribal curriculum at Nippur, Ur, and other centers used his hymns as standard exercises, transmitting his image as the paradigmatic Sumerian ruler for over five centuries after his death. His self-divinization established a model that subsequent rulers (Amar-Sin, Shu-Sin, Ibbi-Sin of Ur; later Shulgi-like figures in Isin) would partly follow — though never to the same totalizing degree.

In modern Assyriology Shulgi has become a focal figure for studies of ancient bureaucracy, royal ideology, and the formation of literate kingship. The contrast between his reign's administrative density and the spectacular collapse of the Ur III state under his great-grandson Ibbi-Sin (within forty years of his death) is one of the most studied transitions in ancient history.

## Disputes
- **Self-divinization motive**. Whether religious-theological commitment, political-imperial ideology, or imitation of Naram-Sin drove Shulgi's self-divinization is unresolved.
- **Authorship of hymns**. Royal hymns are conventionally attributed to court scribes composing in the king's name; the question of Shulgi's own literary involvement is debated, given his hymn-B claims to literary competence.
- **48 versus 46 regnal years**. The administrative archives support 48; the Sumerian King List gives 46. The discrepancy is small and may reflect counting conventions.
