# Pelagius

## Identity
Pelagius (c. 354–418) was a lay ascetic teacher of British — possibly Irish — origin who taught at Rome from the 380s and became the eponym of the doctrine his opponents named **Pelagianism**. Contemporaries describe him as a large, sober, morally rigorous man of no clerical rank; Augustine, before the breach, called him "a holy man" and "a saintly man, as I am told." His theology grew from a single pastoral conviction: that a genuine divine command implies a genuine God-given ability to obey it. If God commands holiness, holiness must be *possible* by the powers God has already given human nature — otherwise the command, and moral responsibility itself, would be a fiction. In the Atlas he is the person-endpoint of [[doctrine-pelagianism]] and the named adversary against whom [[augustine-of-hippo]] built the Latin doctrine of grace and original sin.

## Historical evidence
Almost nothing of Pelagius survives in his own uncontested name. His most important work, the **Commentary on the Thirteen Epistles of Paul** (*Expositiones XIII epistularum Pauli*, c. 405–410), came down through the Middle Ages under the name of Jerome or a pseudo-Jerome and was only reassigned to Pelagius by modern scholarship (Souter's 1922–31 critical edition; Evans 1968). His **Letter to Demetrias** (413) — an exhortation to a young Roman noblewoman entering the ascetic life — is the fullest surviving statement of his own voice. The rest is reconstructed from the **hostile witness** of his opponents: his lost *De natura* (c. 414) is quoted and refuted point-by-point in Augustine's *De natura et gratia* (415); his lost *De libero arbitrio* survives as fragments in Augustine and Jerome; and Augustine's *De gestis Pelagii* (417) preserves the acts of his Eastern trial. This documentary situation is the root of the modern scholarly caution (Bonner 1972; Rees 1988) that "Pelagianism" as condemned may be substantially the construct of Augustine, Jerome, and the more radical disciple Caelestius rather than Pelagius's own moderate teaching.

## The controversy (405–418)
Pelagius drew a threefold distinction — the **capacity** (*posse*) to do good, the **willing** (*velle*) of it, and the **doing** (*esse*) of it. Only the first is God's permanent gift to nature and is inamissible; the willing and doing are the human being's own. Grace in this scheme is real but largely **enabling from without**: the gift of a good created nature, the illumination of the Law, the moral example of Christ, and the forgiveness of past sins in baptism — not an interior force that itself produces the good will. The tradition holds that Pelagius was scandalised, around 405 in Rome, by a line in Augustine's *Confessions* (X.29), *"da quod iubes et iube quod vis"* ("Give what you command, and command what you will"), which seemed to him to dissolve human effort into divine determinism.

The **Sack of Rome (410)** drove Pelagius and his disciple Caelestius to Carthage, into Augustine's own province, and turned a difference of emphasis into an institutional battle. Caelestius, the more systematic and radical disciple, was condemned at **Carthage (411)** for six propositions — including that Adam was created mortal, that his sin harmed only himself, and that infants are born in the state of Adam before the Fall (so that infant baptism cannot be for the remission of an inherited sin). Pelagius himself, tried in the East, was **acquitted at the Synod of Diospolis (Lydda, 415)** — a result that infuriated the African bishops, who reconvened at Carthage and Milevis (416), secured Pope **Innocent I's** condemnation (417), and after Zosimus's brief reinstatement obtained the decisive canons of **Carthage (418)** and the imperial rescript of Honorius. After 418 Pelagius vanishes from the record; his death (probably in Palestine or Egypt) is undated. The doctrine was condemned ecumenically at **Ephesus (431)** and its mediating afterlife — Semi-Pelagianism, associated with [[john-cassian]] — was settled against at the **Second Council of Orange (529)**.

## Cross-tradition significance
Pelagius supplies the Atlas's Latin-Christian occupant of the **libertarian free-will pole** in the trans-traditional controversy over divine sovereignty and human agency ([[free-will-vs-determinism]]). His insistence that the God-given capacity to choose the good is intrinsic and inamissible — so that a human being can originate the *initium fidei* without a prior interior grace — is the structural counterpart of the Islamic **Qadarī–Muʿtazilī** doctrine of *qadar*, that human beings genuinely originate their own acts and are therefore justly rewarded or punished ([[doctrine-mutazila-rationalist-kalam]]). The resemblance must be stated as **convergence, not transmission**: the Basran *qadar* debate arises in the 8th century with no plausible line of contact with the 5th-century Latin controversy, and the two reason from wholly different scriptural bases (Romans 5 and Genesis 3 versus Qur'anic theodicy). What recurs is the *problem-shape* — a monotheism affirming both an omnipotent provident God and a genuinely responsible agent is pressed toward one of two poles — not any borrowing (W. Montgomery Watt, *Free Will and Predestination in Early Islam*, 1948). At the opposite, determinist pole stand Augustinian prevenient grace and, in Second-Temple Judaism, the Qumran [[two-spirits-doctrine]].

Pelagius's more consequential legacy is negative and Christian-internal: by denying inherited guilt he forced [[augustine-of-hippo]] to systematise the doctrine of [[original-sin|original sin]] and prevenient grace that would define Western theology in both its Catholic and Reformation registers. The grace-vs-free-will problem the controversy framed is a **taproot rather than a closed episode**, resurfacing at the Reformation (Luther's *De servo arbitrio* against Erasmus, 1524–25), in the Catholic *De Auxiliis* dispute, in Jansenism (accused of reviving Augustine) and Arminianism (accused of reviving Pelagius). "Pelagian" survives to the present as the standard theological epithet for any doctrine thought to concede too much to unaided human initiative.

## Disputes
- **What did Pelagius actually teach?** Bonner (1972) and Rees (1988) argue that much of the condemned "Pelagianism" is the construct of its opponents and of the radical Caelestius; Pelagius's genuine surviving works (the Romans commentary, the *Letter to Demetrias*) are markedly more moderate than the system attributed to him. How much of the condemned doctrine Pelagius himself held is genuinely uncertain.
- **Origin and dates.** His British (or Irish) origin rests on the epithets "Brito" and "Britannus" in Jerome, Augustine, and Prosper; the birth year is a convention (c. 350–360) and the death is undated and unlocated. Some scholars place the death after 418.
- **Romans 5:12.** The whole controversy turns on *eph' hō pantes hēmarton* — Pelagius reads sin spreading "by imitation" of Adam, Augustine (via the Vulgate's *in quo*, "in whom") "by descent." The Latin rendering is a textual contingency that hardened the Western inherited-guilt reading; see [[doctrine-original-sin-augustinian]].
- **The Manichaean counter-charge.** Pelagius's ablest successor, Julian of Eclanum, turned the heresy accusation back on Augustine, charging him with **crypto-Manichaeism** — smuggling a substantial evil (concupiscence, the fallen body) back into creation under a Christian name. Modern scholarship (Brown 1967) treats Julian's critique as the sharpest ancient reading of the Manichaean residue in Augustine's anthropology, while judging the Church's verdict final.
