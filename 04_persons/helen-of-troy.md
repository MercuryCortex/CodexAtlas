# Helen of Troy

## Identity

Helen (Ἑλένη, *Helénē*) is the daughter of [[zeus]] and the most beautiful of mortal women in Greek tradition — queen of Sparta, wife of Menelaus, and the woman whose departure to Troy with Paris is the *casus belli* of the Trojan War. She is a mortal of the heroic generation rather than an Olympian, yet she sits unusually close to the divine: her father is Zeus in every main version, her brothers are the divinised [[the-dioskouroi]], and she herself received cult at Therapne outside Sparta — Isocrates says the Spartans sacrificed to Helen and Menelaus there *as gods, not as heroes* (Or. 10.63), and Herodotus 6.61 tells of an ugly infant made beautiful by a daily visit to her Therapne shrine. In the Atlas she is typed as a person on the model of [[theseus]] — a legendary figure of saga with a real hero-cult — while the scholarly argument that she is a faded goddess (Clader 1976) is recorded under Disputes.

Two birth-traditions compete from the archaic period onward. In Homer she is simply the daughter of Zeus and Leda, wife of Tyndareus (Il. 3.199, 3.418; Od. 4.184). In the lost cyclic *Cypria* (fr. 10 West, preserved at Athenaeus 8.334b-d) her mother is [[nemesis-greek|Nemesis]]: the goddess flees Zeus through changing shapes, is overtaken in the form of a goose, and lays an egg that is brought to Leda, who rears the child. The Nemesis-parentage is a moral genealogy — the war-bringing beauty is literally the daughter of Retribution — and it is the version the Rhamnous cult of Nemesis knew (Pausanias 1.33.7-8 describes the Rhamnous statue base showing Leda bringing Helen to Nemesis). Sappho fr. 166 already knows the egg.

## In the Greek myth-cycle

**The two abductions.** Helen is stolen twice. As a girl she is carried off by [[theseus]] and Pirithous and lodged at Aphidna; her brothers the Dioskouroi invade Attica, recover her, and take Theseus's mother Aithra into servitude in reprisal (Apollodorus, Epit. 1.23; Plutarch, *Thes.* 31). The episode is the Attic rehearsal of the Trojan theft — in both, an oath-bound coalition or a divine twin-pair must fetch the stolen woman home.

**The oath and the war.** The Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women* (frr. 196-204 M-W) catalogues the princes of Greece wooing Helen at Sparta; Tyndareus, on Odysseus's advice, binds all the suitors by oath to defend the marriage-rights of whichever man is chosen. When Paris — judging the three goddesses and awarded the most beautiful woman by Aphrodite (the *Cypria*'s Judgment) — takes Helen from Sparta while a guest of Menelaus, the oath converts a private wrong into the Achaean coalition. The wrong itself is framed throughout Greek tradition as a violation of *xenia*, guest-friendship under Zeus Xenios: Paris steals his host's wife (with treasure) from under his host's roof, and the war is the enforcement of hospitality's law. The Iliad's Helen is among the poem's most self-aware figures — she weaves the battles fought for her into a web (Il. 3.125-128), reviles herself before Hector (6.343-358), and speaks the last lament over his body (24.761-775).

**The nepenthe scene (Od. 4.219-232).** Back in Sparta after the war, entertaining Telemachus, Helen casts into the wine a drug (*phármakon*) called *nēpenthés* — grief-banishing, *ákholon* (anger-stilling), *kakōn epílēthon hapántōn* (bringing forgetfulness of every ill) — so potent that a man who drank it would not weep that day though his mother and father died. Homer says she had it from Polydamna, wife of Thon, in Egypt, where the grain-giving earth bears the most *phármaka*, many good in mixture and many baneful, and where every man is a physician. This is an early and much-discussed Greek drug passage: it attributes pharmacological pre-eminence to Egypt explicitly, and it makes Helen the mistress of a mind-altering *pharmakon* administered in a ritualised host-guest setting — the vault's [[sacred-pharmacology]] thread in epic form. Modern identifications of nepenthe (opium is the perennial candidate) are speculative and not stated in the text.

**Cult.** Beyond Therapne (votive activity at the Menelaion site from the late 8th century BCE), Rhodes worshipped Helen **Dendritis** ("of the tree"): Pausanias 3.19.10 preserves the local story that Helen, exiled after Menelaus's death, was hanged from a tree by the serving-women of Polyxo — an aetiology for a tree-cult. Pausanias 3.19.11-13 also reports the Black Sea tradition that Helen dwells after death with Achilles on the White Island (Leuke). Theocritus's *Idyll* 18, the Epithalamium of Helen, preserves the Spartan maiden-chorus and plane-tree rite in literary form.

## The phantom and the Egyptian Helen

The most far-reaching ancient revision of Helen is arguably the **eidolon**: the claim that she never went to Troy at all. Stesichorus's *Palinode* (PMG 192, quoted at Plato, *Phaedrus* 243a) recants the blame-tradition — the true story, he now sings, is that Helen never boarded the ships; a phantom double (*eídōlon*) went to Troy in her place, and the war was fought for a wraith. The legend attached to the poem (Stesichorus struck blind for defaming Helen, healed upon recanting) shows how charged her guilt already was. [[herodotus|Herodotus]] 2.112-120 gives a rationalised, phantom-free variant on the authority of the priests of Memphis: Paris, blown to Egypt, was stripped of Helen by King Proteus; she sat out the entire war in Memphis; the Trojans told the besieging Greeks the truth — that they did not have her — and were not believed. Herodotus draws the theological moral that Troy's destruction despite the truthful denial shows great wrongs receiving great punishments from the gods. [[euripides|Euripides]]' *Helen* (-412) stages the full phantom plot — the chaste Helen waiting in Egypt while Greeks and Trojans die for her image — where his *Trojan Women* (-415) had staged the opposite Helen, the self-exculpating survivor debated by Hecuba. [[gorgias|Gorgias]]'s *Encomium of Helen* (DK 82 B11) completes the classical dossier: a display-speech acquitting her under all four possible causes of her going (the gods, force, persuasive *logos*, or *erōs*), with its famous doctrine that speech acts on the soul as drugs (*phármaka*) act on the body — the sophist's theoretical twin of Helen's own nepenthe.

## Cross-tradition significance

**The Indo-European case (contested).** A substantial scholarly line reads Helen as the Greek reflex of a Proto-Indo-European **Sun-maiden**. The structure: a radiant female — daughter of the sky-god — flanked by the Divine Twins, is found in Vedic India (Sūryā́, daughter of the sun, whose bridal procession the twin Aśvins escort in the marriage-hymn RV 10.85) and in Baltic song (the sun-daughter courted by the Dieva dēli, "God's sons"); in Greece the same triad is Helen beside the Dioskouroi, whose own Aśvin cognacy is among the most secure results of comparative mythology (West 2007). On this reading Helen's abduction and recovery is the Greek instance of a PIE sun-maiden mytheme, and Skutsch (1987) argued her very name belongs to the Indo-European sun-vocabulary (*Swelenā). The dissent is equally serious: Edmunds (*Stealing Helen*, 2016) treats the abducted-wife narrative as an **international tale-type** attested far outside Indo-European speech-areas, and regards both the etymology and the sun-maiden reconstruction as insecure; Blondell (2013) likewise reports the name as unexplained. The honest state of the question: the Dioskouroi-Aśvins cognacy is secure, Helen's own place in that inherited structure is plausible but disputed, and the abduction-plot alone proves nothing about inheritance.

**Helen and Sītā (structural parallel, not transmission).** The comparison with [[sita]] is the classic Greece-India pairing of the abducted radiant wife: a queen of more-than-mortal birth is stolen (Paris / Rāvaṇa), a war of recovery follows, and the returned wife must face a test of her fidelity (Menelaus's raised and dropped sword; Sītā's fire-ordeal). Most striking is the shared **phantom-double** solution to the problem of the heroine's purity: as Stesichorus and Euripides send an eidolon to Troy, the later Rāmāyaṇa tradition (*Kūrma Purāṇa*; *Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa*) has Rāvaṇa seize an illusory Māyā-Sītā while the real Sītā hides in the fire. Doniger (1999) develops the comparison at length. No transmission channel is claimed by mainstream scholarship in either direction: the parallel is either common Indo-European inheritance (if the sun-maiden case is accepted) or independent convergence on the same narrative logic — both positions should be kept on the table.

**The Simonian Helen (documented transmission).** Helen's strangest afterlife is inside proto-Gnostic theology. Justin Martyr (*1 Apol.* 26) and Irenaeus (*Adv. Haer.* 1.23.2) report that [[simon-magus]] travelled with a former prostitute of Tyre named Helena whom he proclaimed the **Ennoia** — the first Thought of God, fallen into matter, transmigrating from body to body, imprisoned in one female form after another, and once incarnate as the very Helen for whose sake the Trojan War was fought; Simon had descended to redeem her. Here the Greek epic figure is documentably absorbed into the descent-and-rescue structure that later Gnostic systems elaborate as the fall of [[sophia-gnostic|Sophia]] — a genuine, source-attested transmission of Helen into a new theological system, unlike the structural parallels above. Her later Western afterlife (the Helen conjured for Faustus in Marlowe and Goethe) continues the same pattern of Helen as the summoned image of desire.

## Disputes

**Goddess or heroine?** Clader (1976) and others argue Helen was originally a goddess — pointing to the Therapne cult (where Isocrates says she was worshipped as a god), the Rhodian tree-cult, and the Indo-European background — secondarily humanised into an epic heroine. The alternative reading takes the cult as ordinary heroisation of an epic figure, on the Theseus pattern. The Atlas types her as a person and flags the split rather than resolving it.

**The name.** Skutsch's sun-etymology is attractive to the comparative school and rejected or bracketed by sceptics (Edmunds 2016; Blondell 2013 reports the name as without agreed etymology). Ancient folk-etymology (Aeschylus, *Ag.* 689-690: *helénas, hélandros, heléptolis* — ship-destroyer, man-destroyer, city-destroyer) is wordplay, not evidence.

**Guilt and agency.** Whether Helen went willingly is unresolved *inside* the tradition itself and was argued in antiquity: blame (Alcaeus; the Hecuba of *Trojan Women*) against defence (Sappho fr. 16's reframing; Stesichorus's recantation; Gorgias's four-cause acquittal; Euripides' *Helen*). The vault records the debate as constitutive of the figure rather than adjudicating it.

**Historicity.** No historical Helen is recoverable; what is historical is the cult, the Bronze-Age setting of the saga, and the debate itself.
