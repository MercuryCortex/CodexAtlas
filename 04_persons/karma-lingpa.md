# Karma Lingpa

## Identity
Karma Lingpa (~1326-1386) was a 14th-c. Tibetan *tertön* (*gter ston* — "treasure-revealer") of the Nyingma school of [[tradition-vajrayana-buddhism|Vajrayāna]] Buddhism, and the credited revealer of the **terma cycle from which the [[phase-5-029-bardo-thodol|*Bardo Thödol*]] derives**. In Nyingma terma theology, the cycle is treated not as Karma Lingpa's own composition but as a hidden text concealed in the 8th c. by [[padmasambhava|Padmasambhava]] (Guru Rinpoche) for future revelation when the appropriate karmic conditions ripened. Karma Lingpa, recognized as the destined revealer (a reincarnation of one of Padmasambhava's original 25 disciples), retrieved the cycle from Gampodar mountain in the Dakpo region of southern Tibet. The full revealed cycle is the *Zhi khro dgongs pa rang grol* (*Self-Liberation through the Intention of the Peaceful and Wrathful Deities*); the *Bardo Thödol* — the so-called "Tibetan Book of the Dead" — is one section devoted to navigation of the intermediate-state (*bardo*) between death and rebirth.

## Historical evidence
- **Nyingma school terma lineage records** (Dudjom Rinpoche 1991) — internal transmission documentation including Karma Lingpa's *vita*, the Gampodar revelation account, and the subsequent transmission chain through his son Nyida Choje, then Nyida Sangye, on to the present holders of the lineage.
- **The terma text itself** — the *Zhi khro dgongs pa rang grol* cycle (extensively studied, modern critical edition Lopez 2011 + Coleman/Jinpa/Dorje 2005).
- **The 19th-c. Western reception**: Walter Evans-Wentz's *Tibetan Book of the Dead* (1927, Oxford UP) — Karma Lingpa's text in Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup's translation, heavily framed by Evans-Wentz's Theosophical-Jungian editorial introduction. Lopez 2011 deconstructs this reception layer and recovers the actual Karma Lingpa text.

## Works
- ***Zhi khro dgongs pa rang grol*** — the full revealed terma cycle (the *Bardo Thödol* is one part).
- **The *Bardo Thödol*** itself ([[phase-5-029-bardo-thodol]]) — the bardo-navigation section that became globally famous in 20th-c. Western Buddhist reception.

The terma-theology question of *authorship* — is Karma Lingpa the "author" or the "revealer"? — is internally distinguished in Nyingma terminology: he is the *tertön*, the one through whom the text emerges into time; the *author* is Padmasambhava who concealed it in the 8th c. Modern academic scholarship typically treats Karma Lingpa as the effective compositional source, with the Padmasambhava-attribution operating as a doctrinal warrant within the terma theology.

## Role in the tradition
- **In Nyingma Vajrayāna**: a major *tertön* — one of the principal revealers of the Padmasambhava-attributed terma corpus. His *Zhi khro dgongs pa rang grol* cycle is central to Nyingma death-and-bardo ritual practice.
- **In modern global Buddhism**: through the *Bardo Thödol*'s 20th-c. Western reception via Evans-Wentz 1927 (and its enormous influence — C.G. Jung's psychological commentary, Aldous Huxley's *Doors of Perception*, Timothy Leary's 1960s *The Psychedelic Experience* explicit use as a meditation manual, the modern Western death-cafe + hospice-Buddhism movement), Karma Lingpa's revelation has had a disproportionate cultural-historical impact relative to most 14th-c. Tibetan tertöns.

## Disputes
- **Date of revelation**: Tibetan-tradition dating places the revelation ~14th c.; modern scholarship has not seriously contested this. The Gampodar mountain location is preserved in Nyingma topographical-pilgrimage tradition.
- **Terma authorship**: see the Padmasambhava / Karma Lingpa distinction above. Both readings (concealment-revealer + creative-compositional revealer) are operative in different scholarly frames.
- **Evans-Wentz's editorial framing**: Lopez 2011 demonstrates how Evans-Wentz's 1927 Theosophical-Jungian framing constructed the "Tibetan Book of the Dead" as a Western object substantially distinct from the actual Karma Lingpa *Bardo Thödol* — a major case-study in cross-cultural reception-history.
