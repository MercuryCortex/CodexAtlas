# Ibn Isḥāq (Muḥammad ibn Isḥāq ibn Yasār)

## Identity
**Muḥammad ibn Isḥāq ibn Yasār al-Muṭṭalibī (~704–767 CE)** is the **principal Islamic-tradition biographer of Muhammad** and the author of the foundational *Sīra* (biography) of the Prophet — ***Sirat Rasul Allah*** (Life of the Messenger of God). Born in Medina to a family of Mawlā (non-Arab) freedmen-clients of the Banu Hāshim, Ibn Isḥāq was trained in the Medinan hadith and historical-narrative traditions; he later moved to Iraq under Abbasid patronage (the Abbasid caliph al-Manṣūr, r. 754-775), where he composed the *Sīra* in his mature form. **The original Ibn Isḥāq *Sīra* is lost**; the work survives in three principal forms: **(1) the canonical recension by Ibn Hishām (~833)**, which is the form by which the *Sīra* is normally cited; **(2) extensive quotation in Ṭabarī's *Tārīkh* (~915)**, which preserves Ibn Isḥāq material not in the Ibn Hishām recension (especially the controversial materials Ibn Hishām edited out, including the *gharānīq* / Satanic Verses narrative — [[satanic-verses-incident]] — in its original Ibn Isḥāq form); **(3) various other indirect quotations** in later Islamic-tradition material (Ibn Saʿd, Yāqūt, etc.). The *Sīra* is the **single most influential source for the Islamic-tradition biography of Muhammad** and the founding work of the Arabic *sīra*-genre. (Guillaume 1955; Rubin 1995; Donner 2010.)

## Historical evidence
Documented. Ibn al-Nadim's *Fihrist* (~988) provides the canonical Islamic-bibliographical entry. The Ibn Hisham recension is preserved in multiple Arabic manuscript traditions; Ṭabarī's independent preservation of Ibn Isḥāq material is well-documented. Ibn Isḥāq's biography is recoverable from the early Islamic-biographical literature (al-Khaṭīb al-Baghdādī's *Tārīkh Baghdād*, Ibn ʿAsākir's *Tārīkh Madīnat Dimashq*, etc.).

## Works
- **[[phase-5-043-ibn-ishaq-sirat-rasul-allah|*Sirat Rasul Allah*]]** — the foundational Islamic-tradition biography of Muhammad. **Three-part structure** in the original Ibn Isḥāq form (per the Ibn Hisham recension): **(1) *al-Mubtadaʾ*** (The Beginning — from creation through the prophetic genealogy back to Adam; this section is substantially abbreviated in Ibn Hisham); **(2) *al-Mabʿath*** (The Sending — Muhammad's birth, early life, the first revelation, the Meccan period through the Hegira); **(3) *al-Maghāzī*** (The Campaigns — the Medinan period: Battles of Badr, Uḥud, the Trench; treaty of Hudaybiyya; conquest of Mecca; the *ridda* / apostasy-wars context; Muhammad's death). The Ibn Hisham recension is closely-followed-but-edited (Ibn Hisham removed material he regarded as theologically unfit, including the *gharānīq* incident). The fuller Ibn Isḥāq corpus is partly recoverable through Ṭabarī's independent preservation.

## Role in the tradition
- **Founder of the *Sīra*-genre.** Ibn Isḥāq's *Sīra* is the foundational work of Arabic-Islamic biographical-historical literature. Subsequent *sīra* works (al-Wāqidī's *Kitāb al-Maghāzī* ~822; Ibn Saʿd's *Ṭabaqāt al-Kubrā* ~840; al-Balādhurī's *Ansāb al-Ashrāf* ~890; Ṭabarī's *Tārīkh* ~915) **all engage Ibn Isḥāq as a primary source**. The Ibn Hisham recension becomes the canonical form by which the *Sīra* is read in subsequent Islamic-tradition reception.
- **Tension with hadith-orthodoxy.** Ibn Isḥāq was criticized by some classical Sunni hadith-scholars (especially **Mālik ibn Anas** of Medina, founder of the [[tradition-islam|Mālikī]] *madhhab*) for what they regarded as insufficiently rigorous *isnād* (chain-of-transmission) standards in the *Sīra*'s narrative material; the *Sīra* is therefore canonically read as a **biographical-historical** source rather than as a **hadith** source per se. The post-Sunni-classical hadith-tradition (post-Bukhārī, post-Muslim, post-Mālik) makes a sharper distinction between *sīra* / biographical-narrative and *ḥadīth* / legal-doctrinal-narrative than Ibn Isḥāq did, and Ibn Isḥāq's *Sīra* is partly read as a casualty of this distinction.
- **Source-critical status in modern critical scholarship.** Modern critical scholarship (Watt 1953-56; Rubin 1995; Donner 2010) treats the *Sīra* as **the principal Islamic-tradition source for Muhammad's biography**, with awareness of the source-critical issues: the *Sīra* is c. 130-150 years after Muhammad's death; it is the product of late-7th and early-8th-c. Medinan and Iraqi traditional-historical scholarship; it contains both reliably-attested core narrative and substantial later-tradition reconstructive material. The revisionist Wansbrough-Crone-Cook scholarship (1970s) treated the *Sīra* as largely later-tradition construction; subsequent manuscript and inscriptional evidence has substantially constrained the most radical revisionist positions, and the mainstream contemporary view accepts a reliable historical core with careful attention to the late-7th-c. literary-historical formation.
- **Ibn Hisham's editorial role.** The Ibn Hisham recension (~833) is the canonical Islamic-tradition form, but Ibn Hisham systematically **excluded material he regarded as theologically unfit** — including the *gharānīq* (Satanic Verses) narrative, certain *Asbāb al-Nuzūl* materials, and various controversial reports. The fuller Ibn Isḥāq corpus is partly recoverable through Ṭabarī's independent preservation.

## Disputes
- **Source-critical reliability.** Mainstream contemporary view: reliable historical core + late-7th-c. literary-historical formation. Wansbrough-Crone-Cook revisionist (~1977): substantially later construction. The revisionist position is substantially constrained by subsequent manuscript and inscriptional evidence.
- **Theological orthodoxy.** Classical Mālikī criticism: insufficient *isnād*-rigor. Mainstream Sunni: acceptable as biographical source, not as hadith source.
- **The *gharānīq* / Satanic Verses preservation.** Ibn Isḥāq's original *Sīra* included the *gharānīq* narrative (per Ṭabarī's preservation); Ibn Hisham removed it from his recension. The Ibn Isḥāq material on the Satanic Verses incident is the canonical early Islamic-tradition source for the [[satanic-verses-incident]] before the later-orthodox rejection.
