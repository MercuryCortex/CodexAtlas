# Thales of Miletus

## Identity
Thales of Miletus (c. -624 to c. -546) is conventionally treated as the **first Western philosopher** — the founder of the **Milesian school** of Ionian natural philosophy and (in Aristotle's later judgment) the founder of philosophy as such. Active in the wealthy Greek port-city of Miletus on the Anatolian coast, he is associated with **predicting a solar eclipse** (traditionally May 28, -585, which ended a battle between Lydians and Medes — the earliest precisely-dated event in Western intellectual history), with **measuring the heights of pyramids** by shadow-geometry, with **falling into a well while gazing at stars** (the foundational philosophical anecdote), and most importantly for the Codex Atlas mission with the doctrine that **"all things are water"** (or more precisely: water is the *archê*, the underlying principle of all things) — the first attested attempt to explain the cosmos in terms of a single underlying material principle rather than divine action. He is named among the **Seven Sages of Greece** in the conventional list.

## Historical evidence
Thales left no writings. His doctrines are reconstructed entirely from later testimony:
- **[[aristotle]]** *Metaphysics* 1.3 (983b6–27) — the principal source, naming Thales as "the founder of this kind of philosophy" and reporting water-as-*archê*.
- **Herodotus** 1.74–75, 1.170 — reports the eclipse prediction and Thales's political-strategic counsel.
- **Diogenes Laertius** *Lives* 1.22–44 — full biographical-doxographical entry (3rd c. CE; the standard ancient biographical compilation).
- **Plato** *Theaetetus* 174a — the well-falling anecdote.
- **Apuleius**, *Florida* 18 — astronomical achievements.

His historicity is secure; the specific dates and attributions vary across testimonies.

## Works
**None survive**; ancient titles attributed to him (*On Principles*, *Nautical Astronomy*, *On the Solstice*, *On the Equinox*) are reconstructed from late references and may be late-antique pseudepigrapha rather than genuine Thaletean works.

## Role in the tradition
Thales is the **paradigm of the first philosopher** in Western intellectual self-narration:

1. **Material monism**. The claim that **water is the *archê*** of all things is the first attested attempt to explain the cosmos through a single underlying material principle rather than divine narrative. Aristotle reads this as the foundation of natural philosophy. Whatever Thales actually meant, the doctrine *as transmitted* became foundational for pre-Socratic cosmology — [[anaximander]] (Thales's successor) substitutes the *apeiron* (infinite), [[anaximenes]] substitutes air, [[empedocles]] gives four elements, Democritus gives atoms, but the **method** is Thaletean.

2. **Greek-Egyptian intellectual contact**. Ancient tradition (Aetius, Plutarch, Iamblichus) claims Thales traveled in Egypt and acquired geometric knowledge from Egyptian priests. **Proclus** *In Euclidem* attributes the basic theorems of geometry to Thales as bringing them from Egypt to Greece. Whether the geometry-from-Egypt claim is historically accurate or is later Greek-Egyptian-syncretic ideology (mediated by [[plutarch]], [[diodorus-siculus]], and [[hecataeus-of-abdera]]) is debated — but the **fact of the tradition** is itself a major piece of Greek-Egyptian intellectual genealogy.

3. **Cosmic-water doctrine and Near Eastern cosmogony**. The "water as origin" claim is structurally close to Egyptian [[nun]] (primordial waters), Mesopotamian Apsu/Tiamat ([[phase-1-008-enuma-elish]]), and Genesis 1:2's *tohu va-bohu*. Whether Thales's water-doctrine **derives** from Near Eastern cosmogonic tradition (West 1971 *Early Greek Philosophy and the Orient*) or is independent invention is one of the central comparative questions in pre-Socratic studies. The geographic position of Miletus (a major Ionian trading port with documented Egyptian, Phoenician, and Mesopotamian contact) makes transmission very plausible.

4. **"Seven Sages of Greece" tradition**. Thales is unanimously included; the others vary by source.

## Disputes
- **Did Thales actually predict the -585 eclipse?** The eclipse is astronomically verified. Whether Thales used Babylonian-Saros eclipse cycles (plausible given Milesian access to Babylonian astronomy) or got lucky is debated. Most modern scholars accept the prediction as historical but think Thales used Babylonian data rather than original astronomical theory.
- **What did "all is water" mean?** Aristotle's interpretation (water is the *material substrate*) is the standard ancient reading; modern proposals range from a Near Eastern cosmogonic borrowing to a meteorological observation (everything wet supports life) to a deeper claim about animate matter. The thinness of evidence prevents resolution.
- **Egyptian-borrowing claims.** The Greek tradition of Thales-learned-geometry-in-Egypt is repeated by Proclus, Diogenes Laertius, Iamblichus. Modern scholarship is split — some treat it as Greek-Egyptian-syncretic ideology (no real evidence), others see real Ionian-Egyptian contact (well-documented in this period via Naucratis and Greek mercenaries).
- **Founder of philosophy?** Aristotle's "first philosopher" framing is partly Aristotelian system-building — choosing a clean Greek origin for a tradition that has Near Eastern substrate. Modern scholarship is more cautious about Thales as "the" beginning, treating him instead as the first Greek for whom we have philosophical doxography rather than the first to think philosophically.
