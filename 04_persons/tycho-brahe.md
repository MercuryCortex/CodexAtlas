# Tycho Brahe

## Identity
Danish-Swedish nobleman and astronomer, the greatest naked-eye astronomical observer in history. Built **Uraniborg** ("Castle of the Heavens") on the island of Hven (granted by King Frederick II of Denmark) — a combined observatory, alchemical laboratory, and printing press that was Europe's first purpose-built research institution. After losing Danish royal patronage following Frederick II's death, he accepted **Rudolf II's** invitation, moved to Prague in 1599, and became **Imperial Mathematician**. He died in 1601, leaving his data to his assistant **Johannes Kepler**.

## Historical Evidence
Exhaustively documented — Tycho's instruments, observations (the *Tychonic catalog* of 777 stars, accurate to 1–4 arcminutes), correspondence, printed books, and the physical remains of Uraniborg are all recorded. His 1572 observation of the *Nova Stella* (supernova in Cassiopeia) — proving that the heavens could change, contra Aristotle — is one of the most consequential single observations in the history of science.

## Significance for the Vault

Tycho Brahe is the **empirical pivot** in the vault's Prague nexus. Without his 20 years of naked-eye observations at Uraniborg, Kepler would have had no data precise enough to derive the elliptical orbit laws. The chain: **Tycho's data → Kepler's laws → Newton's gravity → modern physics** runs directly through Rudolf II's court in Prague. This is the vault's most dramatic case of an occult-patronizing court inadvertently funding the scientific revolution.

Tycho's own worldview was **not cleanly proto-modern**: he practiced alchemy (Uraniborg had an alchemical lab), he believed in astrological influences, and his cosmological model (the Tychonic system: planets orbit the sun, the sun orbits the Earth) was a deliberate compromise between Ptolemy and Copernicus that tried to save both the physical reality of geocentrism and the mathematical elegance of heliocentrism. He embodied the moment before the separation of astronomy from astrology and alchemy.

His **lost nose** — replaced by a prosthetic of brass or silver after a duel at 19 — and the **elk** he kept as a pet (which died falling down stairs after drinking too much beer), and the **dwarf jester** Jepp who sat under his table, make him one of the most vivid characters in Renaissance intellectual history.

## The Brahe-Kepler Transfer

Tycho jealously guarded his observational data, refusing to share it fully with Kepler during his lifetime. After Tycho's death (possibly poisoning — mercury was found in his remains at 2011 exhumation, source disputed), Kepler obtained the data and used it to derive his three laws of planetary motion (1609, 1619). The tension between the aristocratic observer (Tycho) and the mathematical genius he employed but didn't fully trust (Kepler) is one of the great asymmetrical collaborations in intellectual history.
