# al-Khiḍr

Quranic figure appearing in **Surah 18:60–82** (al-Kahf), where he is described as a servant of God given *ḥikma* (wisdom/understanding) and *ʿilm ladunī* (direct divine knowledge, 18:65). Moses encounters him at the junction of two seas and requests to accompany him, promising obedience. Al-Khiḍr performs three apparently senseless acts (scuttling a boat, killing a boy, rebuilding a wall without payment); Moses objects each time; al-Khiḍr finally reveals the hidden divine wisdom behind each action and parts from Moses.

## Quranic narrative

The narrative is not given a name in the Quran itself — "al-Khiḍr" comes from Islamic exegetical tradition. The Quran calls him only "one of Our servants to whom We had given mercy from Ourselves and had taught him from Us a [direct] knowledge" (18:65).

The three enigmatic acts:
1. **Boat-scuttling** — to prevent a king from seizing it by force
2. **Boy's killing** — the boy would have oppressed his believing parents; God will replace him with a better son
3. **Wall-repair** — beneath it is buried treasure belonging to two orphan boys whose father was righteous; they must be old enough to claim it

Each act violated conventional moral intuition but followed a deeper divine logic inaccessible to Moses's prophetic perception.

## Sufi significance

In Sufi tradition, al-Khiḍr is the paradigmatic **internal spiritual guide** — a teacher who bypasses the normal chain of human transmission to grant direct initiation. Ibn ʿArabī (*Futūḥāt al-Makkiyya*) claims to have been initiated by al-Khiḍr. He represents the principle that divine knowledge (*ʿilm ladunī*) can flow outside institutional structures, making him a recurring figure in mystical authority claims across Sunni and Shia Islam.

## Immortality tradition

Islamic tradition holds al-Khiḍr to be immortal, having drunk from the Water of Life. This associates him with the Gilgamesh / Alexander / Elijah immortality-quest narratives. His immortality means he is still present in the world, and encounters with him (often in dreams or chance meetings) are a recurring motif in Islamic hagiography.
