# Penelope

## Identity

Penelope (Greek **Πηνελόπεια**, *Pēnelopeia*; later **Πηνελόπη**) is the legendary queen of Ithaca — daughter of Icarius of Sparta, wife of [[odysseus]], mother of Telemachus — and the central female figure of the *Odyssey*. Her fixed Homeric epithet is *periphrōn*, "circumspect, exceedingly thoughtful," and it names her defining quality: Penelope is the epic's mortal exemplar of feminine *mētis* — the cunning, resourceful intelligence that the Titaness [[metis]] personifies at the divine level and that her husband exemplifies among men. The suitor Antinous, her enemy, gives the definitive character sketch: [[athena]] has granted her "skill in beautiful handiwork, good sense, and wiles (*kerdea*)" beyond any of the famous women of old — Tyro, Alcmene, Mycene (Od. 2.116-122). There is no historical evidence for an individual Penelope; like Odysseus she is a heroic-legendary figure, the creation of the oral-formulaic epic tradition stabilised in the 8th-7th centuries BCE. In the Atlas she is placed among the legendary persons (on the model of [[orpheus]] and beside [[odysseus]]) and carries three motifs that have no separate nodes: the **loom-trick**, the **bow contest**, and the **bed-sign**.

## In the Odyssey

**The shroud-trick (the web).** For three years Penelope holds off the suitors besieging her house with a device the epic considers important enough to narrate three times — by Antinous (Od. 2.93-110), by Penelope herself (19.137-156), and by the shade of the suitor Amphimedon in the underworld (24.128-146). She announces she will choose a husband only when she has finished weaving a burial shroud for her father-in-law Laertes; by day she weaves at the great loom, and by night, by torchlight, she unravels the day's work. The trick holds until one of her serving-women betrays her and the suitors catch her unweaving. The web is *dolos* — guile — in its purest Homeric form: a stratagem that manufactures time out of cloth, and the source of the proverbial "Penelope's web," the task endlessly done and undone.

**The bow contest (Book 21).** It is Penelope, not Odysseus, who sets the contest that destroys the suitors: she fetches Odysseus's great backsprung bow from the storeroom and announces that she will marry whichever man can string it and shoot an arrow through twelve axes in a row (Od. 21.68-79). The suitors fail; the disguised beggar — Odysseus — asks for the bow, and Penelope insists over the suitors' objections that the stranger be allowed to try (21.311-342) before Telemachus sends her to her chamber. The contest is the hinge of the entire revenge plot, and the question of how much Penelope intends by it is a classic interpretive crux of the poem, debated in modern scholarship since Harsh (1950) (see Disputes).

**The bed-sign (Od. 23.181-232).** After the slaughter, Penelope refuses to accept the stranger as her husband until she has tested him. She orders the nurse Eurycleia to move the great bed out of the bridal chamber and make it up outside — and Odysseus erupts: no mortal could move that bed, for he built it himself around a **living olive tree**, trimming the trunk into the bedpost so that the tree roots the marriage-bed to the earth (23.183-204). The secret of the bed's construction is the *sēma*, the sign known only to the two of them, and his involuntary anger is the proof of identity she engineered. Penelope is thus the one mortal in the poem who out-tricks Odysseus the deceiver — the point Winkler (1990) makes central — and the scene seals the *homophrosynē*, the "like-mindedness," that Odysseus himself had named as the highest good of marriage (Od. 6.181-185). The shade of Agamemnon pronounces her epitaph: her *kleos* (fame) for excellence will never die, and the immortals will make of "constant Penelope" a song for mortals (24.192-198) — the rare Homeric case of *kleos* awarded to a woman for intelligence and fidelity rather than to a warrior for battle.

## Cross-tradition significance

Penelope anchors the **mortal end of the Greek mētis lineage**. What [[metis]] is as a divine principle — swallowed by Zeus so that cunning becomes internal to sovereignty — and what [[athena]] is as its Olympian inheritor, Penelope is at the human scale: intelligence as a woman's only effective weapon inside a besieged household. Detienne and Vernant's classic study of *mētis* (1978) establishes weaving as one of the paradigm crafts of cunning intelligence, and Winkler (1990) reads Penelope as the epic's fullest demonstration that Homeric *mētis* is not a male monopoly: her web, her contest, and her bed-test are the strategic equal of anything Odysseus devises. The wire to the metis node is thus internal to the Greek tradition — an instance-of-the-category relation, not a cross-cultural claim.

Across traditions, Penelope fills the **tested-wife slot** beside [[sita]] of the *Ramayana*: both hold to an absent hero-husband against pressure (the suitors; Ravana), both are publicly vindicated, and both epics make the wife's constancy the moral centre of the hero's return. The honest framing: this is **typological convergence, not transmission**. The 19th-century diffusion thesis — Albrecht Weber's 1870/71 argument that the *Ramayana* borrowed from Greek epic — is rejected in modern scholarship (Goldman's Princeton *Ramayana* introduction reviews and dismisses it), and no documented line connects the two poems. The comparison remains instructive precisely in its inversion: Sita's trials (the *agnipariksha*, the second exile) are **imposed on her** by husband and public, whereas Penelope **authors her own tests** — she is the agent of the web, the contest, and the bed-sign. Doniger (1999) provides the fullest comparative Greece-India treatment of the doubled/tested-wife complex (its chapters centre on Sita and Helen; Penelope figures only in passing).

Finally, Penelope's loom stands beside — but distinct from — the Greek **weaving-as-destiny** complex catalogued under [[thread-of-fate]]: the Moirai weave what must be, while Penelope weaves and unweaves to *suspend* what must be. The two uses of the loom (fate-weaving and stratagem-weaving) share the same gendered craft-context, and the juxtaposition is structural, not an identification.

## Disputes

- **Does Penelope recognise Odysseus early?** The central Penelope controversy since Harsh (1950), who argued that she covertly recognises the beggar in the Book 19 interview and sets the bow contest *because* she knows. The text gives the thesis real footholds — the intimacy of the interview, the dream of the twenty geese slain by the eagle (19.535-553), and Amphimedon's shade, who assumes husband and wife plotted the contest together (24.167-169). The mainstream position (Russo's commentary; Katz 1991; Felson-Rubin 1994) is that Penelope does not consciously recognise him — Amphimedon's version is a suitor's inference, and the poem trades deliberately in the **indeterminacy** of her intentions; Amory (1963) proposed the influential middle reading of intuitive, subconscious recognition. The Atlas reports the debate without adjudicating: the early-recognition thesis is a minority reading with permanent footholds in the text.
- **The non-Homeric Penelope.** Later and local traditions diverge sharply from the faithful wife: Mantinean tradition showed Penelope's tomb and claimed Odysseus cast her out for infidelity (Pausanias 8.12.5-7), and an Arcadian strand made her the mother of the god **Pan** — by Hermes in Herodotus's version (Hdt. 2.145), by all the suitors together in the scurrilous punning variant reported from later mythographers. These are post-Homeric developments attached to Arcadian cult geography, not evidence of a "real" Penelope behind Homer; the Atlas treats the Homeric figure as primary and the Arcadian material as reception. Despite the Mantinean tomb, she received no significant cult — hence her typing as person, not deity.
- **The name.** Ancient etymology connected *Pēnelopeia* to the *pēnelops*, a kind of wild duck (with a rescue-by-waterbirds story attached), while a folk derivation from *pēnē* (weft-thread) + *lepein* suits her loom too well to be accidental in reception. Levaniouk (2011) gives the fullest modern treatment of the *pēnelops* connection; neither derivation is secure.
