# Vettius Valens

## Identity

A working astrologer of the **2nd century CE**, born at Antioch in 120 and active in Roman Egypt, chiefly Alexandria, where he ran a teaching practice. His nine-book ***Anthology***, compiled roughly 150–175 CE, is the **most valuable single document of ancient astrology** — not because it is the most systematic, which it is not, but because it is the only one that shows the craft from inside the workshop.

Ptolemy's [[phase-3-038-ptolemy-tetrabiblos|*Tetrabiblos*]] is a philosopher's account of why astrology should work. Valens's *Anthology* is a practitioner's record of how he did it, on real clients, with real dates. It contains some **130 worked example horoscopes** with computable positions — charts of actual people, several evidently his own and those of his students — which Neugebauer & Van Hoesen (1959) were able to recompute and date. Those charts are the reason modern scholarship can reconstruct Hellenistic computational practice at all, and they are how Valens's own birth date (120 CE) is known.

## What the *Anthology* preserves

- **The techniques as used, not as idealized.** Valens works through cases, contradicts himself, revises, complains about rival astrologers and about clients who will not pay. The mess is the evidence: it is the only ancient source that shows the [[astrology-horoscope-twelve-places|horoscopic apparatus]] in operation rather than in exposition.
- **The time-lord procedures.** The *Anthology* is the principal surviving witness to **zodiacal releasing** (*aphesis*), an elaborate technique for dividing a life into periods governed by successive planetary lords, released from the Lot of Spirit. Lost to the Western tradition for centuries, it was recovered from Valens's text in the modern translation-and-revival movement.
- **Astrology as initiation.** Valens binds his readers by **oath** — on the sun, the moon, the wandering and fixed stars, and the cosmos itself — not to communicate the doctrine to the uninitiated. The framing is that of a mystery cult applied to a technical craft, and it is one of the sharpest ancient statements of astrology's self-understanding as sacred knowledge rather than a trade skill.
- **Stoic fatalism, argued.** Valens holds a hard determinism: the chart shows what *will* happen, and the value of knowing it is not evasion but *composure*. The astrologer's gift to the client is the capacity to meet a fixed fate without being broken by it. This is Stoic *heimarmenē* ([[philosophy-stoicism]]) put to practical use, and it sits in direct tension with the Christian and Islamic objections that would later be raised against the whole enterprise on free-will grounds — [[providence-and-fate]] is the theme the dispute turns on, and Valens is the clearest ancient statement of the position being objected to.

## The transmission: how a Greek casebook reached Baghdad

The *Anthology*'s afterlife is a second exhibit of the same relay that carried [[dorotheus-of-sidon|Dorotheus]]. The text was rendered into **Middle Persian at the Sasanian court**, and entered Arabic under the title ***Kitāb al-Bizīdaj*** — in which *Bizīdaj* is the Arabic form of Pahlavi *wizīdag*, "selections, anthology" (Pingree 1997). **The Arabic title preserves the Persian word for the Greek word.** The intermediary is embedded in the name of the book.

Valens was consequently a working source for the early ʿAbbāsid astrologers — [[mashallah-ibn-athari|Māshāʾallāh]] and his generation — and through them for the Latin West, even though the Greek text itself was not translated into Latin and remained essentially unread in Europe until modern times.

## Disputes

- **Whose charts are they?** Some of the 130 nativities are widely taken to be Valens's own and his students'; which ones, and how far the autobiographical readings can be pressed, is argued. The *dating* of the charts is secure; the identification of their subjects mostly is not.
- **How representative is he?** Valens is one practitioner in one city. Whether his procedures were standard across the Hellenistic world or a local school's is unresolvable, since the comparative material is thin — a caution that applies to every claim of the form "the Hellenistic astrologers did X".
- **Riley versus the practitioner translations.** Mark Riley's complete translation is by a classicist and is the scholarly standard; several partial translations produced within the astrological revival render the technical vocabulary according to reconstructed practice. Where they differ, the divergence is usually about how a technique was *meant* to work, not about what the Greek says — a distinction worth keeping visible.
