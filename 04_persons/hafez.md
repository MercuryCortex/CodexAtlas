# Khwāja Shams-ud-Dīn Muḥammad Ḥāfeẓ-e Shīrāzī

## Identity

Khwāja Shams-ud-Dīn Muḥammad Ḥāfeẓ (~1315–1390 CE) — known universally as Hafez — is the supreme lyric poet of the Persian tradition and the most widely read Persian poet in the world. His *takhallus* (pen name) "Ḥāfeẓ" means "one who has memorized the Quran" — which he reportedly did, in multiple recitation modes — and the name signals that his entire poetic vocabulary is saturated with Quranic resonance. He spent his entire life in **Shiraz** (Fars province) and worked as a *mudarris* (Quranic teacher) while composing the ~500 *ghazals* that constitute his [[phase-5-053-hafez-divan|Dīvān]].

He is a peer and partial successor to [[sadi-of-shiraz]] in the Shirazi literary tradition; in his mystical-lyric register he inherits from [[rumi]] and [[farid-ud-din-attar]], though his medium — the *ghazal* rather than the mathnawi — is his own supreme formal achievement. His **tomb in Shiraz (Ḥāfeẓiyyeh)** remains one of Iran's most-visited pilgrimage sites and cultural monuments.

## Historical evidence

Hafez is well-attested by contemporary and near-contemporary sources. His court connections under the Muzaffarid rulers of Shiraz are documented; a famous anecdote about his meeting with the young Timur (Tamerlane) during the 1387 Timurid incursion into Shiraz is frequently cited (Hafez reportedly told Timur that he had given away Samarkand and Bukhara for a mole on a beloved's cheek) but may be apocryphal. The compilation of the *Dīvān* by his disciple Muḥammad Gulandam (~1391, one year after Hafez's death) is documented in the manuscript tradition.

## Works

**Texts authored:**
- [[phase-5-053-hafez-divan]] (*Dīvān-e Ḥāfeẓ*, ~1350–1390 CE) — ~500 *ghazals* (lyric odes), compiled posthumously; the supreme achievement of the Persian lyric tradition and the text used for *fāl-e Ḥāfeẓ* (bibliomancy) in Iranian culture for 600 years.

## Role in the tradition

Hafez achieved what no Persian poet before or after him quite matched: the *ghazal* form in his hands operates on simultaneously the erotic, the mystical, the political-satirical, and the philosophical-meditative registers, rendering each individual poem inexhaustible. His wine imagery (*mey*, *sāqī*, *mey-khāna*) is the most elaborated expression of the Sufi divine-intoxication symbolism in all of Persian poetry. The beloved (*maʿshūq*) in his *ghazals* is systematically ambiguous between human and divine — a precision, not an evasion.

**The Goethe connection** is the paradigm case of East-West literary encounter. Goethe read Hafez via Hammer-Purgstall's German translation (1812–13) and recognized a peer: "Du bist der Derwisch, du der Dichter, / Du bist der Stern, der uns Licht gebracht" ("You are the dervish, you the poet, / You are the star that brought us light"). The *West-östlicher Divan* (1819), composed in direct response to Hafez, is Goethe's most lyrical mature work and the most significant East-West literary cross-pollination in European Romanticism. The influence then runs from Goethe through Nietzsche (who read the *Divan*) to Hesse and 20th-century Western spirituality's encounter with the "mystical East."

## Disputes

- **Hafez's Sufi affiliation.** Was Hafez a committed Sufi disciple of a particular *ṭarīqa*, or a poet who used Sufi vocabulary as the culturally prestigious medium for lyric poetry? The debate is ancient; the standard scholarly position is that his Sufism is genuine but personal — he does not align with a specific order and his theology is eclectic.
- **The Timur encounter.** The anecdote about Hafez's wit before Tamerlane is almost certainly a later legend; it has been told in multiple variant forms and serves hagiographic purposes. It is not reliable history but is culturally important as a statement of Hafez's legendary status.
