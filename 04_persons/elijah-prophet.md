# Elijah

## Identity
Northern-Kingdom prophet active under Ahab and Ahaziah of Israel (~870–849 BCE); the antagonist of the Baal cult under Ahab's queen Jezebel; the figure who is "taken up" by a whirlwind without dying (2 Kgs 2), spawning a vast Second-Temple and New-Testament expectation that Elijah will return as the eschatological messianic forerunner. His narrative cycle (1 Kgs 17 – 2 Kgs 2) is widely treated as the most theologically dense prophetic-narrative complex in the Deuteronomistic History.

## Historical evidence
No direct extrabiblical attestation. The Omride dynasty Elijah opposes is firmly historical (Mesha Stele c. -840; Kurkh Monolith of Shalmaneser III c. -853 names Ahab's chariotry at Qarqar). The biblical narrative's specific events (Mount Carmel contest, drought, ravens at the Wadi Cherith) are theologically structured legends; the figure himself is probably historical but the narrative cycle is folkloric-prophetic-redactional in character.

## Role in the tradition
- **Eschatological forerunner.** Malachi 4:5–6 ("I will send you Elijah the prophet before the great and dreadful day of YHWH") establishes Elijah as the messianic forerunner. Second-Temple expectation (Sirach 48:10) and Mishnaic discussion (m. Eduyot 8:7) carry the trope.
- **New Testament.** John the Baptist is identified with the returning Elijah in Mark 9:11–13, Matthew 11:14, Matthew 17:10–13 (after the Transfiguration where Elijah appears with Moses), and Luke 1:17. Jesus on the cross is heard to call for Elijah (Mark 15:35–36; Matthew 27:47–49). James 5:17–18 invokes Elijah as a model of effective prayer.
- **Islamic tradition.** Ilyas is named as a prophet in Quran 6:85 and 37:123–132.
- **Jewish folkloric tradition.** Elijah's continued post-biblical presence (the Passover cup of Elijah; the prophet who walks the earth) is one of the most widely-attested folkloric persistences in rabbinic Judaism.
