# Saladin

## Identity
Salah ad-Din Yusuf ibn Ayyub, born ~1137 in Tikrit (Iraq) to a Kurdish family in service to the Zengid emirs. Rose through the Zengid military to become vizier of the Fatimid caliphate in Egypt (1169), then ended the Shia-Ismaili Fatimid caliphate (1171) and reunified Egypt under nominal Sunni Abbasid suzerainty. Consolidated Egypt and Syria into a single Ayyubid sultanate (1174–1186). On **4 July 1187 at the Horns of Hattin**, destroyed the Crusader field army of Guy of Lusignan in one of the most consequential battles of the medieval Mediterranean: the Latin Kingdom lost most of its military force, the True Cross relic was captured, and Saladin proceeded to take **Jerusalem on 2 October 1187** — ending 88 years of Latin Christian rule. Survived the [[event-third-crusade-1189-1192|Third Crusade]] (1189–1192), retaining Jerusalem at the Treaty of Jaffa. Died of fever at Damascus on 4 March 1193.

## Historical evidence
Extensive Arabic-language documentation: Baha al-Din ibn Shaddad (Saladin's *qadi al-'asakir* — chief military judge — and personal confidant) wrote an unusually detailed and affectionate biography; Imad al-Din al-Isfahani (chancery secretary) wrote the *al-Fath al-qussi*; Ibn al-Athir gives the broader regional perspective; substantial chancery and diplomatic correspondence survives. Latin sources (William of Tyre — sympathetic, written before Hattin; *Itinerarium* — hostile; Ambroise — hostile) provide the Western view.

## Works
Saladin himself authored chancery letters and diplomatic correspondence preserved in the archive of his secretary al-Qadi al-Fadil.

## Role in the tradition

- **Restorer of Sunni orthodoxy in Egypt.** Ending the two-century Fatimid Shia caliphate (1171) and returning Egypt to Sunni Abbasid suzerainty.
- **Recoverer of Jerusalem.** The 1187 recapture (and the famous *clement* surrender-terms granted to the Latin civilian population, in contrast to the 1099 Crusader massacre) becomes the defining moment of Saladin's legacy and of the Crusader-period in Muslim memory.
- **Founder of the Ayyubid dynasty.** Egypt and Syria are ruled by his Kurdish-Arab dynasty through the early 13th c., until the Mamluk takeover in 1250.
- **Iconic figure in *both* traditions.** In Latin/Western memory: Saladin is the *uniquely* positively-imaged medieval Muslim ruler — chivalrous, magnanimous, just. Dante places him in Limbo (*Inferno* 4.129) — the *only* contemporary-or-near-contemporary non-Christian named there. Walter Scott's *The Talisman* (1825), 19th-c. Orientalist iconography, the 20th-c. cinematic Saladin (Youssef Chahine 1963; Ridley Scott *Kingdom of Heaven* 2005).

## Connections

- **→ [[event-third-crusade-1189-1192]]** *principal-adversary*.
- **→ Richard I of England** *military-counterpart-and-correspondent*: the Third Crusade negotiations and the eventual Treaty of Jaffa (1192).
- **→ [[tradition-knights-templar]]** *adversary*: at Hattin (1187), Saladin executed ~230 captured Templar and Hospitaller knights. Templar military prisoners were standing policy not to ransom; Saladin's treatment of them differed from his usually clement treatment of regular captives.
- **→ [[tradition-islam]] (Sunni)** *restorer-in-Egypt*: ending Fatimid Shia rule, restoring nominal Sunni Abbasid suzerainty in Egypt and the Fertile Crescent.
- **→ [[tradition-shia-islam]] (Ismaili/Fatimid)** *ender*: the suppression of the Fatimid caliphate (1171) is a major event of Sunni-Shia history.

## Disputes
- **Was Saladin as chivalrous as his Latin reputation says?** Mixed. The 1187 Jerusalem surrender terms were genuinely generous; the Hattin Templar/Hospitaller execution was harsh; the 1191 Acre prisoner exchange broke down. Möhring 2008 gives the balanced reading.
- **Personal piety vs. dynastic-political motive.** Saladin's pious Sunni-revivalist self-presentation is real (he sponsored madrasa-building, hadith study, Sufi orders) but his political ambitions (Egypt, Syria, Jerusalem, Yemen) were simultaneously dynastic. The two are not really separable in his case.
