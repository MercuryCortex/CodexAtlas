# Alexander the Great

## Identity
Alexander III of Macedon (356-323 BCE) — king of Macedon from 336 BCE, hegemon of the Greek League, pharaoh of Egypt (332), king of Persia (after Gaugamela 331), and conqueror of an empire from the Adriatic to the Indus that he ruled for less than a decade and that fragmented at his death (323 BCE) into the Diadochi kingdoms. His reign **inaugurates the Hellenistic dispensation** (323 BCE – 31 BCE/30 BCE Cleopatra) — the political-cultural matrix in which the Hebrew Bible takes substantially its received form, in which the Septuagint is produced (~250 BCE, [[ptolemy-ii-philadelphus|Ptolemy II Philadelphus]]'s commission), in which Greco-Egyptian cult fusion produces Sarapis-Isis-Hermes-Trismegistus, in which Aramaic-Greek bilingualism creates the linguistic substrate of early Christianity, and in which Greco-Buddhism (Gandhara) emerges as the first sustained East-West religious exchange.

## Historical evidence
Saturated. Four major Greek-Latin narratives: Arrian (*Anabasis Alexandri*, 2nd c. CE — most reliable, drawing on contemporary sources Ptolemy I and Aristobulus), Plutarch (*Life of Alexander*), Quintus Curtius (*Historiae Alexandri Magni*), Diodorus Siculus (book 17). Justin's epitome of the lost Pompeius Trogus. Extensive numismatic evidence. Babylonian Astronomical Diaries give independent contemporary attestation (the entry for Alexander's death is dated to 29 Aiyaru = 11 June 323 BCE). The fragmentary papyrological remains. The Diadochi-period inscriptions and the surviving Hellenistic city foundations are the negative archaeological evidence of his program.

## Role in the tradition

### Hellenization of the Near East — the substrate of Christianity
Alexander's conquest of the Achaemenid Persian Empire (334-330 BCE) and the subsequent Diadochi-period (323-31 BCE) **created the Hellenistic world-system** — a single Greek-language administrative-cultural zone running from Bactria to Egypt to the Aegean. This world is the **immediate political-cultural substrate of New Testament Christianity**. Specifically:
- **Greek (Koine) as the cosmopolitan language** of the eastern Mediterranean — the language of the Septuagint, the New Testament, the early Patristic literature, and most Hellenistic-Jewish philosophy.
- **Greek-language philosophy** (Platonism, Stoicism, Cynicism, Epicureanism) as the standard intellectual idiom — Paul quotes Aratus and Menander; the Wisdom of Solomon and Philo are unimaginable without Greek philosophy.
- **The polis pattern** — urban Greek-style cities (the post-Alexander foundations and refoundations: Alexandria, Antioch, Seleucia, Apamea, Pella in Decapolis, etc.) — providing the network of Christian missionary travel.
- **Hellenistic Jewish syncretism** — Philo, the wisdom literature, the integration of Greek philosophical categories (Logos, sophia) with Hebrew theology — the proximate substrate of John 1:1 ("In the beginning was the Logos...").

### Hellenization of Judaea → Maccabean crisis → apocalyptic Judaism
Alexander's death (323) and the subsequent Diadochi struggle gave Judaea successively to the Ptolemies (323-200 BCE) and then to the Seleucids (200-167 BCE). The Seleucid king [[antiochus-iv-epiphanes|Antiochus IV Epiphanes]]'s 167 BCE attempt at forced Hellenization of Judaean cult (the "abomination of desolation" — Olympian Zeus altar in the Jerusalem Temple) triggered the [[event-maccabean-revolt-167-164-bce|Maccabean revolt]] (167-164 BCE), out of which emerged: the Hasmonean dynasty, apocalyptic Judaism ([[phase-3-008-book-of-daniel|the Book of Daniel]], the [[four-kingdoms-schema]] of which Alexander is the third kingdom), the martyrdom-theology of 2 and 4 Maccabees, and the proximate background of [[jesus-of-nazareth|early Christianity]]. This chain — **Alexander → Hellenization → Maccabean crisis → apocalyptic Judaism → early Christianity** — is the load-bearing political-religious transmission spine of phase 2-into-phase 3.

### Greco-Buddhism (Gandhara)
Alexander's Indian campaign (327-325 BCE) and the post-Alexander Hellenistic states in Bactria and Gandhara (the Seleucid East; the Greco-Bactrian Kingdom; the Indo-Greek Kingdom of Menander) produced the cultural-political conditions for **Greco-Buddhism** — the Gandharan synthesis of Greek artistic form with Buddhist content, producing the first anthropomorphic [[siddhartha-gautama-buddha|Buddha]] images (~1st c. BCE - 1st c. CE), the Heracles-Vajrapāṇi iconographic borrowing, the Apollo-Buddha facial typology, the Tyche-Hārītī assimilation, and the philosophical-dialogical exchange of the *Milindapañha* (King Menander / Milinda in dialogue with the Buddhist sage Nāgasena). The Greek philosophical encounter with Indian thought also produced Pyrrho's Pyrrhonism after Pyrrho accompanied Alexander on the Indian campaign. See [[theme-gandharan-buddhism]].

### Egyptian pharaoh / son of Zeus-Ammon
At Siwa (332 BCE) Alexander received from the oracle of [[amun|Amun-Zeus]] the salutation "son of Ammon" (Egyptian *sa-Amun* — a normal pharaonic titulature; received by Alexander as personal divine sonship). He was crowned pharaoh of Egypt and founded **Alexandria** (331 BCE — see [[event-founding-of-alexandria-331-bce]]), which became the largest city of the Hellenistic world, the center of [[event-school-of-alexandria-300bce-415ce|Alexandrian-Jewish-Christian-philosophical scholarship]], home of the [[event-burning-of-library-of-alexandria|Library]], birthplace of the Septuagint, of Philo, of Origen, of Clement, of Alexandrian Neoplatonism, of Hermeticism, of much of Gnosticism. **No other single city is so densely concentrated with the religious-philosophical streams Christianity emerges from.**

### Imperial-religious model for Caesar and Augustus
Both [[julius-caesar]] and [[caesar-augustus]] consciously modeled their political-religious self-presentation on Alexander. Caesar visited Alexander's tomb in Alexandria; Augustus visited Alexander's tomb in 30 BCE and put Alexander's image on his signet-ring (Suetonius *Augustus* 50; Cassius Dio 51.16); both used the Alexander-typology to legitimize their imperial-divine status. The *divi filius* titulature of the Roman imperial cult that Christianity later inherits-and-inverts (see [[caesar-augustus]]) draws on the Alexander-as-son-of-Zeus-Ammon precedent. Crossan & Reed 2004 on the imperial-cult substrate.

### Dhū al-Qarnayn — the Quranic Alexander
[[phase-4-034-quran|Sura 18:83-101]] (al-Kahf, the Cave) recounts the deeds of **Dhū al-Qarnayn** (the "Two-Horned One") — his journey to the western and eastern ends of the world, his building of a barrier of iron and molten copper against Gog and Magog. The traditional Islamic identification of Dhū al-Qarnayn with Alexander (al-Tabari, Ibn Kathir) draws on a long pre-Islamic Near Eastern tradition — the Syriac Christian *Alexander Legend* (early 7th c., shortly before the Quran), the Talmudic Alexander-cycle, the Persian Iskandar tradition — in which Alexander becomes a quasi-prophetic figure who travels to the world's ends, encounters supernatural realms, and seeks/seeks-not the Water of Life. Modern critical tafsir (Cook 1983; Tesei 2014) is more cautious about the historical identification (Alexander's polytheist religious framing fits poorly with the Quranic Dhū al-Qarnayn's monotheist piety). The Quranic text itself does not name Alexander. The tradition-historical chain is: Alexander → Alexander Romance → Syriac Christian *Alexander Legend* → Quranic Sura 18 pericope. See [[dhu-al-qarnayn]].

### The Alexander Romance — the most widely-translated secular text of the Middle Ages
Pseudo-Callisthenes's *Alexander Romance* (~3rd c. CE, Greek) was translated into Latin (Julius Valerius, ~4th c.; Leo of Naples, 10th c. — the *Historia de Preliis*), Syriac (5th c. — the source of the Persian and Ethiopic), Pahlavi (lost, but feeding the Arabic and Persian), Arabic, Hebrew, Persian (most famously Firdawsi's *Iskandar-nāma* and Nizami's *Iskandar-nāma*), Old French, Middle English, Mongolian, Malay, Javanese, Ethiopic — making **Alexander, by literary diffusion, one of the universally-known figures of pre-modern Eurasia**. The Romance is the dominant pre-modern reception of Alexander; Stoneman 2008 *Alexander the Great: A Life in Legend*.

## Disputes
- **Cause of death.** Malaria, poisoning, alcoholic-acute-pancreatitis, West Nile virus, typhoid, Guillain-Barré syndrome have all been proposed; the contemporary sources are mutually inconsistent. Modern consensus is uncertain; natural causes (likely infectious) most probable.
- **Alexander's religious self-conception.** Whether the Siwa-Ammon episode constituted a personal divine self-identification, a politically-pragmatic acceptance of Egyptian pharaonic theology, or both is debated.
- **The "Hellenization" framing.** Older scholarship (Droysen, Tarn) framed Alexander as a deliberate civilizational unifier; revisionist work (Bosworth, Briant) emphasizes the violence and the unintended-consequences character of the actual Hellenistic cultural diffusion.
- **The Quranic Dhū al-Qarnayn = Alexander identification.** Traditional tafsir affirms; modern critical scholarship (van Bladel 2008, Tesei 2014) traces the Syriac-Christian-Alexander-Legend intermediary but is cautious about identifying the Quranic figure historically.
