# Rumi

## Identity

**Jalāl al-Dīn Muḥammad Rūmī (604/1207 – 672/1273)** — *Mawlānā*, "Our Master," in the Persianate world; *Mevlânâ* in Turkish — is the most widely read Persian poet in history and, in the modern West, the most widely read religious poet from any tradition. He was also a working **Ḥanafī jurist and preacher** in the Seljuk capital of Konya, a fact the popular reception almost entirely omits and without which almost nothing he wrote makes sense.

He was born in the district of **Balkh** in Khurasan (modern northern Afghanistan) — traditionally in Balkh city itself, though Fritz Meier's study of his father and **Lewis 2000** both conclude the family more probably came from **Vakhsh**, a small town in the Balkh administrative region, in what is now Tajikistan. His father **Bahāʾ al-Dīn Walad** (d. 1231) was a preacher and mystic, author of the visionary notebook *Maʿārif*. The family emigrated westward around 1216–17. The traditional explanation is flight from the Mongols; Lewis notes that the departure **predates** the Mongol destruction of Balkh in 1220 and that friction with the Khwārazmshāh's court is at least as likely.

The route ran through Nishapur, Baghdad, the hajj, Damascus, Malatya, and Larande — where Rūmī married Gawhar Khātūn in 1225 — before the family settled about 1229 in **Konya**, capital of the Seljuks of **Rūm**, from which his epithet comes. He is called *Balkhī* in the Persianate world and *Rūmī* in the West; both are place-names, and the modern contest over his nationality between Iran, Afghanistan, Tajikistan, and Turkey is an anachronism applied to a Persian-writing Muslim of the Seljuk Anatolian frontier.

## Historical evidence

Good, and unusually close to the subject. Two early biographies come from inside the circle: **Sipahsālār's *Risāla*** (by a disciple) and **Aflākī's *Manāqib al-ʿĀrifīn*** (compiled c. 1318–53 at the request of Rūmī's grandson) — hagiographies, and to be read as such, but written within living memory. Against them stand Rūmī's own **letters** (*Maktūbāt*), which show him interceding with officials, arranging marriages, and managing a religious community; his father's *Maʿārif*; and Seljuk-era Konya documentation. **Lewis 2000** is the biography that systematically separates the documentary from the hagiographic layers.

## Shams-i Tabrīzī

The decisive event of his life has a date: **29 November 1244**, when Rūmī — then a respected forty-year-old *mudarris* and *muftī* — met **Shams al-Dīn of Tabriz**, a wandering, abrasive, unaffiliated dervish. What followed was a consuming spiritual friendship that took Rūmī out of the lecture hall entirely. His students and townspeople resented it. Shams left for Damascus in 1246; Rūmī sent his son Sulṭān Walad to bring him back; and in **1247–48 he disappeared for good**. The sources conflict: some report murder, with the complicity of Rūmī's other son ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn; others report a simple departure. **Lewis 2000 lays out the evidence and declines to settle it.** Rūmī searched Damascus for him twice, and then stopped — the *Dīvān* records the moment the search turned inward.

He then took two successive companions in the same role: **Ṣalāḥ al-Dīn Zarkūb**, an illiterate goldsmith (d. 1258), and **Ḥusām al-Dīn Chelebī**, who asked him for a didactic poem and then wrote down, for the last eleven years of Rūmī's life, the *Masnavī*. Rūmī died on **17 December 1273**. Konya still keeps the anniversary as *Şeb-i Arûs*, "the wedding night" — the union with the Beloved.

## Works

- **[[phase-5-025-rumi-masnavi|*Masnavī-yi Maʿnavī*]]** — roughly **25,600 couplets in six books**, dictated to Ḥusām al-Dīn from about 1262 until his death; Book VI breaks off mid-story. It is a teaching poem built of nested tales — folk anecdote, Qurʾānic narrative, obscene joke, animal fable, prophetic legend — each opening into commentary that opens into another tale. Jāmī's formula for it, *"the Qurʾān in the Persian tongue,"* is the traditional Persianate estimate. **Nicholson's eight-volume critical edition, translation and commentary (1925–40)** remains the scholarly standard in English.
- ***Dīvān-i Shams-i Tabrīzī*** *(Dīvān-i Kabīr)* — some **3,200 ghazals and 2,000 quatrains**, around 40,000 lines: the ecstatic lyric output of the Shams years and after. Rūmī signs the poems with **Shams's** name instead of his own — an act of authorial self-effacement that is itself the doctrine.
- ***Fīhi mā Fīhi*** — prose discourses recorded by listeners; the plainest statement of his thought.
- ***Majālis-i Sabʿa*** — seven sermons from his preaching years, conventional in form and useful precisely for that.
- ***Maktūbāt*** — letters; the documentary evidence of the civic and juridical life the poetry is written from inside.

## The thought

Rūmī built **no system**, which is why Chittick 1983 has to organize him thematically. What he has instead is a small number of relentlessly worked images.

**Separation and the reed.** The *Masnavī* opens with the **Nay-nāmeh**, eighteen couplets in which a reed flute cries because it was cut from the reed bed; everyone who hears it hears their own severance. *Firāq* / *hijrān* (separation) and *shawq* (longing) are the condition of the soul, and the pain is not a defect to be cured but the instrument of return. This is the vault's [[soul-exile-longing]] pattern in its most quoted form.

**Love as cosmological principle.** *ʿIshq* — passionate, unreasonable love — is not a feeling about God but the force that moves the cosmos: the reason particles turn, the reason the sky wheels. Reason (*ʿaql*) is a competent servant and a hopeless master; Rūmī's recurring joke is the philosopher whose logic is a wooden leg.

**Annihilation and subsistence.** *Fanāʾ* and *baqāʾ* — the passing away of the self and its subsistence in God — arrive in Rūmī as narrative and image rather than as the technical apparatus they are in [[al-junayd|al-Junayd]] or the Akbarians. See [[doctrine-fana-sufi-annihilation]] and [[fanaa-annihilation]].

**Samāʿ.** Audition — music, poetry, movement — as a discipline rather than an entertainment, and contested in his own lifetime among jurists. Rūmī practised and defended it. The formalized whirling liturgy came later; see below.

**The Qurʾānic substrate.** The *Masnavī* is a tissue of Qurʾānic citation, ḥadīth, and legal reference. Rūmī is not a poet who happens to be Muslim; he is a jurist-preacher whose poem is an extended commentary on scripture. This is the single most important thing the popular reception loses.

## Reception

**The Mevlevi order.** Rūmī founded nothing. His son **Sulṭān Walad** (d. 1312) and grandson Ulu ʿĀrif Chelebi organized the **Mawlawiyya** around the tomb at Konya, with a hereditary *çelebi* headship. It became an Ottoman elite order and the patron of one of the great art traditions of Islam — the *āyīn-i şerīf* repertoire, the *ney*, Ottoman calligraphy. The **seven-part [[sema-whirling-mevlevi|semâ]]** ceremony now synonymous with Rūmī worldwide was codified over the following centuries, not by him. Turkey's Law 677 closed all *tekkes* in **1925**; the ceremony returned from the 1950s as heritage performance and was inscribed by UNESCO in 2008. See [[tradition-mevlevi]] and [[music-sufi-sama]].

**Persianate.** The *Masnavī* becomes a commented canon: Jāmī, Ismāʿīl Anqaravī's Ottoman commentary, the Indian Chishtī tradition. It is read continuously in Iran, Afghanistan, Tajikistan, Turkey, and South Asia from the fourteenth century to now.

**Europe and America.** Friedrich Rückert's German ghazal renderings in the 1820s — in the wake of Goethe's Persian-inspired *West-östlicher Divan* (1819) — put Rūmī into European Romanticism. Nicholson's edition and Arberry's translations made him academically available in English in the twentieth century.

**MASSIVE WIN — and the honest note attached to it.** From the 1980s, **Coleman Barks's** English "versions" made Rūmī a mass-market American author; he has been repeatedly described in the press as the best-selling poet in the United States, a claim **Lewis 2000** examines and treats as plausible but loosely sourced. The documented fact is this: **Barks does not read Persian.** He reworks the English of Nicholson and Arberry into free verse. In the process the Qurʾānic citations, the ḥadīth, the legal frame, and the name of Islam largely disappear. Lewis 2000 devotes substantial space to this; later critics (Omid Safi among others) have argued the popular reception performs a spiritual expropriation. The vault records **both** — the reception is genuinely one of the largest cross-cultural transmissions of a premodern religious text in modern history, **and** the object transmitted has been systematically stripped of its tradition. Both facts are the finding.

## Disputes

- **Where was he born?** Traditionally Balkh city; Meier 1989 and Lewis 2000 argue for Vakhsh in the Balkh district. The YAML keeps the region-level statement.
- **Why did the family leave Khurasan?** The Mongol explanation is traditional but chronologically awkward — they left before Balkh fell. Lewis 2000 canvasses the alternatives without settling.
- **What happened to Shams?** Murder (possibly with ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn's involvement) or departure. The early sources disagree and the question is open.
- **Did he meet Ibn ʿArabī?** The hagiography places Rūmī in Damascus during Ibn ʿArabī's residence and some accounts have them meet. **Lewis 2000 finds no reliable evidence.** What is documented is his friendship with **Ṣadr al-Dīn al-Qūnawī**, Ibn ʿArabī's stepson and interpreter, in Konya. Reading the *Masnavī* as Akbarian metaphysics — the habit of the later Persian commentators — imports a system Rūmī never used.
- **Did he meet ʿAṭṭār?** The story of the boy receiving a book from [[farid-ud-din-attar|ʿAṭṭār]] at Nishapur is hagiography. The literary descent from Sanāʾī and ʿAṭṭār, which Rūmī states himself, is not.
- **Is the popular English Rūmī Rūmī?** See above. The vault's position is neither dismissal nor endorsement: record the reception, record the critique, name the mediation chain.
