# Rumi

## Identity
Jalāl al-Dīn Muḥammad Rūmī (1207–1273) — known in the Persianate world as *Mawlānā* ("Our Master") and in Turkey as *Mevlâna* — was the supreme Sufi poet of Persian language and the most-read Persianate religious author of the modern Western world. Born Balkh (modern Afghanistan) 1207; his family fled the Mongol approach and migrated west, eventually settling Konya (Seljuk Anatolian capital) ~1228 where his father Bahāʾ al-Dīn Walad was a prominent Sufi master. Rūmī succeeded his father as community teacher and jurist. The pivotal event of his life was the 1244 encounter with the wandering dervish Shams-i Tabrīzī, a transformative mystical friendship that turned him from learned cleric into ecstatic mystic-poet. After Shams's disappearance (1248 — death by violence is one possibility), the bulk of Rūmī's lyric output (*Dīvān-i Shams-i Tabrīzī*) is dedicated to Shams. The *Masnavi* (~25,000 didactic couplets) belongs to his later period, dictated to Ḥusām al-Dīn Chelebī.

## Historical evidence
Substantial — the *Risāla-yi Sipahsālār* (Sipahsalar's biography of Rūmī's family) and Aflākī's *Manāqib al-ʿĀrifīn* (biographical hagiography of the Mevlevi founders) plus Rūmī's own works.

## Works
*Masnavi* (didactic mystical epic); *Dīvān-i Shams-i Tabrīzī* (lyric *ghazals* and *rubāʿīyāt*); *Fīhi mā Fīhi* (prose discourses); *Majālis-i Sabʿa*; *Maktūbāt*.

## Role in the tradition
After Rūmī's death, his son Sulṭān Walad founded the Mevlevi order; the order spread across the Ottoman Empire and developed the *samāʿ* whirling ritual. Rūmī is the most-read Persian poet in the modern Persianate world (Iran, Afghanistan, Tajikistan, Turkey) and through Coleman Barks's loose English paraphrases became a US bestseller in the 1990s — with significant scholarly critique that the popular American reception strips out the Qur'anic-Islamic substrate.
