# Guru Nanak

## Identity
Founder of Sikhism and the first of the ten Sikh Gurus (1469–1539). Born to a Hindu Khatri family at Talwandi (modern Nankana Sahib, Pakistan); held a position in the Mughal administration as a granary-keeper for Daulat Khan Lodi at Sultanpur Lodhi; experienced a foundational mystical encounter (the *Vein* river vanishing, the divine commission "There is no Hindu, there is no Muslim") in his late twenties; traveled extensively (the *udasis* — journeys to Bengal, Sri Lanka, Tibet, Mecca, and Baghdad per tradition); finally settled at Kartarpur on the Ravi, where he established the first Sikh community combining communal labor, communal eating (langar), congregational singing of devotional poetry (kirtan), and householder spirituality. Designated his disciple Lehna as successor (renamed Guru Angad) shortly before his death in 1539. Nanak's compositions, preserved in the *Adi Granth* / Guru Granth Sahib, are the textual foundation of Sikhism.

## Historical evidence
Documented through Sikh hagiographical literature (the *Janamsakhi* tradition, beginning ~1570; Bhai Bala, Miharban, Adi, and Puratan recensions), the Adi Granth compositions themselves, Mughal-era references, and Nanak's continuous community memory at Kartarpur and Nankana Sahib.

## Works
The compositions in the Guru Granth Sahib attributed to Mahala 1 (Guru Nanak): *Japji Sahib* (the morning prayer, the foundational Sikh credal-meditational composition), *Asa di Var* (a morning *var*), *Sidh Gosht* (dialogue with Hindu Siddhas), *Onkar* (an alphabetical-theological composition), *Babar Vani* (verses on the 1521 Mughal invasion of Punjab by Babur), hundreds of additional *shabads*.

## Role in the tradition
Founder of the Sikh Panth. The combination of Sant-tradition devotional inheritance with the institutional structures of community (Sangat), communal meal (Langar), and Guru-successor (the line of ten human Gurus → the Granth as eternal Guru) made Sikhism a distinct religion rather than a sect within Hinduism or a syncretism.

## Disputes
- **Travels.** The traditional *udasis* (journeys to Mecca, Baghdad, Tibet, Sri Lanka) are variably accepted by historians; the local Punjabi-Sindhi-northwestern journeys are firm; the longer journeys are reported only in the *Janamsakhis*.
- **Relations to Sufism and Bhakti.** Nanak draws on both Sant and Sufi devotional vocabulary; whether he was personally influenced by Sufi teachers, or by Sant-Bhakti predecessors (Kabir, Ravidas, Namdev, all included in the Adi Granth), or both is variously argued.
