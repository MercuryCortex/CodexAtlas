# Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl

## Identity
A late-10th-c. Toltec priest-king of Tula (Tollan), conflated in subsequent Mesoamerican tradition with the god Quetzalcoatl. By the principal narrative tradition, he was born on the day-sign Ce Acatl (One Reed); ruled Tula as a religious-political leader; was deceived by Tezcatlipoca and Toltec court factions into ritual transgression (drunkenness, incest); abandoned Tula in shame; traveled east to the Gulf coast; departed across the sea on a raft of serpents, vowing to return on his birth-name year (Ce Acatl — which fell in 1519, the year of Cortés's landing — a coincidence post-conquest tradition elaborated extensively).

## Historical evidence
- Tula's archaeological reality is established (excavations from 1940s onward; Diehl 1983 *Tula: The Toltec Capital of Ancient Mexico*).
- A historical priest-king named Topiltzin / Ce Acatl is named in: the *Annals of Cuauhtitlan*, the *Leyenda de los Soles*, Sahagún Book III, Diego Durán's *Historia*, and other early-colonial sources.
- These sources are themselves post-conquest compilations of pre-conquest oral and pictographic tradition; their reliability as biographical history of a 10th-c. figure is variable.
- Nicholson (2001) provides the most thorough source-critical reconstruction; defends a historical kernel.
- Florescano (1993) treats the figure as predominantly mythological with possible historical kernel.

## Role in the tradition
The figure became foundational for Mesoamerican political-religious legitimation: subsequent Mexica rulers traced descent from Toltec lineages going back to Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl; the abandonment-and-promised-return narrative became theologically loaded. Post-conquest, Spanish chroniclers (Sahagún, Durán) and Indigenous-Christian writers (Ixtlilxóchitl) seized on the figure as a possible pre-Christian visitor of St. Thomas the Apostle, or as a figure parallel to Christ. Lafaye 1976 traces the colonial-period syncretism.

## Disputes
- **Historicity.** Nicholson 2001 (historical core); Florescano 1993 (predominantly mythological); Graulich (various) treats the figure as essentially cosmogonic.
- **Cortés-as-returning-Quetzalcoatl.** The post-conquest story that Moctezuma II identified Cortés as the returning Quetzalcoatl, which has been taken as historical fact since the 16th c., has been substantially deconstructed by Lockhart, Restall, Townsend, and others as a post-conquest narrative imposed by both Spanish and Indigenous chroniclers to explain the conquest. The actual pre-conquest Quetzalcoatl-return prophecy is less prominent in genuinely pre-conquest sources.
