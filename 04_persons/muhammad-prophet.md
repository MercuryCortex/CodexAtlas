# Muḥammad ibn ʿAbd Allāh

## Identity
**Muḥammad ibn ʿAbd Allāh (c. 570 – 8 June 632 CE)** — in Islam *Rasūl Allāh*, the Messenger of God, and *Khātam al-Anbiyāʾ*, the Seal of the Prophets — is the figure through whom Muslims hold that God delivered his final revelation, the [[phase-4-034-quran|Qurʾān]], and whose remembered practice (*sunna*) became, alongside that scripture, the second source of Islamic law and piety. Born into the Banū Hāshim clan of the Quraysh at [[mecca-place|Mecca]], orphaned in childhood and raised by his grandfather ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib and then his uncle Abū Ṭālib, he worked in the caravan trade and married the older merchant [[khadija-bint-khuwaylid|Khadīja bint Khuwaylid]] (c. 595), remaining monogamous until her death. Islamic tradition dates his first revelation to c. 610, in a cave on Mount Ḥirāʾ, in the words that open Sūra 96. He preached in Mecca for roughly twelve years, emigrated with his followers to Yathrib — thereafter *al-Madīna* — in 622, and died ten years later having brought most of the Arabian peninsula into the orbit of the community he founded.

The vault treats him with the same apparatus it applies to every founder-figure: what the tradition holds, what external evidence attests, and where modern scholarship contests the reconstruction — three distinct registers, kept distinct.

## The Meccan period (c. 610–622)
The Qurʾānic material assigned to these years is short, rhythmic, oath-laden and eschatological: the oneness and sovereignty of God, the coming Judgement, the accountability of the wealthy, the folly of the ancestral cults. The Sīra tradition sets this preaching against the Quraysh custodianship of [[kaaba|the Kaaba]] and the Hijazi cults of [[allat]], [[al-uzza]] and [[manat]] — the [[tradition-pre-islamic-arabian|pre-Islamic Arabian religious milieu]] — and against economic interests bound up with the sanctuary. Landmarks the tradition records: the beginning of public preaching (c. 613); the emigration of a group of followers to Christian Aksum (c. 615); the clan boycott of Banū Hāshim; the disputed [[satanic-verses-incident|*gharānīq* episode]], in which a concession to the three goddesses is uttered and then retracted; the "Year of Sorrow" (c. 619), when Khadīja and Abū Ṭālib both died; and the [[event-muhammads-miraj-619|Night Journey and Ascension]].

**How secure is this?** Much less secure than the Medinan material. Patricia Crone's *Meccan Trade and the Rise of Islam* (1987) dismantled the picture of Mecca as a great international entrepôt that had been assumed by Watt and others, and Mecca is not named in any pre-Islamic external source. The Meccan chronology in the Sīra is largely a scaffold built from Qurʾānic *asbāb al-nuzūl* ("occasions of revelation") rather than an independent record — which is precisely Uri Rubin's argument (1995): the Sīra's episodes are frequently narrative expansions generated to explain verses, and typologically shaped to echo earlier prophetic biographies.

## The Medinan period (622–632)
The [[event-hegira-622|Hijra]] is the hinge of the whole tradition — so much so that [[umar-ibn-al-khattab|ʿUmar]] later made it, not the birth or the first revelation, year 1 of the [[islamic-hijri-calendar|Hijrī calendar]]. In Medina the movement acquires a polity: an arbitration compact between the emigrants, the Medinan clans and the town's Jewish tribes (the *ṣaḥīfa* or "Constitution of Medina", preserved in [[phase-5-043-ibn-ishaq-sirat-rasul-allah|Ibn Isḥāq]]); a redirected prayer-orientation from Jerusalem to Mecca (c. 624); a legal and ritual vocabulary — [[ritual-salat-five-prayers|prayer]], [[ritual-zakat-charity|alms]], [[ritual-ramadan-sawm|the fast]], [[ritual-hajj-pilgrimage|pilgrimage]] — and a decade of warfare and diplomacy: Badr (624), Uḥud (625), the Trench (627), the destruction of the Jewish clan Banū Qurayẓa (627), the truce of Ḥudaybiyya (628), the taking of Khaybar (628), the [[event-conquest-of-mecca-630|conquest of Mecca]] with the clearing of the sanctuary (630), Ḥunayn and the northern expedition to Tabūk. The Farewell Pilgrimage (632) fixes the [[ritual-hajj-kaaba-origins|ḥajj rite]] in its Islamic form. He died in Medina in June 632 without a designated procedure of succession that both later parties could accept — see [[event-death-of-muhammad-632]] and [[event-saqifa-bani-saida-632]].

The Medinan material is generally judged more reliable than the Meccan: it is denser, more circumstantial, contains embarrassing and unresolved details, and includes at least one document (the *ṣaḥīfa*) whose archaic legal formulae and unedited tribal clauses argue against wholesale later invention (Serjeant; Lecker; Donner 2010).

## The sources, and their evidentiary problems
Four bodies of material carry almost everything the tradition says about him, and each has a different problem.

1. **The Qurʾān.** Contemporary with the events by any reading, but it names him only four times and narrates almost nothing of his life. It is a source *for the preaching*, not a biography.
2. **The *sīra* / *maghāzī* literature.** [[ibn-ishaq|Ibn Isḥāq]]'s biography (c. 767) survives only through Ibn Hishām's edited recension (c. 833) — which explicitly removes material the editor found unfit — plus [[al-tabari|al-Ṭabarī]]'s independent preservation of passages Ibn Hishām cut. So the earliest full biography reaches us at two removes, roughly 200 years after the events, in a form its own transmitters describe as filtered.
3. **Hadith.** The reports of his sayings and practice were canonized in the 9th century ([[phase-5-007-sahih-al-bukhari|al-Bukhārī]], [[phase-5-042-sahih-muslim|Muslim]]) with an elaborate science of *isnād* (chain-of-transmission) criticism. Western scepticism about that science begins with Ignaz Goldziher (1890), who argued that much hadith reflects the doctrinal and political controversies of the 8th–9th centuries, and hardens with Joseph Schacht (1950), whose "backward growth of *isnāds*" thesis holds that chains were extended toward the Prophet as authority-claims escalated. G.H.A. Juynboll's common-link analysis (1983) refined the pessimistic case. Harald Motzki's *isnād-cum-matn* method (2002 and after) is the principal optimistic reply: correlating variations in text with variations in chain lets some traditions be dated to the late 7th century with reasonable confidence. The field has not converged, and the honest statement is that **the hadith corpus is datable in parts and undatable in the aggregate.**
4. **Non-Muslim and documentary evidence.** Thin, but decisive on the core. The Greek *Doctrina Iacobi* (c. 634–640) reports that "a prophet has appeared among the Saracens" without naming him. Thomas the Presbyter, writing in Syriac c. 640, mentions a battle in 634 between the Romans and "the Arabs of Mḥmṭ" — the earliest datable text to name him. The Armenian history attributed to Sebeos (c. 660s) describes "Mahmet" as a merchant who preached to the Arabs the God of Abraham and their descent from Ishmael. On the material side: the bilingual papyrus PERF 558 (Egypt, year 22 = 643 CE) shows the Hijrī era already in administrative use; the Zuhayr rock inscription near al-ʿUlā is dated year 24 (644/45) (Ghabban 2008); the profession *muḥammad rasūl allāh* appears on coinage from the 680s–690s and monumentally in the [[dome-of-the-rock-jerusalem|Dome of the Rock]] in 72 AH / 691–92, alongside Qurʾānic and near-Qurʾānic text. Early Qurʾānic manuscripts (Birmingham, Ṣanʿāʾ, Tübingen) carry radiocarbon ranges reaching into the first Islamic decades — see [[event-quranic-manuscript-finds-2015-onward]].

**Net position of the field.** External evidence independently establishes, within a decade of his death, an Arabian prophet named Muḥammad leading a monotheist movement out of the Hijaz. It does not corroborate the Sīra's episodes. The 1970s revisionist programme — John Wansbrough's late-canonization thesis (*Quranic Studies*, 1977) and Crone & Cook's *Hagarism* (1977) — pressed the gap into a wholesale reconstruction of origins outside Arabia; that reconstruction has not been sustained, particularly against the manuscript and epigraphic evidence recovered since, and both authors moderated their positions. What survived from it is a methodological standard rather than a thesis. Donner's "Believers" model (2010) — the earliest movement as a broadly ecumenical monotheist coalition (*muʾminūn*) that only hardens into a distinct confession under [[abd-al-malik|ʿAbd al-Malik]] — is the influential contemporary middle path.

## As the tradition holds him
Islamic doctrine does not treat Muhammad as divine, and the boundary is guarded: the *shahāda* pairs him with God as *ʿabduhu wa-rasūluhu*, "His servant and His messenger." He is the last of a prophetic line running from Adam through Noah, [[ibrahim-prophet|Abraham]], [[moses|Moses]], David and [[jesus-of-nazareth|Jesus]] — "the Seal of the Prophets" (Q 33:40) — which makes the Qurʾān the final correction of earlier revelations rather than a break with them ([[progressive-revelation]]). Sunni and Shīʿī theology alike affirm his *ʿiṣma*, protection from sin and error, though they define its scope differently. His *sunna*, recovered through hadith, is normative: [[moral-islamic-sharia|Islamic law]] is derived from Qurʾān and *sunna* together, and this is the structural reason the hadith sciences matter so much in [[tradition-sunni-islam|Sunni Islam]]. Classical Sunni theology largely declined to build the case for prophecy on miracle-stories, treating the Qurʾān's inimitability (*iʿjāz*) as the evidentiary miracle. Sufi cosmology went the other way and elaborated the *nūr muḥammadī* — the pre-existent Muhammadan Light as the first thing created and the principle through which creation proceeds ([[nur-muhammadi]], [[ibn-arabi|Ibn ʿArabī]]) — a doctrine that later Salafī reformers rejected as an innovation.

## Disputes
- **Date of death.** Traditional 632. Shoemaker 2012 (T2) argues from the *Doctrina Iacobi* and related material that he may have lived to lead the movement into Palestine, i.e. died c. 634. Anthony 2020 and Hoyland 1997 restate the case for 632; it remains the mainstream date.
- **The *gharānīq* / "Satanic Verses" report.** Preserved by Ibn Isḥāq (via al-Ṭabarī) and by al-Wāqidī; excised by Ibn Hishām; accepted by many early exegetes and rejected as fabrication by most authorities from the 13th century on. Shahab Ahmed's *Before Orthodoxy* (2017) documents that the early community transmitted it without embarrassment — a case where the *change in orthodoxy* is better attested than the event. See [[satanic-verses-incident]].
- **Banū Qurayẓa (627).** The Sīra reports the execution of the men of a Medinan Jewish clan after the Trench. W.N. Arafat and others have contested the account's scale and provenance; most historians (Kister; Watt; Lecker) accept a historical core while treating the numbers as unreliable. The episode is heavily used polemically in both directions, which is itself a reason to handle the source-chain carefully.
- **Literacy (*ummī*).** Traditionally read as "unlettered", supporting the Qurʾān's miraculous character; a substantial scholarly line reads *ummī* instead as "gentile" / "of the community without a scripture" (Q 7:157, 62:2). Both readings are defensible from the Arabic.
- **Mecca's pre-Islamic profile.** Crone 1987 against the "great trade emporium" picture; the resulting question of why a sanctuary town in that location mattered is unresolved.

## Cross-tradition connections
- **[[merkabah-miraj-ascent-chain]]** — the Miʿrāj as the Islamic reception of the Jewish heavenly-ascent tradition (Schäfer 2009). The vault's headline transmission wire on this node.
- **[[tradition-judaism]] / [[tradition-christianity-canonical]]** — the Qurʾān argues with an audience already conversant in biblical and para-biblical narrative, much of it circulating in Syriac Christian homiletic form (Reynolds 2010; Griffith 2013; Neuwirth 2019). Its Jesus is prophet and messiah but not divine son, and its rejection of the Trinity is inscribed monumentally on the [[dome-of-the-rock-jerusalem|Dome of the Rock]] by 692.
- **[[tradition-pre-islamic-arabian]]** — the immediate parent milieu, engaged by the Qurʾān as polemic and by the tradition as displacement: [[hubal]] removed from the sanctuary, the [[kaaba]] retained and re-founded as Abrahamic.
- **[[john-of-damascus]]** — writing inside the Umayyad administration, he gives Christianity its first theological account of Islam (*De Haeresibus* 100/101), classifying it as a Christian heresy; the earliest sustained outside description of Muhammad's teaching by someone with access to Muslims.
- **[[tradition-sunni-islam]] / [[tradition-shia-islam]]** — the succession he left unsettled is the origin of the division; see [[event-saqifa-bani-saida-632]], [[event-first-fitna-656-661]], [[event-karbala-680]].
