# Socrates

## Identity

Athenian philosopher (~470–399 BCE), the foundational figure of Western philosophy, known entirely through the writings of others — primarily [[plato]], but also Xenophon's *Memorabilia* and *Apology*, and Aristophanes's comedic portrayal in *The Clouds* (423 BCE). Left no writings of his own.

## Life and death

Born to a stonemason (or sculptor) father and a midwife mother — Socrates applied his mother's trade as a *midwife of ideas* (*maieutics*). He served as a hoplite at Potidaea, Delium, and Amphipolis. By the late 5th century he was a well-known figure in the Athenian agora, known for his poverty, barefoot walking, and relentless cross-examination of anyone who claimed wisdom.

In 399 BCE he was charged with *impiety* (asebeia — failure to recognize the city's gods and introducing new divine beings) and *corrupting the youth*. He refused to flee Athens or propose a substantial alternative penalty, was convicted by a jury of ~501 citizens, and was executed by drinking hemlock. The trial and death, recounted in Plato's *Apology*, *Crito*, and *Phaedo*, became the defining paradigm of the philosopher-martyr condemned by civic authority for speaking truth.

## Method

- **Elenchus** (cross-examination): Socrates questioned interlocutors claiming expertise (courage, piety, justice, beauty) until their definitions were shown internally inconsistent. The purpose was *aporia* — productive perplexity — as the beginning of genuine inquiry.
- **Socratic irony**: Socrates claimed to know nothing, positioning himself as learner rather than teacher. Scholars dispute whether this is genuine (Socrates really thinks he knows nothing) or strategic (Vlastos 1991 argues it is *complex irony* — Socrates does have beliefs, but adopts the role of ignorance to force the interlocutor to examine their own).
- **Maieutics** (midwifery): Socrates helped others give birth to ideas already latent in them — particularly evident in the Meno's geometry demonstration with the slave boy.
- **Daimonion**: Socrates reported an internal divine sign (*daimonion*) that inhibited him from certain actions — never positively commanded, only restrained. This became the basis of his *impiety* charge (introducing a "new divine being").

## Cross-tradition significance

**MASSIVE-win — Socrates as Christian precursor:**

Justin Martyr (~150 CE, *Apologia* 1.46) explicitly calls Socrates a "Christian before Christ" (*meta logou biōsas*): he lived according to the Logos, was persecuted for exposing false gods, and was condemned for turning people to true virtue. This is the founding statement of the Christian appropriation of Socrates as proto-Christian martyr.

The structural parallel is striking:
- Both Socrates and Jesus are condemned by civic/religious authority for corrupting the youth / disturbing the social order
- Both leave no writings; both are known only through disciples
- Both refuse to compromise their mission to avoid death
- Both are vindicated posthumously by movements their deaths catalyze

Justin's identification of the *logos* as the universal principle — present in Socrates as it is in Christ — is the transmission edge for Greek philosophy → Christian apologetics. Clement of Alexandria and Origen develop this extensively.

The Socratic *daimonion* → Christian **conscience** transmission: the idea of an internal divine voice guiding moral decision-making passes from Socratic philosophy (via Cicero, who latinizes it as *conscientia*) into patristic Christianity. Origen's doctrine of the inner rational principle (*hegemonikon*) is in dialogue with both the Stoic rational soul and the Socratic inner divine guide.

## Disputes

- **What did the historical Socrates actually believe?** The "Socratic problem": Plato's Socrates clearly changes over the dialogues (early = aporetic; middle = metaphysical; late = dialectical). Vlastos (1991) argues the early dialogues represent historical Socrates more closely; the middle dialogues (Republic, Phaedo) are primarily Plato's own views voiced through the Socratic persona.
- **The impiety charge:** Waterfield (2009) argues Socrates was tried less for philosophical impiety than for his political associations — several of his associates (Critias, Alcibiades) had been leaders of the anti-democratic oligarchs. The *amnesty of 403 BCE* prevented direct political prosecution; the impiety charge was the legal vehicle for a political execution.
