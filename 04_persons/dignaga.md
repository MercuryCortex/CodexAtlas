# Dignāga

## Identity

**Dignāga** (also *Diṅnāga* / *Dinnāga*; Tibetan *Phyogs-kyi glang-po*; Chinese 陳那; c. 480–540 CE) was a South Indian Buddhist philosopher, the **founder of the Buddhist logico-epistemological (pramāṇa) school** and, with his later successor Dharmakīrti, one of the two architects of classical Indian epistemology. Hagiography makes him a Brahmin from near **Kāñcī** in the Tamil country who took ordination in a Vātsīputrīya (Pudgalavāda) order under Nāgadatta, broke with it over the "person" (pudgala) doctrine, and — in the traditional account — became a pupil of **[[vasubandhu]]** before teaching at **Nālandā** and dying in the forests of Odra (Orissa). The biography is largely legendary; what is documented is the corpus and its epochal influence.

His achievement was to convert Buddhist thought into a rigorous **theory of knowledge and inference**. Before Dignāga, Buddhist argument was largely dialectical; after him, it is *pramāṇa-vāda* — a systematic doctrine of the means of valid cognition. The modern epithet "the father of medieval Indian logic" (Stcherbatsky 1930) is not hyperbole: even his Hindu opponents reorganised their epistemology around the questions he set.

## Works and doctrine

Dignāga's mature system is stated in the **Pramāṇasamuccaya** ("Compendium of the Means of Knowledge"), a verse text with his own prose *vṛtti*, in six chapters (perception; inference-for-oneself; inference-for-others; the example; *apoha* / word-meaning; the refutation of objections). The Sanskrit original is lost; the work survives in two Tibetan translations and is reconstructed by modern scholars (Hattori for ch. 1; Hayes for chs. 2 and 5). His earlier **Nyāyamukha** ("Introduction to Logic") survives only in Chinese. Three signature moves define him:

- **Two pramāṇas.** Against the Naiyāyika four (perception, inference, comparison, testimony), Dignāga admits only **perception** (*pratyakṣa*) and **inference** (*anumāna*), correlated to exactly two kinds of object — the unique **particular** (*svalakṣaṇa*) and the constructed **universal** (*sāmānya-lakṣaṇa*). He defines perception as **free of conceptual construction** (*kalpanāpoḍha*): the senses deliver the bare particular; all naming, judging and generality belong to thought, i.e. to inference.
- **Apoha.** If only bare particulars are real, how can the word "cow" mean anything without real universals? Dignāga's answer, set out in the fifth chapter of the *Pramāṇasamuccaya*, is the **theory of exclusion** (*anyāpoha*): a term does not pick out a positive shared essence but operates negatively — "cow" means *not-non-cow*. This is the Buddhist form of nominalism, and it became one of the most-attacked Buddhist doctrines in classical India.
- **The wheel of reasons.** In the **Hetucakra** ("Wheel of Reasons") Dignāga tabulates the nine ways a proposed inferential mark (*hetu*) relates to similar and dissimilar instances, isolating the valid cases — the analytic groundwork from which the classical **trairūpya** (three-characteristics) criterion of a sound reason is built. This is the point at which Indian logic becomes a formal theory of the valid inferential sign.

His **Ālambanaparīkṣā** ("Investigation of the Object") argues, on Yogācāra grounds, that the object of awareness is not an external thing but an internal cognitive form — anchoring his epistemology in mind-only metaphysics.

## Cross-tradition significance

**The Nyāya debate (documented intra-Indian polemic).** Dignāga's primary comparative importance is that he *opens* the great named controversy of classical Indian philosophy: Buddhist pramāṇavāda versus [[philosophy-nyaya-vaisheshika-realism|Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika]] realism. This is explicit mutual refutation, not loose resemblance — the Naiyāyika **Uddyotakara** wrote the *Nyāyavārttika* expressly against Dignāga; the Mīmāṃsaka **Kumārila Bhaṭṭa** devoted the *Apohavāda* chapter of his *Ślokavārttika* to demolishing apoha; and the Buddhist reply runs through Dharmakīrti, Śāntarakṣita and Ratnakīrti. The whole classical age of Indian epistemology is the working-out of the agenda Dignāga set.

**Apoha and the problem of universals (structural convergence).** Dignāga's exclusion-semantics addresses the same question — *what, if anything, do general terms name?* — that drives the Western realism–nominalism debate from Porphyry and Boethius through Abelard to Ockham. The parallel is genuine and much discussed (the *Apoha* volume of 2011 reads Dignāga beside Frege and modern semantics), but it is **typological convergence, not transmission**: two independent traditions reaching for a non-realist account of meaning, by routes with no exact counterpart in each other. Meaning-by-exclusion is a specifically Buddhist solution.

**Transmission to East Asia and Tibet (documented descent).** In sharp contrast to those convergences, Dignāga's logic passed by **documented transmission** into two receiving traditions. His *Nyāyamukha* and the *Nyāyapraveśa* of his pupil Śaṅkarasvāmin were translated into Chinese by **Xuanzang** in the 7th century and founded the East Asian science of Buddhist logic (Chinese *yīnmíng* 因明, Japanese *inmyō*). And the entire pramāṇa system entered **Tibet** as *tshad ma*, where — through Dharmakīrti and the Tibetan commentators — it remains the epistemology-and-logic core of the monastic curriculum to this day.

## Disputes

**Authorship and the Nyāyapraveśa.** The *Nyāyapraveśa* ("Introduction to Logic"), long ascribed to Dignāga and hugely influential in China, is now generally credited to his pupil **Śaṅkarasvāmin**; Dignāga's own logical primer is the *Nyāyamukha*. The attribution of the *Hastavālaprakaraṇa* to him is also uncertain.

**His dates and his teacher.** The c. 480–540 placement rests on Frauwallner's chronology (1959, 1961), inferred from Dignāga's position before Dharmakīrti and after the early Nyāya; there is no documented birth or death record. The traditional claim that he studied under **[[vasubandhu]]** is doctrinally attractive but chronologically fraught — it is entangled with the unresolved "two Vasubandhus" question and the wide range of proposed Vasubandhu dates — and is best treated as a lineage claim rather than a secured fact.

**Realist or idealist reading.** As with the school he founded, whether Dignāga's epistemology is ultimately a Sautrāntika external-realism or a Yogācāra idealism is debated; the *Ālambanaparīkṣā* pulls toward the mind-only reading, but the perception theory is often taught at the "external-object" level (Hattori 1968; Hayes 1988).
