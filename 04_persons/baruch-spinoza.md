# Baruch Spinoza

## Identity
Baruch (Benedict) Spinoza (1632–1677) was a Dutch philosopher of Portuguese-Sephardic background, the most rigorous early-modern monist, and one of the principal architects of post-Cartesian European philosophy. Born in Amsterdam to a Portuguese-Jewish merchant family that had fled the Iberian Inquisition; educated in the Amsterdam Talmud Torah school and (later) in Latin and contemporary philosophy with the dissenting Latinist Franciscus van den Enden. Excommunicated from the Amsterdam Sephardic community by formal *cherem* (decree of *herem*, religious ban) on 27 July 1656, at age 23 — the harshest such decree the community ever issued, the exact theological reasons for which remain debated. Adopted the Latin name *Benedictus* (translation of Hebrew *Baruch*); supported himself as a lens-grinder for optical instruments while writing philosophy; lived modestly in successive Dutch towns (Amsterdam, Rijnsburg, Voorburg, The Hague). Died of a lung disease (probably exacerbated by glass-dust from lens-grinding) in The Hague 21 February 1677, age 44.

His *Tractatus Theologico-Politicus* (1670), published anonymously and immediately banned and put on the Index, argued for freedom of philosophical inquiry, separation of philosophy from theology, naturalistic interpretation of biblical miracles, and historical-critical reading of scripture — a founding document of biblical criticism. His *Ethica* (composed during the 1660s and early 1670s, published posthumously 1677) presents in geometric (Euclidean) form a metaphysics of *one substance with infinite attributes* (*deus sive natura* — "God or Nature"), a parallelist solution to the mind-body problem, a deterministic account of human action, and an ethics culminating in the *intellectual love of God* (*amor Dei intellectualis*).

## Historical evidence
Densely documented for an excommunicated 17th-century Sephardic Jew. The Amsterdam Portuguese-Jewish community archive preserves the *cherem* decree (full text in Nadler 2018). Spinoza's own letters (~88 surviving, in the *Opera Posthuma* and later editions); contemporaneous correspondence by Leibniz (who visited him in November 1676), by Henry Oldenburg, Christian Huygens, and others; the early biographies by Johannes Colerus (1705) and Jean-Maximilien Lucas (~1719); civic and notarial records from Amsterdam and The Hague.

## Works
- *Renati Descartes Principiorum Philosophiae Pars I et II* (1663) — geometric reconstruction of Descartes's *Principles*, the only work Spinoza published under his own name during his lifetime.
- *Tractatus Theologico-Politicus* (1670, anonymously) — the political-theological-biblical-critical treatise.
- *Ethica* (composed throughout 1660s and 1670s, posthumous 1677) — the principal metaphysical work.
- *Tractatus Politicus* (unfinished at death, posthumous 1677) — political philosophy.
- *Tractatus de Intellectus Emendatione* (early, unfinished, posthumous 1677) — the methodological introduction.
- *Korte Verhandeling van God, de Mensch en deszelvs Welstand* (Short Treatise; early, in Dutch; rediscovered in the 19th century; not published in his lifetime).
- *Opera Posthuma* / *Nagelate Schriften* (1677) — the posthumous collection edited by his friends (Jarig Jelles, Lodewijk Meyer).
- The standard critical text is the Gebhardt 1925 edition; the standard English translation is Curley's *Collected Works* (Princeton 1985–2016).

## Role in the tradition
Spinoza is among the most consequential modern philosophers. His substance-monism ("deus sive natura") provoked the 18th-century *Pantheismusstreit* in Germany (Jacobi vs. Mendelssohn 1785–1786) that reshaped German philosophy and inaugurated the Romantic-Idealist movements (Goethe, Herder, Schelling, Hegel, the early Coleridge). His biblical hermeneutics in the *TTP* is foundational for modern historical-critical Bible scholarship (acknowledged by Reimarus, Lessing, Strauss, Wellhausen). His political theory of free speech, religious tolerance, and democratic republic shaped Locke (probably; the textual evidence is partial), the Enlightenment, and contemporary liberal political theory. His geometric ethics provoked sustained engagement from Leibniz, Hume, Kant, Schopenhauer, Nietzsche, and (in the 20th century) Deleuze, Negri, Hampshire, Davidson, Della Rocca.

In Jewish intellectual history, Spinoza was the *most excommunicated* of philosophers for two centuries; the 19th-c. Reform Jewish reception (Heine, Auerbach) and especially the 20th-c. secular-Zionist reception (Yirmiyahu Yovel's *Spinoza and Other Heretics*, the Israeli Spinoza prize) gradually rehabilitated him.

## Disputes
- **Reasons for the *cherem* (1656).** The exact theological grounds for the unusually harsh excommunication are not stated in the decree itself; modern reconstruction draws on the *Vindiciae Judaeorum* polemic context, Spinoza's later writings, and contemporary memoirs. Probably: denial of personal afterlife, denial of Mosaic authorship of the Pentateuch, denial of the unique providential election of Israel, denial of a transcendent personal creator-god. Nadler 2018, Popkin 2004.
- **Pantheism or panentheism or *deus sive natura*?** Whether Spinoza's *deus sive natura* is best classed as pantheism (God = Nature, no transcendence) or as a more nuanced position requires precise interpretation of the substance-attribute-mode metaphysics. Most contemporary Spinoza scholarship (Della Rocca 2008, Garrett 1996, Curley 1988) reads Spinoza as strictly identifying God and Nature; older interpretations (Wolfson 1934) press more transcendence into Spinoza than the text supports.
- **Connection to Jewish tradition.** Whether Spinoza is fundamentally a *post-Jewish* thinker (Strauss 1965, Yovel 1989) or whether his thought retains substantial Jewish-philosophical content (Maimonidean rationalism, kabbalistic monism — Wolfson 1934, more recently Melamed) is contested.
- **Reception by the German Idealists.** The Spinoza Lessing read (according to Jacobi's 1785 report) inaugurated the *Pantheismusstreit*; whether Lessing actually held the views Jacobi attributed to him, and whether the German-Idealist reception faithfully represents Spinoza, are perennial debates (Beiser 1987, 2002).
- **Influence on biblical criticism.** Whether Spinoza is the *founder* of modern historical-critical biblical scholarship (Strauss, Preus 2001) or one founder among others (with Hobbes's *Leviathan* III, with Richard Simon, with Isaac La Peyrère) is a question of how strictly one defines "founder."
