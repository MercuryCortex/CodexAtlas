# Yeshua ben Sira

## Identity
Early-2nd-c. BCE Jerusalem scribe-sage and author of *Sirach* (Hebrew: *Sefer ben Sira*; Greek: *Sophia Iēsou ben Eirakh*; Latin Vulgate: *Ecclesiasticus*) — the longest extant Hellenistic-Jewish wisdom book, composed in Hebrew c. 195–175 BCE and translated into Greek by his grandson c. 132 BCE in Alexandria. Ben Sira is the rare Second Temple Jewish author whose name we know and whose self-identification (Sir 50:27) the text preserves. He ran a *bet midrash* in Jerusalem; his book combines proverbial wisdom (modeled on Proverbs) with reflections on the Torah, the priesthood, and a famous "Praise of the Fathers" (Sir 44–50) celebrating Israel's heroes.

## Historical evidence
- Documented through self-naming in Sir 50:27.
- The grandson's prologue (Sir Prologue) dates the translation to "the thirty-eighth year of king Euergetes" — King Ptolemy VIII Euergetes II, putting the translation c. 132 BCE.
- Substantial Hebrew fragments recovered from the Cairo Geniza (1896 onward) and Masada (1964); the textual situation is unusually well-attested for a deuterocanonical work.

## Works
- *Sirach / Ecclesiasticus* — the only authenticated work.

## Role in the tradition
- Jewish reception: not included in the rabbinic canon, but cited in the Talmud and known continuously to rabbinic tradition.
- Catholic and Orthodox: deuterocanonical Scripture.
- Protestant: apocryphal.
- A foundational text for [[torah-and-wisdom-identified]] — Sir 24 makes the Wisdom-Torah identification.

## Disputes
- Hebrew vs. Greek text-traditions: the recovery of substantial Hebrew material has significantly revised the textual situation.
