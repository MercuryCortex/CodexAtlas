# Friedrich Max Müller

## Identity
German-born British Indologist (1823–1900) at Oxford University from 1846, Sanskrit philologist, and effective founder of the modern academic comparative study of religion ("science of religion") in the English-speaking world. General editor of the 50-volume *Sacred Books of the East* (Oxford 1879–1910), which provided the first systematic English translation series of the principal scriptures of Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, Zoroastrianism, Confucianism, Daoism, Islam, and selected Jain and other texts — the foundational reference shelf for late-19th and early-20th-century Western readers of non-Western religion.

## Historical evidence
Extensively documented through Oxford university records, his own voluminous correspondence (Müller wrote ~12,000 letters), and contemporary memorials. The Müller papers are at the Bodleian.

## Works
- *A History of Ancient Sanskrit Literature* (1859) — first systematic English-language survey.
- *Sacred Books of the East* (general editor, 50 vols, 1879–1910) — the most influential single project of Victorian comparative religion.
- *Introduction to the Science of Religion* (1873) — manifesto for the new discipline ("he who knows one knows none").
- Edition of the Rig Veda with Sayana's commentary (6 vols, 1849–1874).

## Role in the tradition
Müller is the founding institutional figure of academic comparative religion in the English-speaking world. His coinage "science of religion" (*Religionswissenschaft*, adapted from German) marks the discipline's late-19th-century self-naming. His Aryan-language-family work (the linguistic *Aryan*, with caveats Müller himself increasingly emphasized in his later years) was later misappropriated for racialist projects against which Müller had explicitly warned.

## Disputes
- **"Solar mythology" theory.** Müller argued much ancient mythology decoded to solar-natural phenomena ("nomina numina"); the position was already controversial in his lifetime (Andrew Lang's *Modern Mythology*, 1897, critiqued it) and is now generally rejected.
- **"Aryan" usage.** Müller's linguistic usage of *Aryan* (= proto-Indo-European language family) was later misappropriated as a racial-biological category by Ariosophists and Nazi-aligned writers; Müller himself protested ("Aryan languages, not Aryan races").
