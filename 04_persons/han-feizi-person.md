# Han Fei

## Identity
Han Fei was a prince of the small state of Han (modern Henan), a student of the Confucian philosopher Xunzi at the Jixia Academy, and the synthesizing theorist of Chinese Legalism — a tradition that won the Warring States politically (the Qin's unification of China, -221) and lost it morally (the Qin's brutal reputation and the official Confucianization of subsequent imperial rhetoric). He died by forced suicide in Qin in -233, twelve years before the Qin's unification.

## Historical evidence
Sima Qian's *Shiji* ch. 63 (joint biography of Laozi, Zhuangzi, Shen Buhai, and Han Fei) is the principal narrative source. The biography reports: Han Fei was a prince of the king of Han; he had a stutter and so wrote rather than spoke; he studied under Xunzi alongside Li Si; he wrote a series of treatises advising the king of Han, who ignored them; his writings reached the king of Qin (the future Qin Shi Huangdi), who reportedly said: "If I could meet this man and learn from him, I would die without regret"; the Qin pressured the king of Han to send Han Fei as envoy; in Qin, Han Fei's fellow Xunzi-student Li Si — now Qin chancellor — engineered his disgrace and forced suicide. The Han Feizi text itself contains numerous internal references consistent with this biography.

## Works
- The *Han Feizi*: 55 received chapters (originally 56). Most chapters by Han Fei himself; some chapters clearly later additions. The text is stylistically more consistent than most pre-Qin philosophical corpora. Major chapters: "The Two Handles" (*Er Bing*, chs. 7-9), "Solitary Indignation" (*Gu Fen*, ch. 11), "The Five Vermin" (*Wu Du*, ch. 49), "Explaining Laozi" (*Jie Lao*, ch. 20), "Illustrating Laozi" (*Yu Lao*, ch. 21).

## Role in the tradition
The synthesizing master of Chinese Legalism. Han Fei integrated three earlier Legalist traditions — Shang Yang's *fǎ* (impersonal law), Shen Buhai's *shù* (administrative technique), Shen Dao's *shì* (positional power) — into a single coherent doctrine of statecraft. Twelve years after his death, Li Si applied something close to Han Fei's program as Qin chancellor under Qin Shi Huangdi, producing the first unified Chinese empire. The Qin's reputation for brutality and its rapid collapse (the Qin lasted only fifteen years after unification) discredited Legalism *rhetorically* for the entire subsequent imperial tradition; *operationally*, however, every subsequent Chinese imperial state governed on partly Legalist principles ("Confucian on the outside, Legalist on the inside" — *wài Rú nèi Fǎ* — is the standard Chinese self-description). Han Fei's *Han Feizi* was thus a text every literate official knew and few openly cited — the operational manual of imperial governance in the world's longest-running bureaucratic empire.

## Disputes
- **Date.** Conventional ~-280 to -233. The death-date by forced suicide in -233 is well-attested.
- **Authorship of individual chapters.** Most chapters are clearly his; some chapters (especially the *Jie Lao* / *Yu Lao* Daodejing commentaries) have been disputed at various times though are now generally accepted as Han Fei's own.
- **Han Fei's death.** Sima Qian's account of Li Si engineering Han Fei's forced suicide is well-attested but cannot be independently confirmed. The death in Qin custody is secure.
- **Was Han Fei a "Legalist"?** The "Legalism" / *fǎ jiā* label is partly a Han-era taxonomical retrojection; whether Han Fei thought of himself as belonging to a discrete "school" is debated (Goldin 2013, Pines).
