# Diodorus Siculus

## Identity
Diodorus Siculus (c. -90 to c. -30 BCE) was a Greek-speaking historian from Agyrium in Sicily who composed the *Bibliotheca Historica* — a 40-book **universal history** covering mythic prehistory through Julius Caesar's Gallic Wars. Of the 40 books, 1–5 and 11–20 survive in continuous form; the rest exist in fragments and Byzantine excerpts. **Book 1 is the entire ethnography and dynastic history of Egypt**, the principal late-Hellenistic Greek treatment of Egyptian religion and the bridge text between [[herodotus]] / [[manetho]] (earlier) and [[plutarch]] (later).

## Historical evidence
Diodorus is named in the *Suda*, by Pliny the Elder, and by later antiquarians. He gives autobiographical details in *Bibliotheca* 1.4: he spent thirty years on the work, traveled in Egypt during the reign of Ptolemy XII Auletes (Cleopatra VII's father) c. -59 to -56, and lived in Rome at the height of late-Republican intellectual activity. He is not a major literary figure in ancient critical reputation, but he is invaluable as a **compiler-transmitter**: he preserves earlier sources (Hecataeus of Abdera on Egypt, Megasthenes on India, Agatharchides on the Red Sea) that would otherwise be lost.

## Works
- ***Bibliotheca Historica*** (Βιβλιοθήκη Ἱστορική, "Historical Library") — 40 books in three major sections:
  - **Books 1–6: Mythological / ethnographic prehistory** (non-Greek and Greek). Book 1 = Egypt; Book 2 = Assyria, Media, Persia, India; Book 3 = Ethiopia, Arabia, Libya; Book 4 = Greek mythology; Book 5 = Mediterranean islands; Book 6 lost.
  - **Books 7–17: From the Trojan War to the death of Alexander.** Books 7–10 lost.
  - **Books 18–40: Diadochi through 60 BCE.** Books 18–20 survive (Diadochi wars); 21–40 fragmentary.
  - See [[phase-3-026-diodorus-bibliotheca-book-1]] for the Egyptian book in detail.

## Role in the tradition
Diodorus functions as a **mid-stage Greek transmission node** in the Egypt → Greek → Christian-Hermetic chain:

1. **For Egypt-Greek transmission.** Book 1 is the most extensive Greek-language synthesis of Egyptian religion and history written between Herodotus (~-440) and Plutarch (~+115). It preserves Hecataeus of Abdera's lost *Aegyptiaca* (early Ptolemaic, ~-300) as its principal source for the early Egyptian theology, alongside Manetho's dynastic schema. Plutarch and later Greek-Egyptian writers draw on Diodorus directly.

2. **For early-modern Egyptology.** From the Renaissance through the 18th c., Diodorus is one of the THREE principal classical sources used to construct European understandings of Egypt (alongside Herodotus and Plutarch). [[athanasius-kircher]]'s *Oedipus Aegyptiacus* (1652–1654) cites Diodorus throughout. The Napoleonic Egyptian expedition (1798–1801) and the early-19th-c. decipherment of hieroglyphs proceeded with Diodorus as background knowledge.

3. **For comparative-religion ancestry.** Diodorus articulates a strong **euhemerist** position — the gods of all peoples are deified mortal benefactors (kings, inventors, sages). This anthropological reduction of religion runs from Euhemerus (c. -300) through Diodorus into Christian apologetics (Lactantius, Eusebius), where it serves anti-pagan polemic, and ultimately into modern naturalistic histories of religion.

4. **For Indian-religious transmission.** Diodorus's Book 2, drawing on Megasthenes's lost *Indica* (~-300, Megasthenes was Seleucid ambassador to the court of Chandragupta Maurya), preserves the earliest Greek descriptions of Indian religion — including identifications of Indian gods with Greek (Krishna = Heracles; Shiva = Dionysus). This is the load-bearing **Greek-Indian** religious transmission edge, parallel to the Greek-Egyptian one.

## Disputes
- **Compiler or original thinker?** 19th-c. *Quellenforschung* treated Diodorus as a slavish copyist (Eduard Schwartz: Diodorus is "a wholly mediocre, wholly unoriginal compiler"). Sacks (1990) substantially rehabilitated him: Diodorus has consistent **moralizing and political viewpoints** (anti-tyranny, sympathy for monarchic just rule, *euergetism* as the criterion of greatness) that he imposes on whatever source he uses. The current view is that he is a thoughtful (if unspectacular) Augustan-era Greek intellectual, not just a transcriber.
- **Reliability on Egypt.** Substantial dispute, mediated through how heavily he relies on Hecataeus of Abdera (whose own reliability is contested — Hecataeus was an early-Ptolemaic-court Greek writing partly propagandistic ethnography). Burton 1972 and the post-Murray (1970) school treat Hecataeus-via-Diodorus as **substantially reliable on Egyptian temple-cult and ritual** even where the political-historical narrative is colored.
- **Dates.** Diodorus's own internal dates for his Egyptian visit are debated within a window of -60 to -56. The *terminus ad quem* for the *Bibliotheca* is set by his last datable reference (Octavian renaming Caesarion's town in Egypt, c. -30).
