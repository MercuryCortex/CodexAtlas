# Protagoras

## Identity

Protagoras of Abdera (Greek **Πρωταγόρας**, c. 490–420 BCE) was by ancient report the eldest and most celebrated of the Greek **Sophists** — the itinerant, fee-charging teachers of *aretē* (civic excellence) and rhetoric who flourished in the democratic cities of the fifth century BCE. By ancient report he was the first man to call himself a *sophistēs* and the first to charge a fee for instruction (Plato, *Protagoras* 349a; *Meno* 91d–e). He is remembered above all for two sentences, each the opening of a lost book: the **relativist** formula *"of all things the measure is man"* (DK 80 B1) and the **agnostic** formula *"concerning the gods I am unable to know whether they exist or not"* (DK 80 B4). Like every Sophist, none of his works survives whole; he reaches us through fragments, doxography (chiefly Diogenes Laertius IX.50–56), and — most consequentially — through [[plato]], who named a dialogue after him and made him the great foil of the *Theaetetus*. In the Atlas he is the founding figure of the [[philosophy-sophism|Sophistic movement]], which explicitly flags his node as owed.

## Life and career

Protagoras was born at Abdera in Thrace — the same city as the atomist [[philosophy-atomism-greek|Democritus]], a coincidence that later doxography inflated into a (chronologically doubtful) discipleship. He spent some forty years as a travelling teacher, visiting Athens repeatedly and moving in the circle of Pericles: Plutarch preserves an anecdote of the two men spending a whole day debating a point of legal responsibility (*Life of Pericles* 36). His one datable public act is prestigious — around **444/443 BCE Pericles commissioned him to draft the laws** for Thurii, the new Panhellenic colony in southern Italy (Diog. Laert. IX.50; Heraclides Ponticus). Diogenes reports that he taught until about seventy and died around ninety, and preserves a later tradition — widely doubted by modern scholars — that Protagoras was **prosecuted for impiety** on account of *On the Gods*, that his books were burned in the Athenian agora, and that he drowned crossing to Sicily while in flight. The impiety story is treated as probably legendary (see Disputes); the drowning is a floating topos.

## Doctrine

**Relativism — "man the measure."** Protagoras opened his chief work *Alētheia* (Truth) with the most famous sentence of the whole Sophistic movement: *pantōn chrēmatōn metron anthrōpos, tōn men ontōn hōs estin, tōn de ouk ontōn hōs ouk estin* — "of all things the measure is man: of the things that are, that they are, and of the things that are not, that they are not" (DK 80 B1, quoted at Plato *Theaetetus* 152a and Sextus, *Adv. Math.* VII.60). On the reading Plato gives it, whatever appears to each perceiver *is* true for that perceiver: the wind that feels cold to me *is* cold for me and warm for you if it feels warm — there is no perceiver-independent fact of the matter (*Theaetetus* 152b). Whether "man" (*anthrōpos*) means the individual or the human species, and whether the "measure" is of perception or of value, is debated (Schiappa 1991).

**Agnosticism about the gods.** His book *Peri theōn* (On the Gods) began: *"Concerning the gods, I am unable to know either that they exist or that they do not exist, nor what they are like in form; for many things prevent knowledge — the obscurity of the matter and the shortness of human life"* (DK 80 B4). This is not atheism but a principled suspension: the question is declared humanly undecidable. It stands beside the naturalistic religion-theory of his fellow Sophist Prodicus as among the sharpest surviving fifth-century statements of religious skepticism.

**Antilogic and "the weaker argument."** Protagoras held that *"on every matter there are two arguments opposed to each other"* (DK 80 A1, B6a) and professed to teach how to *"make the weaker argument the stronger"* (*ton hēttō logon kreittō poiein*) — a phrase that, in Plato's and Aristophanes' hostile usage, fixed the pejorative sense of "sophistry." In the *Protagoras* he defends the teachability of civic virtue with the "Great Speech," a myth of Prometheus and Epimetheus in which Zeus distributes justice (*dikē*) and shame (*aidōs*) to all citizens alike (320c–328d).

## In the ancient sources

The Protagoras evidence is a textbook case of the Sophists' source-catastrophe: his own books are lost, and we know him through his critics. **Plato is the fullest and most hostile witness.** The *Protagoras* stages him at the height of his fame, magnificent but evasive; the *Theaetetus* (152a–171d) quotes B1, reconstructs the relativist doctrine with unusual care (the "secret doctrine" of flux behind it), and then destroys it with the celebrated **self-refutation** (*peritropē*, 170e–171c): if every belief is true for the believer, then the belief of the many that Protagoras is *wrong* must itself be true — the doctrine turns on itself. Aristotle takes up the same refutation in *Metaphysics* Γ (1007b–1009a). The direct fragments and testimonia are collected as Diels-Kranz chapter 80 (English in Sprague 1972). Because the prosecutor is also the archivist, everything we say about Protagoras is filtered through the tradition that set out to discredit him — the root of the modern "rehabilitation" debate.

## Cross-tradition significance

**Founding figure of Sophism (secure, internal to Greece).** Protagoras is not merely a member but the doctrinal anchor of the [[philosophy-sophism|Sophistic movement]]: the two positions that node treats as the movement's core — relativism and religious agnosticism — are *his* fragments. The hub's own Disputes section records him as an owed person-node; this node fills that demand.

**Defined-against Platonism (secure, agonistic).** [[philosophy-platonism|Platonism]] is in large part *built against* Protagoras. Plato's distinction between knowledge and mere opinion, and his theory of Forms as perceiver-independent reality, are the constructive replies to "man the measure." Plato both preserved and prosecuted him — naming a dialogue after him and staging Socrates' dismantling of his relativism in the *Theaetetus*.

**Ancestor of Greek skepticism (genealogical-within-Greek).** Protagorean relativism is a recognized upstream tributary of [[philosophy-pyrrhonian-skepticism|Pyrrhonian skepticism]]: Sextus Empiricus engages it directly (*PH* I.216–219; *Adv. Math.* VII.60–64) while carefully distinguishing the skeptics' suspension of judgment (*epochē*) from Protagoras' positive doctrine that each appearance is *true*. His antilogic — two opposed arguments on every matter — is a direct methodological ancestor of the skeptics' balancing of arguments (*isostheneia*).

**Independent convergence with Indian materialism.** Protagoras' agnosticism invites comparison with [[philosophy-carvaka|Cārvāka/Lokāyata]], the materialist-skeptical current of classical India, on two specific points: a naturalistic, this-worldly attitude to inherited religion, and the shared source-catastrophe — both survive almost solely through the polemics of their enemies. There was no contact between fifth-century Greece and early Indian materialism; this is a case of **typological convergence, valuable precisely because transmission is excluded.** The pairing is the Atlas's structural comparison, not a claim advanced in any single monograph.

**Contemporary Abderite milieu (not descent).** Ancient doxography made Protagoras a pupil of Democritus, both from Abdera, placing him next to [[philosophy-atomism-greek|Greek atomism]]. Modern scholarship regards the discipleship as chronologically doubtful (Protagoras is the elder) and probably a later construction; the two are recorded here as a shared local milieu, not a doctrinal filiation.

## Disputes

- **What does "man the measure" mean?** The single most contested sentence in the Sophistic corpus. Is *anthrōpos* the individual perceiver (the reading Plato exploits in the *Theaetetus*) or the human species collectively? Is the "measure" epistemological (of perceptions and beliefs) or also evaluative (of value and justice)? Schiappa (1991) argues for a narrower, more technical rhetorical reading against the sweeping subjectivism Plato attributes to him; Kerferd (1981) defends a genuine relativist core. Because our fullest statement of the doctrine *is* Plato's reconstruction, the dispute cannot be fully settled from the fragments alone.

- **Was Protagoras really tried for impiety?** Diogenes Laertius (IX.52, 54) reports a prosecution over *On the Gods*, a public book-burning, and exile. Many scholars regard this as a **later legend** — a retrojection of the Socrates trial pattern onto the arch-Sophist, or a moralizing tale attached to the striking agnostic opening. The dates and details are inconsistent across sources, and no fifth-century evidence confirms it. It is reported here as tradition, not fact.

- **The rehabilitation of the Sophists.** For two millennia "sophist" was a term of abuse taking Plato's verdict at face value. From Hegel and George Grote onward a major revision read the Sophists — Protagoras foremost — as the Greek Enlightenment, the discovery of subjectivity and the human measure of things. How far to trust Plato's portrait of Protagoras remains live (Kerferd 1981; Schiappa 1991).
