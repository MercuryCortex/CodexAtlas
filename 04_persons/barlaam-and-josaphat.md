# Barlaam and Josaphat

## Identity
Barlaam and Josaphat are a pair of legendary Christian saints whose hagiographic narrative is **a Christianized version of the [[siddhartha-gautama-buddha|Buddha's]] life-story**. The transmission chain is one of the best-documented cross-tradition hagiographic edges in the entire history of religion: **Indian Buddhist *jātaka* + biography of the Buddha → Iranian Manichaean adaptation (the Buddha re-figured as "the prince") → Arabic *Kitāb Bilawhar wa Yūdāsaf* (8th-9th c., Baghdad) → Georgian *Balavariani* (9th-10th c.) → Greek *Barlaam and Ioasaph* (10th-11th c., traditionally but uncertainly attributed to John of Damascus; more likely Euthymius the Iberian / Athonite ~990) → Latin and vernacular European versions (11th c. onward)**. The name *Josaphat / Ioasaph / Iōdasaph / Yūdāsaf* is a corruption of **Bodhisattva** (Sanskrit *bodhisattva* → Manichaean Sogdian *bwdysṭβ* → Arabic *Yūdāsaf* / *Būdhāsaf* → Georgian *Iodasaph* → Greek *Ioasaph* → Latin *Iosaphat*). The narrative was beloved across medieval Europe; **Saint Josaphat was inscribed in the Roman Martyrology by Sixtus V in 1583 with feast day 27 November**, and remained a canonical Catholic saint until removed from the General Roman Calendar revision of 1969. **This is the single most consequential Buddhist-to-Christian transmission ever documented**: the historical Buddha became a Christian saint and was venerated as such for ~400 years.

## Historical evidence
The transmission chain is fully reconstructible from extant intermediate texts:
- **Original Buddhist substrate**: the *Buddhacarita* of Aśvaghoṣa (~2nd c. CE) + the *Lalitavistara Sūtra* + the *Mahāvastu* — all canonical Buddha-biographies featuring the prince, the four sights (old man, sick man, corpse, ascetic), the renunciation. Plus the surrounding *jātaka* tales.
- **Iranian / Manichaean adaptation**: indirect — the Manichaeans took up the Buddha as "Buddha of Light" (Mani's predecessor) and adapted Buddhist hagiographic material. Asmussen 1965 documents the Manichaean-Buddhist textual layer.
- **Arabic *Kitāb Bilawhar wa Yūdāsaf*** (8th-9th c., Baghdad — partial Arabic mss extant) — the first Arabic-Christian version; channels the Buddhist substrate through Islamic-Arabic literary culture.
- **Georgian *Balavariani*** (9th-10th c., Lang 1957 translation) — the Christianized Caucasian intermediate; closest extant version to the lost Arabic original.
- **Greek *Barlaam and Ioasaph*** (10th-11th c.) — the standard Greek text; traditionally attributed to John of Damascus (~675-749) but more likely composed by Euthymius the Iberian (~955-1028) at Iviron monastery on Mount Athos, who translated from the Georgian. Loeb edition standard (Woodward & Mattingly 1914).
- **Latin + vernacular European versions** (11th-16th c.) — the medieval European wide-reception; the Latin enters the *Legenda Aurea* (Jacobus de Voragine, ~1260) and from there into every medieval European Christian saints-calendar.

Modern scholarly identification of the Buddhist source: first proposed by **Diogo do Couto** (Portuguese chronicler in India, 1612) on the basis of Goa-based observation of Indian-Buddhist materials; systematized by **Édouard de Laboulaye** + **Felix Liebrecht** in the 19th c.; established by **Joseph Jacobs** *Barlaam and Josaphat: English Lives of Buddha* (1896); Lopez & McCracken 2014 is the standard modern treatment.

## Role in the tradition
- **In Western Christianity**: a canonical Catholic saint from the late-medieval period; feast day 27 November per the 1583 Roman Martyrology; venerated across medieval Europe; removed from the General Roman Calendar in the 1969 calendar revision (along with many saints whose historicity was deemed weak — but Josaphat is the unique case of a saint whose historicity is weak *because he is the Buddha*).
- **In Orthodox Christianity**: Saint Ioasaph; venerated 26 August (Orthodox calendar); some Eastern Orthodox jurisdictions still observe.
- **In comparative religion**: **the canonical example** of cross-tradition hagiographic transmission. The case is uncontested in modern scholarship; the only debate is the precise routing (Manichaean intermediary vs. direct Sogdian-Christian transmission).

## Disputes
- **Authorship of the Greek *Barlaam and Ioasaph***: traditional attribution to John of Damascus is rejected by modern scholarship (he died ~749 — the Greek text reflects an Arabic source post-dating that). Most scholars (Volk 2009; Lopez & McCracken 2014) attribute to **Euthymius the Iberian** (~955-1028), the Georgian monk-scholar at Mount Athos, who translated from Georgian to Greek.
- **Manichaean vs. direct Buddhist transmission**: did the Buddhist material reach Christianity via Manichaean intermediation, or directly via Christian-Sogdian / Christian-Central-Asian contact? Asmussen 1965 argues Manichaean primary; some recent scholarship adds Sogdian-Christian-Nestorian-via-Silk-Road as a parallel pathway. Most likely both.
- **The name etymology** Bodhisattva → Yūdāsaf → Ioasaph: uncontested (Lang 1957 walks through the philology).
- **Liturgical status post-1969**: Pope Paul VI's 1969 calendar revision removed many quasi-legendary saints; Saint Josaphat is the famous case-study because the historicity-weakness is precisely that he is a Christianization of the historical Buddha.
