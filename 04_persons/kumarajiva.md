# Kumārajīva

## Identity
Kumārajīva (344-413) was a Central Asian Mahāyāna monk and translator whose Chinese translations of major Mahāyāna sūtras and śāstras — produced at the Chang'an translation bureau of the Later Qin dynasty (Yao Xing, r. 394-416) between 401 and 413 — became the operational East Asian Buddhist canon. His Lotus Sūtra translation (406), Diamond Sūtra translation (402), Vimalakīrti Sūtra translation (406), Smaller Sukhāvatīvyūha translation (402), Pañcaviṃśatisāhasrikā Prajñāpāramitā translation, and translation of Nāgārjuna's *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* and other Madhyamaka works (c. 409-410) are the standard texts of East Asian Buddhism. No other single translator in any tradition has had a comparable canonical impact: the Chinese, Korean, Japanese, and Vietnamese Buddhist worlds read sūtras through Kumārajīva's Chinese, not the Sanskrit originals.

## Historical evidence
Well-documented. Huijiao's *Gaoseng Zhuan* (Biographies of Eminent Monks, 519) ch. 2 gives an extensive biography. Kumārajīva's father was an Indian aristocrat; his mother was a Kuchean princess who became a nun. He was educated in Kucha and Kashmir and was already famous as a Mahāyāna teacher by the late 4th century. In 384 he was abducted by the Former Qin general Lü Guang during a military campaign in the Tarim Basin and held captive in Liangzhou for seventeen years before the Later Qin's invasion and his transfer to Chang'an in 401. From 401 to 413 he led an imperially-sponsored translation bureau that produced ~35 works (the conventional count, with some uncertainty); Huijiao reports an extraordinary number (300 disciples; certain translations done in a single morning before an audience).

## Works
Translator, not author. The Chinese translations of the following texts are operationally canonical for East Asia (all dates are completion dates at Chang'an):
- *Lotus Sūtra* (*Miaofa Lianhua Jing*), 406.
- *Diamond Sūtra* (*Jingang Jing*), 402.
- *Vimalakīrti Sūtra* (*Weimoluojie Jing*), 406.
- *Smaller Sukhāvatīvyūha* (*Amituo Jing*, the Smaller Pure Land Sutra), 402.
- *Pañcaviṃśatisāhasrikā Prajñāpāramitā* (*Da Pin Bore Jing*), 404.
- *Aṣṭasāhasrikā Prajñāpāramitā* (*Xiao Pin Bore Jing*), 408.
- Nāgārjuna, *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* (*Zhong Lun*), c. 409.
- *Dvādaśanikāya Śāstra* (*Shi'er Men Lun*), c. 409.
- Āryadeva, *Catuḥśataka* (*Bai Lun*), c. 404.
- Various other Madhyamaka and Mahāyāna works.

## Role in the tradition
The decisive translator of East Asian Mahāyāna. Where earlier Chinese translators (Lokakṣema, Dharmarakṣa) had used a strategy of "matching meanings" (*geyi*) — translating Buddhist terms with pre-existing Daoist vocabulary — Kumārajīva developed a more philologically and doctrinally precise translation idiom that established the standard technical vocabulary for Chinese Buddhism. His Madhyamaka translations founded the Chinese Sanlun (Three-Treatise) school; his Lotus Sūtra translation became the East Asian standard recension underpinning the Tiantai, Tendai, and Nichiren traditions; his Diamond Sūtra and Heart Sūtra (the Heart Sūtra now generally attributed to a later Chinese composition, traditionally though not actually translated by Kumārajīva) became the most chanted texts in East Asian Buddhism. Kumārajīva is among the most consequential single translators in any literary tradition anywhere.

## Disputes
- **Personal history.** The Huijiao biography contains some legendary elaborations (the precocious-child stories, the prophetic dreams); the basic outline (Kuchean education, abduction by Lü Guang, seventeen years in Liangzhou, transfer to Chang'an in 401, twelve years of translation activity until death in 413) is well-attested.
- **Heart Sūtra attribution.** Traditional account credits Kumārajīva with the first Chinese Heart Sūtra translation. Nattier 1992 demonstrated that the standard Heart Sūtra is a 7th c. Chinese composition (apocryphally back-translated to Sanskrit); the Kumārajīva attribution is part of the apocryphal-text mechanism. (See [[phase-5-002-heart-sutra]].)
- **Number of translations.** Huijiao's count is partly inflated; the rigorously-secured number is ~35 works.
- **The "two wives" tradition.** Huijiao reports that Yao Xing made Kumārajīva take two women in succession to perpetuate his lineage (his exceptional intellect, the king reasoned, should not be lost); Kumārajīva reportedly accepted with shame. The historicity is uncertain; the story functions to address the awkwardness of a celibate translator-monk leaving the order.
