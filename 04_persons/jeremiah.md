# Jeremiah

## Identity
Jeremiah ben Hilkiah of Anathoth (a Levitical priestly town just north of Jerusalem), called as a prophet in the 13th year of Josiah (~627 BCE) and prophesying through the entire Babylonian destruction of Judah — Josiah's reform (~622), Josiah's death at Megiddo (609), the Babylonian conquest of Judah (605), the first deportation (597), the destruction of Jerusalem and the First Temple (586), and the post-destruction Judahite remnant including his own forced exile to Egypt with the refugees. His life spanned the collapse of the Judahite kingdom.

## Historical evidence
The book of Jeremiah is one of the longest and most biographically rich prophetic books in the Hebrew Bible. The Baruch narratives (chs. 26, 36, 37–45) supply detailed contemporary detail about Jeremiah's interactions with King Jehoiakim, the officials of the besieged Jerusalem, and the post-destruction Gedaliah regime. Independent attestation in 2 Kings 24–25, 2 Chronicles 35–36, and — significantly — in the recovered bulla seal "Baruch ben Neriah the scribe" found in the City of David excavations, providing tangible archaeological connection to Jeremiah's amanuensis.

## Works
The book of Jeremiah exists in two substantially different ancient forms: the shorter Septuagint (~13% shorter than the Masoretic) reflects an earlier textual stage, while the Masoretic represents an expanded later edition. Both are attested at Qumran. The complex compositional history (oracles dictated to Baruch, narratives composed by Baruch or Deuteronomistic editors, later additions) is among the most reconstructable in the Hebrew Bible. Lamentations is traditionally attributed to Jeremiah but is widely rejected as such by modern scholarship.

## Role in the tradition
The "weeping prophet" of Jewish and Christian tradition. His "new covenant" oracle (Jer 31:31–34) is foundational to the Christian theological self-understanding (Hebrews 8 cites it at length). His themes — the futility of false trust in the Temple, the inwardness of true religion, individual moral responsibility (Jer 31:29–30) — significantly shape later prophetic, apocalyptic, and Christian thought.

## Disputes
- **Compositional layers.** Multiple Deuteronomistic editorial strata are widely recognized. The Septuagint vs. Masoretic divergence reflects ongoing redactional development into the Persian and Hellenistic periods.
- **Lamentations authorship.** Traditional attribution to Jeremiah largely abandoned in critical scholarship.
