# Aśoka the Great

## Identity
Aśoka (r. ~-268 to -232) was the third emperor of the Mauryan dynasty and the single most consequential lay patron in the history of Buddhism. Inheriting the largest pre-modern Indian empire from his grandfather Chandragupta and father Bindusāra — extending from Bactria-Gandhāra to the Tamil Nadu border, from the Iranian frontier across to Bengal — Aśoka completed its expansion with the conquest of Kaliṅga (~-261), then according to his own Rock Edict XIII underwent a moral conversion of revulsion at the casualties (~100,000 killed, ~150,000 deported, "many more died") and committed his reign to *dhamma* — a non-sectarian ethical-religious doctrine of non-violence, religious tolerance, parental respect, kindness to all beings, and care for animals. He sponsored Buddhism specifically (the Third Buddhist Council at Pāṭaliputra; the dhamma-missions to Sri Lanka and the Hellenistic kingdoms; the construction of *stūpa*s; the personal pilgrimage to Lumbinī commemorated by the still-extant Lumbinī pillar of ~-249), but his edicts use a non-sectarian register addressing "all sects" of his subjects.

## Historical evidence
Aśoka is one of the best-attested ancient Indian historical figures, on multiple independent lines:
- **Epigraphic**: ~33 surviving inscriptions in his own voice — rock edicts (14 Major Rock Edicts + 7 Minor Rock Edicts), pillar edicts (7 Major Pillar Edicts + several Minor Pillar Edicts), cave inscriptions (Barabar Hills), and the bilingual Kandahar Greek-Aramaic edict. See [[phase-3-030-asokan-edicts]]. Inscriptions are dated by his regnal years and use his title *Devānāṃpriya Priyadarśin* ("Beloved of the Gods, Looker-with-Kindness"); the identification with Aśoka was established by James Prinsep's 1837 decipherment of Brāhmī.
- **Textual (Buddhist)**: the Pali *Mahāvaṃsa* and *Dīpavaṃsa* (Sri Lankan chronicles, ~4th-6th c. CE) give a detailed but partly hagiographic account; the Sanskrit *Aśokāvadāna* (~2nd c. CE) gives a parallel northern Buddhist account; the Chinese *A-yu wang jing* preserves the Aśokāvadāna in translation.
- **Textual (Hellenistic)**: Athenaeus *Deipnosophistae* preserves a Hellenistic fragment naming "Amitrochates" (Bindusāra, Aśoka's father) corresponding with Antiochus I, confirming the Mauryan-Seleucid diplomatic chain in which Aśoka grew up. Strabo and other Greek sources mention the Mauryan court.
- **Archaeological**: Mauryan-period material culture across the subcontinent; the Aśokan capitals (lion of Sarnath, etc.) constitute a coherent imperial visual program.

## Works
- [[phase-3-030-asokan-edicts]] — the corpus of rock and pillar inscriptions; the only first-person royal voice surviving from pre-Common-Era India. **The single most important primary source for the historical reception of Buddhism in its first 200 years.**
- No literary works in the conventional sense; the edicts ARE his corpus.

## Role in the tradition
- **In Buddhism**: Aśoka is the paradigmatic *cakravartin* ("wheel-turning king") — the lay-monarch counterpart to the Buddha's spiritual *cakravartin*. The Pali tradition gives him an idealized hagiography; the Sanskrit *Aśokāvadāna* gives a more morally ambiguous one (the pre-conversion "Caṇḍāśoka" — Cruel Aśoka — who orders mass executions before his Buddhist conversion). His patronage establishes Buddhism as a translocal religion: Mahinda's mission to Sri Lanka ~-247 founds Sinhalese Theravāda; the dhamma-missions to the Hellenistic kingdoms are the first attested Buddhist proselytization outside India.
- **In modern Indian national identity**: the Lion Capital of Sarnath is the official emblem of the Republic of India (adopted 1950); the Aśoka Cakra (the *dhamma-cakra* with 24 spokes) appears on the Indian national flag. Aśoka was deliberately recovered by Nehru as a non-sectarian, non-violent, religiously tolerant founder-figure of Indian civilization — the historiography is partly post-colonial nation-building (Thapar 1961 navigates the tension).
- **In Mahāyāna**: the Aśokāvadāna circulates as an example of skilful-means transformation: even the violent emperor can become a great patron.

## Disputes
- **Dates**: regnal accession is dated by Hultzsch's reading of Rock Edict XIII's mention of Antiochus II Theos (r. -261 to -246), Ptolemy II Philadelphus (r. -283 to -246), Antigonus Gonatas (r. -276 to -239), Magas of Cyrene (r. -276 to -250), and Alexander II of Epirus (r. -272 to -255 or -242). The overlap window narrows the relevant regnal year to ~-256 (when all five were simultaneously alive); Aśoka's accession is reconstructed back from there.
- **Sectarian sponsorship**: did Aśoka favor Buddhism specifically (Pali tradition) or operate as a non-sectarian *dhamma* sovereign (the edicts' rhetoric)? Modern scholarship reads the edicts' *dhamma* as a deliberately non-sectarian ethical-religious frame that nonetheless privileges Buddhist institutions through specific patronage (Mahāvihāra missions, Lumbinī pillar, monastic-schism edict).
- **The Kaliṅga conversion**: how much is internally-attested moral conversion vs. ex post imperial rhetoric? Lahiri 2015 argues the conversion is genuine; Thapar 1961 reads it as more layered.
- **Genealogical relationship to Mahinda**: Pali tradition says Mahinda is Aśoka's son; some Sanskrit sources say junior contemporary. The Sri Lankan claim is hard to verify but functions as foundational charter.
