# Ram Mohan Roy

## Identity
Bengali Brahmin polymath, religious reformer, and public intellectual (1772–1833); founder of the *Brahmo Sabha* (1828, later Brahmo Samaj) and the principal architect of 19th-century Hindu modernist religious-philosophical reform. Educated extensively in Persian and Arabic (the linguistic-intellectual world of late-Mughal North India), Sanskrit (the Brahmanical Vedic-Vedantic tradition), and English (the world of British colonial Calcutta); employed in the East India Company administration; campaigned successfully for the British abolition of *satī* (1829); founded the *Brahmo Sabha* (1828) as a non-idolatrous, non-caste-restricted Hindu monotheistic congregation centered on Upaniṣadic revelation; traveled to England in 1830 as Mughal envoy to Britain; died at Bristol in 1833.

## Works
- ***Tuhfat-ul-Muwahhidin*** (A Gift to the Monotheists, 1804): his first published work, in Persian with an Arabic preface — a sustained Enlightenment-influenced critique of religious idolatry, ritualism, and clericalism applicable across Hindu, Islamic, and Christian traditions.
- **Upaniṣadic translations** (1815–1819): English translations of the *Kena*, *Isha*, *Katha*, *Mundaka* Upaniṣads and the *Vedanta Sutras*, with extensive introductions arguing that the Upaniṣadic Vedanta is the doctrinal substance of true Hinduism — and that idolatry, polytheism, satī, child-marriage, and caste-prohibitions are non-essential later corruptions.
- ***The Precepts of Jesus*** (1820): a synoptic harmony of the four gospels' ethical teaching with the supernatural and Trinitarian material excised — a Hindu-Unitarian Jesus parallel to (and historically connected with) the contemporary American Unitarian movement of Channing and Emerson.
- **Newspaper and periodical journalism**: founded and edited *Sambad Kaumudi* (Bengali, 1821), *Mirat-ul-Akhbar* (Persian, 1822), *The Brahmunical Magazine* (English, 1821). Among the founders of Indian-language print journalism.

## Role in the tradition
- **Brahmo Samaj**: founder of the first organized Hindu monotheistic-reform institution. The Brahmo Samaj remained an institutionally small movement but its intellectual-cultural influence on 19th–20th-c. Bengali elite culture was decisive: Debendranath Tagore, Keshub Chandra Sen, Rabindranath Tagore (Debendranath's son and the future Nobel laureate), and a large fraction of the Bengali Renaissance intellectual class were Brahmos.
- **Hindu modernist template**: Roy's intellectual program — go back to the Upaniṣads as scriptural foundation, treat idolatry / caste / satī / sectarian ritual as corruptions, locate the true monotheism in the Vedantic-Brahmanic core, find common ground with Christian (Unitarian) and Islamic (Sufi-rational) monotheisms — is the template subsequently elaborated by Dayananda Saraswati (Arya Samaj), Keshub Chandra Sen, Vivekananda, Aurobindo, Gandhi, and Radhakrishnan.
- **First major non-European intellectual in the British public sphere**: his 1830–1833 sojourn in England, where he met Bentham, the Unitarians (William Roscoe, Lant Carpenter), parliamentary leaders, and continental philosophers, marks the first sustained presence of a non-European religious-philosophical reformer in the metropolitan European intellectual culture. The Bristol monument (he died and is buried there) is one of the earliest extant memorials to an Indian intellectual in Europe.

## Cross-tradition connections
- **Explicit Enlightenment-Vedantic-Christian-Islamic synthesis**: Roy is the first thinker in modern Indian intellectual history to combine, with full philological seriousness in all four traditions, post-Enlightenment European rationalism, Upaniṣadic Vedanta, Unitarian Christianity, and Islamic-Persian rational theology. The *Tuhfat-ul-Muwahhidin* and the *Precepts of Jesus* are the explicit documentary record. His correspondence with the American Unitarians (Channing wrote about him; Joseph Tuckerman knew him; the Boston Unitarian press published his works) constitutes an early Indian-American religious-intellectual connection that the later 19th-c. Theosophical Society and Vivekananda's 1893 visit elaborated.
- **Brahmo-Unitarian connection**: the historical-documentary connection between the Brahmo Samaj and the British/American Unitarian movement is the clearest 19th-c. organizational Hindu-Christian liberal-reform alliance. Zastoupil 2010 traces it in detail.
- **Persian-Arabic intellectual formation as substrate**: Roy's first language of religious-philosophical writing was Persian, not English or Sanskrit; his formative reading included Razi, Ibn Sina (Avicenna), Mulla Sadra, and Sufi-rational *kalām*. The Brahmo theological vocabulary owes as much to Islamic *tawḥīd* (strict monotheism) as to Vedantic *brahman* — an underappreciated substrate of modern Hindu reform.
- **Foundational figure for "Hinduism" as a self-conscious modern category**: King 1999, Pennington 2005, and others have argued that "Hinduism" as a self-consciously bounded religion (rather than a category aggregating diverse Brahmanical, devotional, Tantric, and folk traditions) is substantially a 19th-c. construction — and that Roy's reform-monotheistic-Vedantic articulation is one of the principal moments in this self-conscious construction.

## Disputes
- **Was Roy a "Hindu" reformer or a Hindu-Unitarian syncretist?**: 19th-c. orthodox Hindu critics treated him as effectively Christian; 19th-c. Christian missionaries treated him as obstinately Hindu; he insisted he was a Hindu monotheist returning Hinduism to its Vedantic essence. The modern scholarly consensus reads him as a Hindu modernist with substantive Unitarian and Islamic inputs.
- **Satī abolition**: Roy's role is firmly attested but the British abolition decree (Bentinck, 1829) was the product of multiple converging forces (missionary, colonial-administrative, Indian reformist); Roy's specific contribution has been variously assessed.
