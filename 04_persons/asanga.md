# Asaṅga

## Identity
4th-c. CE North Indian Buddhist philosopher, founder (with his half-brother Vasubandhu) of the Yogācāra (Cittamātra, "mind-only") school of Mahāyāna Buddhism. Tradition holds Asaṅga received the foundational Yogācāra teaching directly from the future Buddha Maitreya during a vision; modern scholarship treats the *Maitreya works* (e.g., *Mahāyānasūtrālaṃkāra*, *Madhyāntavibhāga*, *Abhisamayālaṃkāra*) as either Asaṅga's own compositions attributed to Maitreya or as works by a historical Maitreyanātha. Asaṅga's distinctive contributions include the doctrines of the *ālaya-vijñāna* (storehouse consciousness), the *trisvabhāva* (three natures: the imagined, the dependent, the perfected), and the systematic Yogācāra account of consciousness as the sole reality whose objects are projections.

## Historical evidence
Documented via Chinese-translation chronology (5th-c. Paramārtha translations) and via biographies by Paramārtha and Hsüan-tsang. Date c. 300–370 CE, North India.

## Works
- *Mahāyānasaṃgraha* (Compendium of the Great Vehicle) — the systematic statement of Yogācāra doctrine.
- *Abhidharma-samuccaya* — Yogācāra abhidharma.
- *Bodhisattvabhūmi* — the "stages of the bodhisattva," part of the larger *Yogācārabhūmi-śāstra*.
- *Mahāyānasūtrālaṃkāra*, *Madhyāntavibhāga*, *Dharmadharmatāvibhāga* — attributed (per tradition) to Maitreya through Asaṅga; per modern scholarship variously assigned.

## Role in the tradition
- **Yogācāra/Cittamātra**: foundational figure; the school competes with Madhyamaka through the rest of Indian Buddhist history.
- **East Asia**: profoundly influential through the Chinese Faxiang school (Xuanzang's 7th-c. translation program); Japanese Hossō school.
- **Tibet**: Yogācāra continues as an integral component of the curriculum; the *Five Treatises of Maitreya* are central.

## Disputes
- The *Maitreya works*: traditionally Maitreya's, in modern scholarship variously assigned (Maitreyanātha as a historical teacher; Asaṅga as author).
- The Yogācāra-Madhyamaka relationship: rivalry or complementarity, throughout Indian and Tibetan philosophical history.
