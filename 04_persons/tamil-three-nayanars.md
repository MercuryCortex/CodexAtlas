# Appar, Sambandar, and Sundarar (the Mūvar — the Three Tamil Shaiva Nayanars)

## Identity
The three foundational poets of Tamil Shaivism, traditionally grouped as the *Mūvar* ("the Three") within the canonical list of 63 Nāyaṉārs (Tamil Shaiva saints). Their hymns — collected as the *Tēvāram* (Books 1–7 of the *Tirumurai* canonical anthology) — established the Tamil Shaiva devotional voice that runs from the 7th to the 9th centuries CE.

- **Appar (Tirunāvukkaracar)** — c. 570–650. Vēḷḷāḷa convert from Jainism; per tradition, was a Jain monk who returned to Shaivism after a near-death healing. The Pallava king Mahendravarman I (r. 600–630) is reported to have likewise been brought back from Jainism by Appar — the Jain–Shaiva displacement contest of 7th-c. Tamilakam is the political backdrop. Author of Tirumurai books 4–6 (~3,066 surviving verses).
- **Sambandar (Tirujñānasambandar)** — c. 7th c. Brahmin child-saint per tradition, who reportedly composed his first verse at age three after being suckled by Pārvatī. Strongly associated with the Madurai Pandyan court's reversion from Jainism to Shaivism, traditionally including the legendary impalement of 8,000 Jain monks at Madurai — an event of profound political-religious significance and considerable historical complexity. Author of Tirumurai books 1–3 (~4,158 verses).
- **Sundarar (Cuntaramūrtti)** — c. 8th c. (some place him 9th c.). The latest of the Three; intimate-friendly tone (the "Friend of God" — *tampirān tōḻan*); author of Tirumurai book 7 (~1,026 verses). Composed the *Tiruttoṇṭattokai*, the foundational list of Nayanars that Cekkiḻār's *Periya Purāṇam* later expanded into the canonical 63.

## Historical evidence
- The Pallava and Pandya inscriptional record for the 7th–9th centuries firmly attests Shaiva temple endowment and royal patronage, providing the political context.
- The 11th-century Chola-period canonization (Nampi Aṇṭār Nampi compiled the Tirumurai under Rajaraja I, ~1000) marks the textual fixing.
- Cekkiḻār's *Periya Purāṇam* (~1135) is the standard hagiographical source; it is itself a sophisticated 12th-century Chola devotional-political work, not a neutral biography.

## Works
The *Tēvāram* — the seven-book Tamil Shaiva devotional anthology. Books 1–3 are Sambandar's *patikams* (eleven-verse hymn-sets); books 4–6 are Appar's; book 7 is Sundarar's. Total surviving: ~8,250 verses across 796 patikams. Sung in temple liturgy by hereditary *ōtuvārs* (singers) into the present.

## Role in the tradition
- **Tamil Shaivism's founding voices**: the *Tēvāram* and Manikkavacakar's *Tiruvācakam* together constitute the devotional core of Tamil Shaiva tradition; the Nayanars are the source of the cult of the 63 saints whose iconographic depiction is standard in Tamil Shaiva temples.
- **The Tamil Shaiva-Jain political-religious displacement**: the Mūvar tradition is the textual record of the 7th–9th-century displacement of Tamil Jain monastic institutional dominance by Shaiva royal-temple culture. The Madurai conflict is the most violent and politically consequential moment.
- **South-to-North bhakti diffusion**: the *Tēvāram* corpus, alongside the Alvars' Vaishnava poetry, is the textual basis for Hardy's (1983) and Hawley's (2015) accounts of how *bhakti* emerged in Tamil country and diffused northward.

## Cross-tradition connections
- **Shaiva-Jain displacement as comparative religious-displacement event**: structurally comparable to early Christianization of late-Roman pagan zones, the displacement of indigenous Andean religion by Christian colonial institutions, or 7th-c. Sasanian Zoroastrianism's displacement by Islamic conquest — a high-stakes royal-religious realignment in which devotional poetry was the public idiom of conversion.
- **Continuous oral-liturgical transmission as a comparative case**: the *ōtuvār* singing tradition is one of the longest continuously transmitted devotional oral lineages worldwide — comparable in continuity to Vedic recitation, Tibetan Buddhist *gyaling* / *dunglag* lineages, and the Eastern Christian psalmody tradition.
- **Tamil Shaiva canon's authority claim alongside the Sanskrit Veda**: the formal canonization of the *Tirumurai* alongside Sanskrit Shaiva *āgamas* parallels the Tamil Vaishnava claim (Sri Vaishnavism) that the *Divya Prabandham* is canonically equal to the Sanskrit Veda — a coordinated late-medieval South Indian assertion of vernacular scripture authority.

## Disputes
- **Dates**: especially for Sambandar (sometimes early 7th, sometimes late 7th); Sundarar (8th vs. 9th).
- **The Madurai impalement**: historicity vs. hagiographical inflation; the most-discussed atrocity in the Tamil Shaiva-Jain conflict literature. Modern Tamil Jain scholarship has produced sustained re-readings.
- **The 63 list**: which Nayanars are historical, which are archetypal, and which are syncretic absorptions of local saints is a long-running scholarly debate.
