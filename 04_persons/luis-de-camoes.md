# Luís Vaz de Camões (~1524–1580)

## Life
Camões's biography is only partially documented. Born in Lisbon (~1524, exact date unknown), educated in classical Latin and Italian Renaissance literature, he served as a soldier and was involved in a street brawl that landed him in prison (1552). Released to serve in Morocco, he lost his right eye in battle at Ceuta. He returned to Lisbon (1553), then departed for India in the fleet of 1553 as a common soldier.

He spent **17 years in Asia** — in Goa (twice, including one imprisonment for debt), on the coast of Mozambique, in Macau (where he is said to have written portions of Os Lusíadas). He nearly drowned in the Mekong Delta, reportedly saving the manuscript of the epic by swimming with it held above water. He returned to Lisbon in 1570, published Os Lusíadas in 1572, received a small royal pension, and died in poverty in 1580 — reportedly on the same day (June 10) that is now Portugal's National Day.

## As literary mythologist
Camões is not merely a poet — he is the **mythological architect of Portuguese identity**. *[[phase-6-041-camoes-os-lusiadas]]* performs several acts simultaneously:
- It synthesizes Greco-Roman mythology with Christian theology into a single providentialist frame
- It invents **[[adamastor]]** — the only genuinely original mythological figure created in the Renaissance that was subsequently treated as a real deity across multiple cultural traditions
- It establishes **Inês de Castro** ([[ines-de-castro]]) as the sacred victim-figure of Portuguese history
- It grounds **Sebastianismo** and the **[[fifth-empire-quinto-imperio]]** theology by narrating Portuguese history as divinely appointed mission

The hermetic tradition that runs from Bandarra (c. 1520) through Vieira (17th c.) through Pessoa (*Mensagem*, 1934) through Gandra (contemporary) is incomprehensible without Os Lusíadas as its mythological substrate.

## The irony
Camões wrote a poem about Portuguese greatness and died in poverty. The irony was immediately noticed and became itself a national myth: Eduardo Lourenço (1978) analyzes how Portugal's self-understanding as a nation is built on the trope of the unrewarded genius — the man who made Portugal immortal in verse while Portugal failed to keep him alive. This trope shapes the Sebastianist imagination of hidden greatness waiting to be recognized.
