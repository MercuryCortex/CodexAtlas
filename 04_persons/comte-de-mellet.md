# Comte de Mellet

## Identity

**Louis-Raphaël-Lucrèce de Fayolle, comte de Mellet** (1727–1804) was a French army officer and antiquarian. He appears once in the history of Western esotericism, anonymously, as "Le Comte de M***" — the byline over a short essay, ***Recherches sur les Tarots, et sur la Divination par les Cartes des Tarots***, printed inside volume 8 of [[antoine-court-de-gebelin]]'s *Le Monde primitif* in **1781**.

That essay is the origin of the tarot–Kabbalah correspondence.

## The retrofit, and its date

Court de Gébelin's own essay in the same volume made the tarot **Egyptian**. De Mellet's made it **Hebrew** — and it is de Mellet's claim, not Court de Gébelin's, that supplied the structure every later occult tarot system actually runs on:

- He **mapped the 22 trumps to the 22 letters of the Hebrew alphabet** — the correspondence later carried by [[eliphas-levi]] onto the 22 paths of the sefirotic tree, and by the Golden Dawn onto the planets and zodiac.
- He **read the trumps in reverse**, from the World down to the Fool, as a descent from the divine to the material — an ordering the French school partly kept and the Golden Dawn discarded.
- He gave the **earliest published account of how to lay out and read the cards**.

The number 22 is the whole hinge, and it is a coincidence of two unrelated systems:

- The Italian pack has **22 trumps** because a 15th-century trick-taking game needed a fifth suit of fixed-rank cards, and 21 numbered trumps plus an unnumbered excuse card is what the northern Italian card-makers settled on (Dummett 1980).
- The Hebrew alphabet has **22 letters** because of the history of the Northwest Semitic consonantal script — see [[alphabet-hebrew-aleph-bet]] and [[alphabet-sefer-yetzirah]], where the 22 letters carry a cosmogonic function that is genuinely ancient and genuinely Jewish.

Nothing connects them. No Kabbalistic source before 1781 mentions cards; no Italian card-maker's record mentions Hebrew. **De Mellet noticed that two numbers matched and proposed that the match was meaningful.** That proposal is the retrofit, it is dated 1781, and it is the load-bearing member of the modern esoteric tarot.

## Role in the tradition

- **The Kabbalah correspondence has an author and a date, and they are not Lévi's.** Both of this vault's tarot nodes previously attributed the correspondence to [[eliphas-levi]] (1854–1856). Lévi *systematised* it — he added the Tree-of-Life paths, the Tetragrammaton architecture, and the authority of his own reputation. He did not originate it. The seventy-three-year gap between de Mellet's proposal and Lévi's system is itself evidence that the correspondence was a slow-built construction rather than a recovered secret.
- **A second false-antiquity claim.** De Mellet, like Court de Gébelin, credits transmission to "les Bohémiens" (the Roma). This is unsupported: Roma presence in western Europe is documented from the early 15th century, after playing cards were already circulating, and 16th-century notices of Roma divination describe **palmistry** ([[palmistry-chiromancy]]), not cards.
- **The vault's methodological point.** A retrofit is not a lie and not a discovery. It is a real intellectual act, performed by a named person, on a date, using materials that did not previously belong together. Recording it as such — rather than repeating it or deleting it — is what the four-tier system is for.

## Disputes

- **Attribution.** The 1781 essay is signed only "Le Comte de M***". The identification with Louis-Raphaël-Lucrèce de Fayolle, comte de Mellet, is the settled scholarly reading (Decker, Depaulis & Dummett 1996); it is not attested in the volume itself.
- **Priority within the volume.** Court de Gébelin states he had de Mellet's essay in hand while writing his own, so influence ran between them; which idea reached which man first is not recoverable. What is fixed is that both appeared in print together in 1781, and that the Hebrew-letter mapping appears in de Mellet's text, not Court de Gébelin's.
- **Whether any earlier oral correspondence existed.** Lévi and his heirs asserted that the tarot–Hebrew link was an ancient transmitted secret. No pre-1781 source of any kind has ever been produced. The scholarly position (Dummett 1980; Decker & Dummett 2002) is that none exists.
