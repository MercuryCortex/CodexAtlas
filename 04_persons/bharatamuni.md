# Bharatamuni

## Identity
The traditional sage-author of the *Nāṭyaśāstra* — the encyclopedic Sanskrit treatise on dramatic, musical, and aesthetic theory (~200 BCE – 200 CE composition) that founded the entire classical Indian discourse on theatre, dance, music, prosody, and aesthetic experience. The Nāṭyaśāstra's own mythological frame presents Bharata as a celestial sage to whom Brahmā taught the "fifth Veda" (the *Nāṭya-Veda*) — accessible to all four *varṇas*, unlike the Sanskrit Vedas — for the purpose of dramatic instruction during a moment of cosmic-moral crisis. The historical Bharata is presumably the compiler-redactor of an earlier oral-performative tradition; the text is layered, with discernible stylistic strata, and is not the work of a single author in any single moment.

## Historical evidence
- The text itself: the *Nāṭyaśāstra*, in 36 chapters (37 in some recensions), ~6000 verses with prose passages, surviving in two main recensions (the longer "northern" and shorter "southern"; the Kashmir recension that Abhinavagupta knew is intermediate).
- The earliest substantive commentary tradition: Lollaṭa, Śaṅkuka, Bhaṭṭa Nāyaka (~9th c.), and the supreme commentary, [[abhinavagupta]]'s *Abhinavabhāratī* (~1000 CE).
- No external biographical attestation; Bharata's biography is text-internal-mythological.

## Works
- ***Nāṭyaśāstra***: the encyclopedic treatise — 36 chapters covering:
  - The mythological origin of *nāṭya* (drama).
  - Theatre architecture (*nāṭya-gṛha*).
  - Performance preliminaries (*pūrva-raṅga*).
  - *Rasa* theory (chapter 6 — the foundation of all subsequent Indian aesthetic theory).
  - *Bhāva* (emotional states — the eight *sthāyi-bhāvas* / stable emotions that, when properly conjoined with the *vibhāvas* / determinants and *anubhāvas* / consequents, generate the eight *rasas*).
  - Detailed technical treatment of: gesture and movement (*aṅgika-abhinaya*), vocal performance (*vācika-abhinaya*), costume and makeup (*āhārya-abhinaya*), and inner emotional rendering (*sāttvika-abhinaya*).
  - Music: *svara* (notes), *grāma* (basic scales), *mūrcchanā* (modes), and *jāti* (melodic types) — the foundation of all subsequent classical Indian music theory.
  - Dance: *karaṇas* (the 108 fundamental movement-units, depicted iconographically in medieval Tamil and Karnataka temple sculpture).
  - Prosody and Sanskrit dramatic poetics.

## Role in the tradition
- **Foundational document of Indian classical aesthetics**: every subsequent classical Indian theory of art — Sanskrit poetics (the *alaṃkāra-śāstra* tradition: Bhāmaha, Daṇḍin, Vāmana, Ānandavardhana, Mammaṭa, Viśvanātha), music theory (Sangita-Ratnakara, 13th c.), dance theory (Abhinaya Darpaṇa) — is downstream of the Nāṭyaśāstra's rasa-bhāva framework.
- **Rasa theory**: the *rasa* doctrine (aesthetic emotion is produced when the spectator's stable emotion *sthāyi-bhāva* is fully evoked by determinants, consequents, and transient emotional states *vyabhicāri-bhāvas*) is one of the most influential pre-modern theoretical frameworks in any art tradition. Abhinavagupta's elaboration of *rasa* as *camatkāra* (aesthetic astonishment / bliss) and as structurally akin to non-dual mystical experience (~1000 CE) is the principal classical-medieval theoretical refinement.
- **Performing arts continuity**: the *Nāṭyaśāstra*'s detailed performance specifications shaped Sanskrit drama as practiced in Kerala's Kūṭiyāṭṭam tradition (Sanskrit drama with continuous transmission from ~1000 CE), in the Bharatanatyam reconstruction of the 1930s (Rukmini Devi, E. Krishna Iyer), in Odissi, Kuchipudi, Manipuri, and the classical Indian dance traditions broadly. The Nāṭyaśāstra is one of the longest-influential single performance treatises in world cultural history.

## Cross-tradition connections
- **Theatre as ritual / "fifth Veda" cross-tradition framing**: Bharata's framing of drama as a divinely-instituted teaching medium accessible to all four *varṇas* makes drama a deliberate cross-caste, lay-accessible spiritual technology — structurally adjacent to the medieval European mystery-play tradition (drama as ritual instruction for the laity), to the Japanese Noh tradition's Shinto-Buddhist ritual depth, and to the religious-pedagogical functions of Indonesian Wayang shadow-puppet theatre (Hindu-Javanese: the *Mahābhārata* and *Rāmāyaṇa* are the standard repertoire).
- **Hellenistic-Indian aesthetic contact?**: the structural parallels between Bharata's *rasa-bhāva* psychology of the spectator and Aristotle's *Poetics* (mid-4th c. BCE) — specifically the theory of *catharsis* (purification through tragic emotion) and the analysis of plot, character, and spectacle — have been periodically noted (Keith 1924, Gnoli 1956). Direct historical influence is unproven; the parallel is most plausibly read as structurally convergent aesthetic theories rather than as historical borrowing. McEvilley 2002 includes the Aristotle-Bharata parallel within his broader Hellenistic-Indian contact thesis (~300 BCE – 300 CE Bactrian-Gandharan contact zone); the contact thesis remains contested.
- **Aesthetic experience as quasi-religious experience**: Abhinavagupta's late development of *rasa* into *rasa-camatkāra* (the aesthetic-astonishment doctrine) gives Indian art-experience an explicit soteriological-mystical structure — drama as a *moksha*-adjacent experience. The structural parallel to Sufi music-as-ecstatic-vehicle (the *samāʿ* tradition of the Mevlevi and Chishti Sufi orders), to the medieval Christian theology of sacred art (Pseudo-Dionysius onward), and to the German Romantic philosophical aesthetics (Schopenhauer's reading of aesthetic experience as a moment of will-less knowing) is striking.

## Disputes
- **Historicity**: the consensus is that "Bharata" is a school-compiler figure rather than a single historical author.
- **Date**: 200 BCE – 200 CE is the standard range; some scholars argue for 100 BCE – 100 CE.
- **Recensional history**: the relationship between the longer and shorter recensions (especially relevant for which text Abhinavagupta is commenting on) is a continuing philological question.
