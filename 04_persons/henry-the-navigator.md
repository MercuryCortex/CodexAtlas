# Henry the Navigator (1394–1460)

## Identity
Infante Dom Henrique — posthumously named "the Navigator" by 19th-century Romantic historiography — was the Portuguese prince who transformed systematic maritime exploration from sporadic raiding into an institutional, state-funded, long-term project. He was not a navigator himself (he went to sea only for the 1415 Ceuta campaign); he was an **organizer and patron**, financing expeditions from his income as Grand Master of the Order of Christ and Duke of Viseu.

## The Order of Christ connection
When Henry became Grand Master of the **[[tradition-order-of-christ|Order of Christ]]** in 1420, he used the Order's institutional wealth and crusading-mission mandate to fund African exploration. The Papal Bull *Dum Diversas* (1452) and *Romanus Pontifex* (1455) — granted partly through Henry's diplomatic efforts — gave Portugal the right to trade and enslave in Africa, framing the exploration as a crusading enterprise. The crosses on Portuguese caravel sails — the cross of Christ, the Order of Christ's mark — are Henry's institutional signature, made literal in canvas.

## The Prester John motivation
A key but under-emphasized motivation for Henry's systematic African exploration was the **search for [[prester-john]]** — the legendary Christian king believed to rule somewhere in Africa (or Asia). If Portuguese ships could reach Prester John's kingdom, Portugal would gain a powerful Christian ally against the Islamic powers of North Africa and the Middle East. This theological-strategic logic — crusade against Islam via African circumnavigation — drove the coastal exploration program that led, 28 years after Henry's death, to Bartolomeu Dias rounding the Cape (1488) and Vasco da Gama reaching India (1499).

## What Henry actually did
Russell (2000) demolishes several Romantic myths:
- The "school of navigation at Sagres" is largely legendary — Henry's court at Sagres was a castle, not an academy
- He was a religious-military figure motivated by crusading ideology, not a Renaissance scientist
- He never rounded any significant cape himself

What he actually achieved: systematic, year-on-year, expedition-by-expedition exploration of the African coast, advancing the known limit each time. By his death in 1460, Portuguese ships had reached Sierra Leone (~10°N). Within 30 years of his death, they had reached the Cape.
