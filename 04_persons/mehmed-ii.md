# Mehmed II

## Identity
Mehmed II (1432-1481) — Ottoman sultan 1444-46 and 1451-1481 — known by the canonical Ottoman honorific **Fatih** ("the Conqueror"), is the political-military hinge between the medieval and early-modern Mediterranean. His seven-week siege and conquest of Constantinople (29 May 1453) **ended the thousand-year Eastern Roman / Byzantine Empire** under [[constantine-xi-palaiologos|Constantine XI Palaiologos]] and inaugurated the Ottoman imperial dispensation that lasted in unbroken succession 1453-1924. His thirty-year reign extended Ottoman rule to all of Anatolia, the Balkans, the Black Sea littoral, much of the Aegean, and southern Italy (Otranto 1480-81); reorganized the religious-administrative structure of the conquered Byzantine populations through the **millet system**; established the bureaucratic-legal framework that the Ottomans elaborated for the next four centuries; and self-consciously claimed Roman imperial succession via the title *Kayser-i Rûm* ("Caesar of Rome").

## Historical evidence
Documented from multiple perspectives:
- **Greek pro-Ottoman**: Kritoboulos of Imbros (*History of Mehmed the Conqueror*, ~1467, addressed to Mehmed himself — pro-Mehmed; consciously classicizing in the Thucydidean register; remarkable as Greek scholarship in Ottoman service).
- **Greek pro-Byzantine**: Doukas, Sphrantzes, Chalkokondyles (latter two writing in exile, hostile to Mehmed).
- **Ottoman court**: Tursun Beg (*History of Mehmed the Conqueror*).
- **Italian**: Nicolò Barbaro's *Diary of the Siege* (Venetian doctor); Leonard of Chios's *Letter to Pope Nicholas V*; later Renaissance accounts.
- **Visual**: Gentile Bellini's 1480 portrait of Mehmed (now National Gallery, London).

## Role in the tradition

### The conquest of Constantinople and the *translatio imperii*
The 53-day siege of Constantinople (6 April - 29 May 1453) — using unprecedented heavy artillery (Orban's cannons), the overland-portage of fleet into the Golden Horn, sustained bombardment of the Theodosian land walls, the final massed assault before dawn 29 May — culminated in the death of Constantine XI in the breach and Mehmed's triumphant entry. Mehmed rode directly to **Hagia Sophia**, ordered it converted to a mosque (the *fetih namazi* — conquest prayer — performed there the following Friday); the city was sacked for three days per the rule of conquest and then declared the new Ottoman capital (now *Konstantiniyye* in Ottoman official usage; later *Istanbul*). The conquest closes the Byzantine imperial tradition and inaugurates the Ottoman, with conscious *translatio imperii* — Mehmed adopting the title *Kayser-i Rûm* in his correspondence and on coinage. See [[event-fall-of-constantinople-1453]], [[constantine-xi-palaiologos]].

### The millet system and the dhimmi administration
Mehmed reorganized the religious-administrative governance of the conquered Byzantine populations through the **millet system** — recognising non-Muslim religious communities (Greek Orthodox, Armenian Orthodox, Jewish, later Catholic) as legally-autonomous corporations under their own religious heads (Patriarch, Catholicos, Hakham Bashi), with internal jurisdiction over personal-status law (marriage, inheritance, religious education) under the overall sovereignty of the sultan. He personally appointed **Gennadios II Scholarios** as the first Patriarch of Constantinople under Ottoman rule (January 1454), a deliberate political act of legitimation: the Greek-Christian population received an autocephalous Patriarchate independent of Latin influence (a reversal of the 1439 Union of Florence that had been so divisive within Constantinopolitan Greek Christianity). The millet system shaped Ottoman religious-political administration for ~450 years; its dissolution in the 19th-20th c. nationalisms produced much of the modern eastern-Mediterranean political map (Cyprus, Lebanon, the Balkans, the population exchanges).

### The Kayser-i Rûm claim and the third *imperium romanum*
Mehmed's title *Kayser-i Rûm* (Caesar of Rome) — used in his Greek-language correspondence with western powers and on certain coinage — was a formal claim of Roman imperial succession by right of conquest. The claim was contested in Latin Christendom (the Holy Roman Emperor disputed it; the popes disputed it; the Russian "Third Rome" doctrine of Filofei of Pskov, ~16th c., disputed it by claiming Moscow as Constantinople's heir). Mehmed's claim, however, **was the politically-realized one** — the Ottoman sultans held Constantinople and ruled the eastern Mediterranean as the Byzantine emperors had done. The competition between three claimant *imperium romanum* lines (Holy Roman Empire / Russian "Third Rome" / Ottoman *Kayser-i Rûm*) is one of the durable structural features of early-modern European-Ottoman political-religious history.

### The cosmopolitan Renaissance court
Mehmed's Edirne and Istanbul courts were strikingly cosmopolitan in their Italian-Renaissance and Greek-Byzantine contacts. He invited **Gentile Bellini** from Venice to paint his portrait (1479-81). He commissioned Greek translations of historical works; Kritoboulos's classicizing history is his court's product. He read Persian poetry (composing his own divan under the pen-name *Avni*), Arabic religious-philosophical literature, and (per Kritoboulos) had Arrian's *Anabasis* of Alexander read to him on campaign. He kept a Greek-language imperial chancery alongside Ottoman Turkish and Persian. The cultural posture is consciously synthetic: an Ottoman-Islamic sovereign who incorporates Byzantine-Greek-Roman imperial signs and Renaissance-Italian artistic contacts into a single imperial culture.

### Eastern Christianity preserved as a subject religion
Paradoxically, Mehmed's conquest preserved Eastern Orthodox Christianity in a form that Latin-Catholic conquest would not have. Latin conquest (e.g., 1204-1261 Latin Empire of Constantinople) had subordinated Greek Christianity to Roman primacy. Ottoman conquest preserved the Greek-Christian ecclesiastical hierarchy as an autonomous *millet* with its Patriarch confirmed by the Sultan — formally subordinate politically but ecclesiastically and theologically free to maintain Eastern-Orthodox doctrine, liturgy, and canon law. The famous Notaras line attributed by Doukas — "I would rather see the Turkish turban in the midst of the city than the Latin mitre" — captures the (post hoc rationalized) reality: under Ottoman conquest, Greek Orthodox theology was preserved unchanged through ~450 years; under Latin conquest it would not have been.

### Renaissance Hermeticism — indirect downstream
The Greek manuscript diaspora triggered by the fall of Constantinople — preceded by the Council of Florence 1438-1439 emigrations and the late-14th c. Manuel Chrysoloras circle — fed the Italian Renaissance's Greek-philosophical recovery. Cosimo de' Medici's commission of [[marsilio-ficino]] to translate the Hermetica (1463 — from a manuscript brought to Florence by Leonardo da Pistoia) and the complete Plato (1484) is **direct downstream of the Greek manuscript flow that Mehmed's conquest accelerated**. The Renaissance Hermetic tradition that runs from Ficino through Pico della Mirandola, Bruno, Dee, and on into the early-modern occult sciences is indirectly licensed by 1453. See [[tradition-renaissance-hermeticism]], [[event-fall-of-constantinople-1453]].

## Disputes
- **The deliberateness of the *Kayser-i Rûm* claim.** Some scholarship (Babinger 1978) emphasizes Mehmed's serious Roman-imperial self-conception; revisionist work emphasizes the title's primarily diplomatic-tactical use in Latin-Greek correspondence rather than systematic doctrine.
- **Mehmed's religious posture.** Sunni orthodox in formal terms; some traditions (and Bellini's portrait iconography) suggest more cosmopolitan / philosophically-curious / occasionally heterodox inclinations than the standard sultanic image. The Ottoman *ulama* were not uniformly comfortable with his Italian-Renaissance contacts.
- **Cause of death (1481).** Some traditions (and modern scholarship) point to poisoning, possibly by his own son Bayezid II or the Persian *ulama* opposed to Mehmed's Italian-court program; gout / acute illness is the natural-cause alternative.
- **The Otranto campaign (1480).** Whether Mehmed planned a serious western Mediterranean conquest (Rome was rumoured to be the target) or merely a diplomatic-pressure operation is debated; his death in 1481 ended the project.
