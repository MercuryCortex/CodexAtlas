# Manetho of Sebennytos

## Identity
Manetho (c. 320 – c. 260 BCE) was an Egyptian priest of Sebennytos (Delta) writing under [[ptolemy-i-soter]] and [[ptolemy-ii-philadelphus]]. He is the **first Egyptian to write a history of Egypt in Greek for a non-Egyptian audience**. His major work — the *Aegyptiaca* (Αἰγυπτιακά, "Egyptian Matters") — established the dynastic schema (Dynasties 1–30) still used by Egyptology, transmitted partly through Josephus and partly through later Christian chronographers (Africanus, Eusebius, Syncellus). Tradition (Plutarch, Tacitus) also names Manetho — alongside the Greek priest Timotheus of Eleusis — as a co-architect of the Serapis cult under Ptolemy I.

## Historical evidence
No work of Manetho's survives in continuous textual form. He is reconstructed from quotations and summaries in:
- **[[josephus]]** *Contra Apionem* 1.73–105, 1.227–287 — extensive verbatim quotations on the Hyksos and on early Egyptian history.
- **Africanus** (3rd c. CE), preserved in Eusebius's *Chronicon* and the *Chronicle* of Syncellus (early 9th c.).
- **Eusebius** *Chronicon* (early 4th c.), preserved in Jerome's Latin translation and in the Armenian version.
- **Syncellus**, *Ecloga Chronographica* (early 9th c.) — the latest and most extensive transmission.

Manetho is also named directly by [[plutarch]] in *De Iside et Osiride* (chs. 9, 28, 49, 62) as a source on Egyptian theology and on the Serapis cult.

## Works
- ***Aegyptiaca*** (Αἰγυπτιακά) — see [[phase-3-025-manetho-aegyptiaca]]. A history of Egypt in three books, organized into 30 (later 31) dynasties from mythic prehistory through the Persian conquest. The dynastic scheme is **Manetho's own innovation** and remains the basis of modern Egyptological periodization.
- ***Sacred Book*** (Ἱερὰ Βίβλος) — on Egyptian religion, theology, and ritual; lost in continuous form.
- *Against Herodotus* — a corrective treatise on Egyptian matters that [[herodotus]] got wrong; lost.
- *On Festivals*, *On Antiquity and Religion*, *On the Preparation of Kyphi* (a temple incense) — fragmentary.

## Role in the tradition
For the Codex Atlas mission Manetho is **the principal Egyptian-priestly counter-voice to Herodotus** in the Greek-language record. The framework matters:

1. **Egyptological backbone.** Manetho's dynastic schema is so durable that modern Egyptology, with full access to Egyptian primary sources, still uses his dynasty numbers and most dynasty boundaries. This is a remarkable case of a Hellenistic Greek-language reconstruction of pre-Hellenistic native tradition surviving the recovery of the original sources.

2. **Greek-Egyptian theological synthesis.** Manetho — alongside Timotheus of Eleusis — is the tradition-named co-founder of the Serapis cult ([[serapis]]), the deliberately engineered Greco-Egyptian high-god of Ptolemaic Alexandria (Plutarch *DIO* 28; Tacitus *Histories* 4.83–84). This makes Manetho a **load-bearing transmission node** from Egyptian religion to Hellenistic mystery cult and onward to Roman-imperial religion.

3. **Anti-Herodotean corrective.** Manetho writes *against* Herodotus from the Egyptian side, giving us a glimpse of how Egyptian priests in the early Ptolemaic period saw the Greek ethnographic tradition about them. This is one of the rare ancient cases of the colonized speaking back to the colonizer in the colonizer's language.

4. **Hyksos narrative → Jewish exodus polemic.** Manetho's account of the Hyksos (the Semitic-speaking rulers of Egypt c. -1650 to -1550) was redeployed by Josephus and by Christian apologists to argue for the historicity of an Israelite presence in Egypt. The Hyksos = Israelites equation is rejected by modern scholarship but the polemical use of Manetho is historically formative for Christian-Jewish-Egyptian apologetic literature.

5. **Christian chronography.** Manetho's dynastic dates, filtered through Africanus and Eusebius, become foundational for **all Christian universal chronologies** through the medieval period: Augustine's *City of God*, Bede's chronologies, Joseph Scaliger's *De emendatione temporum* (1583). Manetho is one of the buried structural elements underneath every medieval Christian timeline.

## Disputes
- **Date of birth / death.** Roughly secure within the range -320 to -260, but no specific dates documented.
- **Patron identity.** Tradition is split between Ptolemy I and Ptolemy II as Manetho's patron; the strongest argument is for both (he served Ptolemy I in the founding of the Serapis cult, then wrote the *Aegyptiaca* under Ptolemy II).
- **Religious identity and Hellenization.** Older scholarship (Bickerman) read Manetho as a Hellenizing collaborator. Moyer (2011) and Dillery (2015) have substantially recast him as an Egyptian priestly intellectual using Greek as a strategic medium while remaining culturally Egyptian — a model now dominant.
- **Source-criticism of the transmitted text.** Africanus and Eusebius give different dynasty lists; Syncellus gives a third. Reconstructing what Manetho actually wrote is a complex source-critical exercise (Waddell 1940 collates the variants; Dillery 2015 ch. 5 is the most recent treatment).
