# Farīd ud-Dīn ʿAṭṭār

## Identity

Farīd ud-Dīn ʿAṭṭār (~1145–1221 CE) was a Persian Sufi poet, pharmacist (*ʿaṭṭār* = druggist/perfumer, whence his pen name), and spiritual writer from Nishapur in Khorasan — then the pre-eminent intellectual city of the eastern Islamic world. He is considered one of the three greatest poets of Persian Sufism alongside [[sadi-of-shiraz]] and [[rumi]], and the most direct predecessor of Rūmī in both form and theological vision. His death in the **Mongol sack of Nishapur in 1221** — the destruction of his city by the invading forces — was one of the catastrophic cultural losses of the era.

His most celebrated work is [[phase-5-051-attar-conference-of-birds]] (*Manṭiq al-Ṭayr*, ~1177 CE), a narrative mathnawi (~4,500 couplets) in which 30 birds quest for the Simorgh and discover themselves to be what they sought. He also wrote the *Tadhkirat al-Awliyāʾ* (Memorial of the Friends of God) — one of the most important hagiographic collections of Sufi saints — as well as the *Muṣībatnāma*, *Ilāhīnāma*, *Asrārnāma*, and approximately 15–20 other works.

## Historical evidence

ʿAṭṭār's biography is not well documented in contemporary sources; much of what we know derives from later hagiographic accounts and retrospective accounts by poets like Rūmī. His occupation as a pharmacist (he ran a pharmacy in Nishapur) is consistently attested; his death in the Mongol sack of Nishapur (1221) is generally accepted. The tradition that he met the young Rūmī and his family as they passed through Nishapur as refugees (~1215) — and gave the young Rūmī his own work as a gift — is a widespread legend that cannot be verified but has been widely repeated for 800 years.

## Works

**Texts authored:**
- [[phase-5-051-attar-conference-of-birds]] (*Manṭiq al-Ṭayr*, ~1177 CE) — the definitive Sufi allegorical mathnawi; 30 birds quest for the Simorgh through Seven Valleys.
- *Tadhkirat al-Awliyāʾ* (Memorial of the Friends of God) — a prose collection of biographies of 72 Sufi saints from Hasan al-Basri to Ḥallāj; the most important hagiographic source for early Sufism.
- *Muṣībatnāma* (The Book of Affliction) — a mystical narrative poem
- *Ilāhīnāma* (The Book of the Divine) — another major mathnawi
- *Asrārnāma* (The Book of Secrets)

## Role in the tradition

ʿAṭṭār is the crucial structural link between the earlier Khorasani Sufi poets (Sanāʾī of Ghazni, ~1080–1141, author of the first major Persian Sufi mathnawi) and the mature tradition of [[rumi]]. Rūmī himself said: "ʿAṭṭār traversed the seven cities of love; we are still at the turn of one lane." The *Manṭiq al-Ṭayr*'s Seven Valleys structure, its use of allegory and narrative digression, and its theological resolution in *fanāʾ* (annihilation) are all inherited and expanded in the *Masnavi*.

## Disputes

- **The Rūmī meeting tradition.** Whether the young Rūmī actually met ʿAṭṭār is uncertain; Rūmī's family passed through Nishapur around 1215, when Attar was ~70 years old. The story is hagiographically significant but historically unverifiable.
- **Attribution of works.** The number of works genuinely authored by ʿAṭṭār is disputed; some scholars accept ~20 works, others are more cautious. The *Manṭiq al-Ṭayr* and *Tadhkirat al-Awliyāʾ* are universally accepted; several other works in the corpus may be later attributions.
