# Farīd ud-Dīn ʿAṭṭār

## Biography

Farīd ud-Dīn ʿAṭṭār ("the Perfumer" — *attar* = essence/perfume/druggist) was born in Nishapur, Khorasan (present-day northeastern Iran) around 1145 CE. He operated a pharmacy (*ʿaṭṭārī*, hence his pen-name), and is said to have experienced spiritual awakening when a wandering dervish died in his shop, remarking that he could die easily because he had nothing to lose — shaming the prosperous pharmacist out of his mercantile life and into decades of mystical composition. The story may be hagiographic, but its point is theologically precise: Attar's conversion is a first lesson in the *fanāʾ* (annihilation of the self) that would become the center of his entire poetic project.

He spent the remainder of his life in Nishapur composing, and reportedly met a young Rūmī as a child (approximately 1215, when Rūmī's family passed through Nishapur as refugees from the Mongol advance) and gave him a copy of the *Asrārnāma* (Book of Secrets). Rūmī's tribute is the most eloquent testimony in the Persian Sufi tradition: "ʿAṭṭār traversed the seven cities of love; we are still at the turn of one lane." And elsewhere: "ʿAṭṭār was the soul, Sanai his two eyes; we came after Sanai and ʿAṭṭār." Said to have been killed by Mongol soldiers during the sack of Nishapur in 1221 — one of the catastrophic cultural destructions of the era — though the date and manner of his death remain uncertain in sources.

## Works

ʿAṭṭār's authenticated corpus is large, and the attribution of individual works is disputed; scholars generally accept around fifteen major works while a few have argued for a narrower core. The universally accepted centerpieces are:

- **[[phase-5-051-attar-conference-of-birds]]** (*Manṭiq al-Ṭayr*, "The Speech of Birds," ~1177 CE) — his masterpiece; ~4,500 rhyming couplets in the *masnavi* form; the journey of thirty birds through Seven Valleys to find the Simorgh.
- **Tadhkirat al-Awliyāʾ** (*Memorial of the Friends of God*) — a prose hagiographic collection of 72 Sufi saints from Ḥasan al-Baṣrī to [[al-hallaj]], which constitutes one of the most important primary sources for early Sufism. Its treatment of [[al-hallaj]] (martyred 922 CE for claiming "I am the Truth" — *Ana'l-Ḥaqq*) is the fullest pre-Ritter account and situates Ḥallāj as Attar's central spiritual hero.
- **Muṣībatnāma** (*Book of Affliction*) — a mystical narrative poem in 40 chapters; the seeker questions forty prophets and mystics, but none can answer, until the seeker discovers the answer lies within the heart.
- **Ilāhīnāma** (*Book of the Divine*) — a major mathnawi; a king's six sons each describe their deepest desire; the king demonstrates that all desires point toward the divine.
- **Asrārnāma** (*Book of Secrets*) — the work reportedly given to the young Rūmī.

Total output across the accepted corpus exceeds 45,000 verses in the major works — one of the largest poetic outputs in classical Persian literature.

## The Conference of the Birds — Structure and Non-Dual Revelation

[[phase-5-051-attar-conference-of-birds]] is the structural masterpiece of Persian Sufi allegory. Thirty birds, led by the hoopoe (*hudhud*), undertake a journey through Seven Valleys to find the Simorgh — the mythical divine bird, king of all birds, whose name they have heard but never seen. Each valley is a stage in the Sufi spiritual itinerary:

1. **Ṭalab** — Seeking (the will to begin the quest at all)
2. **ʿIshq** — Love (consuming passion that burns away self-interest)
3. **Maʿrifat** — Gnosis / mystical knowledge (not intellectual knowledge but direct recognition)
4. **Istighnāʾ** — Detachment / needlessness (poverty of desire; contentment)
5. **Tawḥīd** — Divine Unity (the mystic's dissolution into the One)
6. **Ḥayrat** — Bewilderment / stupor (the mind's collapse before the magnitude of the divine)
7. **Faqr wa Fanāʾ** — Poverty and Annihilation (total extinction of the self in God; *fanāʾ fi'llāh*)

Most birds turn back at each valley, defeated by their specific attachments. Thousands begin; only thirty survive to the Simorgh's dwelling. They arrive. They look into the mirror before the throne. They see themselves.

The pun that encodes the entire philosophical thesis: *sī murgh* in Persian means "thirty birds." **The Simorgh they sought IS the thirty birds that sought it.** The seeker is the sought. The soul is God. The journey that seemed to lead outward to an external king was a journey inward to the recognition of one's own nature. *Fanāʾ fi'llāh* (annihilation in God) does not end in merger with something external — it ends in the discovery that there was never a separation to begin with.

This is the Sufi reformulation of the Neoplatonic *epistrophē* (the soul's return to the One, discovering it never truly left) — and it is simultaneously the most elegant literary statement of what the Upanishads call *tat tvam asi* ("that art thou") and what [[meister-eckhart]] will say in Latin-and-German a century later: "the eye through which I see God is the same eye through which God sees me."

## Structural Role in the Tradition

ʿAṭṭār is the crucial structural bridge between the earlier Khorasani Sufi poets and Rūmī's fully realized mature tradition:

- **Sanāʾī of Ghazna** (~1080–1141) established the Persian Sufi mathnawi form (*Ḥadīqat al-Ḥaqīqa*, "Garden of Truth"); but his theology is less radical than Attar's.
- **ʿAṭṭār** took the mathnawi form and loaded it with the systematic *fanāʾ* theology — annihilation as the full program, not as one moment among many.
- **[[rumi]]** (~1258) explicitly inherits from ʿAṭṭār. The *Masnavi*'s opening *Nay-nāmeh* (the reed cut from the reed-bed) is Rūmī's version of the same exile-and-longing-toward-dissolution structure. The [[soul-exile-longing]] theme flows from ʿAṭṭār directly into Rūmī.

ʿAṭṭār also wrote the *Tadhkirat al-Awliyāʾ* biography of [[al-hallaj]], whose execution and cry "Ana'l-Ḥaqq" ("I am the Truth / Reality") he treated as the supreme Sufi martyrdom — the executed divine claimant as the fullest expression of *fanāʾ*. See [[executed-divine-claimant]].

## Cross-Tradition Connections — MASSIVE WINS

**The si-morgh/Simorgh pun as the cross-tradition convergence node.** The thirty-birds revelation is simultaneously:

1. **The Sufi formulation of *fanāʾ fi'llāh*** — annihilation of the self in God — but resolved not into extinction but into *recognition*. The self that dissolves was always already what it sought.

2. **The Plotinian *epistrophē*** — [[plotinus]] (*Enneads* VI.9) teaches that in contemplating the One, the soul discovers it *is* the One. The structural identity with the Simorgh pun is exact. No transmission link between Persian Sufism and Plotinus has been demonstrated; the convergence is independent.

3. **The Upanishadic *tat tvam asi*** — "That art thou" (*Chandogya Upanishad* 6.8.7): the individual self (*jīva*) is identified with Brahman; the quest to find God ends when the seeker recognizes themselves as what they sought. Independent convergence.

4. **The Eckhartian union** — [[meister-eckhart]] (Sermon 57): "The eye through which I see God is the same eye through which God sees me: my eye and God's eye are one eye, one seeing, one knowing, one love." No transmission between Attar and Eckhart; two mystics in different traditions reach the same formulation within a century.

5. **A *katabasis/ascent* narrative** — the Seven Valleys as stages of ascent maps onto the seven heavens of Muhammad's *Mi'raj*, Dante's seven terraces of Purgatorio, and the seven-stage alchemical process. The [[katabasis-and-anabasis]] structure runs through ʿAṭṭār's architecture.

6. **The Quranic substrate** — the hoopoe (*hudhud*) appears in Quran 27:20–28 as Solomon's messenger to the Queen of Sheba. ʿAṭṭār makes this Quranic bird the guide of all birds to the divine — anchoring the most radical non-dual assertion in Islamic mystical poetry in the scripture itself. The guide-figure pattern is documented in [[theme-guide-through-underworld]].

7. **The annihilation parallel** — *fanāʾ* (Sufi: annihilation of the self in God) is structurally cognate with Buddhist *nirvāṇa* (extinction of craving and the illusion of a separate self), Hindu *mokṣa* (liberation from the illusion of separation), and Plotinus's *henosis* (the soul's union with the One). The [[soul-exile-longing]] theme and its resolution in dissolution is one of the vault's strongest cross-tradition convergences; ʿAṭṭār's *Manṭiq al-Ṭayr* is its most narratively elaborate instantiation.

## Disputes

- **The Rūmī meeting tradition.** Whether the young Rūmī actually encountered ʿAṭṭār in Nishapur is uncertain; Rūmī's family passed through Nishapur around 1215, when Attar was ~70 years old. The story is hagiographically significant but historically unverifiable.
- **Attribution of works.** The number of works genuinely authored by ʿAṭṭār is disputed; some scholars accept ~20 works, others are more cautious. The *Manṭiq al-Ṭayr* and *Tadhkirat al-Awliyāʾ* are universally accepted.
- **Date of death.** 1221 (during the Mongol sack of Nishapur) is the traditional date; some scholars place his death as late as 1230.
- **Transmission to Dante.** Miguel Asín Palacios (1919) argued for Islamic influence on Dante's *Divine Comedy* via Andalusian transmission; the Seven Valleys / seven terraces structural homology is striking. Modern scholarship has not reached consensus.
