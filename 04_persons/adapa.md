# Adapa

## Identity
**Adapa** is the primordial sage-priest of **Eridu**, the first and greatest of the seven **[[apkallu]]** (the antediluvian sages of Mesopotamian tradition), and a creature and servant of the wisdom-god **[[enki-ea]]**. He is the central figure of the **Adapa myth** ([[phase-1-034-adapa-myth]]), the Mesopotamian meditation on why human beings must die.

## The myth
While fishing for the temple of Ea, Adapa's boat is capsized by the **South Wind**, whose wing he breaks with a curse. Summoned to answer for this before the sky-god **Anu** in heaven, Adapa is coached by Ea: he is warned to accept a garment and oil offered to him but to **refuse any bread or water**, for they will be the "bread of death." When Anu, impressed by Adapa's wisdom (and, in some readings, mollified), instead offers the **bread and water of *life***, Adapa — obeying Ea's caution — refuses them, and so forfeits the immortality that was within his grasp. He returns to earth mortal, and mortality is fixed for humankind.

## Cross-tradition significance — the Mesopotamian Eden
Adapa's story is the closest Mesopotamian analogue to the **Eden narrative**: a divinely-made first man, a forbidden food, a god who withholds (or seems to withhold) life, and the loss of immortality for all humanity that follows a single decision. The parallel to **[[adam]]** — set out classically by Andreasen (1981) and Batto (1992) — is treated as a **shared structural pattern**, not a demonstrated literary borrowing: both cultures ask, through a first-man figure and a forbidden food, why humans die. The contrast is as telling as the likeness — Adam eats and is punished; Adapa *refuses* and loses immortality by obedience.

Adapa also stands beside **[[gilgamesh-king]]**, who likewise comes within reach of immortality (the plant of life) and loses it — the two great Mesopotamian statements that eternal life is offered to humankind and slips away.
