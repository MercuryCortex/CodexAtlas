# Gaius Julius Caesar

## Identity
Gaius Julius Caesar (100–44 BCE) was a Roman general, statesman, and author whose career ended the Roman Republic and set in motion the religious-political machinery of the Principate. A patrician of the *gens Iulia* — a family that claimed descent from the goddess Venus through Aeneas — he held the office of *pontifex maximus* (chief priest of the Roman state religion) from 63 BCE until his death, fusing supreme political and sacral authority in a single person. After his assassination the Senate formally consecrated him as **Divus Iulius** (the Deified Julius), the first historical Roman to receive state-sanctioned divine honours, and his adopted heir Octavian built the entire imperial cult — and the title *Divi filius* ("son of the god") — on that precedent.

## Historical evidence
Among the best-documented figures of antiquity. His own *Commentaries* on the Gallic and Civil Wars survive; he is the subject of Suetonius's [[phase-3-024-suetonius-twelve-caesars|*Lives of the Caesars*]], Plutarch's parallel *Life*, Appian, and Cassius Dio, and he appears throughout Cicero's contemporary correspondence. The comet that appeared during the funeral games of 44 BCE (the *sidus Iulium*) was interpreted as Caesar's soul ascending to the gods — a publicly stage-managed apotheosis recorded by multiple sources.

## Religious significance
Caesar matters to the Atlas not primarily as a soldier but as the hinge of Roman state religion:

- **Pontifex maximus.** As lifelong head of the priestly colleges he controlled the calendar, the auspices, and the official cults of [[tradition-roman-religion]].
- **The Julian calendar (46 BCE).** Drawing on Egyptian solar reckoning mediated by the astronomer Sosigenes of Alexandria, Caesar replaced the chaotic Roman lunisolar calendar with a 365.25-day solar year — the direct ancestor of the Gregorian calendar still in civil use.
- **Divus Iulius.** His consecration created the [[tradition-imperial-cult]]: a living emperor could now be the son of a god, and after death a god himself.

## Cross-tradition findings
- **Caesar → the imperial cult → the Christological milieu.** The deification of Caesar and the *Divi filius* titulature of Augustus established, by the time of the [[event-pax-romana-begins-27-bce|Augustan settlement]], a Mediterranean-wide vocabulary of "son of god," "savior," "bringer of peace," and "good news" (*euangelion*) attached to the emperor. Early Christian Christology articulated itself *against* this imperial-divine background, appropriating and inverting its terms (Crossan & Reed 2004; Gradel 2002). This is a **substrate/contrast** transmission, not a borrowing of person, and is wired through [[apotheosis]] and [[tradition-imperial-cult]].
- **Alexander → Caesar (model of divine kingship).** Caesar consciously modelled his self-presentation on [[alexander-the-great]] (Suetonius, *Caesar* 7 records him weeping at Alexander's statue); the Alexander-as-son-of-Zeus-Ammon precedent licenses the later Roman ruler-cult. See [[divine-kingship]].

## Disputes
- **Did Caesar seek monarchy / divinity in life?** Sources disagree on whether the divine honours voted in 45–44 BCE (a temple, a *flamen*, the title *Iupiter Iulius*) were sought by Caesar or pressed on him. Weinstock (1971) reads a deliberate divine-kingship programme; Gradel (2002) and North emphasise that full state-cult deification only came posthumously. The Atlas carries both: the living honours are contested, the posthumous consecration is documented.
