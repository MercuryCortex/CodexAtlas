# Thomas Aquinas

## Identity
Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274) was the supreme medieval Latin theologian, principal architect of the Christian-Aristotelian synthesis, and author of the *Summa Theologiae* — the most influential single work of Catholic theology. Born to the Counts of Aquino (southern Italy); entered the Dominicans against family opposition; studied at Paris under Albertus Magnus; held the Paris Dominican chair twice; taught in Italy (Naples, Orvieto, Rome — where the *Prima Pars* was composed). Mystical experience during Mass at Naples December 1273 ("all I have written seems to me like straw"); stopped writing; died on the way to the Council of Lyon, March 1274. Canonized 1323; declared Doctor of the Church 1567; declared the official Catholic philosopher by Leo XIII's *Aeterni Patris* (1879).

## Works
*Summa Theologiae* (~1265–1274, his magnum opus); *Summa contra Gentiles* (~1259–1265); *Scriptum super Sententiis* (Sentences commentary ~1252–1256); *De Ente et Essentia* (early metaphysical treatise); commentaries on most of Aristotle's works; commentaries on Pseudo-Dionysius's *Divine Names* and on the Pauline epistles, Job, and the Gospels; many *Quaestiones Disputatae* (Disputed Questions); the Eucharistic hymns.

## Role in the tradition
Foundational; the Thomistic synthesis defined Catholic theology for centuries and was institutionalized as the official Catholic philosophy from 1879 to Vatican II (still influential post-Vatican II, but no longer monopolistic). 219 propositions including some attributable to Aquinas were condemned at Paris in 1277, three years after his death — a serious shock to early Thomism, gradually reversed.
