# Thomas Aquinas

## Identity
**Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225 – 7 March 1274)** was a Dominican friar, twice regent master of theology at Paris, and the author of the *Summa Theologiae* — the most influential single work of Catholic theology, and the text through which Aristotle entered Christian doctrine for good. He is the pivot of the largest documented intellectual transmission in Western history: Aristotle → the Abbasid translation movement → al-Fārābī, Ibn Sīnā, Ibn Rushd → the twelfth-century Toledo and Sicilian translators → the Latin universities → Aquinas. He is not the origin of that chain. He is its terminus, and he knew it: the *Summa* is built out of Greek philosophy read in Latin translations made from Arabic, argued against Muslim and Jewish philosophers he read with unusual care, in a Christian synthesis that outlived every one of its opponents.

## Life
Born at the family castle of **Roccasecca**, near Aquino in the Kingdom of Sicily, to the counts of Aquino. Placed as a boy oblate at the Benedictine abbey of **Monte Cassino** (c. 1231); moved to the **University of Naples** around 1239, after Frederick II's conflict with the abbey. Naples mattered: Frederick's *studium* taught the "new" Aristotle — the natural-philosophical and metaphysical books then still restricted at Paris — and it was there that Thomas met the Dominicans.

He entered the **Order of Preachers** in 1244, against determined family opposition; his brothers intercepted him on the road and his family held him for about a year before giving way. He then studied at **Paris** (1245–48) and at **Cologne** (1248–52) under [[albertus-magnus|Albert the Great]], who was building the first Dominican *studium generale* north of the Alps and whose life's project was to make the whole Aristotelian corpus available to Latin readers.

- **1252–1256, Paris** — bachelor of the *Sentences*; produces the *Scriptum super libros Sententiarum* on [[phase-5-017-peter-lombard-sentences|Peter Lombard's *Sentences*]], the standard theological textbook, and the early *De ente et essentia*.
- **1256–1259, Paris (first regency)** — regent master in theology, his inception delayed by the bitter dispute between the secular masters and the mendicant orders.
- **1259–1268, Italy** — Naples, then Orvieto with the papal curia, then Rome, where in 1265 he was sent to found a Dominican *studium* at Santa Sabina and began the **Prima Pars** of the *Summa Theologiae*. The *Summa contra Gentiles* belongs to this period (c. 1259–1265). In 1264 Urban IV instituted the feast of Corpus Christi; the office, with its hymns, is attributed to Thomas and the attribution is generally accepted.
- **1268/69–1272, Paris (second regency)** — an exceptional second term, called back into a university in crisis. Here he fought on two fronts at once: against the radical Aristotelians of the arts faculty (Siger of Brabant and others) in *De unitate intellectus contra Averroistas* (1270), and against conservative theologians who wanted the new Aristotle curtailed altogether. In December 1270 Bishop **Étienne Tempier** condemned thirteen propositions at Paris.
- **1272–1273, Naples** — sent home to found a Dominican *studium generale*; writes the *Super Librum de causis*.
- **6 December 1273** — after Mass in the chapel of St Nicholas at Naples he stopped writing and dictating. Pressed by his secretary Reginald of Piperno, he is reported to have said that everything he had written seemed to him like straw beside what he had seen. The *Tertia Pars* breaks off mid-treatise; the *Supplementum* printed with the *Summa* was assembled after his death out of his early *Scriptum*.
- **7 March 1274** — died at the Cistercian abbey of **Fossanova**, travelling to the Second Council of Lyon at Gregory X's summons. He was forty-nine.

## The Aristotelian recovery — the transmission that made the *Summa* possible

Until the twelfth century the Latin West had almost no Aristotle: the *logica vetus* — the *Categories* and *De interpretatione* in Boethius's translations, with Porphyry's *Isagoge* — and nothing else of substance. The *Physics*, *De anima*, the *Metaphysics*, the *Nicomachean Ethics*, the *Politics* were unread and largely unknown.

They came back along **two routes**, and the second is the one this vault is built to trace:

1. **Greek → Latin**, directly: James of Venice in the twelfth century, then and above all **William of Moerbeke OP** in the thirteenth, who produced new or corrected versions of most of the corpus from the Greek, along with Proclus and other Neoplatonic texts.
2. **Greek → Syriac → Arabic → Latin**: the Baghdad translation movement rendered Aristotle into Arabic and then generated a live commentary tradition around him; the twelfth-century translators at **Toledo** and in Norman **Sicily** — Gerard of Cremona, Dominicus Gundissalinus, Michael Scot — carried that whole package into Latin. See [[event-toledo-translations-1085-1150]] and [[mathematics-house-of-wisdom]].

The second route did not merely deliver texts. It delivered **readings**. What arrived in Paris alongside Aristotle was [[al-farabi|al-Fārābī]]'s classification of the sciences, [[ibn-sina|Avicenna]]'s metaphysics of essence and existence, [[ibn-rushd|Averroes]]'s Long Commentaries, Ibn Gabirol's *Fons vitae*, and [[maimonides|Maimonides]]'s *Guide* — the *Dux neutrorum* of the Latins. Latin theologians did not receive a bare Aristotle to interpret freshly. They received an Aristotle already argued over for three centuries in Arabic, and they entered that argument in the middle.

**Aquinas read none of it in the original.** He had no Greek, no Arabic, no Hebrew. Every text named above reached him in Latin, and the quality of those translations is part of the history of his thought — one reason Moerbeke's revisions mattered to him so much.

His specific debts, and where he breaks with each, are wired in the YAML above and summarised here:

| Source | What Aquinas takes | Where he refuses it |
|---|---|---|
| **Aristotle** | act/potency, hylomorphism, four causes, teleology, virtue as habit, demonstrative science | creation *ex nihilo*, providence over particulars, personal immortality, the supernatural end of the human person |
| **Avicenna** ([[ibn-sina]]) | the real distinction of essence and *esse*; the argument from contingency (the Third Way) | existence as an accident of essence; the separate Agent Intellect; necessitarian emanation |
| **Averroes** ([[ibn-rushd]]) | the Long Commentaries as the working exegesis of Aristotle | the unicity of the possible intellect — attacked head-on in 1270 |
| **Maimonides** | the limits of language about God; the indemonstrability of the world's beginning in time | strictly negative theology — replaced by analogy |
| **Pseudo-Dionysius / Proclus** | participation, hierarchy, the *exitus–reditus* architecture, the divine names | the emanationist ontology those structures came wrapped in |

## The works

**[[phase-5-024-aquinas-summa-theologiae|*Summa Theologiae*]]** (c. 1265–1273), unfinished. Written not for masters but for beginners in theology, to replace the disordered sequence of the *Sentences* with an order that follows the subject matter itself. Four parts: **Prima Pars** (119 questions — God, the Trinity, creation, the angels, the human person), **Prima Secundae** (114 — beatitude, human action, passions, habits, virtue and vice, law, grace), **Secunda Secundae** (189 — faith, hope and charity; prudence, justice, fortitude, temperance; the states of life), **Tertia Pars** (90, breaking off in the treatise on penance — Christ and the sacraments). Chenu's reading, now standard, sees the whole as an *exitus–reditus*: all things proceed from God and return to God, with Christ in the Tertia Pars as the way of that return.

Each **article** runs the same machine: *videtur quod* (the objections, stated at full strength), *sed contra* (an authority on the other side), *respondeo dicendum* (the determination), then a reply to each objection in turn. The form is an argument you can lose. It is why the *Summa* is still usable: the strongest case against Aquinas is printed above his answer, in his own hand.

**Summa contra Gentiles** (c. 1259–1265) — four books, the first three proceeding by natural reason alone and the fourth from revelation. Long described as a missionary handbook for use among Muslims and Jews in Spain; that account rests on a late tradition and is doubted by modern scholarship, which reads it rather as a work of philosophical theology addressed to an educated readership that did not share Christian premises.

**Aristotle commentaries** (twelve, several unfinished), the **biblical commentaries** and the *Catena aurea*, the *Quaestiones disputatae* (*De veritate*, *De potentia*, *De malo*), the polemical opuscula, and the Corpus Christi liturgy.

## The shape of the thought

- **Theology as a science.** *Summa Theologiae* Ia q.1 a.2: sacred doctrine is a genuine science, but a *subalternated* one — it takes its first principles on authority from a higher knowledge, that of God and the blessed, as optics takes its principles from geometry. This is what licenses the whole enterprise of arguing about revelation.
- **The [[five-ways|Five Ways]]** (Ia q.2 a.3): from motion, from efficient causation, from the possible and the necessary, from the gradation of perfections, from the governance of things. All five are *a posteriori*, from effects in the world; Aquinas explicitly rejects Anselm's argument from the concept of God. Each terminates in a formula — *and this everyone calls God* — rather than in a full doctrine of God.
- **Essence and *esse*.** In every creature, what a thing is (*essentia*) is really distinct from the sheer fact that it is (*esse*); in God alone they are identical — God is *ipsum esse subsistens*, subsistent being itself. This is Aquinas's most original metaphysical move, and it is built out of Avicennian materials. It grounds [[doctrine-divine-simplicity|divine simplicity]].
- **[[analogia-entis|Analogy]].** Language about God is neither univocal with creaturely language (which would make God one being among beings) nor merely equivocal (which would make theology noise). It is analogical. This is Aquinas's answer to the problem Maimonides solved by negation and the Muʿtazila by stripping the attributes (see [[philosophy-mutazila]]); the resulting doctrine sits between [[doctrine-cataphatic-via-positiva|cataphatic]] and [[doctrine-apophatic-via-negativa|apophatic]] theology and is native to neither.
- **One substantial form.** The rational soul is the single substantial form of the human body (Ia q.76). Not a pilot in a ship: a human being is one substance, not a spirit using a body. This was the most contested thesis he held, and the one his critics went after first.
- **Grace perfects nature.** *Gratia non tollit naturam sed perficit* (Ia q.1 a.8 ad 2). Nature is real, intelligible and good in its own right, and revelation completes rather than replaces it — the licence for taking a pagan philosopher and two non-Christian philosophical traditions seriously.
- **[[moral-natural-law|Natural law]]** (IaIIae q.91 a.2): the rational creature's participation in the eternal law. Precepts are known by practical reason from the goods that human nature is ordered to. This is the seed of [[moral-just-war-cross-tradition|just-war doctrine]], of [[moral-doctrine-of-double-effect-catholic|double effect]], and — through the sixteenth-century Salamanca theologians Vitoria and Soto — of the modern law of nations.
- **Virtue and beatitude.** The [[moral-aristotelian-virtues-golden-mean|Aristotelian]] cardinal virtues, acquired by habituation, together with the infused theological virtues of faith, hope and charity (see [[moral-cardinal-theological-virtues]]). The end of the human person is the [[doctrine-beatific-vision|beatific vision]] — a direct knowledge of God's essence that no created nature can reach by its own powers.
- **[[doctrine-transubstantiation|Transubstantiation]]** (IIIa q.75): the substance/accident distinction applied to the Eucharist — the substance of bread and wine becomes the body and blood of Christ while the accidents remain. The term was already used at the Fourth Lateran Council of 1215; Aquinas gives it its philosophical articulation, and Trent's definition is written in his vocabulary. See [[mass-eucharist-catholic]].

## Condemnation, correction, canonization

Aquinas was not received as an authority. He was received as a suspect.

- **1270** — Étienne Tempier, Bishop of Paris, condemns thirteen propositions, aimed at the radical Aristotelians of the arts faculty rather than at Aquinas.
- **7 March 1277** — Tempier condemns **219 propositions**, three years to the day after Aquinas's death. Most target the arts faculty; whether and how far any target Aquinas is a live scholarly question. Hissette's article-by-article study (1977) identifies a small number as touching Thomistic theses; estimates in the subsequent literature (Wippel, Thijssen) run from a handful to about twenty.
- **18 March 1277** — eleven days later, **Robert Kilwardby OP**, Archbishop of Canterbury and himself a Dominican, condemns thirty propositions at Oxford, including the unicity of substantial form. That one is unambiguously Aquinas's. His Franciscan successor **John Pecham** renews the condemnation in 1284 and 1286.
- **The *correctoria* war** — the Franciscan William de la Mare writes a *Correctorium fratris Thomae* (c. 1278–79) listing Thomistic errors; the Franciscan chapter of Strasbourg (1282) rules that the *Summa* may be read in the order's houses only accompanied by it. Dominicans reply with a series of *Correctoria corruptorii* — "corrections of the corrupter". The Dominican general chapters of 1278 and 1279 move to defend Thomas's writings as the order's own.
- **18 July 1323** — canonized by John XXII at Avignon.
- **1325** — Étienne de Bourret, Bishop of Paris, annuls the 1277 articles *insofar as they touch the teaching of Thomas*. The condemnation is not withdrawn; Aquinas is lifted out of it.
- **1567** — declared **Doctor of the Church** by Pius V. By long-standing tradition the *Summa* lay on the altar beside the Scriptures during the deliberations of the [[event-council-of-trent-1545-1563|Council of Trent]]; the story is repeated everywhere and its historicity is not firmly established.

## Afterlife — Thomism

The commentators come first: Capreolus, **Cajetan** (Thomas de Vio, whose commentary is printed with the Leonine *Summa*), Sylvester of Ferrara, John of St Thomas. Then **Second Scholasticism** — the Iberian Dominicans and Jesuits of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, Vitoria, Soto, Báñez, Suárez — who take Aquinas's natural law and build from it the first theory of the rights of peoples, including the rights of the peoples of the Americas against their conquerors.

Leo XIII's encyclical ***Aeterni Patris*** (1879) then makes Aquinas the normative philosopher of Catholic seminary teaching, a status confirmed by the 1917 Code of Canon Law. **Neo-Thomism** in the twentieth century runs in several directions at once: the strict-observance line (Garrigou-Lagrange), the historical line (Gilson, Chenu), the cultural-political line (Maritain, whose natural-law commitments fed into the drafting of the 1948 Universal Declaration of Human Rights), and the transcendental line (Maréchal, Rahner, Lonergan) that reads Aquinas through Kant.

The [[event-second-vatican-council-1962-1965|Second Vatican Council]] ends the monopoly without ending the authority — *Optatam totius* still names Aquinas as master of seminary formation, but alongside a plurality of theologies. The most vigorous recent Thomism is **analytical**: Anscombe, Geach, Kenny, MacIntyre, Finnis, Stump and Davies read Aquinas as a working philosopher rather than a school authority, which is very close to how he read Aristotle. See [[philosophy-thomism]], [[philosophy-scholasticism]], [[philosophy-aristotelianism]].

## MASSIVE WIN — the receiver, not the origin

The value of Aquinas to this vault is that he is a **documented terminus** of a transmission chain rather than a spontaneous Christian achievement. Every link is textually evidenced: Greek originals; Syriac and Arabic translations made in Abbasid Baghdad; three centuries of Arabic commentary by [[al-farabi|al-Fārābī]], [[ibn-sina|Ibn Sīnā]] and [[ibn-rushd|Ibn Rushd]], with [[al-ghazali|al-Ghazālī]]'s attack on the philosophers and Ibn Rushd's reply ([[phase-5-045-al-ghazali-tahafut-al-falasifa]], [[phase-5-046-ibn-rushd-tahafut-al-tahafut]]) shaping what arrived; Jewish philosophy in Arabic from [[maimonides|Maimonides]] and Ibn Gabirol; Latin translations at Toledo and in Sicily; and finally the Paris masters. The manuscripts, the translators' names, the dates and the citations all survive.

The finding this supports is uncomfortable for two constituencies at once, which is why it is worth having as data rather than as polemic. The intellectual architecture of Latin Catholic theology — the philosophy that Rome would make normative for its own seminaries in 1879 — was assembled out of Greek pagan philosophy transmitted, preserved and argued into shape by Muslim and Jewish thinkers, and Aquinas cites those thinkers by name on nearly every page.

The second finding is the *shape* of the reception. Aquinas is not a passive receiver: he takes Avicenna's central distinction and inverts its metaphysics, uses Averroes constantly while writing a treatise to destroy his psychology, follows Maimonides on the limits of God-talk and then refuses his conclusion. That is what a live transmission looks like — argument, not transcription. Compare [[zhu-xi]], who is doing the same operation on Huayan Buddhism at the other end of Eurasia in the same century, with no contact whatever (MASSIVE-WINS-INDEX entry 32).

## Disputes

- **How far did 1277 touch him?** Hissette (1977) argues that few of the 219 articles are genuinely Thomist; Wippel (1977) and later work reads the condemnation as a broader disciplinary act against the arts faculty in which some Thomistic positions were caught. The Oxford condemnation of the same month is not in doubt: the unicity of substantial form is his.
- **How Aristotelian is he?** The Gilson–Chenu generation read Aquinas as fundamentally Aristotelian with Neoplatonic decoration. Later scholarship (Wippel, and the *Liber de causis* work) treats participation and the *exitus–reditus* frame as structural rather than ornamental, making him a genuinely hybrid thinker. This is still argued.
- **The purpose of the *Summa contra Gentiles*.** The traditional account — a manual for Dominican missionaries preaching to Muslims and Jews in Spain, supposedly at Ramon de Penyafort's request — depends on a late source and is now widely doubted (Torrell 1996).
- **The hymns.** Aquinas's authorship of the Corpus Christi office is generally accepted; *Adoro te devote* specifically is contested and is recorded here as an attribution, not an authorship.
- **What happened in December 1273.** The testimony that he stopped writing after an experience at Mass is well attested in the canonization inquiry, at one remove (Bartholomew of Capua reporting Reginald of Piperno). What it *was* — mystical experience, a stroke, exhaustion, or some combination — is not recoverable, and confident readings in either direction go beyond the evidence.
