# William Sinclair, 1st Earl of Caithness

## Identity
Scottish nobleman, 1st Earl of Caithness, hereditary Grand Master of the Masons of Scotland (a pre-speculative-Freemasonry title relating to operative craft guilds). He founded and built **Rosslyn Chapel** (officially the Collegiate Church of St Matthew) in Midlothian, Scotland, beginning in 1446 and continuing until shortly before his death in 1484. The chapel was never completed — only the choir was built of what was intended to be a much larger structure.

## Historical Evidence
Well documented in Scottish historical records: his titles, lands, and the chapel's construction are attested in contemporary charters and chronicles. The Sinclair family's claim to hereditary Grand Mastership of Scottish operative masonry is attested in a 1601 charter (the "Schaw Statutes" aftermath) and earlier. Their status as Scotland's leading noble family in the mid-15th century is unquestioned.

## Rosslyn Chapel: The Architecture
The chapel is one of the most **iconographically extraordinary buildings in Scotland**. Its stone carvings include:
- **Green Man** heads (~110 carved examples) — pagan vegetation deity imagery in a Christian building
- **Apprentice Pillar** (the most elaborate column in the chapel) — associated with the legend of a murdered apprentice, structurally identical to the **Hiram Abiff** legend in Freemasonry: a master craftsman kills his apprentice out of jealousy upon seeing the apprentice's superior work
- **Possible New World plants** (aloe vera, maize-like carvings) dated before Columbus (1492) — claimed evidence of pre-Columbian contact, though this is debated
- **Angels playing bagpipes**, danse macabre motifs, Templar crosses, Seven Deadly Sins, and Seven Cardinal Virtues
- **Enochian-like script** (disputed) in certain carved panels

The **Templar cross** motifs are prominent. The Sinclair family had Crusader connections: Henri de Saint-Clair accompanied Godfrey of Bouillon on the First Crusade (1096).

## The Masonic Connection
The Sinclair family's claim to hereditary Grand Mastership of Scottish operative masons was formally transferred when **William St. Clair** (a later descendant, ~1736) gave up the hereditary claim and was elected the **first Grand Master of the Grand Lodge of Scotland** at its founding in 1736. The family thus directly bridges operative craft masonry and speculative Freemasonry. The Apprentice Pillar legend — an exact structural parallel to the Hiram Abiff degree ritual — sitting inside a Sinclair-built, Templar-adjacent chapel, is the most concentrated physical nexus of the Templar→Masonic transmission hypothesis.

Whether the Sinclairs *intentionally* encoded Masonic mythology in stone, or whether later Freemasons *read* Masonic mythology into the carvings, is debated and possibly unresolvable. The carvings are undeniably there; the interpretation is not.
