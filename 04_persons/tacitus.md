# Tacitus

## Identity
The principal Latin historian of the early Empire (Augustus to Domitian). Senator, consul, proconsul of Asia. His *Annales* covers ~+14 to +68 (Tiberius through Nero, with substantial gaps); his *Historiae* covered +69 to +96 (Year of Four Emperors through Domitian). **Annals 15.44** — describing Nero's persecution of Christians as scapegoats for the Great Fire of Rome (64) — is among the most-debated extra-Christian sources for Jesus and early Christianity.

## Historical evidence
The *Annales* survives in Medicean manuscripts; the Christian passage in 15.44 is universally accepted as authentic (the late-19th-c. interpolation hypothesis has been definitively rejected). Tacitus writes (in slightly polemical translation):

> "Nero fastened the guilt and inflicted the most exquisite tortures on a class hated for their abominations, called Christians by the populace. Christus, from whom the name had its origin, suffered the extreme penalty during the reign of Tiberius at the hands of one of our procurators, Pontius Pilatus, and a most mischievous superstition, thus checked for the moment, again broke out not only in Judaea, the first source of the evil, but even in Rome..."

## Works
- *Annales* (incomplete; books 7–10, 17–18 lost; books 5, 6, 11, 16 partly lost)
- *Historiae* (books 1–4 plus part of 5 survive)
- *Germania*
- *Agricola* (biography of his father-in-law, governor of Britain)
- *Dialogus de oratoribus*

## Role in the tradition
- **Earliest non-Christian Latin attestation of Christ's crucifixion under Pilate**: independent of Christian sources, this is critical historical evidence — it establishes that Jesus's execution by Pilate was *known* to the Roman senatorial class within two generations.
- **Nero-fire persecution**: Tacitus is the principal source for the Christian persecution under Nero (64) — independent of and supplementary to the Christian martyrological tradition.
- **Procurator vs. prefect**: Tacitus calls Pilate a *procurator*; the Pilate inscription found at Caesarea Maritima in 1961 calls him a *praefectus*. Tacitus's terminology reflects the later imperial title applied retrospectively — historically inaccurate but understandable.

## Disputes
- **Authenticity of *Annals* 15.44**: settled in favor of authenticity. The minority "interpolation" view is no longer maintained by mainstream scholarship.
- **Christus / Chrestus**: the earliest manuscript has *Chrestianos* and *Christus*; the slip suggests early scribal confusion of the two names.
- **Source of Tacitus's information**: whether from Roman court records, oral tradition, or Christian source is debated.
