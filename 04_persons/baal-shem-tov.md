# Israel ben Eliezer (the Baal Shem Tov)

## Identity
Polish-Podolian Jewish faith-healer, Kabbalist, and the figure later regarded as the founder of Hasidism (~1698–1760). Born in the Podolian town of Okopy on the Polish-Ottoman frontier; spent his early adulthood as a *melamed* (children's teacher), folk-healer (*ba'al shem*, "master of the Name"), and itinerant Kabbalistic practitioner. From ~1735 he settled at Międzybóż (Mezhbizh) and gathered a circle of disciples. The Besht left no writings; his teachings were preserved (and substantially shaped) by disciples, principally Dov Ber of Mezeritch (the Maggid), Jacob Joseph of Polonnoye, and the hagiographical *Shivchei ha-Besht* (1814). Hasidism as a mass movement developed under the Maggid and his students in the generation after the Besht's 1760 death; by 1815 Hasidism had become the dominant religious form of Eastern European Jewry.

## Historical evidence
Substantially documented but mixed: Rosman's archival work in the Międzybóż town records firmly establishes the historical Israel ben Eliezer; the religious-theological figure is a more difficult reconstruction because the principal sources are post-mortem disciples' compositions (with the *Shivchei* a half-century later).

## Works
None directly. The teachings collected as *Tzava'at ha-Rivash*, *Keter Shem Tov*, and embedded in his disciples' writings represent the early Hasidic tradition's reconstruction of his teaching.

## Role in the tradition
The Besht is the founder figure of Hasidism — the most consequential single development in early modern Ashkenazi Judaism. The Hasidic combination of Lurianic and Cordoveran Kabbalah, democratized mystical experience, *devekut* (cleaving to God) accessible to all rather than only learned elites, and the institution of the *tzaddik* (charismatic leader) transformed Eastern European Jewry. Hasidism's response to the Sabbatean-Frankist crisis was to channel mystical energies back into normative halakhic life with a populist theology.
