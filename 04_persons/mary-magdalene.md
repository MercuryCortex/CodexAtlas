# Mary Magdalene

## Identity
Mary of Magdala (Migdal on the Sea of Galilee) was a prominent female disciple of Jesus, financially supporting his mission, present at the crucifixion, and — by the testimony of all four canonical Gospels — the first witness of the empty tomb and the resurrection. The Gospel of John (20:11–18) presents her as the first to encounter the risen Jesus, who commissions her to announce his resurrection to the male disciples. Medieval Western tradition gave her the epithet *Apostola Apostolorum* — "Apostle to the Apostles."

## Historical evidence
Among the best-attested individual figures of the disciple group, with the rare distinction of independent multiple attestation across all four Gospels for her role at the cross and tomb. Her name's specificity (associated with the town Magdala) helps distinguish her from the many other Marys in the New Testament. **The conflation of Mary Magdalene with (a) the unnamed sinful woman of Luke 7 who anoints Jesus's feet, (b) Mary of Bethany (sister of Lazarus and Martha) was officially proposed by Pope Gregory I in 591** — and was the dominant Western interpretation until the Catholic Church formally reversed it in 1969. The "Mary Magdalene = repentant prostitute" identification is a *medieval Western invention*, absent from Greek-Christian tradition and unsupported by any New Testament evidence.

The Gnostic and apocryphal traditions (Gospel of Mary, Gospel of Philip, Gospel of Thomas, Pistis Sophia) repeatedly elevate her as Jesus's preferred disciple — sometimes in tension with Peter — suggesting that the egalitarian-leaning early Christian streams retained memory of her prominence which the institutional Petrine tradition tended to downplay. Pagels (*The Gnostic Gospels*, 1979) develops this argument. Modern fictional speculations (Dan Brown etc.) about Mary as Jesus's wife have no historical basis but draw on the genuine Gnostic-tradition elevation. For this vault, Mary matters as: (a) the *resurrection witness* foundational to the entire Christian movement; (b) a hinge figure between mainstream and Gnostic Christianities; (c) a key data point in tracing gender-and-authority dynamics in early Christianity.
