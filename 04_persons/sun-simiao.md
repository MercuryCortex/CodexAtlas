# Sun Simiao

## Identity

Sun Simiao (孫思邈, c. 581–682 CE) is the greatest physician of the Tang dynasty and one of the most consequential figures in the history of Chinese medicine — the person who synthesized the entire pre-Tang medical tradition into a single encyclopedic work and articulated the Chinese tradition's foundational medical ethics. He is venerated as **Yaowang** (藥王, "King of Medicine") and temples to him are found across China.

He refused multiple imperial appointments to remain a working physician, reportedly saying: "What good is fame to a physician? I serve patients." This refusal of courtly preferment to serve the sick is the biographical motif that qualified him for posthumous divine status in Chinese popular religion.

## The Chinese Hippocratic Oath

Sun Simiao's *Dayi Jing Cheng* (大醫精誠, "On the Absolute Sincerity of Great Physicians," Chapter 1 of the *Qianjin Yao Fang*, 652 CE) is the Chinese tradition's closest analogue to the Hippocratic Oath:

> "When a great physician treats illness, he must be mentally calm and his disposition firm. He should not give way to wishes and desires, but must first develop a heart full of mercy... If someone comes to him with a complaint, he should not ask whether they are noble or common, rich or poor, old or young... friend or enemy, native or foreigner, foolish or wise. He should treat them all equally, as if they were close relatives."

The document establishes:
- Egalitarian treatment regardless of status or wealth
- Prohibition on exploiting patients' vulnerability
- Obligation of continued learning and humility
- Ethical duty to treat emergency cases without delay

This parallel to the Hippocratic Oath — articulated 1,000 years later, with no documented Greek-Chinese contact on this specific document — is a case of independent convergence on the physician's oath as the ethical foundation of medical practice.
