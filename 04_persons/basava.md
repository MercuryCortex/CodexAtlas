# Basava

## Identity
Twelfth-century Kannada Brahmin-born minister, poet, and devotional-social reformer; founder (or principal organizer — the tradition's own self-understanding varies) of the *Lingāyat* / *Vīraśaiva* movement (1131–1167). Born to Mādirāja and Mādalāmbike, a Smārta Brahmin family at Bāgevāḍi (modern Karnataka); educated at the Shaiva center Kūḍalasangama; served as chief minister (*Prime Minister-equivalent*: *bhāṇḍāri / treasurer*) to King Bijjala II of the Kalachuri dynasty at the capital Kalyana (modern Basavakalyan). Used his court position to organize the *Anubhava Mantapa* ("Hall of Spiritual Experience") — an open assembly of devotees across all castes and both sexes (revolutionary for 12th-century South India) where the Sharanas (devotees) composed and exchanged Kannada *vacanas*. The movement's anti-caste, anti-Vedic, anti-temple, anti-ritualism, and pro-vernacular orientation provoked political conflict; an inter-caste marriage celebrated by Basava precipitated a crisis with King Bijjala; Bijjala was assassinated (Basava's followers' involvement is contested in the sources) and the movement was dispersed. Basava died at Kūḍalasangama in 1167.

## Historical evidence
- Kalachuri inscriptional record: King Bijjala II is firmly attested; Basava's place as his minister is attested by multiple sources.
- The *vacana* corpus itself: a vast 12th-c. body of Kannada devotional-philosophical-social poetry preserved through Lingayat manuscript tradition.
- Palkurike Somanatha's *Basava Purāṇa* (~1240) is the earliest sustained hagiography (Telugu).
- The Channabasava-Allama-Mahādēviyakka biographical network within the *vacana* corpus itself.

## Works
~1300+ *vacanas* attributed to Basava (the actual historical-Basava corpus is hard to disentangle from the broader vacana movement — a textual-historical problem comparable to the Mira and Sur corpora). Vacanas are not metered verse but Kannada prose-poems (sometimes called India's first "free verse"), often radically short, often addressed to Basava's *iṣṭadeva* Kūḍalasaṅgama-deva (the linga form of Śiva at Kūḍalasangama).

## Role in the tradition
- **Lingayat / Vīraśaiva founder**: Lingayatism is one of the major South Indian Hindu traditions (in modern Karnataka, ~17% of the state's population identify as Lingayat); the question of whether Lingayatism is a sect of Hinduism or a separate religion has been a live legal-political question in modern Karnataka.
- **Anti-caste, anti-temple, pro-egalitarian South Indian movement of the 12th c.**: Basava is one of the most thorough-going anti-caste reformers in pre-modern Indian history. Anubhava Mantapa included women (the great female mystic [[mahadeviyakka]] is one of its central figures, often considered Karnataka's counterpart to Mirabai or Andal), Dalits, and members of all occupational castes. The *iṣṭa-liṅga* (the small portable linga worn on the body) replaces caste birth-status with personal devotion as the locus of religious identity.
- **Kannada literature**: the *vacana* corpus is one of the foundational achievements of pre-modern Kannada literature.

## Cross-tradition connections
- **12th-c. South Indian anti-caste reform paralleling 12th-c. European reform movements**: structurally comparable to contemporaneous Cathar, Waldensian, and other European 12th-c. heretical-reformist movements that contested established ecclesiastical authority (the parallel is structural, not causal — Lingayat is no more "influenced by" Catharism than vice versa).
- **Sant tradition deep root**: the 15th–16th-c. North Indian Sant tradition (Kabir, Ravidas, Nanak) — anti-caste, anti-temple, anti-ritual, pro-vernacular, devotional to a formless divine — has frequently been read as a North Indian Hindi-language descendant of the same impulses Basava embodied three centuries earlier in Karnataka. Hawley (2015, *A Storm of Songs*) traces the "bhakti movement" historiography in which Basava is positioned as a foundational moment in a longer 12th–17th-c. anti-caste devotional arc.
- **Anti-caste theology with Christian / Islamic Reformation structural parallels**: Schouten and others have drawn comparative-religious parallels with the Protestant Reformation's anti-clerical, anti-sacramental, vernacular-scripture orientation. The structural similarity is genuine; the historical-causal independence is total.
- **Modern Indian constitutional politics**: Basava is a central figure in modern Karnataka's anti-caste / Dalit political imagination; B.R. Ambedkar studied Lingayatism with sympathetic interest.

## Disputes
- **Founder vs. organizer**: tradition's self-understanding is variable; Allama Prabhu, who presided over the Anubhava Mantapa, is sometimes presented as the true initiator with Basava as the political-administrative organizer.
- **Bijjala assassination**: the involvement (or not) of Lingayat devotees in the assassination of King Bijjala (1167) is a long-running point of dispute.
- **Lingayat vs. Vīraśaiva**: the modern legal/political question of whether these are the same tradition or two related-but-distinct traditions has generated extensive contemporary scholarly and political literature.
- **Vacana attribution**: the standard 1300+ Basava vacana corpus is partly historical-Basava and partly later attribution.
