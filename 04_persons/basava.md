# Basava

## Identity
Twelfth-century Kannada Brahmin-born minister, poet, and devotional-social reformer; founder (or principal organizer — the tradition's own self-understanding varies) of the *Lingāyat* / *Vīraśaiva* movement ([[tradition-lingayat]], a reform current within [[tradition-shaivism]]). Born to Mādirāja and Mādalāmbike, a Smārta Brahmin family at Bāgevāḍi (modern Karnataka); educated at the Shaiva center Kūḍalasangama; served as chief minister and treasurer (*bhāṇḍāri*; the sources also style him *daṇḍanāyaka*) to King Bijjala II of the Kalachuri dynasty at the capital Kalyana (modern Basavakalyan). Used his court position to organize the *Anubhava Mantapa* ("Hall of Spiritual Experience") — an open assembly of devotees across all castes and both sexes (revolutionary for 12th-century South India) where the Sharanas (devotees) composed and exchanged Kannada *vacanas*. The movement's anti-caste, anti-Vedic, anti-temple, anti-ritualism, and pro-vernacular orientation provoked political conflict; an inter-caste marriage celebrated by Basava precipitated a crisis with King Bijjala; Bijjala was assassinated (Basava's followers' involvement is contested in the sources) and the movement was dispersed. Basava died at Kūḍalasangama in 1167.

## Historical evidence
- Kalachuri inscriptional record: King Bijjala II is firmly attested; Basava's place as his minister is attested by multiple sources.
- The *vacana* corpus itself: a vast 12th-c. body of Kannada devotional-philosophical-social poetry preserved through Lingayat manuscript tradition.
- Palkurike Somanatha's *Basava Purāṇa* (~1240) is the earliest sustained hagiography (Telugu).
- The Channabasava-Allama-Mahādēviyakka biographical network within the *vacana* corpus itself.

## Works
Between ~1300 and ~1500 *vacanas* are attributed to Basava depending on the recension (the actual historical-Basava corpus is hard to disentangle from the broader vacana movement — a textual-historical problem comparable to the Mira and Sur corpora). Vacanas are not metered verse but Kannada prose-poems (sometimes called India's first "free verse"), often radically short, often addressed to Basava's *iṣṭadeva* Kūḍalasaṅgama-deva (the linga form of Śiva at Kūḍalasangama — "the lord of the meeting rivers" in A.K. Ramanujan's rendering).

## Core doctrine — the body as temple
The single most-quoted vacana states the movement's central inversion (trans. Ramanujan 1973):

> "The rich will make temples for Śiva. What shall I, a poor man, do? My legs are pillars, the body the shrine, the head a cupola of gold. Listen, O lord of the meeting rivers, things standing shall fall, but the moving ever shall stay."

The doctrine — the body as temple, Śiva dwelling within the devotee rather than in endowed stone — carries the whole Lingāyat programme in one figure. If the shrine is the body, then temple endowment, priestly mediation, and the caste purity that regulates access to sacred space all lose their function at once. The *iṣṭa-liṅga* worn on the person is the ritual expression of the same claim. The closing line ("things standing shall fall, but the moving ever shall stay") sets the built and the permanent against the living and the mobile — a critique of institutional religion made in the vocabulary of architecture.

## Role in the tradition
- **Lingayat / Vīraśaiva founder**: Lingayatism is one of the major South Indian Hindu traditions (in modern Karnataka, ~17% of the state's population identify as Lingayat); the question of whether Lingayatism is a sect of Hinduism or a separate religion has been a live legal-political question in modern Karnataka.
- **Anti-caste, anti-temple, pro-egalitarian South Indian movement of the 12th c.**: Basava is one of the most thorough-going anti-caste reformers in pre-modern Indian history. The Anubhava Mantapa included women (the great female mystic Mahādēviyakka / Akka Mahādēvi, often considered Karnataka's counterpart to [[mirabai]] or [[andal]], is one of its central figures), Dalits, and members of all occupational castes. The *iṣṭa-liṅga* (the small portable linga worn on the body) replaces caste birth-status with personal devotion as the locus of religious identity.
- **The Anubhava Mantapa as institution**: a standing assembly in which theological and social questions were debated across caste and gender lines, and in which the vacana corpus was composed and circulated. It is among the earliest such deliberative religious assemblies documented for the Indian subcontinent; how far its openness extended in practice, and how much of its description is later hagiographical idealization, is debated (Chandra Shobhi 2005).
- **Kannada literature**: the *vacana* corpus is one of the foundational achievements of pre-modern Kannada literature.

## Cross-tradition connections
- **12th-c. South Indian anti-caste reform paralleling 12th-c. European reform movements**: structurally comparable to contemporaneous Cathar, Waldensian, and other European 12th-c. heretical-reformist movements that contested established ecclesiastical authority (the parallel is structural, not causal — Lingayat is no more "influenced by" Catharism than vice versa).
- **Sant tradition deep root**: the 15th–16th-c. North Indian Sant tradition ([[kabir]], Ravidas, [[guru-nanak]] — [[tradition-bhakti-sant]]) — anti-caste, anti-temple, anti-ritual, pro-vernacular, devotional to a formless divine — has frequently been read as a North Indian Hindi-language descendant of the same impulses Basava embodied three centuries earlier in Karnataka. Hawley (2015, *A Storm of Songs*) traces the "bhakti movement" historiography in which Basava is positioned as a foundational moment in a longer 12th–17th-c. anti-caste devotional arc ([[event-bhakti-movement-c-700-1700]]).
- **Anti-caste theology with Christian / Islamic Reformation structural parallels**: Schouten and others have drawn comparative-religious parallels with the Protestant Reformation's anti-clerical, anti-sacramental, vernacular-scripture orientation. The structural similarity is genuine; the historical-causal independence is total.
- **Pratyabhijñā (Kashmir Shaivism)**: the *recognition* framework of [[philosophy-pratyabhijna-recognition]] ([[tradition-kashmir-shaivism]]) shares the premise that Śiva is already present in the devotee and that the path is recognition rather than acquisition. The resemblance is at the level of premise only — the Lingāyat tradition is held to be philosophically distinct from Kashmir Shaivism, and the two developed in different regions and lineages.
- **Modern Indian constitutional politics**: Basava is a central figure in modern Karnataka's anti-caste / Dalit political imagination; [[ambedkar]] studied Lingayatism with sympathetic interest.

### The Basava / Eckhart structural parallel
Set beside [[meister-eckhart]] (1260–1328, Rhineland), Basava's position shows a close structural isomorphism. **This is a parallel, not a transmission.** There was no channel between 12th-century Karnataka and 14th-century Rhineland Germany — no shared texts, no intermediaries, no documented contact in either direction — and none is claimed here. The two positions are independently derived, which is precisely what makes the convergence worth recording.

| Feature | Basava (1131–1167, Karnataka) | Meister Eckhart (1260–1328, Rhineland) |
|---|---|---|
| Vernacular vehicle | Kannada *vacanas* | Middle High German sermons |
| Locus of the divine | Śiva already within; the body is the shrine | The soul's uncreated spark (*Fünklein* / *scintilla animae*) is continuous with the Godhead |
| Relation to built religion | Temple endowment and priestly mediation displaced by the *iṣṭa-liṅga* on the body | External observance subordinated to the birth of the Word in the soul's ground |
| Path | Devotion to Kūḍalasaṅgama-deva, not ritual qualification | *Abgeschiedenheit* — detachment; union recognized rather than achieved |
| Audience | Anubhava Mantapa — all castes, women included | Vernacular preaching to Beguines, nuns, and laypeople |
| Institutional outcome | Crisis with King Bijjala; the movement dispersed after 1167 | Posthumously condemned by John XXII, *In Agro Dominico* (1329) |

**Tier and status of this claim.** The descriptions on each side are T1 — Ramanujan 1973 and Schouten 1991 for Basava, McGinn 2001 and the Eckhart node's apparatus for Eckhart. The *pairing itself* is vault-authored and carries no published source: it is a `parallel-motif` edge in the Parallel bucket, not a transmission edge, and it should never be rendered as one.

**On the literature.** The vault has not located a sustained published comparison of Basava with Eckhart, and it is offered here as an open research lead rather than as a discovery. That is a bounded statement about what has been searched, not a claim that no such scholarship exists — the surrounding comparative genre is large and long-established, and a specific treatment may well sit in Kannada-language or Indian comparative-theology literature not surveyed here. Eckhart has been compared at book length with Śaṅkara (Otto 1926), with Zen (Suzuki 1957), and with Advaita, Sufi, Daoist, and Kabbalistic material by many hands; comparisons of the Lingāyat movement with the European Reformation are standard (Schouten 1991). The Basava pairing is arguably tighter than Otto's Śaṅkara pairing on the specific axes of vernacularity, embodiment, and institutional critique — which is the reason to record it and the reason to check it against the literature before treating it as new.

## Disputes
- **Founder vs. organizer**: tradition's self-understanding is variable; Allama Prabhu, who presided over the Anubhava Mantapa, is sometimes presented as the true initiator with Basava as the political-administrative organizer.
- **Date of death**: 1167 is the date carried here and is the most commonly cited; some accounts give 1168. The dating is tied to the Bijjala assassination and is not independently fixed.
- **Bijjala assassination**: the involvement (or not) of Lingayat devotees in the assassination of King Bijjala (1167) is a long-running point of dispute.
- **Lingayat vs. Vīraśaiva**: the modern legal/political question of whether these are the same tradition or two related-but-distinct traditions has generated extensive contemporary scholarly and political literature.
- **Vacana attribution**: the attributed corpus (~1300–1500 vacanas depending on recension) is partly historical-Basava and partly later attribution.
