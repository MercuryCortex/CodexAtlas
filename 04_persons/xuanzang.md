# Xuanzang

## Identity
Xuanzang (~602-664 CE) was a Tang-dynasty Chinese Buddhist monk who in 629 set out on a 17-year overland pilgrimage from Chang'an to India, traveling via the Silk Road through Turfan, Samarkand, Bactria, and over the Hindu Kush, studying for years at the great Buddhist universities of Nālandā and other Indian centers, and returning in 645 with 657 Sanskrit manuscripts. The Tang emperor Taizong received him personally and established a state-sponsored translation bureau for him at Chang'an, where he and his team translated 75 sūtras (~1,335 fascicles) over the remaining 19 years of his life — the most consequential single body of Buddhist translation in Chinese history. He founded the Faxiang (Yogācāra-Vijñānavāda) school of Chinese Buddhism, whose central text *Cheng Weishi Lun* (*Treatise on the Establishment of Consciousness-Only*, 659) is his integrated digest of Indian Yogācāra philosophy. His travelogue *Da Tang Xiyu Ji* (*Records of the Western Regions*) is the single most important historical-geographical source for 7th-c. Central Asian and Indian Buddhism. His canonical 260-character Chinese translation of the [[phase-5-002-heart-sutra|Heart Sūtra]] (~649) is the standard text recited in East Asian Buddhism to this day.

## Historical evidence
Xuanzang is among the best-attested figures of medieval Chinese religious history. Independent attestations:
- His own *Da Tang Xiyu Ji* (646), an imperially-commissioned travelogue.
- His disciple Huili's *Daci'ensi Sanzang Fashi Zhuan* (大慈恩寺三藏法師傳, *Life of the Tripiṭaka Master of the Great Ci'en Monastery*) — a detailed biographical account compiled within decades of his death.
- Tang imperial records of his audience with Taizong, the construction of the Wild Goose Pagoda (大雁塔) at Chang'an's Da-Ci'en monastery as the housing for his translations, and his ongoing imperial patronage.
- His translations themselves, with extensive prefaces and colophons.
- Indian/Buddhist references in subsequent pilgrimage and translation literature (Yijing 義淨's continuation).
- Archaeological + epigraphic: his pagoda at Xingjiao Monastery near Xi'an still stands and held his cremation relics until they were transferred to multiple sites in modern times.

## Works
- ***Da Tang Xiyu Ji*** (646) — the travelogue; 12 fascicles. Documents the political, religious, and material culture of every region he passed through.
- **Translations** — including:
  - The full *Mahāprajñāpāramitā-sūtra* (600 fascicles — the largest Buddhist text in any language)
  - *Yogācāra-bhūmi-śāstra* (Asaṅga, ~5th c.)
  - *Madhyāntavibhāga* and *Mahāyāna-saṃgraha* (Yogācāra core texts)
  - *Abhidharmakośa* (Vasubandhu) — the most important Sarvāstivāda-Abhidharma text
  - [[phase-5-002-heart-sutra|Heart Sūtra]] in its standard 260-character form
- ***Cheng Weishi Lun*** (成唯識論, 659) — his integrated Chinese synthesis of Indian Yogācāra commentary on Vasubandhu's *Triṃśikā*; the foundational text of the Faxiang school.

## Role in the tradition
- **In Chinese Buddhism**: Xuanzang is the patron of the Faxiang school (a Yogācāra-Vijñānavāda variant); his translations remain the standard Chinese versions of the texts they cover. His establishment of an imperially-sponsored translation bureau is the institutional model that subsequent Chinese Buddhist translation projects (notably under the Song) followed.
- **In Chinese culture more broadly**: Xuanzang's pilgrimage was fictionalized in the 16th-c. Ming novel *Journey to the West* (西遊記, by Wu Cheng'en) as the framing narrative for the magical adventures of Sun Wukong, Zhu Bajie, and Sha Wujing accompanying the holy monk "Tripiṭaka" to India. *Journey to the West* is among the four classic novels of Chinese literature and Xuanzang's name as "Tripiṭaka" / Sanzang is universally recognized in Chinese popular culture.
- **In Japan**: Hossō (法相, the Japanese transmission of Faxiang) is established at Yakushiji and Kōfukuji temples in Nara from the 7th c. onward; remains an extant Japanese Buddhist school today.
- **In modern scholarship**: the *Xiyu Ji* is the foundational source-text for the historical geography of medieval Buddhist Asia. Alexander Cunningham used it to identify ancient Indian Buddhist sites in his 1860s-1880s Archaeological Survey of India — Nālandā, Vikramaśīla, Bodh Gaya, Sārnāth, Sānchī, Lumbinī, and others were rediscovered and excavated on the basis of Xuanzang's reports.

## Disputes
- **Heart Sūtra authorship**: did Xuanzang translate an Indian Sanskrit text, or compose the Chinese Heart Sūtra himself from passages of the Larger Prajñāpāramitā plus a closing mantra? Nattier 1992 argues the latter — that the Sanskrit Heart Sūtra is a back-translation from Chinese, and that Xuanzang may have been the compiler. This is now the majority Western-scholarship view. See [[phase-5-002-heart-sutra]].
- **Translation philosophy**: Xuanzang's translation style (*xin yi*, "new translations") versus Kumārajīva's earlier style (*jiu yi*, "old translations"). Xuanzang aimed for technical-philosophical precision and used phonetic transliteration for many Indic technical terms; Kumārajīva used adaptive Chinese vocabulary and elegant literary style. Both styles remain in use; many later Chinese Buddhists preferred Kumārajīva's more readable versions even when Xuanzang's were available.
- **Date of return**: 645 is universal.
