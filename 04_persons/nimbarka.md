# Nimbarka

## Identity
Vaishnava Vedantic philosopher and founder of the *Nimbarka sampradaya* (the Sanaka-sampradāya in the standard *catuh-sampradāya* enumeration of the four medieval Vaishnava lineages: Ramanuja's Sri sampradāya, Madhva's Brahma sampradāya, Vallabha's Rudra sampradāya, Nimbārka's Sanaka sampradāya). Dates contested: traditional Nimbarka tradition places him in the 7th c.; mainstream scholarship places him post-Ramanuja (12th c., c. 1130–1200), with some arguments for 13th–14th c. Origin variously placed: a Telugu Brahmin from Andhra who settled at Vrindavan, or born at Nimbāgrāma near Vrindavan itself. Established the seat at Nimbāgrāma; established the school's distinctive joint worship of Radha-Krishna as the supreme divinity — Nimbarka is in fact the earliest known systematic theologian of Radha-Krishna paired worship, predating the Gaudiya, Vallabha, and Radhavallabha Radha-theologies.

## Works
- ***Vedānta-Pārijāta-Saurabha*** (the Fragrance of the Vedanta Wishing-Tree): his Brahmasutra commentary — terse, ~200 short verses; the basic statement of the *Dvaitādvaita* ("Dual-Non-dualism" / "Difference-and-Non-difference") position.
- ***Daśaślokī*** (Ten Verses): the school's foundational creed.
- ***Mantra-Rahasya-Ṣoḍaśī***: meditation manual.
- The major *Vedānta-Kaustubha* commentary on the Brahmasutras (a much fuller commentary) was composed by Nimbarka's principal disciple Śrīnivāsācārya and is conventionally read as the school's primary systematic statement.

## Role in the tradition
- **Dvaitādvaita Vedanta**: the doctrine that Brahman, souls, and the world are simultaneously different (the dualist truth — souls and matter are real and dependent on Brahman) and non-different (the non-dualist truth — they have no independent existence apart from Brahman). The system is intermediate between Shankara's pure non-dualism and Madhva's strict dualism, structurally similar to Ramanuja's Vishishtadvaita but with a different formal articulation. Chaitanya's later *achintya-bheda-abheda* ("inconceivable difference-and-non-difference") is a structurally cognate position.
- **Radha-Krishna theology**: Nimbarka is the earliest extant systematic Vaishnava theologian to make Radha co-supreme with Krishna; the school treats Radha as Krishna's *hlādinī-śakti* (bliss-power) and his eternal counterpart in the supreme reality.
- **Vrindavan as theological center**: Nimbarka's institutional anchor at Vrindavan-area sites (Nimbāgrāma, Salemabad) made the school an early node in the medieval transformation of the Vrindavan region into the principal Krishna-bhakti pilgrimage landscape (the parallel transformation by Chaitanya's Six Goswamis is two centuries later).

## Cross-tradition connections
- **Catuh-sampradaya systematization**: the standard list of four Vaishnava sampradayas — each with its founding *ācārya*, its philosophical position, and its territorial sphere — is a 16th–17th-century theological-political construction. Hawley 2015 traces how this taxonomy crystallized; Nimbarka is positioned in it as the *Sanaka-sampradāya* counterpart, granting his school canonical status alongside the larger Ramanuja and Madhva traditions.
- **Pre-Chaitanya Radha-Krishna paired theology**: the development of Radha as Krishna's supreme consort, central to the later Gaudiya tradition's signature theology, has Nimbarka as one of its earliest formal theological articulators. The parallel medieval rise of bridal-mystical paired-divinity theology in Tantric Shakta (Lalitā-Tripurasundarī with Śiva), in Kashmir Trika (Śakti as Śiva's dynamic aspect), and in Sufi *‘ishq-i ḥaqīqī* (the soul-bride and the Divine Beloved) is a striking medieval cross-tradition pattern.

## Disputes
- **Dates**: traditional 7th c. vs. mainstream 12th c. vs. revisionist 13th–14th c. — unresolved; the textual-philological evidence (citation patterns, references to Ramanuja and Madhva) points to a 12th–14th-c. range.
- **Authorship of *Vedānta-Kaustubha***: traditionally Nimbarka, philologically more likely Śrīnivāsa.
- **The school's relation to Ramanuja**: structural similarity to Vishishtadvaita raises questions of priority.
