# Heraclitus of Ephesus

## Identity
Heraclitus of Ephesus (~-535 to ~-475) was a Presocratic philosopher of aristocratic background — Diogenes Laertius reports he was the heir to the *basileus*-priesthood of Demeter at Ephesus but renounced it in favor of his younger brother. His written work, conventionally titled *Peri Phuseōs* (*On Nature*), is preserved only in some 130 short fragments (collected as DK22 — Diels-Kranz fragment number 22), quoted in later sources. His thought centers on the *logos* as the principle of cosmic order, the unity of opposites, perpetual flux ("you cannot step into the same river twice"), and fire as the basic cosmic element. He is one of the most-quoted and most-interpreted of the Presocratics.

## Historical evidence
The historical evidence is largely doxographical: Diogenes Laertius's *Lives* IX, fragments preserved by Plato (*Cratylus* 402a, *Theaetetus* 152d–e), Aristotle (*Metaphysics* IV.5, *Physics* I.2), Plutarch, Clement of Alexandria, Hippolytus's *Refutation*, and others. The fragments themselves are aphoristic, oracular, and resistant to systematic reconstruction — the "obscurity" (*ho skoteinos*) was already an ancient nickname. The chronology rests on Diogenes' floruit notice at the 69th Olympiad (-504/-501), which is approximate.

## Works
*On Nature* (lost as a continuous text). The ~130 fragments are catalogued in Diels-Kranz; standard modern editions are Kahn 1979 (with commentary), Robinson 1987, Graham 2010. The work is sometimes thought (Kahn) to have been deposited as a votive at the temple of Artemis at Ephesus, in keeping with the oracular tone of the fragments.

## Role in the tradition
Heraclitus is foundational for the doctrine of the *logos* (cosmic reason, principle of order) that Stoicism systematizes ([[phase-3-005-stoic-foundational-texts]]); the Stoic *logos spermatikos* descends, with substantial modification, from Heraclitus's fragment B1 ("of this *logos* that is for ever men prove uncomprehending"). Through Stoicism, the Heraclitean *logos* enters Hellenistic-Jewish thought (Philo) and Johannine Christianity (the Logos-Christology of John 1:1–18 — see [[logos-johannine]], [[logos-philonic]]). Plato's portrait in the *Theaetetus* of Heraclitus as the doctrine-of-flux philosopher (against which Plato develops the doctrine of Forms) sets the terms of much subsequent interpretation. Aristotle treats Heraclitus as a denier of the principle of non-contradiction (*Metaphysics* IV.3, IV.5) — a polemically simplified portrait.

Modern reception: Hegel admired Heraclitus and credited him with the first dialectical philosophy; Nietzsche regarded him as the supreme Greek philosopher (preferring him to Plato and Aristotle); Heidegger devoted intensive late-life seminars to the Heraclitean fragments.

## Disputes
- **Doctrine of flux.** Plato's *Theaetetus* portrayal of Heraclitus as a doctrine-of-pure-flux philosopher is contested by modern scholarship (Kirk 1954, Kahn 1979). The fragments themselves emphasize *unity of opposites* and *measured exchange* as much as flux — Heraclitus's cosmos is in tension, but it is *ordered* tension. The "everything flows" (*panta rhei*) slogan is a later doxographical summary, not a verbatim fragment.
- **Fragment-arrangement.** With no continuous text, the order and grouping of the fragments is reconstructive; different editors group them differently. Kahn 1979 attempts a thematic-philosophical reconstruction; the order remains scholarly hypothesis.
- **Was Heraclitus a monist or pluralist about the elements?** Fire as primary substance vs. fire as one of several elements in cosmic exchange — Aristotle's monist reading is debated.
- **Heraclitus's politics.** The fragments contain politically-charged material (criticism of the Ephesians, of his contemporary Pythagoras, of mass opinion); whether these add up to a coherent political philosophy is contested.
