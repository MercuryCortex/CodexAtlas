# Cyril of Alexandria

## Identity
Patriarch of Alexandria from 412 until his death in 444. Theological architect of the condemnation of Nestorius at the Council of Ephesus (431) and of the *Theotokos* (God-bearer) title for Mary. His Christological vocabulary — particularly *mia physis tou theou logou sesarkomene* ("one incarnate nature of the divine Word") — became the formula around which the later Chalcedonian / non-Chalcedonian split would crystallize. A formidable polemicist and political operator: he succeeded his uncle Theophilus and continued the Alexandrian patriarchate's combative posture toward both rival sees and pagan-philosophical centers in the city.

## Historical evidence
Voluminous Greek corpus (commentaries, dogmatic treatises, letters), preserved in continuous transmission. The acta of Ephesus 431 are a primary source. The murder of Hypatia (415) occurred under his patriarchate; while Cyril's direct responsibility is disputed (Socrates Scholasticus blames the Alexandrian *parabalani*; Damascius blames Cyril personally), his anti-pagan and anti-Jewish policies provided the climate (Watts 2017, *Hypatia: The Life and Legend of an Ancient Philosopher*).

## Works
- *Twelve Anathemas* — the polemical capstones appended to his third letter to Nestorius.
- *On the Unity of Christ* — late-career dialogue defending the single-subject Christology.
- *Commentary on John*, *Commentary on Luke* — extensive exegetical works.
- *Against Julian* (Contra Julianum) — book-length refutation of Julian the Apostate's *Against the Galileans*; valuable for preserving fragments of Julian's lost work.
- *Scholia on the Incarnation* — compact summary of Cyrillian Christology.

## Role in the tradition
The decisive figure of 5th-c. Christology. Both the Chalcedonian (Catholic / Orthodox) and the non-Chalcedonian (Oriental Orthodox) traditions claim him as authoritative — the Chalcedonian definition of 451 quotes his letters, and the Cyrillian Christology of *mia physis* remains the framework of Coptic, Ethiopian, Syriac and Armenian theology.

## Connections — substrate and influence
- His [[mary-theotokos]] definition at Ephesus is geographically loaded: Ephesus was the great cult center of [[artemis]] (Acts 19), and the substrate of devotion to a great virgin-mother goddess at the site has been read by Pelikan, *Mary Through the Centuries* (1996) and Limberis, *Divine Heiress* (1994) as conditioning the popular reception of the Theotokos title — though direct cultic continuity is disputed (Shoemaker, *Mary in Early Christian Faith and Devotion*, 2016, cautions against simple substitution narratives).
- His Christological vocabulary draws on Aristotelian and Stoic metaphysics: *ousia*, *physis*, *hypostasis*, *prosopon* — Russell 2000: 39–61.
- His doctrine of *theosis* (deification) inherits and develops Athanasius and the Cappadocians, ultimately resting on Platonic *homoiosis theo* ("becoming like God," *Theaetetus* 176b) Christianized — Russell 2004 is the standard treatment.
- *Against Julian* shows direct engagement with the Iamblichean-Neoplatonic theology that Julian had championed; Cyril knows Porphyry and Iamblichus well enough to refute them.

## Disputes
- **Hypatia's murder (415).** Cyril's direct role is disputed; the climate of anti-pagan violence he tolerated is not.
- **Single-subject Christology.** Whether Cyril's *mia physis* formula is compatible with Chalcedonian dyophysitism remains the theological core of the ongoing Oriental Orthodox–Eastern Orthodox dialogue.
- **The *Theotokos* title.** Patristically attested before Cyril (Origen, Alexander of Alexandria); Cyril made it the test of orthodoxy.
