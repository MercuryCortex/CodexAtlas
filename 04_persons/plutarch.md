# Plutarch of Chaeronea

## Identity
Plutarch (c. 46 – c. 120 CE) was a Greek Middle Platonist philosopher, biographer, and lifelong priest of Apollo at Delphi. He is the most prolific surviving Greek author of the high Roman Empire, with the *Parallel Lives* (paired Greek and Roman biographies) and the *Moralia* (a sprawling collection of philosophical, religious, and antiquarian essays). For the Codex Atlas his most load-bearing work is *De Iside et Osiride* — the single most influential Greco-Roman interpretation of Egyptian religion, read continuously from antiquity through Renaissance Hermeticism and modern Egyptology.

## Historical evidence
Plutarch is exceptionally well-attested: he served two terms as priest at Delphi (documented in Delphic inscriptions); held Roman citizenship under the *Mestrius* nomen (Mestrius Florus, a friend of Vespasian, was his patron); his works survive in unbroken Byzantine manuscript transmission. The *Suda* and Eusebius preserve biographical notices, but the autobiographical material in his own essays is the primary source.

## Works
- ***Parallel Lives*** — 23 surviving paired biographies (Theseus/Romulus, Alexander/Caesar, Demosthenes/Cicero, etc.).
- ***Moralia*** — 78+ surviving essays including:
  - **De Iside et Osiride** — see [[phase-4-072-plutarch-de-iside-et-osiride]]. Read as a Platonist allegorical reading of Egyptian myth, identifying Osiris/Isis/Typhon with Platonic principles.
  - *De E apud Delphos*, *De Pythiae oraculis*, *De defectu oraculorum* — the three "Delphic dialogues" on oracle-cult theology.
  - *De facie in orbe lunae* — cosmology and daimonology.
  - *De genio Socratis* — daimonology + Pythagorean material.
  - *Quaestiones Convivales* — table-talk including comparative-religion observations.
- Lost works including a *Life of Heracles* and commentaries.

## Role in the tradition
Plutarch is the principal Greco-Roman channel through which Egyptian religious vocabulary entered later European thought:

- **For [[clement-of-alexandria]]** — Clement's *Stromata* repeatedly cites and engages Plutarch's interpretive method for handling pagan myth; Plutarch's allegorical reading of Egyptian gods provides a Christian apologetic template.
- **For [[origen]]** — the Platonist allegorical method Plutarch deploys on Homer and Egyptian myth is directly parallel to Origen's allegorical exegesis of Hebrew scripture (Runia 1993 documents Philonic and Plutarchan precedents).
- **For Renaissance Hermeticism** — Plutarch's identification of Isis with universal cosmic principle becomes load-bearing for [[marsilio-ficino]], [[giordano-bruno]], and [[athanasius-kircher]]; *De Iside* is among the most-printed classical texts of the 16th–17th c. (Stinger 1985).
- **For modern Egyptology** — every standard treatment of Egyptian Osiris-myth quotes Plutarch's narrative as the most complete surviving account, while critically noting its Greek-philosophical overlay (Hornung 1982; Assmann 2001).

He is also the source of the famous "Great Pan is dead" passage (*De defectu oraculorum* 17), a late-pagan / proto-Christian topos that Eusebius and later Christian apologists redeploy as evidence for the supersession of paganism by the Incarnation.

## Disputes
- **Religious self-identification.** Plutarch was a practicing Greek priest who simultaneously wrote sympathetically about Egyptian, Persian, and Jewish religion. Whether this counts as syncretism, Platonic universalism, or a sophisticated polytheist pluralism is debated (Dillon 1996 ch. 4; Brenk 1987).
- **Date and circumstances of *De Iside*.** Composition is conventionally late (after 100 CE) and the work is dedicated to Clea, a Delphic priestess and Isiac initiate; whether Plutarch himself was an Isiac initiate is debated (Griffiths 1970 inclines yes; others read the work as outsider sympathy).
- **Reliability on Egyptian religion.** Plutarch's report of the Osiris myth is the most complete narrative we have, but the Egyptian primary sources ([[phase-1-002-pyramid-texts]], [[phase-1-009-coffin-texts]], [[phase-1-010-book-of-the-dead]]) do not match it in detail. How much of Plutarch's narrative is Egyptian, how much Hellenistic-Egyptian (i.e., Ptolemaic-era Egyptian priestly synthesis), and how much Plutarch's own Platonic systematization is the central interpretive question (Griffiths 1970; Quack 2002).
