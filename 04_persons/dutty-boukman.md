# Dutty Boukman

## Identity
Vodou priest (*oungan*) and slave-revolt leader who presided, together with the *manbo* Cécile Fatiman, at the Bois Caïman ceremony of August 14, 1791 — the religious-ritual moment commonly identified as the inaugurating event of the Haitian Revolution. Boukman was a Jamaican-born enslaved African transported to Saint-Domingue; the nickname "Boukman" (from English "book man") suggests he was literate. He was killed by French forces in November 1791, his head displayed publicly to demoralize the revolutionaries — which did not succeed.

## Historical evidence
Multiple early-19th-c. sources attest the Bois Caïman ceremony and Boukman's role: Antoine Dalmas's *Histoire de la Révolution de Saint-Domingue* (1814) gives the principal early account; Hérard Dumesle (1824) records a version of the Boukman prayer that has been transmitted as the founding Vodou-revolutionary invocation. Skeptical historiography (Geggus 1991, 2002) has examined whether the Bois Caïman ceremony's specific details are securely historical or whether they have been embellished retroactively; the *occurrence* of a ritual gathering of revolutionary leaders in mid-August 1791 is well-attested, the *details* (Cécile Fatiman, the black-pig sacrifice, the specific prayer text) are debated.

## Works
- The Boukman prayer / Bois Caïman invocation, transmitted in multiple variant forms; the principal published variant (Dumesle 1824 → Aimé Césaire and others) includes the lines: "Bondye ki fè soley ki klere nou anwo, ki soulve lanmè, ki fè loraj gronde... Bondye-a sa, ki kache nan yon nyaj, l'ap gade nou, l'ap wè tout sa blan yo ap fè..." ("The God who made the sun that lights us from above, who raises the sea, who makes thunder roar... that God, hidden in a cloud, watches us, sees everything the whites do...").

## Role in the tradition
Boukman is the foundational ritual-political figure of Haitian Vodou's revolutionary identity. He is venerated within Vodou as the *oungan* who opened the gate to liberation; he is honored by Haitian national narratives across political-religious lines (Catholic, Vodou, secular nationalist) as a founding figure of Haitian independence.

## Disputes
- **Historicity of Bois Caïman.** Léon-François Hoffmann (1990) controversially argued the ceremony was substantially fictional, a 19th-c. national-mythological construction. Geggus 2002 (the leading current historian of the Haitian Revolution) accepts the ceremony's historicity but is cautious about details; the broad consensus is that *something* happened in mid-August 1791 with Vodou-ritual mobilization of slave-revolt leadership, but the specific transmitted details may be partly mythologized.
- **Religious vs. political weighting.** Whether Bois Caïman should be read as primarily a religious or primarily a political event (the answer is presumably "both, indissociably") is a historiographic question that maps onto Haitian national-religious identity questions.
