# Ibn Ṭufayl (Abū Bakr Muḥammad ibn ʿAbd al-Malik ibn Muḥammad ibn Ṭufayl al-Qaysī)

## Identity
**Abū Bakr Muḥammad ibn ʿAbd al-Malik ibn Muḥammad ibn Ṭufayl al-Qaysī al-Andalusī (~1105–1185 CE)** — known in medieval Latin as **Abubacer** — is the Andalusi philosopher-physician who **introduced [[ibn-rushd|Ibn Rushd / Averroes]] to Almohad caliphal patronage** and authored the medieval Islamic philosophical novel that would become **one of the most consequential Islamic → European Enlightenment transmission edges**: ***Risālat Ḥayy ibn Yaqẓān*** (*The Epistle of Ḥayy ibn Yaqẓān* — "*Alive, son of Awake*"), the story of a child raised by a gazelle on a desert island who, through unaided natural reason, ascends to knowledge of the structure of the cosmos, the existence and attributes of God, and the mystical union of the human intellect with the Active Intellect. The *Ḥayy ibn Yaqẓān* extends an older Avicennian allegory of the same name (a brief encounter with the personified Active Intellect) into a full philosophical novel that anticipates simultaneously **(a) the philosophical-novel form**, (b) **the natural-religion / *deus sive natura* Enlightenment trope**, and (c) **the autodidact-on-a-desert-island narrative form** that descends — directly, via Pococke's 1671 Latin translation — into Locke's *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* and Defoe's *Robinson Crusoe*. Ibn Ṭufayl served as **court physician and vizier** to the Almohad caliph Abū Yaʿqūb Yūsuf (r. 1163-1184), and at the caliph's request **recommended the young Averroes for the Aristotelian-commentary project** — making Ibn Ṭufayl the **direct human link** between the Almohad caliphal patronage and the canonical Arabic Aristotelian commentary tradition. (Goodman 1972; Corbin 1960; Ben-Zaken 2011.)

## Historical evidence
Documented. Andalusi / Maghribi biographical sources (al-Marrākushī, Ibn al-Khaṭīb, al-Maqqarī) provide the biographical entries. The Almohad-caliphal patronage episode — Ibn Ṭufayl introducing Averroes to Abū Yaʿqūb Yūsuf c. 1169 — is independently attested in Averroes's own preface to his *Long Commentary on the Physics*, where Averroes recounts the caliph's request and Ibn Ṭufayl's intercession. Ibn Ṭufayl's medical and astronomical writings are mostly lost; his *Ḥayy ibn Yaqẓān* survives intact.

## Works
- **[[phase-5-047-ibn-tufayl-hayy-ibn-yaqdhan|*Risālat Ḥayy ibn Yaqẓān*]]** — Ibn Ṭufayl's philosophical novel. **Structure:** Ḥayy is born / placed on an uninhabited island in the Indian Ocean, raised by a gazelle, observes nature, learns by induction and reasoning to know the structure of the natural world, ascends through three septenary phases (0-7 / 7-21 / 21-49) to a vision of the necessary First Being and mystical union with the Active Intellect. In the final third of the work, an inhabitant of a nearby populated island (Absāl) arrives on Ḥayy's island; Absāl teaches Ḥayy human language; Ḥayy and Absāl travel to the populated island and find that the people there practice a literal-revealed religion they cannot move beyond; Ḥayy and Absāl return to the desert island to continue their philosophical-mystical practice in solitude. The Avicennian framework is explicit; the Sufi-mystical *fanāʾ* (annihilation) terminology is present; the political-theological message — **philosophical truth is the inner content of revealed religion, accessible to the unaided intellect, but the multitude cannot be moved beyond the literal-revealed form** — is the keystone esoteric falsafa position.
- **Medical / astronomical writings** — Ibn Ṭufayl was court physician to Almohad caliph Abū Yaʿqūb Yūsuf and undertook astronomical observations (the Almohad astronomical reform program); most of this material is lost.

## Role in the tradition
- **The Averroes-introduction.** Ibn Ṭufayl's recommendation of Averroes for the Aristotelian-commentary project c. 1169 is **the direct human link** between the Almohad caliphal patronage and the canonical Arabic Aristotelian commentary tradition. Without Ibn Ṭufayl's intercession, the Averroist commentaries — and therefore the Latin-Averroist transmission — would have had a different and probably narrower path.
- **The Andalusi peripatetic terminus.** Ibn Ṭufayl is the middle term of the canonical Andalusi peripatetic sequence: **Ibn Bājja → Ibn Ṭufayl → Ibn Rushd**. The "solitary-philosopher / *mutawaḥḥid*" theme — the philosopher who completes his intellectual ascent apart from the political community because the unphilosophical multitude cannot follow — is an Andalusi-specific accent inherited from Ibn Bājja, extended by Ibn Ṭufayl into the *Ḥayy* novel, and engaged critically by Averroes.
- **The 1671 Pococke Latin translation and the European Enlightenment.** **Edward Pococke the Younger** (under the supervision of his father, the great English Hebraist Edward Pococke the Elder) published *Philosophus Autodidactus, sive Epistola Abi Jaafar, Ebn Tophail* at Oxford in 1671 — the Latin translation that **enters the European Enlightenment debate on natural religion**. **Locke** read it (some Locke scholars argue *Ḥayy ibn Yaqẓān* substantially influences the *Essay Concerning Human Understanding*'s discussion of the *tabula rasa* and innate-vs-acquired ideas); **Daniel Defoe**'s *Robinson Crusoe* (1719) draws on the desert-island autodidact form; **Quaker and Behmenist** circles in 17th-c. England read it as evidence that natural-religion / inner-light is universally accessible (George Keith published an English version 1674; Simon Ockley published a more accessible English version 1708, prefaced by claims that the book vindicates Quaker natural-religion). **Spinoza** owned a copy in his library; the *Ḥayy* is partly behind the 17th-c. European debate on whether revealed religion is necessary for moral / theological knowledge. (Ben-Zaken 2011; Russell 1994; Wahba 1996.)
- **Pre-Pococke Hebrew transmission.** Moses ibn Tibbon translated *Ḥayy ibn Yaqẓān* into Hebrew (c. 1349); Moses of Narbonne wrote a Hebrew commentary (1349). The Hebrew transmission is the bridge to the 17th-c. Christian Hebraist circles (Pococke père was a Hebraist; the Pococke 1671 Latin used both Arabic and Hebrew witnesses).

## Disputes
- **The Active Intellect / Sufi-mystical-union ending.** Is the ending of *Ḥayy* a Sufi-mystical union, an Avicennian Active-Intellect conjunction, or both? Corbin 1960 reads it as fundamentally Avicennian-Illuminationist; Hourani and others read it as more Sufi-inflected. The two readings are not mutually exclusive.
- **The esoteric political-philosophical message.** Does the *Ḥayy* recommend the strict separation of philosophical truth from the multitude (the Andalusi-peripatetic *mutawaḥḥid* "solitary" position), or does it recommend a careful political program of esoteric-philosophical / exoteric-religious teaching? Strauss-tradition readings (Hourani 1956 *Averroes on the Harmony of Religion and Philosophy*; Mahdi) lean toward the esoteric political-philosophical reading; mainstream readings see the Sufi-mystical individual-ascent as the principal message.
- **Direct influence on Locke / Defoe.** The 1671 Pococke edition's influence on Locke and Defoe is established (Ben-Zaken 2011); the *strength* of the influence is contested. Most cautious modern view: *Ḥayy ibn Yaqẓān* is one of several inputs into the 17th-c. European natural-religion / desert-island-autodidact form, with the strongest direct influence on the Quaker / Behmenist reception.
