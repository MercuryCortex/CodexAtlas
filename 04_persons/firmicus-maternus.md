# Firmicus Maternus

## Identity
4th-century Latin author from Sicily, **uniquely a pagan astrological writer who subsequently converted to Christianity and wrote a major anti-pagan polemic**. Both his pre-conversion ***Mathesis*** (8 books on astrology, ~334–337 CE) and his post-conversion ***De Errore Profanarum Religionum*** (*On the Error of Profane Religions*, ~346–350 CE) survive — providing a uniquely paired source on the Christian-conversion intellectual trajectory of the Constantinian decades.

For the Codex Atlas wedge, Firmicus's ***De Errore*** is **the load-bearing witness for mystery-cult content**: while polemically hostile, his treatise provides **the most extensive surviving Christian description of the rites and theology of the major Mediterranean mystery-cults** — the Egyptian Isis-Osiris mysteries (ch. 2), the Phrygian Cybele-Attis mysteries (ch. 3 — including the unique detailed description of the cult's central night-rite and the famous formula *de tympano edi, de cymbalo bibi, religionis secreta perdidici* — "I have eaten from the drum, I have drunk from the cymbal, I have learned the secret of the religion" — chapter 18), the Eleusinian-Demeter mysteries (ch. 4), the Mithraic mysteries (ch. 5), and other cults. **The *De Errore* is paradoxically the single most-cited primary source by modern mystery-cult scholarship**, despite its polemical hostility — because no internal mystery-cult sources survive in comparable detail.

## Historical evidence
Two surviving Latin works in their author's hand; modest contemporary external mention; substantial Late-Antique manuscript transmission (the *De Errore* preserved in a single 10th-c. manuscript, Vatican Pal. lat. 165, from which all later copies derive).

## Works
- ***Mathesis*** (~334–337) — 8-book Latin astrological treatise; the most extensive surviving Latin astrological-philosophical work of late antiquity. Substantial use of Greek astrological sources (Vettius Valens, Manilius, Hermetic astrological corpus). The work is **a major source for Greco-Egyptian-Hermetic astrology in its Latin reception**, and explicitly engages [[hermes-trismegistus|Hermes Trismegistus]] as authority.
- ***De Errore Profanarum Religionum*** (~346–350) — 28-chapter Christian anti-pagan polemic addressed to the emperors Constantius II and Constans (jointly ruling 337–350; the work pre-dates Constans's death). The chapter sequence treats the major mystery-cults in turn: Egyptian (2), Phrygian-Cybele (3), Eleusinian (4), Mithraic (5), then the *symbola* (passwords) of various cults (18, the famous formula list), and culminating in a call for the emperors to *destroy* pagan temples.

## Role in the tradition
- **Principal Christian-polemical witness on mystery-cult content.** Burkert 1987, Smith 1990, and every specialist on Cybele-Attis, Eleusis, Mithras, and Isis cite Firmicus.
- **Documented conversion-narrative.** Firmicus's parallel pagan-and-Christian texts give unusual visibility into a 4th-c. intellectual conversion process.
- **Astrological-Christian transitional figure.** The *Mathesis* contains a passage praising the supreme God in language that some scholars read as proto-Christian (Bram 1975 thesis); whether Firmicus's conversion was *gradual* or *abrupt* is debated.
- **Direct address to imperial power.** *De Errore* is one of the principal **proto-Theodosian** anti-pagan textual interventions — predating the 380 Edict of Thessalonica and the 391-392 closure-edicts by ~40 years, but explicitly calling for imperial destruction of pagan temples.

## Disputes
- **Same Firmicus for both works?** Yes, with strong scholarly consensus, though the question was occasionally raised in 19th-c. scholarship.
- **How gradual was the conversion?** Bram 1975 argues for proto-Christian elements in the *Mathesis*; others see a more abrupt shift.
- **How accurate is the *De Errore* on cult content?** Polemically distorted but evidentially load-bearing — Burkert 1987 and Smith 1990 use Firmicus while noting the polemical bias.
