# Sotaesan — 少太山

## Overview
**Pak Chungbin** (박중빈, 1891–1943), known by his religious title **Sotaesan** (少太山, "Young Great Mountain"), was the founder of [[tradition-won-buddhism|Won Buddhism]]. Born to a peasant family in Jeolla Province during the last years of the Joseon Dynasty, he pursued spiritual questions from childhood, underwent a 20-year period of intensive seeking, and experienced a decisive enlightenment in 1916 — the year he judged that "all things are one dharma, and all dharmas are one mind."

Sotaesan is distinctive among modern religious founders for combining a world-reforming vision with an insistence on practical, everyday-rooted practice. His first act after enlightenment was to organize his disciples in a communal land-reclamation project (draining a coastal marsh), framing manual labor as the first Won Buddhist practice. This founding gesture — no retreat, no isolation, work-as-practice — encodes Won Buddhism's core reform.

## Enlightenment and founding
Sotaesan's 1916 experience has four characteristics that [[william-james]] identifies as marks of genuine mystical experience:
1. **Ineffability** — he could not describe it in conventional religious language; coined the Irwon-sang as the nearest symbolic approximation
2. **Noetic quality** — reports having understood the structure of all things at once (*"all doctrines are one"*)
3. **Transiency** — the acute state passed; what remained was transformative insight, not permanent altered consciousness
4. **Passivity** — described as "something holding him" rather than a willed achievement

He then spent months comparing his experience with existing religious traditions (Buddhist sutras, Confucian classics, Daoist texts, the Korean Bible) — concluding that all were pointing at the same thing his experience had revealed. This comparative-religion methodology is unusual for a premodern or semi-literate peasant founder; it reflects the specific historical moment (colonial Korea with new access to world religious texts).

## Key contributions
- **Irwon-sang** (一圓相) — invented the One Circle as the new dharma seal, replacing the Buddha-image as focal object. Radical implication: no form to worship, only the formless absolute symbolized by a geometric figure.
- **Saenghwal Bulgyo** ("Everyday Buddhism") — abolished the sacred/secular divide as the principle of practice. Every act becomes potential practice; no retreat required.
- **Wonbulgyo Gyojeon** — supervised the compilation of the canonical scripture until his death; completed by Chŏngsan (1943, revised 1962).
- **Social service** (eunhye repayment) — built hospitals, schools, and rural cooperatives as expressions of *eunhye* reciprocity; the social-service dimension is larger in Won than in most Buddhist traditions.

## Cross-tradition position
Sotaesan belongs to a cluster of ~1880–1950 reformers who claimed convergence between all traditions in response to colonial modernity: [[swami-vivekananda]] (Ramakrishna Mission, 1893), the perennial philosophy theorists, and the Bahá'í revelation model. His specific contribution to this cluster is anchoring it in a rigorous Korean Buddhist framework rather than in the universalism-without-discipline that Western perennialism often produced.

The closest structural parallel is [[choe-je-u]] ([[tradition-donghak|Donghak]]): both are Korean founders executed (Choe) or dying in crisis (Sotaesan dies 1943 during Japanese occupation), both synthesize multiple traditions under a Korean-centric frame. The 56-year gap between Donghak (1860) and Won Buddhism (1916) maps onto the arc from political-millennial response to colonial pressure toward quietist-practical response.
