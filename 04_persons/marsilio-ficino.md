# Marsilio Ficino

## Identity
Italian Renaissance Platonist, ordained priest (1473), Medici-funded translator and head of the informal Platonic Academy at Careggi outside Florence, and theological-philosophical synthesist of Christian Platonism and Renaissance Hermeticism (1433–1499). Ficino single-handedly produced the first complete Latin Plato (1484), the first complete Latin Plotinus (1492), Latin translations of substantial Iamblichus and Proclus, and — at Cosimo de' Medici's specific instruction — the first Latin translation of the Greek *Corpus Hermeticum* (1463, printed 1471 as the *Pimander*). His own *Theologia Platonica* (1474) and *De vita libri tres* (1489) systematize a Christianized Platonist-Hermetic theology and a magical natural medicine respectively. Ficino is the founder of Renaissance Hermeticism as a self-conscious intellectual movement and one of the central figures of the Florentine humanist Quattrocento.

## Historical evidence
Densely documented: extensive personal correspondence (twelve books, published in his lifetime), prefaces to his translations, Medici family records, contemporary references (Pico, Poliziano, Lorenzo de' Medici). One of the best-documented intellectual lives of the 15th century.

## Works
- *Theologia Platonica de immortalitate animorum* (1474, printed 1482).
- Latin translations of: Corpus Hermeticum (1463, printed 1471), Plato (1484), Plotinus (1492), and selections from Iamblichus, Porphyry, Proclus, Synesius, Psellus, and others.
- *De Christiana religione* (1474).
- *De vita libri tres* (1489) — natural-magical medicine.
- *De amore* (commentary on Plato's *Symposium*).
- Twelve books of letters.

## Role in the tradition
Ficino was treated by his contemporaries and successors as the principal restorer of Platonic philosophy in the Christian West and as the legitimate channel of Hermetic ancient wisdom. His framing of the *prisca theologia* (Hermes → Orpheus → Aglaophamus → Pythagoras → Plato → Christ) was foundational for all subsequent Renaissance esoteric thought. He was substantially Catholic-orthodox and avoided the Inquisitorial troubles that later esoteric figures (Bruno, Agrippa) attracted; his careful balance between Christian orthodoxy and Hermetic-Platonist enthusiasm became the model for the Catholic Renaissance esoteric current.

## Disputes
- **Inquisitorial scrutiny.** Ficino's *De vita* was cautiously investigated by the Inquisition; he was never formally charged.
- **Originality vs. compilation.** Kristeller emphasized originality; recent scholarship (Allen, Hankins) attends to his careful synthesis.
