# Cerdo

## Identity
A Christian teacher of Syrian origin who came to Rome under Bishop Hyginus (c. 138–140) and is named by Irenaeus, Tertullian, and Hippolytus as the predecessor or teacher of [[marcion-of-sinope]]. Cerdo's reported doctrine is the framework that Marcion will develop into a full sectarian movement: there are *two gods* — the *just* god of the Hebrew Bible (the creator, the lawgiver) and the *good* unknown god revealed by Christ; the Hebrew scriptures testify only to the just god; the Christian gospel announces a different and superior deity who has no prior dealing with creation or law.

## Historical evidence
- **Irenaeus** (*Adv. Haer.* 1.27.1; 3.4.3, ~180 CE) is the principal source. He places Cerdo's arrival at Rome under Hyginus (~138–140) and identifies him as "from the Simonians" — i.e., from the Antiochene-Syrian Simonian-Menandrian-Saturninian succession. Irenaeus is writing roughly forty years after the events and has access to the Roman ecclesial memory of Cerdo and Marcion.
- **Tertullian** (*Adv. Marcionem* 1.1, 1.22, 4.17, ~207–212) repeatedly names Cerdo as Marcion's teacher, in service of his polemical strategy of grounding Marcion in an older heresiological succession.
- **Hippolytus** (*Refutatio* 7.37) routes Cerdo's two-gods doctrine through Saturninus.
- **Eusebius** (*HE* 4.11), **Epiphanius** (*Pan.* 41) depend on Irenaeus.

What is reliably attested: a teacher named Cerdo was active at Rome around the time Marcion arrived (~140); he taught some form of two-gods Christianity; Marcion had contact with him. What is less reliable: whether Cerdo is a fully-formed predecessor whose doctrine Marcion merely systematized, or whether the heresiologists exaggerate Cerdo's role precisely to *deprive* Marcion of theological originality by giving him a teacher. Lieu 2015 inclines toward the latter; Harnack 1924 (classic) accepts a substantial historical Cerdo.

## Works
None survive. No fragments are quoted by the heresiologists.

## Role in the tradition
- **Bridge from the Antiochene Simonian succession to Marcionism.** The heresiological succession Simon → Menander → Saturninus → Cerdo → Marcion is partly a polemical construct, but it preserves the *milieu* claim: that Marcion's Christianity, far from being a clean rupture, draws on older Syrian-Antiochene proto-Gnostic currents already at work in the Roman church.
- **The two-gods doctrine as the load-bearing innovation.** Cerdo's reported teaching — that the OT god and the gospel god are distinct deities, the former merely *just*, the latter alone *good* — is the structural core of all later Marcionism. Whether Cerdo or Marcion is responsible for the full development is precisely the disputed point.
- **A test case for heresiological reliability.** Cerdo is named only by his opponents; no independent attestation exists. The question of how much of his doctrine is genuine to him vs. retrojected from Marcion's later system is one of the cleanest test cases for the methodological problem of heresiological history.

## Connections — substrate and influence
- **The Antiochene proto-Gnostic substrate.** Cerdo's reported Syrian origins and his connection to "the Simonians" place him within the same Antiochene milieu as [[menander-of-samaria]] and [[saturninus]]. The two-gods doctrine, in particular, has structural affinities with Saturninian theology (the unknown Father vs. the seven creator-angels; the unknown good God vs. the just OT creator).
- **Hellenistic-Jewish substrate of the just-vs.-good divine attributes.** The distinction between the divine *justice* and the divine *goodness* — which Cerdo turns into two separate gods — has a pre-Christian Jewish-Hellenistic substrate. Philo of Alexandria distinguishes the two divine *powers* (the *creative* power named *theos*, the *ruling* power named *kyrios*) as expressions of the one God's relations to creation; in *Quaestiones in Genesim* and *De Cherubim* the schema is developed. Cerdo (and Marcion after him) take what is in Philo a *distinction* within one God and *split* it into two gods. Lieu 2015 discusses the Philonic-to-Marcionite transmission; Räisänen 2005 *Marcion, Muhammad and the Mahatma* engages the substrate question.
- **Iranian dualistic-cosmological substrate, indirectly.** The just/good doubling has been argued (by Couliano 1992, more cautiously by Lieu 2015) to reflect mediated Iranian influence through the Syrian-Mesopotamian milieu of the Simonian succession.
- **Roman ecclesial context.** Cerdo's arrival under Hyginus (~138–140) places him in Rome at a moment of intense theological diversification: Valentinus arrives around the same time; the Roman church is host simultaneously to Cerdo, Valentinus, Marcion, and emerging proto-orthodox bishops. The Roman church's response (Marcion's eventual excommunication ~144; the heresiological project that emerges) is shaped by this Cerdo-Valentinus-Marcion convergence. Lampe 2003 *From Paul to Valentinus* on Rome.

## Disputes
- **Existence as a discrete teacher.** Most scholars accept Cerdo existed; Lieu 2015 entertains the possibility that he is partly a heresiological construct providing Marcion with an ancestry.
- **Originality of the two-gods doctrine.** Cerdo's or Marcion's? The sources favor Cerdo as predecessor; Lieu and others note this is exactly the kind of claim heresiologists *would* make.
- **Syrian origin.** Plausible from Irenaeus's "from the Simonians" language but not certain.
