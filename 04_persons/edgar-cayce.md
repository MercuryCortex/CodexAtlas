# Edgar Cayce

## Identity
Edgar Cayce (1877–1945) was an American photographer and lay Bible teacher who, from 1901 onward, gave thousands of "readings" while in a self-induced sleep state. The readings, transcribed by stenographers, address medical diagnosis (the largest single category), past lives (the karmic-reincarnational "life readings"), Atlantis and ancient history, biblical prophecy, and "Earth Changes" predictions. The Association for Research and Enlightenment (A.R.E.), founded 1931 in Virginia Beach, has preserved the ~14,306-reading archive and remains the institutional custodian and publisher. Cayce became internationally known after Thomas Sugrue's 1942 biography *There Is a River*; his posthumous fame expanded substantially in the 1960s–1970s New Age moment.

## Historical evidence
Cayce is comprehensively documented: stenographic transcripts of every reading from 1923 onward (Gladys Davis Turner's records); contemporary newspaper coverage (Birmingham, Selma, Dayton, Virginia Beach press, 1900s–1940s); his own family's correspondence; Sugrue's 1942 biography written in collaboration with the family; Hugh Lynn Cayce's archival management; and the comprehensive A.R.E. archive. The biographical facts — his Disciples-of-Christ upbringing, his photography business, the failed 1928–1931 Cayce Hospital, the founding of A.R.E. — are securely established. The puzzle is interpretive: what to make of the contrast between ordinary waking Cayce (modest education, small-town life) and the encyclopedic, vocabulary-rich, prophetic-toned readings.

## Works
- The trance readings themselves, catalogued by client-number and serial: see [[phase-7-035-cayce-atlantis-readings]] for the Atlantis subset.
- Books published during his lifetime were limited; Cayce's textual output is overwhelmingly the trance-transcript corpus, with Cayce himself functioning as the medium rather than author in the usual sense. Posthumous compilations (Edgar Evans Cayce *Edgar Cayce on Atlantis* 1968 etc.) are the principal accessible textual form.

## Role in the tradition
Cayce is the principal early-20th-century American figure linking older Spiritualist trance-mediumship to mid-century New Age esotericism. His distinctive contributions: (a) integrating reincarnation and karma — Theosophical-Hindu doctrines — into a nominally Christian framework, with serious resulting theological tension (his own Disciples-of-Christ church was uncomfortable; Cayce remained nominally observant); (b) producing very specific Atlantean past-life narratives that gave the lost-continent tradition concrete personal-biographical anchors; (c) the Bimini prediction, which became a focal point of the late-20th-c. underwater-Atlantis search (see [[phase-7-035-cayce-atlantis-readings]] Disputes for the geological evaluation). Cayce's institutional legacy through A.R.E. is substantial — the A.R.E. press, conferences, and archive continue actively into the present.

## Disputes
- **Veracity of the Atlantis material.** Comprehensively rejected by mainstream archaeology, geology, and paleoanthropology; see [[phase-7-035-cayce-atlantis-readings]] Disputes.
- **Bimini "Road."** Identified as natural Pleistocene beachrock by USGS (Shinn 2004); A.R.E.-aligned researchers continue to dispute, without peer-reviewed corroboration.
- **"Earth Changes" predictions.** Specific failed predictions documented (Japan inundation, California-Atlantic-coast inundation, New York destruction); A.R.E. interpretation has variously rescheduled or metaphorized.
- **Medical readings.** A separate evidential domain from the Atlantis content; the medical-reading record contains a mix of cases (some apparent successes per A.R.E.'s own follow-up; absence of double-blind testing). Even sympathetic biographers separate the two evidentially.
- **Trance methodology.** Hanegraaff 1998 analyzes the readings as cultural products drawing on (a) Cayce's Bible knowledge, (b) ambient American-esoteric content of the 1910s–1940s, (c) questions posed by the conductor (which primed output), (d) suggestion effects. The cultural-substrate analysis substantially explains the encyclopedic appearance of the readings without requiring channeled-source claims.
