# Clement of Rome

## Identity
Author of **1 Clement** — a long letter (65 chapters) sent from the Roman Christian community to the Corinthian Christian community ~+95/+96, addressing a leadership crisis in which younger Corinthians had deposed their presbyters. Traditionally identified as the **third or fourth bishop of Rome** (after Peter, Linus, Anacletus — order varies); critical scholarship is cautious about reading the monoepiscopal-bishop category back into 1st-c. Rome (where 1 Clement itself uses "bishops and deacons" without singular-bishop language).

## Historical evidence
1 Clement itself is the principal evidence; it is anonymous in the manuscript tradition but Hegesippus, Dionysius of Corinth (~+170), Irenaeus, and Clement of Alexandria all attribute it to a Clement of Rome (Eusebius *HE* 3.16, 4.23). The letter is the **earliest extant non-canonical Christian text** and one of the most important early-Christian documents for: (a) the development of episcopal/presbyteral authority; (b) the use of Old-Testament typology and Stoic-rhetorical convention in Christian discourse; (c) Roman Christianity's early extension of pastoral oversight to Corinth (read by some — Sullivan, Brown — as an early stage of Roman primacy claims).

## Works
**1 Clement** is genuine. **2 Clement** is a separate 2nd-c. homily pseudonymously attached. The **Pseudo-Clementines** (Recognitions and Homilies, ~+260) are a fascinating Jewish-Christian-Gnostic novelistic corpus in his name. The **Two Epistles to Virgins** are 4th-c. pseudonymous.

## Role in the tradition
**Apostolic Fathers**: foundational figure of the corpus. **Roman bishopric**: the first Roman ecclesiastical writer whose work survives; pivotal for Roman primacy historiography. **Catholic tradition**: Pope Clement I, martyr (the martyrdom legend — exiled to the Crimea, tied to an anchor and drowned — is late and historically unsupported).

## Disputes
- **Monoepiscopal status.** 1 Clement does not present its author as *the* singular bishop; the picture of a college of presbyter-bishops fits late-1st-c. Rome better.
- **Martyrdom.** No early attestation; late legend.
- **Authorship attribution.** Accepted by Eusebius onward but is itself a 2nd-c. attribution.
