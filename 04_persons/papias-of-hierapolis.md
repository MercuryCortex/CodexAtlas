# Papias of Hierapolis

## Identity
Bishop of Hierapolis in Phrygia (modern Pamukkale, Turkey, just north of Laodicea), early-2nd-century Christian writer whose lost five-book work *Exposition of the Sayings of the Lord* survives in some 25 fragments preserved especially in Eusebius's *Ecclesiastical History* 3.39. Papias is critical for two reasons: (a) he preserves the **earliest tradition about Gospel origins** ("Mark, the interpreter of Peter..."; "Matthew compiled the *logia* in the Hebrew language..."); (b) he distinguishes **John the Elder** from **John the Apostle**, a critical data point in the Johannine-authorship debates.

## Historical evidence
Lost work, surviving only in citations. The principal preserver is **Eusebius** (HE 3.39), who finds Papias's millenarianism distasteful and editorializes — but who quotes the prologue at length:

> "I shall not hesitate to set down for you, along with my interpretations, anything that I once well learned from the elders and well memorized, confidently asserting their truth. For unlike most people I delighted not in those that have a great deal to say but in those that teach the truth, nor in those that recount commandments foreign to ours but in those that recount the commandments handed down by the Lord from the faith and arrived from the truth itself..."

This passage is the foundation document of early-Christian preference for **oral testimony from named witnesses** over written compilation.

## Works
*Exposition of the Sayings of the Lord* — lost; ~25 fragments preserved. Best collection: Ehrman's Loeb edition vol. 2.

## Role in the tradition
- **Gospel-origin testimony**: the foundation of traditional attributions to Mark (Petrine interpreter) and Matthew (Hebrew sayings).
- **Two-Johns problem**: his distinction between John-the-apostle and John-the-Elder (HE 3.39) is foundational to later Johannine-authorship debates (Dionysius of Alexandria already exploited it to dissociate Revelation from the apostle).
- **Millenarianism**: Papias was a millenarian (vivid descriptions of vines bearing 10,000 branches in the messianic age) — embarrassing to later proto-orthodox tradition. Eusebius calls him "a man of very limited intelligence."

## Disputes
- **Date of composition.** Mainstream: ~+110/+135. Some scholars argue earlier (~+95–110, making him a near-contemporary of the apostolic age).
- **Reliability of his oral-tradition reports.** Bauckham (2017) takes them very seriously; Ehrman and many critics treat them as legendary.
- **Mark-as-Petrine-interpreter.** Defended by Bauckham; questioned by Ehrman.
- **Two Johns.** Real distinction in Papias or Eusebian editorial fiction? Critical scholarship is divided.
