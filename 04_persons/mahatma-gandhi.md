# Mahatma Gandhi

**Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi** (1869–1948) — known as *Mahatma* ("Great Soul") — was the primary leader of the Indian independence movement against British colonial rule and the inventor of **satyagraha** ("truth-force" or "soul-force"), the method of nonviolent resistance that became the template for 20th-century anti-colonial and civil rights movements worldwide.

Gandhi's religious philosophy was a syncretic construction that drew simultaneously from:
- **Hinduism** (*Bhagavad Gītā* as his "spiritual dictionary," *ahimsa* as cosmic law, *Ram-Rajya* as the eschatological ideal)
- **Jainism** — *ahimsa* (non-violence) and *anekāntavāda* (many-sidedness of truth) absorbed through his Gujarati merchant community
- **Tolstoy** — *The Kingdom of God is Within You* (1894); Gandhi corresponded with Tolstoy; Tolstoy's Christian anarchism confirmed Gandhi's anti-statism
- **Thoreau** — *Civil Disobedience* (1849); Gandhi encountered it in South Africa
- **Sermon on the Mount** — Gandhi read Matthew 5–7 as the clearest expression of the moral law he already knew from Hindu and Jain sources

His doctrine of *satyagraha* — the idea that suffering willingly undertaken for truth has a force that changes opponents rather than defeating them — is the most influential political theology of the 20th century. It directly shaped:
- **Martin Luther King Jr.** — explicitly modeled the Civil Rights Movement on Gandhi; visited India 1959; called satyagraha the "only logical and moral method open to oppressed people"
- **Nelson Mandela** (early ANC period) — before the turn to armed struggle
- **Cesar Chavez** — United Farm Workers boycotts
- **The Dalai Lama** — Tibetan nonviolent resistance strategy

## Gandhi vs. Ambedkar: the unresolved theological-political debate

Gandhi's approach to untouchability — the "Harijan" ("children of God") campaign, reform from within Hinduism, changing hearts — was directly contested by [[ambedkar|Ambedkar]], who saw it as paternalistic preservation of a system whose theological foundations (karma, varna) made reform impossible. Their 1932 Poona Pact negotiation (over separate electoral representation for Dalits) forced Ambedkar to choose between his political demands and Gandhi's life; he conceded under duress. Ambedkar's conversion to Buddhism in 1956 was the final verdict on that debate: if Hinduism cannot be reformed from within, exit it.

## Gandhi's assassination as theology

Gandhi was assassinated on January 30, 1948, by Nathuram Godse, a member of the RSS (Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh) who argued that Gandhi's insistence on Hindu-Muslim unity was a betrayal of Hindu civilization. The assassination embeds the fundamental tension of South Asian religious politics — between Gandhi's syncretic universalism and Hindu nationalist exclusivism — into the foundation myth of the Indian Republic.
