# Celsus

## Identity
A Middle-Platonist philosopher (Roman period) whose **Alēthēs Logos** ("True Word" / "True Doctrine," ~+178) is the **first known systematic philosophical critique of Christianity** from a learned pagan perspective. The work is **lost in its original form** but reconstructible to about 90% from [[origen]]'s line-by-line refutation in ***Contra Celsum*** (~+248). Celsus's identity is uncertain — Origen tentatively associates him with **Celsus the Epicurean** (a friend of Lucian of Samosata, addressee of Lucian's *Alexander*), but Celsus's actual position is firmly Platonist, not Epicurean. Most likely a different Celsus.

## Historical evidence
Origen's *Contra Celsum* preserves the text in fragments. Origen's procedure — long verbatim quotations followed by detailed refutation — is unusually friendly to reconstruction. Hoffmann 1987 attempts a reconstructed continuous text.

## Works
**Alēthēs Logos** — Celsus's central arguments:
- Christianity is a **derivative and degraded form** of older traditions — Jewish, Egyptian, Greek philosophical;
- Jesus was an illegitimate child of a Roman soldier (Panthera) and Mary; he learned magic in Egypt; he was no son of God;
- The Christian rejection of pagan custom (the *patrios nomos*) is morally and politically destructive;
- The Christian conception of God as concerned for human beings *specifically* is anthropocentric vanity;
- Christianity recruits among slaves, women, and the uneducated — a sociological-class slur;
- Resurrection is philosophically impossible (the dead body has perished);
- The intra-Christian doctrinal wars demonstrate that the tradition is incoherent.

## Role in the tradition
- **The intellectual standard of anti-Christian polemic** until Porphyry (~+270) supersedes him.
- **Origen's interlocutor**: Origen takes Celsus very seriously — *Contra Celsum* is 8 long books — making Celsus by accident one of the **most-quoted pagan philosophers** in late antiquity.
- **Sociological observation**: Celsus's class-and-gender analysis of Christianity (it attracts the marginalized) is historically valuable, not just polemic.
- **Jewish source-use**: Celsus appears to have drawn on a Jewish anti-Christian source (the "Jew" character in the work's opening), providing indirect evidence for late-2nd-c. Jewish anti-Christian polemic.

## Disputes
- **Identity**: probably *not* the Epicurean Celsus.
- **Date**: ~+177/+180 — Marcus Aurelius's later reign.
- **Reliability of Origen's quotations**: very high — verbatim where possible.
- **Panthera tradition**: also in [[babylonian-talmud]] and Tertullian's *De spect.* 30, suggesting an older Jewish anti-Christian counter-narrative that Celsus has picked up.
