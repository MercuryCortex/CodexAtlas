# Pythagoras

## Identity
Pythagoras of Samos (~-570/-495) is the founding figure of Pythagoreanism — a religious-philosophical community first established at Croton in Magna Graecia (~-530) that combined ascetic communal discipline, doctrines of soul-transmigration and purification, mathematical study of number and proportion, and political activism in the cities of southern Italy. Pythagoras emigrated from Samos to Croton in the wake of the tyranny of Polycrates (~-535) and there founded a community that took on political authority for several decades until the anti-Pythagorean uprising (~-510) scattered the community. Pythagoras himself probably died in exile at Metapontum.

The historical Pythagoras is one of the most difficult biographical reconstructions in ancient philosophy. He wrote nothing (the early Pythagoreans observed strict oral-secret transmission); the earliest direct testimonium is Heraclitus's hostile fragment (~-500) accusing Pythagoras of "much-learning that does not teach understanding" and of having compiled his "wisdom" from the writings of others. By the time of Aristotle the legend was already accreting heavily. By the late-antique biographies of Porphyry and Iamblichus (~+270/+300), Pythagoras has become a quasi-divine philosopher-sage-magician with miraculous powers — golden thigh, bilocation, conversation with rivers, capacity to remember his previous incarnations — and a wholly imaginary master-curriculum has been assembled. **The methodological crux of modern Pythagoras-scholarship is distinguishing the historical kernel from the centuries of legendary and pseudepigraphic accretion** — which is the project Burkert (1972) executes with definitive rigor.

## Historical evidence
- **Earliest references (5th c. BCE):** Heraclitus fr. 40, 81, 129 (hostile); Xenophanes fr. 7 (mocking Pythagoras's claim to recognize a friend's voice in a beaten puppy — earliest secure testimonium for the transmigration doctrine); Empedocles fr. 129 (admiring, attributing immense knowledge); Ion of Chios; Herodotus 2.81, 4.95.
- **4th c. BCE:** Plato's allusions (Pythagoras named only once at *Rep.* 600b but Pythagorean influence pervasive); Aristotle's lost *On the Pythagoreans* (extensive fragments preserved by later sources, esp. Aristoxenus and Dicaearchus, who attended Aristotle's school).
- **Late-antique biographies:** Diogenes Laertius VIII (~+220), Porphyry *Life of Pythagoras* (~+270), Iamblichus *On the Pythagorean Life* (~+300) — these are extensive but late and heavily legendary; they preserve some authentic 4th-century material (especially when citing Aristoxenus) alongside extensive accretion.
- **Pythagorean fragments:** Philolaus (~-470/-385) — the earliest Pythagorean to publish, his fragments are extensively studied (Huffman 1993); Archytas (~-435/-347) — mathematician-statesman of Tarentum, fragments edited by Huffman 2005. These give us access to mid-5th-and-4th-century Pythagorean doctrine independent of the biographical legend of Pythagoras himself.

## Works
None by Pythagoras. The works pseudepigraphically attributed to him (and to Philolaus, Archytas, Ocellus Lucanus, Timaeus of Locri, and other early Pythagoreans) by Hellenistic and late-antique Neo-Pythagoreans constitute a vast body of pseudepigrapha — the so-called *Pythagorean Pseudepigrapha* studied by Thesleff (1965). The most influential of these in later tradition is the *Golden Verses* (~+300), a 71-line hexameter ethical-mystical compendium that circulated widely in the late Roman, Byzantine, Islamic, and Renaissance worlds.

## Role in the tradition

### Pythagorean community at Croton
A religious-philosophical communal order with initiatory grades (akousmatikoi / mathēmatikoi — those who heard / those who learned), strict discipline (silence, vegetarianism, daily examination of conscience), and substantial political influence in Croton and other Greek cities of southern Italy. The community survived (after the -510 uprising) as a scattered network into the 4th century BCE and revived periodically in the Neo-Pythagorean movement of the 1st century BCE onward.

### Doctrines
The early Pythagorean doctrines (those that can be attributed to Pythagoras or the first-generation community with reasonable security) include:
- **Transmigration of souls (metempsychosis).** The soul is immortal and passes through successive incarnations in humans, animals, and possibly plants. This is the most securely attested early Pythagorean doctrine — Xenophanes already mocks it ~-540. The vault credits Pythagoras as the originator of [[reincarnation]] *as a doctrine in Greek philosophical discourse* (per the tradition), with the caveat that (a) Pythagoras left no writings; (b) the doctrine likely entered the Greek world from Orphic and possibly Indian channels, with Pythagoras as the first attested Greek teacher to formalize and propagate it.
- **Salvation through purification (katharsis).** Specific dietary, ritual, and behavioral disciplines purify the soul for better incarnation and ultimate release. Vegetarianism, abstention from beans, ritual purity, music as soul-attunement.
- **Mathematics and proportion.** That musical harmony reflects numerical ratios was certainly an early discovery (the octave 2:1, fifth 3:2, fourth 4:3 ratios on the monochord). Whether the more elaborate "number-is-the-substance-of-everything" cosmology is original to Pythagoras (so the tradition) or a later 5th-century development by Philolaus-and-school is the major Burkertian question.
- **Akousmata.** Cryptic ritual-ethical sayings ("do not stir the fire with a knife," "do not eat the heart," "abstain from beans") preserved as the practical-religious rule of the community.

### Influence
Pythagoras's influence on the subsequent tradition is enormous, but mostly mediated:
- → **[[plato]]:** Plato spent time in Magna Graecia and (per the ancient biographical tradition) studied with Archytas; the Pythagorean themes in the *Phaedo* (immortality of soul, transmigration, purification), the *Republic* (myth of Er, harmony-of-the-spheres at 617b), and the *Timaeus* (mathematical cosmology, world-soul) are extensive and explicit.
- → **Aristotle:** wrote (lost) *On the Pythagoreans*; the *Metaphysics* contains extensive Pythagorean-critique material.
- → **Neo-Pythagoreanism (1st c. BCE onward):** Nigidius Figulus at Rome, Apollonius of Tyana, Nicomachus of Gerasa, [[numenius-of-apamea]]; merges with Middle Platonism.
- → **Neoplatonism:** Plotinus, Porphyry, Iamblichus (whose *On the Pythagorean Life* is the most extensive ancient biography), Proclus — all Neoplatonists treat Pythagoras as a senior authority alongside Plato.
- → **Christian and Islamic reception:** the *Golden Verses* circulate extensively; the Pythagorean theorem becomes a basic school text; Pythagorean numerology feeds into Christian-Hermetic numerology and the medieval Latin *quadrivium* (arithmetic, geometry, music, astronomy).

The relationship with Orphism (see [[orpheus]]) is intimate: shared transmigration, purification, vegetarianism. Whether Pythagoreanism is a "philosophical" successor to "religious" Orphism (so the older view), or whether the two are parallel currents with shared participants and partial inter-influence (so Burkert and West), or whether the doctrines were absorbed into Pythagoreanism from Orphism (Burkert's tendency) is debated.

## Disputes
- **Historicity vs. legend.** Some 19th-century scholarship (E.L. Schwartz) was so radically skeptical as to deny Pythagoras's historicity entirely. Modern consensus (Burkert, Huffman, Riedweg) treats Pythagoras as a real 6th-century-BCE figure but most biographical detail as legendary.
- **Pythagoras as religious founder vs. mathematician-philosopher.** Burkert (1972) argues the historical Pythagoras was primarily a religious-charismatic founder; the mathematical-scientific Pythagoras is a 4th-century Aristotelian-Academic construction. This has not been universally accepted; Huffman, Riedweg, and others reserve more space for genuine early Pythagorean mathematical interest.
- **The Pythagorean theorem.** That Pythagoras "discovered" or first proved the theorem is essentially a late-antique legend; the geometric proposition was known in Babylonian and Egyptian mathematics much earlier; whether the first formal Greek proof belongs to Pythagoras, Philolaus, or some other early Pythagorean is unknown.

## Cross-Tradition MASSIVE WIN

### I. Metempsychosis / Samsara — Greek-Indian convergence before Alexander

The Pythagorean soul-transmigration doctrine (attested c. 540 BCE by Xenophanes) and the Upanishadic/Buddhist samsara doctrine (attested c. 700–500 BCE, Brihadaranyaka Upanishad, early Buddhism) are structurally identical and temporally overlapping. The Greek tradition itself — Iamblichus, Porphyry — claims Pythagoras traveled to Persia and India. If true, this is the **first documented Greek-Indian intellectual contact**, 200 years before Alexander's campaign. If parallel, it is the most striking independent convergence in ancient philosophy. The [[reincarnation]] theme is the hinge between these two civilizations. No other Greek thinker came close to this position — it is structurally isolated in the Greek world and structurally native to the Indian world.

### II. Number = Substance of Reality (three traditions, possible chain)

Pythagorean "number is the arche of all things" (Aristotle's report, *Metaphysics* 985b) + Kabbalistic sefirot as numerical substrate of creation ([[alphabet-sefer-yetzirah]]) + Islamic ilm al-huruf ([[alphabet-ilm-al-huruf]], letters/numbers as divine blueprints). All three treat numbers as intrinsically constitutive of reality rather than descriptive of it. The Pythagorean → Neoplatonic → Kabbalistic chain is documentable: medieval Kabbalists in Provence and Spain (11th–13th c. CE) had direct access to Neoplatonic texts via Arabic translations. The Pythagorean decad (1+2+3+4=10) and the Kabbalistic ten sefirot are not only numerically identical — they are philosophically identical: ten is the perfect number that encodes all of reality. The Sefer Yetzirah (3rd–6th c. CE) explicitly uses Pythagorean-language "paths of wisdom" for its ten divine numbers.

### III. Vegetarianism from Soul-in-Animals (four traditions, one conclusion)

Pythagoreanism is uniquely isolated in the Greek world for prohibiting meat-eating. The metaphysical basis — you might eat a former human soul — is structurally identical to Jain ahimsa's basis (animals contain jivas/souls of equal standing), to Buddhist karma-based non-harm ([[moral-buddhist-five-precepts]]), and to Hindu brahmin vegetarianism. Four traditions arrive at the same practical conclusion through structurally identical metaphysical reasoning: **animals carry souls that matter morally**. The Pythagorean community is the Greek outlier that maps onto the Indian mainstream. Whether this reflects Indian contact, Egyptian influence (Herodotus 2.81 links Pythagorean purity laws to Egyptian ones), or independent convergence is the oldest unresolved question in comparative religion.

### IV. The Akousmatikoi/Mathēmatikoi Split — Oldest Esoteric/Exoteric Institutional Structure

The Pythagorean community's division between those who receive only practical-ritual rules (akousmatikoi — the "listeners") and those who receive the full mathematical-philosophical curriculum (mathēmatikoi — the "learners") is the **oldest documented institutional esoteric/exoteric split**. The same two-tier architecture appears in every tradition with "inner teachings":
- Gnostic pneumatic/psychic/hylic (Valentinus, 2nd c. CE)
- Sufi zahir/batin (outer/inner, al-Ghazali, 11th c. CE)
- Kabbalistic nigleh/nistar (revealed/concealed, Zohar, 13th c. CE)
- Hindu jnana marga vs. bhakti marga (knowledge path vs. devotion path)
- Tibetan vajrayana/sutrayana (esoteric/exoteric Buddhist vehicle)

Every tradition that generates "inner teachings" independently invents this structure. Pythagoreanism is the oldest attested case. The structure itself — not its content — appears to be a cognitive universal of organized religious-intellectual communities.

### V. Music of the Spheres — Cross-Tradition Cosmological Convergence

Pythagoras's claim that planetary distances are in the same ratios as musical intervals (harmonia sphairon, attested through Aristotle's reports) is not metaphor but a literal cosmological thesis. The structurally identical claim appears in:
- **Sama Veda theology** ([[music-sama-veda]]): precise chant ratios are cosmologically operative — the wrong ratio breaks the correspondence between ritual and cosmos
- **Al-Kindi's music therapy** (9th c. CE, Islamic Golden Age): musical ratios govern bodily and cosmic health through the same proportional relationships
- **Kepler's Harmonices Mundi** (1619 CE): planetary orbital velocities produce a chord identifiable through the musical ratios — an explicit revival of Pythagorean harmonia sphairon, 2,100 years later

The transmission chain: Pythagoras → Plato (*Timaeus* world-soul scale) → Ptolemy → Boethius (*De Institutione Musica*) → medieval quadrivium → Islamic falsafa (al-Farabi's *Kitab al-Musiqa*) → Kepler. The [[music-pythagorean-harmony]] node is the musical expression; the [[mathematics-pythagorean-sacred]] node is the mathematical-theological expression. Both descend from the same Croton community.
