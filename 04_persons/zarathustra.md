# Zarathustra

## Identity
Zarathustra (Greek: Zoroaster; New Persian: Zardošt; Avestan: Zaraθuštra) is the founding prophet of Zoroastrianism — a Bactrian or eastern Iranian *zaotar*-priest who, by his own first-person account in the Gathas, experienced direct visionary commissioning by Ahura Mazda. Trained in the inherited Indo-Iranian sacrificial tradition, he turned against the *daēva*-cults of his time, proclaimed Ahura Mazda as the one supreme lord, and established the cosmic-moral opposition of Truth (*aša*) vs. the Lie (*druj*) as the structuring principle of existence. His 17 surviving hymns — the Gathas — constitute the oldest first-person prophetic voice in Iranian history and one of the oldest in world religious literature.

## The dates dispute — the central problem of Zarathustra scholarship

The date of Zarathustra is **one of the most contested problems in the history of religion**, with two major scholarly camps separated by roughly six centuries.

### The "early" / linguistic chronology (~1500–1000 BCE)
The dominant current view among philologists and Iranists. The primary argument is **linguistic**: the Old Avestan of the Gathas is an archaic dialect comparable in its stage of development to the oldest layer of the *Rigveda* (~1500–1200 BCE). Both texts exhibit the same archaic Indo-Iranian poetic vocabulary, metrical structures, and grammatical forms — forms that had visibly evolved into the "younger" stages by the time of the Younger Avesta. Since the Vedic comparison provides a rough independent chronological anchor, the Gathas are most plausibly dated to approximately the same era as the early Vedic hymns: somewhere in the range **1500–1000 BCE**, with 1200–1000 BCE a common consensus.

Principal defenders: **Mary Boyce** (*A History of Zoroastrianism* vol. I, 1975 — the definitive modern treatment); **Prods Oktor Skjærvø** (Yale, *Encyclopaedia Iranica* "Avestan"); **Jean Kellens** (Collège de France); **Martin Schwartz** (Berkeley). Boyce's formulation: Old Avestan exhibits *archaic linguistic features* shared with the earliest Vedic stratum that had disappeared from the Younger Avestan and Young Vedic, making any date significantly after 1000 BCE untenable on purely philological grounds.

The social world depicted in the Gathas reinforces this dating: a pre-state pastoralist society, no cities or kings named, cattle and settled "houses" (*nmāna*) the economic frame — consistent with eastern Iranian Bronze Age pastoralism, not the Achaemenid imperial world.

### The "traditional" / late chronology (~628–551 BCE)
The date transmitted by the Zoroastrian tradition itself and reported by Greek sources. The Zoroastrian Bundahishn and related Pahlavi texts state that Zarathustra lived "258 years before Alexander" — which, computed from Alexander's conquest of Persia in 330 BCE, gives approximately **588 BCE** for Zarathustra's birth. According to traditional calculations, he lived 77 years, yielding lifespan dates of roughly **628–551 BCE** (with some variants: 630–553 or 618–541 BCE depending on which life event the "258 years" counts from).

This late date would make Zarathustra a near-contemporary of Cyrus the Great and an "Axial Age" figure in Karl Jaspers's sense — contemporary with early Greek philosophy, Confucius, and the Hebrew prophets.

Principal defenders: **W.B. Henning** (1951; one of the most important Iranists of the 20th century); **Gherardo Gnoli** (*Zoroaster in History*, 2000); **Richard Frye** (Harvard).

**Why most current scholars reject the late date:** The "258 years before Alexander" tradition is almost certainly a **back-projection** — a conventional religious number anchoring the tradition to the Achaemenid period for theological and political reasons. The linguistic argument is independent of this tradition and extremely hard to refute on its own terms. The consensus of philologists (Boyce, Skjærvø, Kellens, Schwartz) is that the gap between Old and Young Avestan "requires several, perhaps many, centuries" (Kellens, *Encyclopaedia Iranica* "Gāthās") — a gap incompatible with a ~600 BCE date for the Gathas if the Younger Avesta was already in use by the Achaemenid period.

**The vault carries -1200/-1000** following Boyce's linguistic dating. The late traditional date is noted as a live scholarly minority position.

## The Gathas as personal testimony — the direct-voice evidence

The Gathas are extraordinary as historical evidence because **Zarathustra speaks in the first person throughout**. Unlike most ancient religious founders whose voices are reconstructed through later tradition, the Gathas give us something close to primary testimony:

- **His priestly identity:** He identifies himself as a *zaotar* (sacrificial priest) by training — the ritual functionary who tends and interprets the sacred fire. The *zaotar* function is cognate with the Vedic *hotṛ* priest.
- **His vision:** In Yasna 43–44, Zarathustra describes his direct encounter with Ahura Mazda, structured as a progressive recognition — "As the holy one I recognized Thee, O Mazda Ahura!" — constituting his prophetic call.
- **His opposition:** The *karapans* (priests of the *daēva*-cults, the old warrior-deities the Gathas condemn) and the *kavi* (chieftains who patronized them) appear explicitly as his enemies — men who "by their rule destroy existence" (Yasna 32.12). This opposition tells us something concrete: Zarathustra was a reformer within a living religious conflict, not an abstract philosopher.
- **His patron:** Yasna 28, 51, and 53 name *Vishtaspa* (not the Achaemenid Vishtaspa/Hystaspes but a local eastern Iranian king) as the patron who converted and gave him protection — the turning-point of his mission. His daughter **Pouruchista**'s marriage to one of Vishtaspa's nobles is recorded in Yasna 53, the "wedding hymn."
- **His loneliness and pleas:** In Yasna 29, Zarathustra describes himself as crying out to Ahura Mazda — "To what land shall I flee? Where shall I go to seek refuge?" — a lament with no parallel for personal intensity among the world's early religious founders.
- **His cosmological questioning:** Yasna 44 — the "question hymn" — consists entirely of questions to Ahura Mazda: "Who set the sun and stars on their paths? Who holds the earth from below? Who made the waters and the plants?" This is proto-philosophical cosmological inquiry embedded in hymn form, constituting the oldest sustained philosophical questioning in Iranian literature.

This intimate personal voice is the most powerful argument for Zarathustra as a single historical individual — a position held by Boyce, Skjærvø, and the mainstream of scholarship, though Molé (in the 1960s) and Kellens (more recently) have raised questions about whether the Gathas have a single author.

## Social context — a reform from within

The Gathas are set in a **pre-state pastoralist society** of eastern Iran, probably Bactria or Sogdiana. Their social world: cattle herders, settled "houses" and clans, a priestly class, raiding warfare. Zarathustra defends the cattle-owning communities against raiding warriors whose religion — the *daēva*-cult, with its martial deities headed by Indra's Iranian cognate — sanctified violence. His theology is thus not just spiritual but **politically embedded**: Ahura Mazda is the patron of settled righteousness; the *daēvas* are patrons of raiding disorder.

**The inversion of the Indo-Iranian deva/asura distinction** is crucial: in Vedic India, *devas* (cognate Avestan *daēvas*) are the good gods; *asuras* (cognate Avestan *ahuras*) become the demons. In Zarathustra's reform, precisely the reverse: the *daēvas* are denounced as demonic, and the *ahuras* — above all Ahura Mazda — are exalted as the true divine. This theological inversion is the clearest evidence that Zoroastrianism is a **reform** of the shared Indo-Iranian religion rather than an independent creation.

## The Nietzsche misappropriation — what "Thus Spoke Zarathustra" has to do with Zarathustra

Friedrich Nietzsche's *Also Sprach Zarathustra* (1883–85) is one of the most famous philosophical works in Western literature — and one of the most spectacular misfires of source material in intellectual history.

**Why Nietzsche chose Zarathustra:** Nietzsche explains his choice in *Ecce Homo* (1888), in the chapter "Why I Am a Destiny." His reasoning is deliberate and inverted: the historical Zarathustra was, in Nietzsche's view, the **inventor of morality** — specifically, the first thinker to transform the struggle between good and evil into the "essential wheel in the working of things," to translate morality into metaphysics. This made the historical Zarathustra responsible for what Nietzsche considered the most catastrophic error in human thought. Therefore, Nietzsche chose Zarathustra as his fictional prophet-figure on the logic that *the person who created morality is uniquely suited to overcome it*:

> "Zarathustra created this most portentous of all errors — morality; therefore he must also be the first to recognize it." — Nietzsche, *Ecce Homo*, "Why I Am a Destiny"

And: "The self-overcoming of morality through truthfulness, the self-overcoming of the moralist into his opposite — into me — that is what the name Zarathustra means in my mouth."

**The actual Zarathustra is Nietzsche's opposite in almost every respect:**
- Nietzsche's Zarathustra preaches the *Übermensch*, the death of God, the eternal recurrence, the will to power — a rejection of transcendent moral order.
- The historical Zarathustra preaches **cosmic moral order** (*aša* / Truth) as the structuring principle of reality; the supreme importance of good thoughts, good words, and good deeds; universal **judgment** of every soul; and eventual universal **renewal** (*frashokereti*) — essentially an intensified, universalized moralism.
- Nietzsche's Zarathustra celebrates individual self-creation beyond good and evil. The historical Zarathustra insists on the irreducible reality of **cosmic good and evil** and the obligation to choose sides.

**The scholarly verdict:** Boyce (1975), Gnoli, and Stausberg (2008) all note this misappropriation. Stausberg's *Zarathustra and Zoroastrianism* (2008) traces the entire Western reception history — including Voltaire's 18th-century use of "Zoroastre" as a paradigm of rational natural religion, Hegel's use of Persian Zoroastrianism as the beginning of the "World Spirit's" historical unfolding (*Lectures on the Philosophy of History*, 1831), and Nietzsche's inversion. What connects these appropriations is that **Western philosophy consistently used a fictional Zarathustra** as a vehicle for its own concerns, while the actual Gathas remained largely unknown to non-specialists until Insler's 1975 critical edition.

**For the vault:** The Nietzsche appropriation is significant as a cross-tradition theme: it documents how a major religious founder's identity was completely overwritten in the Western reception. Nietzsche's Zarathustra functions in Western cultural history as a **shadow-tradition** — a set of ideas attributed to a name that has nothing to do with the actual bearer.

## Hegel's Zoroastrianism — the first moment of World Spirit

Before Nietzsche, Hegel positioned Zoroastrianism as philosophically significant in a different way. In his *Lectures on the Philosophy of History* (~1830), Hegel treats **Persia as the strict beginning of world history** — the first moment when Spirit (Geist) awakens to itself as a principle of light opposed to darkness:

> "The Persian Empire is the first that passes away; it is the first that lets the principle of freedom — the pure light of Spirit — emerge." — Hegel, paraphrase.

For Hegel, Zarathustra's theology of light (Ahura Mazda as pure light) vs. darkness (Angra Mainyu) represents the first **abstract universal principle** in world religion — a move beyond Egyptian particularism and Indian undifferentiated unity toward a properly self-conscious spirituality. Whether or not Hegel's reading is historically accurate, it made Zoroastrianism the philosophical anchor for the beginning of "rational religion" in the German Idealist tradition.

## Historical evidence
Direct first-person voice in the Gathas (Yasna 28–34, 43–46, 47–50, 51, 53) — the strongest evidence for a historical author of any major "Axial Age" prophetic figure. The Gathas name Zarathustra repeatedly, describe his social circumstances (priestly training, persecution, eventual royal patronage by Vishtaspa), and convey a coherent personal theological voice. Linguistic dating of Old Avestan places these hymns at roughly the same era as the early Vedic Sanskrit (~1500–1000 BCE). No archaeological inscription or contemporary record outside the Gathas survives from his lifetime.

## Works
- **Authored:** the 17 Gathas embedded in the Yasna — Yasna 28–34 (Ahunavaiti Gāthā), 43–46 (Ushtavaiti Gāthā), 47–50 (Spenta Mainyu Gāthā), 51 (Vohū Khshathra Gāthā), 53 (Vahishtōishti Gāthā).
- **Attributed (later school products):** some Younger Avestan hymns carry Zarathustra's name but are linguistically and theologically posterior.

## Role in the tradition
Founder, prophet, and exemplar. Zoroastrian liturgy is structured around his hymns; every fire-temple service performs the Yasna with the Gathas at its core. The later Pahlavi tradition (Bundahishn, Denkard, Zardošt-namah) elaborates his miraculous birth, childhood trials, prophetic call, and death. Greek tradition from at least the 5th century BCE recognized him as a great Eastern sage — Plato, Aristotle, Hermippus, and later Hellenistic philosophers cited him, sometimes as a paradigmatic figure of ancient wisdom.

## Disputes
- **Date.** Two camps: linguistic/Boyce camp (~1200–1000 BCE based on Old Avestan = early Vedic comparison); traditional/late date (~628–551 BCE, from "258 years before Alexander" tradition, defended by Gnoli). Current philological majority: early date. We carry -1200/-1000.
- **Single historical author.** Mainstream: yes, a single visionary author. Molé (1963) and Kellens (2000s) have raised questions; the personal voice of the hymns remains decisive for most.
- **Region.** Bactria / Sogdiana / Khwarezm — all plausible eastern Iranian locales. Bactria (roughly modern Afghanistan/Uzbekistan) is most commonly argued.
