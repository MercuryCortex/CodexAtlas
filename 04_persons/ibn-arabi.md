# Ibn Arabi

## Identity

**Muḥyī al-Dīn Ibn ʿArabī (560/1165 – 638/1240)** — *al-Shaykh al-Akbar*, "the Greatest Master," from which his school takes the name **Akbarī** — is the most systematically ambitious metaphysician Sufism produced, and one of the largest authors in the history of any religious literature. He wrote somewhere above three hundred authenticated works, including the **560-chapter *al-Futūḥāt al-Makkiyya*** — the longest sustained work of speculative mysticism ever composed — and the short, dense **[[phase-5-021-ibn-arabi-fusus-al-hikam|*Fuṣūṣ al-Ḥikam*]]**, which generated a commentary tradition running for seven centuries across Arabic, Persian, Ottoman Turkish, Urdu, and Malay.

Born at **Murcia** in 1165 into an Arab family of the Ṭāʾī, he grew up in **Seville** under Almohad rule, where his father served the administration. At about twenty he entered a retreat (*khalwa*) from which he emerged with the first of the "openings" (*futūḥāt*) that structure his entire self-account; he names **Jesus** as his first teacher on the path. He studied under many masters of the Andalusi and Maghribi Sufi milieu — including **two women**, Fāṭima bint Ibn al-Muthannā of Córdoba and Shams Umm al-Fuqarāʾ of Marchena, whose teaching he records with great respect in the *Rūḥ al-Quds*.

He left the West permanently in 1200 and never returned: Cairo, Jerusalem, and then **Mecca in 1202**, where he began the *Futūḥāt* and met the Persian shaykh Makīn al-Dīn's daughter **Niẓām**, the addressee of the *Tarjumān al-Ashwāq*. Then Anatolia — **Konya**, where the Seljuk sultan Kaykāʾūs I patronized him and where he married the widowed mother of **Ṣadr al-Dīn al-Qūnawī**, who became his stepson and the systematizer of his thought — then Baghdad, Aleppo, and Mosul, where he received the *khirqa khaḍiriyya*, the initiatic cloak associated with [[al-khidr|al-Khiḍr]]. From **1223 he settled in Damascus** under Ayyūbid protection, completed the second recension of the *Futūḥāt* in 1238, and died there in November 1240. He is buried at Ṣāliḥiyya on the slope of Mount Qāsiyūn; the Ottoman sultan Selim I built the mosque and mausoleum over the grave in 1518 — a state act of rehabilitation after three centuries of controversy.

## Historical evidence

Unusually rich. The *Futūḥāt* contains a very large autobiographical stratum — visions dated and located, teachers named, cities and patrons identified. In 1234 he wrote out a certificate (*ijāza*) of his own works for the Ayyūbid ruler al-Malik al-Muẓaffar of Ḥamā, listing some **290 titles**, which gives a dated authorial catalogue almost no medieval writer supplies. Osman Yahia's *Histoire et classification de l'œuvre d'Ibn ʿArabī* (1964) catalogued around 850 attributed titles, of which a large fraction is pseudepigraphic; Addas 1993 works from the *ijāza* and the dated manuscripts. Student *ijāzas* and the Qūnawī line document the transmission directly.

## Works

- **[[phase-5-021-ibn-arabi-fusus-al-hikam|*Fuṣūṣ al-Ḥikam* (The Bezels of Wisdom, 1229)]]** — twenty-seven chapters, one for each of twenty-seven prophets from Adam to Muhammad, each treating the particular "wisdom" of which that prophet is the setting or bezel (*faṣṣ*). He states that the Prophet handed him the book in a vision at Damascus. It is the most commented Sufi text in Islamic history and the single most contested.
- ***al-Futūḥāt al-Makkiyya* (The Meccan Openings)** — 560 chapters begun at Mecca in 1202 and revised until 1238: cosmology, prophetology, sainthood, ritual law, letter-symbolism, eschatology, and the metaphysics of the divine names, structured throughout — as Chodkiewicz demonstrated — as an exegesis of the Qurʾān.
- ***Tarjumān al-Ashwāq* (1215)** — sixty-one love poems, followed by his own commentary (*Dhakhāʾir al-Aʿlāq*) written when jurists in Aleppo objected that the eroticism was literal. The commentary is his defence that the beloved is a divine reality.
- ***Rūḥ al-Quds*** and ***al-Durrat al-Fākhira*** — memoirs of the Andalusi and Maghribi Sufis of his youth; a primary source for western Islamic spirituality that would otherwise be lost.
- ***Kitāb al-Isrāʾ*** — the Miʿrāj re-read as the stage-by-stage ascent of the knower; see [[merkabah-miraj-ascent-chain]].

## The thought

**Waḥdat al-wujūd — the oneness of being.** Only *wujūd* — being, existence — is real, and it has exactly one referent: **al-Ḥaqq**, the Real. The cosmos is not a second thing added to God; it is the field of God's **self-disclosure** (*tajallī*), an unrepeating succession of forms in which the divine names manifest. Ibn ʿArabī's own formula for the relation of any creature to God is **"He / not He"** (*huwa lā huwa*). This is not pantheism, which he explicitly rejects — God is not the sum of the cosmos — and it is not the standard Ashʿarite picture of a creator ontologically separate from creation. The term *waḥdat al-wujūd* is not his: it was fixed by al-Qūnawī's school and then weaponized by his opponents. See [[wahdat-al-wujud]] and [[philosophy-akbarian-ibn-arabi]].

**The immutable entities (*al-aʿyān al-thābita*).** Every creature has an essence eternally known to God but never itself existent — a "fixed entity" that receives existence without being altered by it. This lets Ibn ʿArabī hold divine determination and creaturely particularity together: God does not impose on things what they are not; things are, eternally, what they are, and God gives them being.

**The divine names.** Creation is the manifestation of the names, and each existent is the locus of a particular configuration of them — its own *rabb*, its particular Lord. Worship is therefore always worship of a God configured by the worshipper's own receptivity, which is why he says that the God of belief differs from believer to believer while the Real does not.

**The imaginal (*khayāl*, *ʿālam al-mithāl*) and the barzakh.** Between pure spirit and gross body there is an intermediate ontological order — real, not merely psychological — in which meanings take sensible form. It is the ground of veridical dream, of vision, of the resurrection body, and of the isthmus (*barzakh*) between any two things. **Henry Corbin's *mundus imaginalis*** is Corbin's rendering of this, and the vehicle by which the concept entered European thought.

**The Perfect Human (*al-insān al-kāmil*).** The complete theophany, in whom all divine names are simultaneously manifest and through whom the cosmos is sustained — realized paradigmatically in the **Muhammadan Reality** (*al-ḥaqīqa al-muḥammadiyya*; see [[nur-muhammadi]]), the first created principle and the archetype of every prophet and saint.

**Sainthood and the Seal.** *Walāya* (sainthood) has a hierarchy with a fixed constitution — the poles, the substitutes, the solitaries — and a terminus: Ibn ʿArabī claims for himself the **Seal of Muhammadan Sainthood**, an office parallel to but lower than Muhammad's seal of prophecy. Chodkiewicz 1993 is the definitive treatment; this claim, more than the metaphysics, is what made him intolerable to his critics.

## Transmission and reception

**In the Islamic world.** **Ṣadr al-Dīn al-Qūnawī** (d. 1274) turned the visionary corpus into a technical philosophy; the *Fuṣūṣ* commentary tradition — al-Jandī, ʿAbd al-Razzāq al-Kāshānī (d. c. 1330), Dāʾūd al-Qayṣarī (d. 1350) — made it teachable; ʿAbd al-Karīm al-Jīlī's *al-Insān al-Kāmil* systematized the Perfect Human; and Jāmī (d. 1492) carried it into Persian poetry and prose. Downstream it becomes **[[mulla-sadra|Mullā Ṣadrā]]**'s transcendent philosophy in Safavid Iran, the metaphysical idiom of Ottoman Sufism, and — in India — the pole of a great controversy, when **Aḥmad Sirhindī** (d. 1624) proposed *waḥdat al-shuhūd*, unity of witnessing, as a corrective. Against him: [[ibn-taymiyya|Ibn Taymiyya]] and a long line of Sunni polemicists, whose "Ibn ʿArabī" **Knysh 1999** shows to be substantially a constructed image.

**MASSIVE WIN — the European channel is modern, not medieval.** Ibn ʿArabī is habitually described as a source of Western esotericism. The vault's finding is that the **well-documented** channel is twentieth-century and the **medieval** channels are contested — the reverse of the popular picture.

- **T1, documented.** The **Traditionalist / Perennialist school** takes Akbarian metaphysics as its doctrinal core. **[[rene-guenon|René Guénon]]** affiliated to the Shādhiliyya, moved to Cairo in 1930 as ʿAbd al-Wāḥid Yaḥyā, and built his doctrines of the Supreme Identity and the multiple states of being out of it; **Titus Burckhardt** translated the *Fuṣūṣ* into French, **Michel Vâlsan** the *Futūḥāt*, and Schuon and Lings continued the line. Sedgwick 2004 documents all of this. *(Note the tier layering: that Guénon drew on Ibn ʿArabī is T1 history; Guénon's own metaphysical claims are T3 alternative-school per CODEX §IV, and this wire does not endorse them.)*
- **T1, documented.** **Henry Corbin**'s *Creative Imagination in the Ṣūfism of Ibn ʿArabī* (1958/1969) carried the *ʿālam al-mithāl* into the Eranos circle as the *mundus imaginalis*; [[james-hillman|James Hillman]] named Corbin a father of archetypal psychology alongside Jung. Wasserstrom 1999 is the critical history of that channel — and is itself a critique of it, arguing that the Eranos reading systematically detached these materials from law, community, and history.
- **T2, contested.** **[[dante-alighieri|Dante]]**. Asín Palacios argued in 1919 for Islamic ascent-literature sources and in 1931 for Ibn ʿArabī specifically. Cerulli's 1949 *Liber Scale Machometi* discovery vindicated the *general* channel — the miʿrāj text was in Latin at Alfonso X's court in 1264, and Dante's teacher Brunetto Latini was there. The *specific* Ibn ʿArabī dependency is not accepted by mainstream Dante scholarship.
- **T2, contested.** **[[ramon-llull|Ramon Llull]]**. The *dignitates* of the *Ars* are structurally the Islamic divine names; Llull read Arabic; Asín again proposed Ibn ʿArabī. Scholarship accepts the Islamic engagement, not the specific dependency.
- **T3, catalogued as a claim.** Guénon's *L'Ésotérisme de Dante* (1925) makes the *Fedeli d'Amore* an initiatic organization in contact with Islamic esotericism. This is a Traditionalist reading, not established scholarship, and is recorded here as such per CODEX §I.3 (disclosure, not concealment).

## Disputes

- **Was he a pluralist?** The *Tarjumān*'s famous line — his heart, he writes, has become receptive to every form, pasture for gazelles and cloister for monks alike (Nicholson's 1911 rendering) — plus the *Fuṣūṣ* on the idols of Noah's people, made him the patron saint of modern perennialism. **Chittick 1994 and Chodkiewicz 1993 both reject the reading**: Ibn ʿArabī holds that every object of worship receives a genuine divine self-disclosure *and* that the Muhammadan law is the comprehensive revelation that supersedes the others. Both halves are his; taking only the first is the error.
- **Philosopher or not?** He rejects the philosophers' method and is not a *faylasūf*; his vocabulary is nonetheless saturated with Arabic Neoplatonism. Rosenthal 1988 and Chittick 1989 disagree in emphasis about how much is inherited and how much is his own.
- **Corbin's Ibn ʿArabī.** Corbin's reading was decisive for the West and is contested in the field: Chodkiewicz's *An Ocean Without Shore* is in part an argument that the *Futūḥāt* is Qurʾānic exegesis from end to end, and that a reading which brackets scripture and law has replaced its subject with something else.
- **The authenticity problem.** Of ~850 titles attributed to him, a large share is pseudepigraphic. Claims sourced to a minor "Ibn ʿArabī treatise" should be checked against Yahia's catalogue and Addas before being taken as his.
