# Ibn Arabi

## Identity
Muḥyī al-Dīn Ibn ʿArabī (1165–1240) was the supreme Sufi metaphysician of medieval Islam — author of more than 350 works, founder (with his students al-Qūnawī and others) of the Akbarī school of Sufi metaphysics, and architect of the doctrine of *waḥdat al-wujūd* (oneness of being). Born Murcia 1165; spent youth in Seville; first major mystical opening ~1184 (his celebrated encounter with the ancient Sufi shaykhs); traveled across the Maghreb and to Mecca (where the *Futūḥāt* was begun ~1202 in response to a visionary command); subsequently lived in Konya, Mosul, Baghdad, Cairo; settled finally in Damascus, where he died and is buried.

## Historical evidence
Substantial autobiographical material in the *Futūḥāt*; contemporary references; the *Ijāza* documents listing his student-transmissions.

## Works
*Fuṣūṣ al-Ḥikam* (Bezels of Wisdom, ~1230, his final and most-read systematic work); *al-Futūḥāt al-Makkiyya* (Meccan Openings, ~1202–1231, his vast main work); *Tarjumān al-Ashwāq* (Interpreter of Desires — mystical love poetry); *Mishkāt al-Anwār* (a compilation of 101 ḥadīth qudsī); a vast array of shorter mystical, exegetical, and biographical works.

## Role in the tradition
Foundational; *al-Shaykh al-Akbar* — "the Greatest Master." His school dominated Sufi metaphysics across the Persianate, Ottoman, Maghrebi, Indian, and African Islamic worlds; was sharply condemned by Ibn Taymiyya (~1300) and remains the target of Wahhabi and Salafi critique. Influenced Persian Sufism (Rūmī's circle via Ṣadr al-Dīn al-Qūnawī), Mullā Ṣadrā and Safavid philosophy, Ottoman Sufi orders, Indian Naqshbandi-Mujaddidi thought.
