# George Smith (Babylonian cuneiformist)

## Identity
**George Smith** (1840–1876) was a self-taught British Museum cuneiformist whose **3 December 1872 lecture to the Society of Biblical Archaeology** (see [[event-george-smith-flood-tablet-1872]]) was one of the most consequential single discoveries in the history of comparative religion. Smith read, on a fragmentary cuneiform tablet from the Library of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh (excavated 1849–1854 by Austen Henry Layard and Hormuzd Rassam), a **Babylonian flood-narrative remarkably parallel to Genesis 6–9** — the tablet now known as Tablet XI of the Standard Babylonian Gilgamesh Epic, narrating [[utnapishtim]]'s flood-account to Gilgamesh.

The announcement triggered the 19th-century *Babel und Bibel* controversy: for the first time it was demonstrably (not merely speculatively) the case that the Hebrew Bible's foundational narratives had Mesopotamian precursors, and that the cuneiform sources *pre-date* the biblical text. This is the single most consequential moment in the modern critical reception of the Mesopotamian-Hebrew religious-textual relationship.

## Historical evidence

Smith's life is extensively documented:

- **Self-taught Assyriologist** — Smith began at the British Museum as a banknote-engraver-apprentice; taught himself cuneiform through obsessive study of the Layard-Rassam tablet-collection. Hired full-time by the British Museum in 1867 on the strength of his pattern-recognition.
- **The 1872 announcement** — 3 December 1872 lecture at the Society of Biblical Archaeology in London, attended by Prime Minister William Gladstone among other dignitaries. The *Daily Telegraph* offered to fund a Nineveh expedition to find missing fragments — Smith departed 1873.
- **Three Nineveh expeditions** (1873, 1874, 1876) — recovered additional Gilgamesh-flood fragments + Assyrian-historical material + the Chaldean Genesis cosmogony (which Smith published as *The Chaldean Account of the Genesis* 1876).
- **Death** — Smith contracted dysentery at Aleppo en route home from his third expedition; died 19 August 1876 at age 36.

## Works

- **The Chaldean Account of the Genesis** (1876, posthumous) — the foundational popular-scholarly presentation of the Mesopotamian-Hebrew parallels. Reissued in expanded form by Sayce 1880 and remained influential into the 20th century.
- **Assyrian Discoveries** (1875) — Smith's own account of his first Nineveh expedition.
- **Assyrian Eponym Canon** (1875) — chronological scaffolding for Neo-Assyrian history.
- **Numerous technical papers** in the *Transactions of the Society of Biblical Archaeology* (1870s).

## Role in the tradition

Smith's 1872 discovery is comparable in 19th-century religious-cultural impact to the Darwinian controversies of 1859–1870s. The subsequent **Babel und Bibel controversy** (Friedrich Delitzsch's 1902–1904 lectures, supported and then problematized by Kaiser Wilhelm II) institutionalized the comparative-religion question in German Protestant biblical scholarship and reshaped late-19th/early-20th-c. critical biblical studies.

The downstream consequences:

1. **The entire field of biblical-Assyriological comparative study** became possible — Heidel 1949 *The Babylonian Genesis* + *The Gilgamesh Epic and Old Testament Parallels*; Pritchard's ANET 1950 + 1969; Frymer-Kensky 1977 *The Atrahasis Epic and Its Significance for Our Understanding of Genesis 1–9*.
2. **The Documentary Hypothesis** of biblical source-criticism (Wellhausen 1878) was developed in the same decade; the Mesopotamian-textual-priority demonstration provided supporting evidence for the post-exilic redaction theory.
3. **Conservative biblical apologetics** developed responses that continue to the present.

## Disputes

- **Smith's reading accuracy.** Smith's 1872 reading of the flood tablet has been substantially refined by subsequent scholarship (George 2003), but the core identification — that Tablet XI parallels Genesis 6–9 — has held.
- **Was the discovery accidental or intentional?** Smith's autobiographical narrative (the eureka-moment of recognizing the flood narrative on a single afternoon) is the standard story; Damrosch 2007 presents a more nuanced picture of years of pattern-recognition work.
