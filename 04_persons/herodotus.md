# Herodotus of Halicarnassus

## Identity
Herodotus (c. 484 – c. 425 BCE) is the earliest surviving Greek prose historian. His single massive work — the *Histories* in nine books — narrates the Greco-Persian Wars (490, 480–479 BCE) but does so through an enormous ethnographic frame: each people the Persians encounter (Lydians, Egyptians, Scythians, Babylonians, Indians, Libyans, Thracians, etc.) receives its own ethnographic excursus. **Book 2 is the entire ethnography of Egypt** — the earliest extended Greek-language description of Egyptian religion, history, geography, and customs, and the foundational document of Greek-Egyptian intellectual contact.

## Historical evidence
Herodotus is securely historical: he is cited by Thucydides, Aristophanes, Aristotle, Cicero, and many subsequent ancient historians. He was born in Halicarnassus (Caria, in modern Turkey), spent time in Periclean Athens, and ended his life in the pan-Hellenic Athenian colony of Thurii in southern Italy (founded -444). The internal evidence of the *Histories* shows him traveling personally in Egypt (he names sites and reports priestly conversations at Memphis, Heliopolis, Thebes/Karnak), the Black Sea region, and Mesopotamia.

## Works
- ***Histories*** (Ἱστορίαι) — nine books, conventionally named for the nine Muses by Alexandrian editors:
  - Book 1 (Clio) — Lydia, rise of Persia.
  - **Book 2 (Euterpe) — Egypt.** See [[phase-2-028-herodotus-histories-book-2]].
  - Book 3 (Thalia) — Persian conquest of Egypt under Cambyses (525 BCE), early Achaemenid history.
  - Books 4–9 — Scythia, Libya, Ionian revolt, Marathon, Thermopylae, Salamis, Plataea.

## Role in the tradition
For the Codex Atlas mission Herodotus is the **earliest documentary witness** to the Greek interpretation of Egyptian religion. His foundational moves:

1. **Greek-Egyptian theological equation.** In Book 2.42–59 Herodotus systematically identifies Greek gods with Egyptian gods: Zeus = Amun (2.42), Demeter = Isis (2.59), Dionysus = Osiris (2.42, 2.144), Apollo = Horus (2.144, 2.156), Artemis = Bubastis/Bastet (2.137, 2.156), Hermes = Thoth (implicit; explicit later in Plutarch and others), Pan = Mendes (2.46), Heracles = Khonsu/Chons (2.43–44). This is the locus classicus of *interpretatio graeca*, the move that [[plutarch]] systematizes and that flows into [[clement-of-alexandria]] and into Renaissance Hermeticism.

2. **Diffusionist claim.** Herodotus argues (2.49–53) that **the names of nearly all the Greek gods came from Egypt** — that the Greeks learned the gods' names from the Egyptians and that the Pelasgians did not originally name their gods. He extends this to specific cults (the oracle of Dodona, 2.54–57). This is the earliest articulated **Egyptian-priority hypothesis** for Greek religion. Most modern Egyptologists and Hellenists treat it as wrong (Greek religion has independent Mycenaean / Indo-European roots), but the claim is *itself* a load-bearing fact about how Greeks thought about cultural transmission.

3. **Comparative-religion methodology.** Herodotus is the first surviving author to systematically treat religion as something one can compare across cultures and describe ethnographically. The category "[[comparative-religion-method]]" begins here, two and a half millennia before its modern academic form.

He is reread continuously: cited by [[plutarch]] in *De Iside et Osiride* (~+115), by [[diodorus-siculus]] in *Bibliotheca* Bk 1 (~-40), by [[josephus]] (anti-Apion), and is foundational for every later Greek and Roman treatment of Egypt. Modern critical reading begins with German Quellenforschung in the 19th c. and is now dominated by Lloyd's three-volume Brill commentary.

## Disputes
- **Did Herodotus actually visit Egypt?** Largely yes (the dominant view: Lloyd 1975; Moyer 2011), with the caveat that some of his reports clearly come from Greek informants in Egypt or from earlier logographic sources (Hecataeus of Miletus) rather than direct autopsy. Some details are demonstrably wrong about Egyptian geography.
- **Father of History or Father of Lies?** [[plutarch]] wrote an entire essay (*De Herodoti malignitate*) accusing Herodotus of slander against the Greeks. Plutarch's hostility is largely tribal-defensive (defending Boeotians and Corinthians). Modern scholarship has rehabilitated Herodotus's reliability while recognizing his folkloric and rhetorical layers (Pritchett 1993).
- **The Egyptian-origin claim.** Almost certainly wrong in its strong form. Greek religion has Indo-European and Mycenaean roots independent of Egypt. But the *direction of borrowing* between Egyptian, Levantine, and Greek religion in the Late Bronze and Iron Ages is genuinely complex (cf. Burkert 1992 *The Orientalizing Revolution*; West 1997 *The East Face of Helicon*).
