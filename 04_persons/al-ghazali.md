# Al-Ghazali

## Identity

**Abū Ḥāmid Muḥammad ibn Muḥammad al-Ghazālī (traditionally 450/1058 – 505/1111)** — *Ḥujjat al-Islām*, "the Proof of Islam" — is the pivot of post-classical Sunni intellectual history. He is at once the most effective critic the Greek-Arabic philosophical tradition (*falsafa*) ever had, the man who made Aristotelian logic a compulsory instrument of Islamic law and theology, and the author of the book that made Sufi interiority a normal part of Sunni religious life rather than a suspect specialism. The three achievements are usually told as three separate careers. They were one.

Born at **Ṭūs** in Khurasan (near modern Mashhad) to a family of modest means, orphaned young, he was educated at Ṭūs, Jurjān, and finally **Nishapur** under **Imām al-Ḥaramayn al-Juwaynī** (d. 1085), the outstanding Ashʿarite theologian and Shāfiʿī jurist of the age. On Juwaynī's death he attached himself to the itinerant court-camp (*muʿaskar*) of the Seljuk vizier **Niẓām al-Mulk**, and in **1091, aged about thirty-three, was appointed to the chair of the Niẓāmiyya madrasa in Baghdad** — the most prestigious teaching post in the Sunni world.

Four years later he abandoned it. *(Griffel 2009 argues the traditional birth year 450/1058 is a slightly later reconstruction and that c. 1055–56 fits the evidence better; the traditional date is retained in the YAML for continuity with the vault's date fields.)*

## Historical evidence

Documented, and unusually so. ***al-Munqidh min al-Ḍalāl*** *(Deliverance from Error, ~1108)* is a first-person account of an intellectual and spiritual crisis — one of the very few premodern autobiographies of its kind in any tradition, and the source for almost every popular retelling of his life. It must be read as apologia as well as memoir: Griffel 2009 and Garden 2014 both show it is a late, retrospective, purposeful document, written to justify a return to public teaching. Alongside it stand contemporary biographical notices (ʿAbd al-Ghāfir al-Fārisī's account, transmitted by al-Subkī), his own letters (the Persian *Fażāʾil al-anām*), and a corpus of some seventy securely-attributed works whose relative chronology is now well established.

## The crisis of 1095

In the *Munqidh*, Ghazālī describes reaching a state in which he could no longer speak: a physical impediment struck him in the lecture hall, he lost the ability to eat, and the physicians pronounced the condition beyond medicine because its seat was the heart. In **November 1095** he left Baghdad, announcing a pilgrimage, having quietly settled his affairs and provided for his family. He did not return to public teaching for eleven years.

The *Munqidh* frames this as a purely spiritual event — the discovery that certainty is not delivered by *kalām*, by *falsafa*, or by the authoritarian teaching (*taʿlīm*) of the Ismāʿīlīs, but only by the "tasting" (*dhawq*) of the Sufis. **Garden 2014 and Griffel 2009 add the political layer** the *Munqidh* omits: Niẓām al-Mulk had been assassinated in 1092 and Sultan Malikshāh died weeks later; Ghazālī's patronage network had collapsed; his caliph-commissioned polemic against the Ismāʿīlīs had made him a named target of the Nizārī assassins; and the new Seljuk authority in Baghdad was hostile. Neither reading cancels the other. The vault carries both.

He spent the withdrawal in **Damascus** (retreat in the minaret of the Umayyad Mosque), **Jerusalem**, **Hebron**, and on the **hajj** to Mecca and Medina, then returned to Ṭūs, where he established a *madrasa* and *khānqāh* and wrote the **[[phase-5-015-al-ghazali-ihya|*Iḥyāʾ ʿUlūm al-Dīn*]]**. In **1106** the vizier Fakhr al-Mulk — Niẓām al-Mulk's son — persuaded him back to the Niẓāmiyya of Nishapur. He justified the return by the ḥadīth that God sends a **renewer (*mujaddid*)** at the head of each Islamic century, and 500 AH had just begun. He retired again to Ṭūs and died there in **December 1111**.

## Works

- **[[phase-5-015-al-ghazali-ihya|*Iḥyāʾ ʿUlūm al-Dīn* (The Revival of the Religious Sciences)]]** — forty books in four quarters: acts of worship (*ʿibādāt*), norms of daily life (*ʿādāt*), the vices that destroy (*muhlikāt*), and the virtues that save (*munjiyāt*). Its argument is architectural rather than devotional: the outward sciences of law and ritual are incomplete without the inward *science of the heart*, and the two are one discipline, the *ʿilm al-ākhira* — the science of the hereafter. It became one of the most copied and commented books in Islamic history, and Ghazālī produced his own Persian abridgement of it, the *Kīmiyā-yi Saʿādat*.
- **[[phase-5-045-al-ghazali-tahafut-al-falasifa|*Tahāfut al-Falāsifa* (The Incoherence of the Philosophers, ~1095)]]** — twenty discussions against the falāsifa; the first sixteen metaphysical, the last four on natural science. Three theses are declared unbelief; seventeen, innovation. See below.
- ***Maqāṣid al-Falāsifa* (The Aims of the Philosophers)** — a neutral, expository summary of Avicennian logic, metaphysics, and physics, written to establish that he understood what he was about to attack. Its Latin afterlife is the subject of the transmission section below.
- ***al-Iqtiṣād fī al-Iʿtiqād*** — the systematic Ashʿarite creed, contemporaneous with the *Tahāfut*; his most technical work of *kalām*.
- ***al-Mustaẓhirī*** *(Faḍāʾiḥ al-Bāṭiniyya)* — the refutation of Ismāʿīlī Bāṭinism commissioned by the caliph al-Mustaẓhir.
- ***Fayṣal al-Tafriqa*** — on the criterion separating Islam from unbelief; a deliberately restrictive account of what may be anathematized, and a document of considerable importance in modern debates about takfīr.
- ***Mishkāt al-Anwār* (The Niche of Lights)** — a late commentary on the Light Verse (Q 24:35) and the ḥadīth of the veils, in an idiom so Avicennian that Watt doubted its authenticity. Later scholarship (Landolt, Treiger 2012, Griffel 2009) accepts it; the disputed "Veils Section" remains the hardest text in the corpus.
- ***Miʿyār al-ʿIlm***, ***Miḥakk al-Naẓar***, ***al-Qisṭās al-Mustaqīm*** — the logic manuals.
- ***al-Mustaṣfā min ʿilm al-Uṣūl*** (~1109) — the late summa of Shāfiʿī legal theory, and the place where he declares that one who has not mastered logic is not to be trusted in any branch of knowledge.

## The thought

**Against the philosophers — and with them.** The popular picture of the *Tahāfut* as an assault on reason does not survive contact with the text. Ghazālī's targets are specific: the *pre-eternity of the world*, the claim that God knows particulars only *in a universal mode*, and the *denial of bodily resurrection* — three theses he holds to be incompatible with revelation and therefore unbelief. The other seventeen are errors, not apostasies. And in the very years he wrote it, he was writing the logic manuals that made Aristotelian syllogistic a required instrument of *fiqh* and *kalām*, rebranded as a religiously neutral "balance" (*mīzān*) with Qurʾānic warrants. Since Frank 1992 and Griffel 2009, the mainstream reading is that Ghazālī is the principal **conduit** by which Avicennian cosmology, psychology, and prophetology entered Sunni theology — that he domesticated *falsafa* by absorbing it while condemning three of its conclusions. The polemic and the absorption are the same act.

**Occasionalism and the 17th Discussion.** The most philosophically consequential pages he wrote deny that the connection between what we habitually call a cause and what we habitually call its effect is *necessary*. Fire placed against cotton does not burn it; God creates the burning at the occasion of the contact, and does so with such regularity that we mistake habit for necessity. The purpose is to make miracle metaphysically cheap. **Griffel 2009 insists on a complication usually dropped in summaries:** Ghazālī sets out *two* ways to secure the miracle, and the second concedes real natures with God-given causal powers. He does not unambiguously commit to the occasionalist horn. The vault's [[occasionalism]] node carries the doctrine; [[philosophy-asharite-occasionalism]] carries the school.

**The Iḥyāʾ and the science of the heart.** The heart (*qalb*) is the organ of knowledge; the vices (pride, envy, greed, love of status) are the diseases that occlude it; the disciplines of the path (*dhikr*, self-examination, scrupulousness, the ethics of companionship) are its medicine; and the goal is *unveiling* (*mukāshafa*), a knowledge not reducible to demonstration. Crucially, none of this is offered as an alternative to law. The forty books begin with ritual purity and prayer. That is the whole argument: **the interior life is the missing half of the ordinary religious sciences, not a rival to them.**

## Transmission and reception

**Within Islam.** The *Iḥyāʾ* became normative across the Sunni world — Maghrebi, Ottoman, and South Asian alike. His formula for the relation of law to inwardness is the settlement inside which later Sufi metaphysics is arguable as mainstream Islam at all: without it, [[ibn-arabi|Ibn ʿArabī]]'s *waḥdat al-wujūd* and [[rumi|Rūmī]]'s ecstatic poetics would have had no orthodox space to occupy. *(Karamustafa 2007 cautions against the tidy version of this: the rapprochement was already well advanced in al-Junayd's "sober" lineage and in al-Qushayrī's *Risāla* a generation earlier — Ghazālī consolidated a process rather than inventing it.)* His younger brother **Aḥmad al-Ghazālī** (d. 1126), author of the *Sawāniḥ*, belongs to the Persian love-mysticism line that runs on to [[farid-ud-din-attar|ʿAṭṭār]] and [[rumi|Rūmī]]. Against him: [[ibn-rushd|Averroes]] on philosophical grounds, [[ibn-taymiyya|Ibn Taymiyya]] (d. 1328) on the Sufi-metaphysical grounds, and modern Salafism after him.

**MASSIVE WIN — the Latin misreading of Algazel.** Of Ghazālī's enormous corpus, the Latin Middle Ages received essentially **one** book: the *Maqāṣid al-Falāsifa*, translated at Toledo in the twelfth century by Dominicus Gundissalinus as *Logica et philosophia Algazelis Arabis* — **without the preface** in which Ghazālī states that he is expounding the philosophers' views in order to demolish them in a following volume. The Latins therefore read "Algazel" as a **follower** of Avicenna and cited him as such; [[thomas-aquinas|Aquinas]] lists him with [[ibn-sina|Avicenna]] and [[ibn-rushd|Averroes]] as one of the Arab philosophers. The most effective critic of *falsafa* in Islamic history entered Christendom as one of its exponents, on the strength of a missing paragraph. *(Salman 1935; d'Alverny 1994; Griffel 2009.)*

**Occasionalism afterwards.** The 17th Discussion's structure recurs in Nicholas of Autrecourt, in Malebranche's Cartesian occasionalism, and — most strikingly — in Hume's argument that necessary connection is never observed, only habitually inferred. The resemblance is standard in the literature (Perler & Rudolph 2000; Nadler 2011; Fakhry 1958). **No reading-chain from Ghazālī to Malebranche or Hume has been established**, and the vault therefore records this as a *parallel*, not a transmission. It is exactly the kind of claim that gets upgraded to "influence" in popular retellings; it should not be.

## Disputes

- **Did Ghazālī end Islamic science?** A widely-circulated modern claim, repeated in popular science broadcasting, holds that the *Tahāfut* extinguished the scientific culture of the Islamic world. Historians of Islamic science reject it: the most productive period of Islamic mathematical astronomy — the **Marāgha school**, al-Ṭūsī and the Ṭūsī-couple, al-ʿUrḍī, Ibn al-Shāṭir — falls entirely **after** Ghazālī, and the institutional home of that work (the madrasa) is the institution he served. **Tier note: the claim is T4 (popular, rejected); the rebuttal is T1 (Saliba 2007; Griffel 2009).**
- **Occasionalist or not?** Frank 1992 argued that Ghazālī quietly abandoned Ashʿarite occasionalism for an Avicennian secondary-causality cosmology; Marmura contested this. Griffel 2009's mediating reading — that Ghazālī deliberately leaves both open — is currently the most widely accepted, but the question is live.
- **Authenticity of the *Mishkāt*'s Veils Section.** Watt 1953 doubted it; Landolt, Treiger 2012, and Griffel 2009 defend it. The passage's apparent identification of the highest veil with an Avicennian First Mover is the crux.
- **What the 1095 departure was.** Spiritual crisis (the *Munqidh*'s own account), political flight (Garden 2014), or both. The vault carries both readings rather than choosing.
- **Whether the *Munqidh* is reliable biography.** It is late, retrospective, and written for a purpose. Every modern biography treats it as a source *about* Ghazālī's self-presentation as much as about his life.
