# Al-Ghazali

## Identity
Abū Ḥāmid Muḥammad al-Ghazālī (1058–1111) was the supreme medieval Sunni theologian, the architect of the orthodox Sunni-Sufi synthesis, and the most influential single figure in post-Avicennan Islamic intellectual history. Born Ṭūs in Khurasan; educated under al-Juwaynī; appointed to the Niẓāmiyyah madrasa at Baghdad 1091; the celebrated 1095 spiritual crisis (psychosomatic mutism in the lecture hall, abandonment of public career under cover of pilgrimage); a decade of Sufi withdrawal in Damascus, Jerusalem, Hebron, Medina, Mecca; return to teaching at Nishapur briefly; final retirement to Ṭūs. Died 1111.

## Historical evidence
Extensively documented through his own autobiography *al-Munqidh min al-Ḍalāl* (one of the most striking spiritual autobiographies in any premodern tradition), through contemporary biographical sources, and through the massive surviving corpus of works (~70 are securely his).

## Works
The *Iḥyāʾ ʿUlūm al-Dīn* (Revival of the Religious Sciences, 40 books, ~1100, his magnum opus); *Tahāfut al-Falāsifa* (Incoherence of the Philosophers, ~1095, the famous critique of Avicenna and Fārābī); *Maqāṣid al-Falāsifa* (summary of Avicennan philosophy, written as preliminary to the *Tahāfut*); *al-Munqidh* (autobiography); *Mishkāt al-Anwār* (Sufi cosmology); *al-Iqtiṣād fī l-Iʿtiqād* (Ash'arite theology); legal works.

## Role in the tradition
Foundational — the title *Ḥujjat al-Islam* (Proof of Islam) captures the medieval estimate. Al-Ghazālī's synthesis of jurisprudence, kalām theology, and Sufi piety became the operative Sunni orthodoxy of the post-1100 Islamic world. Ibn Taymiyya (~1300) and modern Salafism critique al-Ghazālī's Sufi metaphysics; mainstream Ottoman, Maghrebi, and South Asian Sunnism honors him. The thesis that al-Ghazālī's anti-philosophical polemic single-handedly stalled Islamic science is now largely rejected by historians of Islamic science.
