# Muhammad al-Mahdi (the Hidden Imam)

## Identity
Per Twelver Shi'i doctrine: son of [[hasan-al-askari]] by a Byzantine slave-mother named Narjis (variously Narjis, Saqil, Susan, or Maryam — the names varying across sources); born in Samarra ~869 (the birth concealed because of Abbasid hostility); succeeded his father as **Twelfth Imam** in 874 at age ~5, and at that moment entered *ghayba* — occultation — communicating with the Imami community only through four successive *safīrs* (special deputies) during the **Minor Occultation** (874–941):
1. Uthman ibn Sa'id al-Amri (d. ~880)
2. Muhammad ibn Uthman al-Amri (d. 917)
3. Husayn ibn Ruh al-Nawbakhti (d. 938)
4. Ali ibn Muhammad al-Samari (d. 941)

The death of the fourth *safīr* in 941 — who reportedly received a final letter from the Imam declaring that no further deputy would be designated and that the **Major Occultation** had begun — ends regular communication with the Hidden Imam. Per Twelver doctrine he remains *alive*, in occultation, will return at the end of days as the *Mahdī* (the Rightly-Guided One; the *Qāʾim* — the Riser; the *Ṣāḥib al-Zamān* — Lord of the Age) to fill the earth with justice as it has been filled with injustice, and to inaugurate the final age of righteousness preceding the resurrection.

## Historical evidence

The historicity of the Twelfth Imam is the foundational disputed claim of Twelver Shi'i Islam. Critical historiography — most carefully **Modarressi 1993** *Crisis and Consolidation* and **Hussain 1982** *The Occultation of the Twelfth Imam* — establishes that:

- The Twelfth Imam's existence was *not publicly known or claimed* at the time of [[hasan-al-askari]]'s death in 874. Sunni-Abbasid sources of the period treat al-Askari as having died without surviving male issue.
- The early Imami community fragmented into ~14 sub-sects in the immediate post-874 period (see [[hasan-al-askari]]), only one of which held the position eventually canonical in Twelver Islam — that al-Askari had had a son who went into occultation.
- The *Minor Occultation* doctrine — with the four-*safīr* sequence — develops over the late 9th and early 10th c. as one of the contested interpretations of the post-Askari succession.
- The *Major Occultation* doctrine (no further public deputy after 941; the Imam alive in supernatural hiddenness; to return at end-of-days) is the **Buyid-era consolidation** — articulated firmly in the great Imami compendia of Kulayni (d. 941), ibn Babawayh (d. 991), and al-Mufid (d. 1022).
- The doctrine of the Twelfth Imam is therefore *historically* better understood as a *gradual late-9th-to-early-11th-century consolidation* of Imami theology under the conditions of (a) repeated experience of Imami failure (the rapid succession of Imams 818–874 under Abbasid surveillance), (b) the development of the *wakāla* / safir system as substitute institutional structure, and (c) the increasing influence of the eschatological-messianic *Mahdi*-tradition in early Islam.

The Twelver position, by contrast, holds that the historical Twelfth Imam is real, that his concealment from public view was a contemporary necessity given Abbasid persecution, and that the gradual articulation of the occultation doctrine reflects only the progressive clarification of an underlying reality.

**Both readings are surfaced.** The critical reading does not deny that the Twelfth Imam is religiously real and structurally central in Twelver Islam; it locates the formation of the doctrine in identifiable historical processes rather than treating it as a publicly-known fact of 874.

## Role in the tradition

### The institutional consequence of the Major Occultation
With no living Imam available to direct the community, Twelver Islam developed a distinctive institutional structure: the **'general deputyship' (*niyāba ʿāmma*) of the jurists (*fuqahāʾ*)**. In the absence of the Imam, qualified religious-legal scholars exercise (varying conceptions of) deputyized authority on his behalf. The conception is *quietist* in classical Twelver tradition (the jurists may issue *fatāwā* and direct religious life but do not exercise sovereign political authority — that belongs only to the Imam and awaits his return) but the limits of jurist-authority become a major doctrinal issue across the Twelver tradition.

### The Iranian Revolution and *vilāyat-i faqīh*
The single most consequential modern development is **[[event-iranian-revolution-1979]]** and Ayatollah Khomeini's doctrine of *vilāyat-i faqīh* (guardianship of the jurist), articulated in his 1970 Najaf lectures *Hukumat-i Islami* (Islamic Government). Khomeini's innovation was to extend the deputyship-of-the-jurists to include *political-sovereign* authority during the occultation: in the absence of the Imam, the most qualified jurist exercises supreme political-religious authority on the Imam's behalf. The doctrine reversed centuries of Twelver *political quietism* (the conventional Twelver position had been that legitimate political authority requires the present Imam and is therefore impossible during occultation; the jurists confine themselves to religious-legal direction).

The 1979 Constitution of the Islamic Republic of Iran institutionalized *vilāyat-i faqīh*; Khomeini became the first *Wali al-Faqih* / Supreme Leader. This is **load-bearing for understanding the entire theological-political structure of the modern Islamic Republic of Iran**: the regime is not a caliphate (no caliph), not a secular republic (sovereignty derives from God through the Imam through the jurist), but a doctrinally-novel structure in which the absence-of-the-Imam authorizes the deputyship-of-the-jurist. The doctrine remains contested within Twelver Islam itself — the *quietist* school centered in Najaf (Sistani and predecessors) does not accept Khomeini's expansive version.

### Messianic / eschatological theology

The expected return of the Mahdi — to fill the earth with justice, to defeat the *Dajjāl* (the Antichrist analogue), to inaugurate the final righteous age, often accompanied by the return of Jesus (whose death-and-second-coming Islam shares with Christianity, though in different doctrinal framing) — is the most fully-developed eschatological scheme in Islam. The Sunni tradition has its own Mahdi-expectations (Mahdi as a future descendant of the Prophet, not yet existing; Sunni Mahdi-claimants have arisen episodically — the Sudanese Mahdi of 1881; Juhayman al-Otaibi's 1979 Mecca uprising) but does not identify the Mahdi with a particular historical figure who has already been born and is currently in occultation. Only the Twelver tradition has this specific structure.

## Cross-tradition connections

- ↔ [[hasan-al-askari]] (father, per Twelver tradition) — the foundational link whose contested historicity is the central question.
- ↔ [[event-iranian-revolution-1979]] — the *vilāyat-i faqīh* doctrine is structurally a doctrine *about* the Twelfth Imam's occultation; the Islamic Republic of Iran is theologically a *substitute* structure during the Imam's absence.
- ↔ [[tradition-shia-islam]] — the entire institutional shape of post-941 Twelver Islam is shaped by the Twelfth Imam's occultation.
- ↔ [[messianism]] — the most fully-developed Islamic messianic figure; structurally parallel to the Jewish-Hebrew-Bible messiah, the Christian returning-Christ, and the Zoroastrian *Saoshyant*. All four eschatological-redeemer figures share a late-antique Near Eastern matrix.
- ↔ **Jewish messianism / *deus absconditus* tradition.** The structural motif of the *hidden redeemer* — a designated savior figure who is alive but withdrawn from the world and will return at the appointed time — has rough analogues in Jewish messianic tradition (the Messiah ben Joseph who must precede the Messiah ben David; the various hidden-righteous-one traditions; later Lurianic and Sabbatean material). Sachedina 1981 explicitly registers the comparative parallel. Whether the parallel implies historical transmission via the rich Jewish-Christian-Mandaean Mesopotamian milieu in which the occultation doctrine consolidated, or merely structural parallel across late-antique apocalyptic, is debated.
- ↔ [[hidden-god]] — the *deus absconditus* trope across late-antique religious thought has structural cognate in the Hidden Imam.
- ↔ [[apocalyptic-cosmic-warfare]] / [[end-times-judgment]] — the return of the Mahdi inaugurates the final cosmic battle (often joined by the returning Jesus against the *Dajjāl*).
- ↔ Zoroastrian *Saoshyant* — late-Sasanian Zoroastrian eschatology features a savior born of Zarathustra's preserved seed who will return to defeat evil at end-of-days. The structural-religious parallel to the Mahdi is widely noted (Halm 1997); the Iraqi-Iranian geographic-religious matrix in which Imami doctrine formed is precisely the matrix in which late-Sasanian Zoroastrian eschatology was operative.

## Disputes

- **Historicity of the Twelfth Imam's birth and existence.** See *Historical evidence* above. The foundational question; both Twelver-doctrinal and critical-historiographical readings register.

- **Gradual consolidation of the Mahdi-doctrine.** Sachedina 1981, Modarressi 1993, Amanat 2009 — the doctrine consolidates 874–~1050 rather than being public knowledge of 874. This is mainstream critical history.

- **Sunni vs. Twelver Mahdi-doctrines.** Different structurally (Sunni: future descendant not yet existing; Twelver: present-but-hidden specific historical figure).

- ***Vilāyat-i faqīh* vs. classical quietism.** The Najaf (Sistani) vs. Qom (Khomeini-Khamenei) divergence is the live intra-Twelver dispute. Quietism reads the Imam's occultation as *invalidating* sovereign political authority; activism reads it as *transferring* it to the jurist.
