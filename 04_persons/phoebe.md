# Phoebe

## Identity
A wealthy Christian woman of **Cenchreae** (the eastern port of Corinth) commended by Paul at the opening of his letter-of-introduction to the Romans (Rom 16:1–2). Paul identifies her with two technical terms: *diakonos* of the church at Cenchreae (often translated "deacon" or "minister" — the masculine form pointedly used of a woman) and *prostatis* (often "patron" — a term of substantial financial-civic support). Standardly understood as the **courier of the letter to the Romans** and Paul's authorized representative in Rome.

## Historical evidence
A single Pauline mention but exceptionally specific. The terms *diakonos* and *prostatis* are technical and indicate institutional position and financial means respectively. Phoebe is one of the most direct pieces of evidence for women holding ministerial office in the Pauline mission.

## Works
None — but as letter-courier, she carried and likely *read aloud* the letter to the Romans, which in oral-performance culture made her its first authoritative interpreter to its Roman audience (Jewett 2007).

## Role in the tradition
**Feminist and women-in-ministry scholarship**: Phoebe is the foundational New Testament data point for women holding *diakonos* office (Rom 16:1–2). Many Protestant and Catholic ordination-debate arguments turn on the translation of *diakonos* here.

**Patristic tradition**: largely overlooked; rediscovered through 20th-century critical scholarship.

## Disputes
- **Translation of *diakonos*.** "Servant" (functional) vs. "deacon" (institutional title) — the Greek favors the institutional reading and is so translated of male deacons elsewhere.
- **Translation of *prostatis*.** "Patron / benefactor" vs. minimizing "helper" — the lexicography favors the patronage reading.
- **Authority in Rome.** Whether she was Paul's *authorized interpreter* of Romans is inferential but well-supported.
