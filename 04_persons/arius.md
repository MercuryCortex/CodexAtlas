# Arius

## Identity
Alexandrian presbyter, possibly Libyan in origin; ascetic and popular preacher attached to the parish of Baucalis in Alexandria. Around 318 his teaching that the Son of God was a creature — "there was when he was not" (*ēn hote ouk ēn*), made out of nothing, of a different *ousia* from the Father — brought him into open conflict with Bishop Alexander of Alexandria. The dispute escalated through provincial synods, the intervention of Eusebius of Nicomedia (Arius's powerful supporter at court), and ultimately the Council of Nicaea (325), which condemned him. Arius died, dramatically and suspiciously, in a Constantinople public latrine in 336, the night before his planned readmission to communion.

## Historical evidence
Arius's own writings survive only in fragmentary quotation in the works of opponents (Athanasius, Marcellus of Ancyra, Epiphanius). The *Thalia* survives partly in Athanasius's hostile transcriptions. Three letters survive (to Alexander of Alexandria; to Eusebius of Nicomedia; to Constantine). The Nicene acta and Constantine's letters round out the documentation.

## Works
- ***Thalia*** ("Banquet" — though the title's exact reference is disputed) — a popular theological poem, perhaps designed for catechetical singing. Survives only in citations.
- Three letters.

## Role in the tradition
Eponym of the most-fought-over heresy of the 4th c. "Arianism" became the catch-all term for any subordinationist Christology — though Rowan Williams 2001 stresses that **what later came to be called Arianism** (especially the developed Anomoean and Homoean theologies of the 350s–60s) was not exactly Arius's own position. After Nicaea, "Arian" remained the official Christian designation of barbarian (Gothic, Vandal, Lombard) Christianity through the 6th c. — Ulfilas, the Gothic missionary, was an Arian, and the Goths and their successors held an Arian Christology against the Nicene Romans for centuries.

## Connections — substrate and influence
- Arius's theology is widely read as **late Middle Platonist** in its structure — Williams 2001: 215–229 traces the Alexandrian Christian-Platonist background (Origen, the Alexandrian theological school) of Arius's Logos-as-mediator schema. The Son as a high but created intermediate is structurally close to the Middle Platonist Demiurge / Second God of Numenius and the Hellenized-Jewish *Logos* of Philo, just with a stronger creation-out-of-nothing line. **Arius is a Middle Platonist Christian** in a way Athanasius works hard to avoid being.
- Gregg & Groh 1981 read Arianism not primarily as a metaphysical thesis but as a **soteriological** one: Christ as the supreme moral exemplar (the perfectly-obedient creature) — a reading whose substrate is Stoic-ethical (the *sophos* / sage) rather than Platonic-metaphysical.
- Subordinationism itself has substrate in the earlier Logos-theology of Justin Martyr, Origen, and ultimately the Hellenistic-Jewish Philonic Logos — which Arius radicalizes by making the Logos a creature rather than a divine attribute.
- The Goths' adoption of Arianism (via Ulfilas, mid-4th c.) preserved the doctrine and its associated Latin / Gothic biblical translation as **the form of Christianity that the barbarian kingdoms brought into the post-Roman West** — Vandals (until 533), Visigoths (until 587), Lombards (until 7th c.) — making "Arianism" the religious-political marker of a centuries-long East-West and Roman-barbarian dispute.

## Disputes
- **What Arius actually taught.** Williams 2001 (and earlier Lorenz, Stead, etc.) shows that the simple "Arius said the Son was a creature" caricature obscures a more sophisticated Origenist-derived position. The developed "Arianism" of the 350s–360s (Aetius, Eunomius) departs from Arius in important ways.
- **Arius's death.** Cause unclear — natural, divine judgment (the official Nicene reading), or poisoning. Athanasius's account is gleeful and not neutral.
