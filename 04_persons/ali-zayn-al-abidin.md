# Ali Zayn al-Abidin

## Identity
Son of [[husayn-ibn-ali]] by Shahrbanu (per Shi'i tradition, a Sasanian Persian princess captured after the Arab conquest of Iran — a tradition charged with the symbolism of the Husaynid line uniting Arab-prophetic and Persian-imperial heritage; the historicity of the Shahrbanu identification is debated). Born ~658 in Medina. The **sole surviving adult-male of the Husaynid line after Karbala** — saved (per the unanimous tradition) by an illness that prevented him from fighting on 10 Muharram 680. Taken captive with the women, paraded to Kufa and Damascus, eventually released to return to Medina. Lived the remainder of his life in Medina as a quietist Imam, focused on devotion, teaching, and the production of supplicatory prayers; died 712 (poisoned per Shi'i tradition; the historical evidence is suggestive but not conclusive).

For Shi'i Islam, the **Fourth Imam**, the bottleneck through which the entire Twelver line passes after Karbala. Without Zayn al-Abidin's survival, the Twelver Imami line ends in 680.

## Historical evidence
Better-evidenced than some of the later Imams because of his role as Karbala-survivor (witnessed by both Sunni and Shi'i sources), his teaching activity in Medina (he is recognized as a *muhaddith* — hadith-transmitter — in Sunni biographical works), and the attributed prayer-collection *al-Sahifa al-Sajjadiyya* (whose textual transmission is studied in both classical and modern scholarship).

## Works
- ***al-Sahifa al-Sajjadiyya*** ("Scripture of al-Sajjad," sometimes "the Psalms of the Family of Muhammad") — a collection of ~54 supplicatory prayers attributed to Zayn al-Abidin. After the Qur'an and the *Nahj al-Balagha* of Ali, the *Sahifa* is often considered the third great text of the Shi'i religious tradition. Chittick's English translation (1988) is the standard scholarly rendering. The collection is a vast and densely theological devotional corpus, addressing themes of repentance, divine intimacy, eschatology, and the longing of the soul.
- ***Risalat al-Huquq*** ("Treatise of Rights") — a systematic statement of religious-ethical obligations.

## Role in the tradition
The Fourth Imam is the **quietist-devotional foundation of post-Karbala Shi'ism**. After the catastrophe of Karbala, the surviving Imam withdrew from political activity and concentrated on prayer, teaching, and ethical formation. The model is decisive for the long Twelver tradition: in periods of political pressure, the Imams generally pursued quietism (*taqiyya*) rather than open resistance; political confrontation was the exception (Husayn, Zayd ibn Ali) rather than the rule.

The *Sahifa al-Sajjadiyya* is the bridge from the Karbala-trauma to a sustainable post-trauma religious life: rather than political confrontation, *prayer-as-resistance* — the long elaboration of religious-devotional vocabulary that becomes the matrix of Shi'i piety. The Persian-Sasanian heritage of the Shahrbanu tradition is a recurring theme in Shi'i devotional self-understanding (the Imami line as inheritor of both Muhammad's prophethood and Persian royal-charismatic kingship — *farr* meeting *imama*).

## Disputes
- **The Shahrbanu tradition.** The narrative that Zayn al-Abidin's mother was a captured Sasanian princess is widely cited in Shi'i hagiography but historically uncertain. Critical scholarship treats it as a later (Buyid-era?) tradition that fuses two political claims (the Husaynid line as Persian-imperial-charismatic) at a moment when Iranian–Shi'i alignment was politically meaningful. The historical mother of Zayn al-Abidin may have been a slave-woman; the specifics are unrecoverable.

- **Sahifa authenticity.** The classical Twelver tradition holds the *Sahifa* as authentically by Zayn al-Abidin. Critical assessment is more cautious: a core of devotional material plausibly tracing to the Fourth Imam, with later expansion and editorial elaboration. The collection clearly reflects post-Karbala Shi'i devotional vocabulary in its mature form.

- **Death by poison.** Standard Shi'i tradition (he was poisoned by Walid I or al-Hisham). Historical evidence is suggestive but not conclusive; the *imams-died-as-martyrs* topos becomes a recurrent Shi'i hagiographical pattern, sometimes (Husayn, Musa al-Kazim) historically well-grounded, sometimes (other imams) more uncertain.
