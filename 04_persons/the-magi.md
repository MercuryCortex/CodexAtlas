# The Magi (Zoroastrian Priest-Astrologers)

## Identity
The Magi (Greek *Magoi*, from Old Persian *Magūš*) are **the priestly class of Zoroastrian religion** — originally a Median tribe (one of the six Median tribes per [[herodotus]] *Histories* 1.101) that became the **institutional priesthood of all Iranian religion from the Median through the Achaemenid, Parthian, and Sasanian periods** (approximately -700 to +651, a span of 1,350 years). The Magi were responsible for **sacrificial ritual, fire-temple maintenance, recitation and preservation of the Avestan liturgical corpus, dream interpretation, astrological-mantic practice, and (in the Achaemenid period) royal-religious-political advisory functions**. The word *magic* in Western languages descends etymologically from *Magūš → Magoi → magicus / magic* — making the Magi the **etymological source of an entire Western vocabulary** of religious-and-occult-practice. For the Codex Atlas mission the Magi are doubly load-bearing: (1) they are the **institutional custodians** of Zoroastrian religion across 1,350 years (without the Magi the Avestan corpus would not survive); (2) they appear in **Matthew 2:1–12 as the "Wise Men from the East"** — **the single most direct Zoroastrian-Christian textual transmission in the entire New Testament**, providing the canonical-Christian Nativity narrative with explicitly-Persian-Zoroastrian astrological-religious figures.

## Historical evidence
The Magi are exceptionally well-documented:
- **[[herodotus]]** *Histories* 1.101, 1.107–108, 1.128, 1.132 (~-440) — earliest Greek ethnographic description. Names the Magi as one of the six Median tribes; describes their priestly ritual functions and dream-interpretation; reports Cyrus the Great's grandfather Astyages employing Magi as dream-interpreters.
- **Behistun Inscription** (Darius I, ~-518) — names a Magus, Gaumata, as the false-Smerdis-pretender Darius overthrew. The "Magophonia" festival ("Magus-slaying") commemorating Darius's purge of the Magi-conspirators is reported by Herodotus 3.79.
- **[[xenophon]]** *Cyropaedia* — extensive Magi-as-Persian-priestly-class material in idealized form.
- **Greek philosophical-religious literature** (Plato, Aristotle, the later doxographers) — the Magi are frequently invoked as exemplars of ancient priestly-wisdom. Plato's pseudonymous *Alcibiades I* 122a famously says the Persian royal princes are taught "the magianism of Zoroaster son of Horomazes — that is, the cult of the gods."
- **Hellenistic-Roman** — the Magi become a stock figure of ancient-priestly-wisdom in Hellenistic and Roman literature; Apollonius of Tyana, Pythagoras, and others are reported to have studied with Magi.
- **Matthew 2:1–12** — the Christian Nativity Magi.
- **Sasanian period** — extensive primary documentation of the Magi as institutional priesthood (the term shifts to *mowbed*).
- **Post-651** — the Magi institutional class survives in Parsi diaspora (India) and Iranian Zoroastrian community to the present.

## Works
The Magi are an **institutional-priestly class**, not individual authors. Their custodial role:
- **Preservation of the Avestan corpus** ([[phase-2-002-gathas-of-zarathustra]], [[phase-2-004-yasna-younger-avesta]]) across centuries of oral-and-then-written transmission.
- **Pahlavi-period** redaction and elaboration of the Zoroastrian theological tradition (Bundahishn, Denkard, etc. — late-Sasanian and post-Sasanian compilations).
- **Hellenistic-Roman pseudepigrapha attributed to Zoroaster / Ostanes** — Greek-language magical, alchemical, and astrological works circulating under Magus-names. These are pseudepigraphic but reflect real Zoroastrian-Hellenistic religious-cultural interchange.

## Role in the tradition

### THE MASSIVE-WIN: Matthew 2:1–12 — the Zoroastrian-Christian Nativity edge

**Matthew 2:1–12** describes "*Magoi apo anatolon*" — **Magi from the East** — who follow a star to Bethlehem, find the infant Jesus, and offer gifts of **gold, frankincense, and myrrh**. The text is explicit: these are **Zoroastrian priest-astrologers** (the Greek *magoi* is the standard term for Persian-Zoroastrian priests in Hellenistic-Greek literature; Matthew's Greek-educated audience would recognize this immediately). The Magi's role in the Nativity story is **astrological** — they have observed an astronomical sign and traveled to investigate. Their offering of frankincense and myrrh (Arabian-Persian luxury imports used in Zoroastrian and other Near Eastern religious-ritual contexts) is consistent with Persian priestly practice.

**The textual function of the Magi in Matthew 2 is the legitimation of Christian Messianic claims by Zoroastrian astrological-religious authority.** The Magi — representatives of the highest pre-Christian astrological-priestly wisdom recognized by the Greco-Roman world — recognize Christ as the universal King. The Matthew narrative is **deliberately deploying Zoroastrian religious-cultural authority** as cross-tradition vindication of the Christian Christological claim.

### The Magi in subsequent Christian tradition

The Magi figure transforms through Christian reception:
- **Patristic period**: Origen, Tertullian, and others discuss the Magi's significance; Tertullian *Adversus Judaeos* 9.4 identifies them as **kings** (drawing on Psalms 72:10 — "kings of Sheba and Seba shall offer gifts").
- **Three Magi tradition** crystallizes by the 3rd c. (the three Magi corresponding to the three gifts — gold/frankincense/myrrh).
- **Names crystallize** in Western Christian tradition by the 6th c.: Caspar (the elder, who brings gold), Melchior (who brings frankincense), Balthasar (who brings myrrh).
- **Cologne Cathedral** (12th c. onward) houses the "Shrine of the Three Kings" containing reputed relics of the Magi, brought from Milan by Frederick Barbarossa.
- **Modern Christian celebration**: Epiphany (January 6) commemorates the Magi's arrival; major liturgical-cultural celebration in many Christian traditions.

### Etymology of "magic"
The Greek *magoi* → Latin *magus / magicus* → English *magic / magician* is the etymological-philological line. The English word "magic" thus directly preserves the Zoroastrian priestly-title — a remarkable case of Western vocabulary preserving Eastern religious-historical-institutional terminology.

## Disputes
- **Historicity of Matthew 2 Magi.** Whether actual historical Persian Zoroastrian Magi visited Bethlehem is debated. Most modern critical scholarship treats the Matthew narrative as **theological-symbolic rather than historical** — the Magi are deployed by Matthew as the highest-status astrological-religious witnesses to legitimate Christ's universal kingship. The Lucan birth narrative (which has shepherds, not Magi) suggests the Magi are a Matthean-specific theological-narrative move.
- **Number of Magi.** Matthew gives no number; the three Magi tradition is post-biblical (derived from the three gifts).
- **Names of Magi.** Caspar / Melchior / Balthasar are 6th-c. Western Christian-legendary names; the Syrian Christian tradition has different names; the Ethiopian Christian tradition has different names again.
- **Magi-vs-Mōbed terminology.** In the Sasanian-Pahlavi period the priestly class is called *mowbed* (from older *magupat*, "chief of the Magi"). The relationship between Avestan-period *magu* and later *mowbed* is debated but the institutional continuity is uncontested.
