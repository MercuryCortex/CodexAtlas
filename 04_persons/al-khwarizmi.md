# Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi

## The Two Words He Gave the World

Every time anyone uses the word **"algebra"** or **"algorithm"** they are invoking al-Khwarizmi. 

**Algebra** derives from *al-jabr* — "completion" or "restoration" — in the title of his foundational 830 CE work. The operation al-jabr referred to: moving a negative term from one side of an equation to the other (completing it). His book *Kitāb al-mukhtaṣar fī ḥisāb al-jabr wa-l-muqābala* gave systematic procedures for solving linear and quadratic equations, and named the discipline.

**Algorithm** derives from *Algoritmi* — the Latin transliteration of his name. When his *Kitāb al-ḥisāb al-hindī* was translated into Latin in the 12th century, the text began "Dixit Algoritmi..." (al-Khwarizmi says...). Medieval Europeans understood "algorismus" or "algorithmus" to mean a systematic numerical procedure — exactly what his book described. Every computer algorithm is named after a 9th-century Baghdad mathematician.

## The Algebra Book — What He Actually Did

The *Kitāb al-mukhtaṣar fī ḥisāb al-jabr wa-l-muqābala* is not algebra in the modern symbolic sense — al-Khwarizmi wrote everything in words, not symbols. But it accomplished something revolutionary: **a systematic, general method for solving equations by manipulation rules that work regardless of the specific numbers involved**.

The book treats six types of problems (in modern notation: ax² = bx; ax² = c; bx = c; ax² + bx = c; ax² + c = bx; bx + c = ax²) and provides a procedure for each. The procedures work geometrically — "completing the square" involves literally constructing a square and completing it — but the logic is algorithmic: follow these steps and you will always get the answer.

This is the conceptual breakthrough: **procedure as the object of study**. Not "here is how to solve this specific problem" but "here is the general method for solving all problems of this type." This abstraction from specific numbers to general procedure is what made modern mathematics (and computer science) possible.

## The Numerals Book — Transmitting Zero to the World

Al-Khwarizmi's *Kitāb al-ḥisāb al-hindī* introduced the Indian decimal numeral system — including zero — to the Islamic world. The book itself is lost in Arabic; it survives in Latin translation (*Algoritmi de numero Indorum*, c. 1126 CE). But its impact was enormous: by 976 CE the Indian numerals were in use across the Islamic world, and by 1202 CE Fibonacci's *Liber Abaci* had brought them to Europe.

The chain: **Brahmagupta** (Ujjain, 628 CE) → **Arabic translation of Indian mathematics** (c. 775 CE, Caliph al-Mansur) → **al-Khwarizmi** (Baghdad, c. 825 CE) → **Latin translation** (c. 1126 CE, Adelard of Bath / Robert of Chester) → **Fibonacci** (Pisa, 1202 CE) → **European mathematics** → every number ever written or computed in the modern world.

## MASSIVE WIN — The Three Rivers Converge

Al-Khwarizmi's work represents the most significant **synthesis** event in the history of mathematics: three independent mathematical traditions converging in one person's work in one city in one generation.

| Tradition | What it contributed | Channel to al-Khwarizmi |
|---|---|---|
| **Babylonian** | Sexagesimal system, astronomical computation | Via Persian intermediaries; Zīj tradition |
| **Indian** | Decimal positional numerals, zero, algebraic procedures | Brahmagupta → Arabic translation (c. 775 CE) |
| **Greek** | Geometric proof, Euclidean method, Pythagorean number theory | Euclid's *Elements* in Arabic (c. 800 CE) |

From this synthesis: algebra (procedural equation-solving), the algorithm concept, the decimal numeral system, and astronomical tables that combined all three computational traditions. Every subsequent mathematical development in Islamic, European, and eventually global science builds on this synthesis.
