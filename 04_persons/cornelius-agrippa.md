# Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa von Nettesheim

## Identity
German Renaissance magus, soldier, physician, court astrologer, lawyer, theologian, and synthesist of Renaissance ceremonial magic (1486–1535). Studied at Cologne, served at the Habsburg court of Maximilian I, lectured at Dôle (1509) on Reuchlin's *De Verbo Mirifico* before being driven out for heresy charges, taught at Pavia and Turin, served various princes (sometimes simultaneously and to multiple masters' confusion), and ended as historiographer to Margaret of Austria at Mechelen. His three-volume *De Occulta Philosophia* (drafted 1510 as a manuscript circulated to Trithemius, expanded over two decades, printed 1531–1533) is the central single compendium of Renaissance magic. His paradoxical *De Vanitate Scientiarum* (1530) appears to recant the magical program; Nauert and current scholarship read it as humanist *paradoxon* rather than serious recantation. His *De Nobilitate Foeminei Sexus* (1529) is among the earliest published arguments for women's intellectual and spiritual equality. Repeatedly persecuted by Inquisitorial and university authorities; died poor in Grenoble.

## Historical evidence
Densely documented: extensive correspondence, court records, the 1510 Trithemius manuscript of *De Occulta Philosophia* (extant), the 1531/1533 printed editions, contemporary references.

## Works
- *De Occulta Philosophia Libri Tres* (1510 ms / 1531–1533 print).
- *De Vanitate Scientiarum et Artium* (1530).
- *De Nobilitate et Praecellentia Foeminei Sexus* (1529).
- *De Triplici Ratione Cognoscendi Deum* (1515).

## Role in the tradition
Agrippa is the principal channel by which Pichian and Reuchlinian Christian Kabbalah, Ficinian Hermeticism, and the broader Renaissance magical synthesis enter the Northern European and English magical tradition. His three-worlds schema (elemental / celestial / intellectual = natural / celestial / ceremonial magic) became the standard organizing structure of Renaissance and post-Renaissance Western magic; the James Freake English translation (1651) made him available to the English-speaking esoteric tradition.
