# Abū Bakr al-Ṣiddīq

## Identity
**Abū Bakr ʿAbd Allāh ibn Abī Quḥāfa (c. 573 – 23 August 634 CE)**, of the Banū Taym clan of the Quraysh, was a Meccan cloth-merchant, the closest of [[muhammad-prophet|Muhammad's]] Companions, his father-in-law through [[aisha-bint-abi-bakr|ʿĀʾisha]], and — in the Sunni reckoning — the first of the four *Rāshidūn* or "Rightly Guided" caliphs (r. June 632 – August 634). He is *al-Ṣiddīq*, "the one who affirms as true", an honorific the Sīra ties to his immediate acceptance of the [[event-muhammads-miraj-619|Night Journey]] when others baulked. Sunni tradition makes him the first adult male convert; Shīʿī tradition assigns that place to [[ali-ibn-abi-talib|ʿAlī]]. He accompanied Muhammad alone on the [[event-hegira-622|emigration to Medina]] in 622, an episode the exegetical tradition universally reads into Q 9:40 — "the second of two, when the two were in the cave" — and the single most-cited Qurʾānic warrant for his precedence.

His caliphate lasted twenty-six months. In that time the Arabian tribal coalition that Muhammad had assembled came apart and was forced back together, the title and office of *khalīfa* were invented, the armies that would take Syria and Iraq were dispatched, and — according to the tradition — the first collection of the Qurʾān was ordered. Almost everything consequential about the institutional shape of Islam after 632 begins in those two years.

## The Saqīfa and the invention of the caliphate
On the day of Muhammad's death the Medinan *Anṣār* gathered at the covered courtyard of the Banū Sāʿida to name a leader of their own; [[umar-ibn-al-khattab|ʿUmar]] and Abū Bakr intervened, Abū Bakr argued that leadership must lie with the Meccan Quraysh, and the assembly gave him allegiance (*bayʿa*). [[ali-ibn-abi-talib|ʿAlī]] and the Prophet's clan were absent, occupied with the burial. See [[event-saqifa-bani-saida-632]] and [[event-death-of-muhammad-632]].

The office had no precedent and no name. Abū Bakr took *khalīfat rasūl Allāh* — "successor of the Messenger of God" — which claims political succession while conceding that prophecy has ended. That distinction is load-bearing for [[tradition-sunni-islam|Sunni]] political theology: the caliph inherits the Prophet's authority to govern and none of his authority to reveal or to define doctrine. Crone and Hinds (1986) argued that the early Umayyads in fact claimed considerably more than this — *khalīfat Allāh*, "deputy of God" — and that the modest Sunni formulation was retrofitted later by the scholars who had won the argument about religious authority; the debate over how early the limitation was real is live.

## The Ridda wars (632–633)
Muhammad's death detonated the Arabian settlement. Many tribes had entered treaty relations with him personally, and treated those relations as lapsing with his death; several repudiated the *[[ritual-zakat-charity|zakāt]]* levy while professing Islam; others followed rival prophets — Musaylima among the Banū Ḥanīfa in Yamāma, Ṭulayḥa among the Banū Asad, Sajāḥ among the Tamīm, al-Aswad al-ʿAnsī in Yemen. Abū Bakr refused every partial settlement, treating withholding of *zakāt* as secession from the community itself, and sent columns under Khālid ibn al-Walīd and others across the peninsula. The decisive engagement was **ʿAqrabāʾ at Yamāma (633)**, where Musaylima was killed and the Muslim losses among the *qurrāʾ* (Qurʾān-reciters) reportedly prompted the first collection of the text. Against advice, he also insisted on dispatching the Syrian expedition under Usāma ibn Zayd that Muhammad had ordered before his death.

By 633 the peninsula was reconsolidated, and the same armies were probing outward: Khālid into Sasanian Iraq (633), and the Syrian columns that fought at Ajnādayn in mid-634, weeks before Abū Bakr died. The conquests conventionally credited to [[umar-ibn-al-khattab|ʿUmar]] were begun under his predecessor.

**The source problem here is acute.** Almost the entire Ridda narrative in [[al-tabari|al-Ṭabarī]] comes through **Sayf ibn ʿUmar (d. c. 796)**, whom classical hadith critics graded weak and who is known to have supplied circumstantial detail and named informants generously. Landau-Tasseron (1990) shows that neither wholesale rejection nor uncritical use is defensible: Sayf preserves genuinely early material and also embroiders it, and the two cannot always be separated. Shoufani (1973) makes the further structural point that *ridda* — "apostasy" — is itself a retrospective label: the tribes' withdrawal was in most cases a political repudiation of a treaty relationship, and reclassifying it as religious defection was the act that turned a tribal confederation into a polity with a confessional boundary.

## The collection of the Qurʾān
Sunni tradition, in a report preserved in [[phase-5-007-sahih-al-bukhari|al-Bukhārī]], has ʿUmar urging Abū Bakr after Yamāma to gather the Qurʾān before its reciters died; Abū Bakr commissioned Zayd ibn Thābit, who assembled the material into *ṣuḥuf* (sheets) that passed to ʿUmar and then to his daughter Ḥafṣa, and that [[uthman|ʿUthmān]] used c. 650 for the recension that became the standard text.

Western assessments span the full range. John Burton (1977) argued that the collection reports are constructions of later jurists and that the text was already complete in the Prophet's lifetime; Wansbrough argued for a far later canonization; Harald Motzki (2001), applying *isnād-cum-matn* analysis, showed that the reports can be traced back at least to al-Zuhrī (d. 742) and are considerably older than the sceptical consensus had assumed — which is a claim about the age of the *tradition*, not proof of the event. The physical evidence ([[event-quranic-manuscript-finds-2015-onward]]) independently confirms that an editorial standardization under ʿUthmān was real, and that Companion codices with variant readings circulated before it; it says nothing either way about an Abū Bakr stage.

## Role in the tradition, and the Shīʿī counter-reading
For [[tradition-sunni-islam|Sunni Islam]] Abū Bakr is the paradigm of legitimate succession: chosen by the qualified members of the community, holding no share in prophecy, ruling briefly and austerely, and himself naming a successor rather than founding a dynasty. His having led the congregational prayer during Muhammad's final illness is the standard Sunni proof-text for his precedence; he is ranked first among the Companions, and Sunni piety treats criticism of him as an attack on the reliability of the whole Companion generation — which is precisely why the hadith corpus, transmitted by that generation, depends on his standing.

For [[tradition-shia-islam|Shīʿī Islam]] the same events read as usurpation. Muhammad had designated [[ali-ibn-abi-talib|ʿAlī]] at Ghadīr Khumm in March 632; Saqīfa was convened while the Prophet's family was at his grave; and Abū Bakr's refusal of [[fatima-bint-muhammad|Fāṭima's]] claim to the estate at **Fadak** — on the ground of a hadith that prophets leave no inheritance — is remembered as the first injury of the Imams' house. Wilferd Madelung's *The Succession to Muhammad* (1997) is the most important modern treatment because it is a non-Shīʿī critical historian's judgement that the textual evidence does favour an early expectation of ʿAlī's succession, and that Saqīfa is better described as a successful political manoeuvre than as an orderly consultation.

Neither reading is a report from 632. Both are 9th–10th-century constructions by communities that had by then become confessions, projected back onto a two-day political crisis — which is the reason the same handful of episodes (Saqīfa, Ghadīr Khumm, Fadak, the prayer-leadership) recur in both bodies of literature with incompatible framing and near-identical detail.

## Historical evidence
There is no contemporary documentary attestation naming Abū Bakr. What external evidence exists is for the *sequence*: 7th-century Syriac and Armenian chronicles register the succession of Arab leaders after Muhammad and the campaigns into [[byzantine-empire|Byzantine]] and [[sasanian-empire|Sasanian]] territory in exactly the years the Islamic sources give (Hoyland 1997). The [[islamic-hijri-calendar|Hijrī dating system]] is in administrative use within a decade of his death. His reign is thus securely located and thinly documented — a two-year caliphate reconstructed almost entirely from narrative material written down four to ten generations later.

He died at Medina on 23 August 634, having designated ʿUmar in writing, and was buried beside Muhammad in ʿĀʾisha's chamber, now within the Prophet's Mosque.

## Disputes
- **The legitimacy of Saqīfa** — the Sunni/Shīʿī fault-line in its original form; see above and [[event-first-fitna-656-661]], [[event-karbala-680]] for how it becomes armed conflict.
- **Fadak** — whether the Prophet's estate was heritable property or state *ṣadaqa*; a legal question in Sunni sources, a founding grievance in Shīʿī ones.
- **The Ridda source-chain** — Sayf ibn ʿUmar's reliability (Landau-Tasseron 1990), and whether "apostasy" is a description or a retrospective reclassification (Shoufani 1973).
- **The first Qurʾān collection** — Burton vs. Motzki vs. the manuscript evidence, as above.
- **How much authority the first caliphs actually claimed** — Crone & Hinds's *God's Caliph* thesis that the limited Sunni conception of the office is a later scholarly retrofit; contested, and important for how the caliphate is read.

## Cross-tradition connections
- **[[tradition-sunni-islam]] ↔ [[tradition-shia-islam]]** — the whole divergence turns on the evaluation of this man's elevation, which makes him the vault's cleanest case of a single political event generating two confessions.
- **Succession without designation, comparatively.** The 632 crisis is the Islamic instance of a problem every founder-tradition faces: what authority survives a founder who leaves no institutional mechanism. Compare the Jerusalem leadership after Jesus, the Buddhist councils after the Buddha's *parinirvāṇa*, and the Roman episcopate's later claim of apostolic succession — the same structural gap, resolved as election, as council, and as ordained lineage respectively. Sunnism resolved it as consensual acclamation; Shīʿism denied there was a gap at all.
- **[[phase-4-034-quran]] ↔ canon formation.** The *ṣuḥuf* narrative places the beginning of Qurʾānic codification within twenty years of the revelation's close — far tighter than the comparable intervals for the Hebrew Bible or the New Testament, and the reason Islamic canon-formation is testable against manuscripts where the others are not.
- **[[moral-islamic-sharia]]** — his rulings on *zakāt*, on the treatment of the seceding tribes and on the Prophet's estate are among the earliest cited precedents in Sunni jurisprudence, and enter the legal literature as Companion-practice (*āthār*) rather than as prophetic hadith.
