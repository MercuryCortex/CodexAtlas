# Darius I (the Great)

## Identity
Darius I (c. -550 to -486; *Dārayava(h)uš*, "Holding the Good") was the **third Achaemenid king of Persia** (after Cyrus the Great and Cambyses II), reigning -522 to -486 — **the systematizer of the Achaemenid imperial-religious-political administration** and **the most-extensively-documented pre-Hellenistic Iranian king** through his **Behistun Inscription** (the longest Achaemenid royal inscription, ~-518, in Old Persian + Elamite + Akkadian). Darius came to power through the **Gaumata-and-the-seven-noble conspiracy** of -522: when Cambyses II died returning from Egypt, a Magus named Gaumata claimed to be Cambyses's secretly-killed brother Bardiya/Smerdis and seized the throne; Darius and six other noble Persian houses conspired to assassinate Gaumata in -522 and Darius was selected as king. He spent the next ~5 years (-521 to -518) **suppressing nine major rebellions** across the empire — narrated in the Behistun Inscription. He systematized **imperial administration** (the *satrapy* system, the imperial postal-road *Royal Road* from Sardis to Susa, the standardized coinage including the gold *daric*), **religious-political legitimation** (his framing of cosmic-religious *arta-vs-drauga* metaphysical-political combat, see [[druj]]), and **Persian-Jewish relations** (the continuation of Cyrus's pro-Jewish policy — Ezra 6 records Darius's royal authorization for completing the Second Temple rebuilding). He died of natural causes -486 and was succeeded by his son Xerxes I.

## Historical evidence
Darius is exceptionally well-documented:
- **The Behistun Inscription** (DB, ~-518) — Darius's own first-person account of his accession + suppression of the nine rebels. **The single most-important Old Persian text.** Critical text in Schmitt 1991.
- **Persepolis inscriptions** (DPa, DPb, DPc, etc.) — extensive royal-architectural inscriptions at Darius's capital.
- **Naqsh-e Rustam tomb-inscription** (DNa, DNb) — Darius's tomb-inscription summarizing his reign + theological-political program.
- **Susa Foundation Tablets** — the famous Susa palace dedication-text listing materials and craftsmen from across the empire.
- **Egyptian inscriptions** — Darius rebuilt the temple of [[ptah]] at Memphis and is depicted in Egyptian pharaonic-iconographic style.
- **[[herodotus]]** *Histories* 3.61-160, 5-7 — extensive Greek narrative of Darius's reign (with the standard Herodotean caveats).
- **Ezra 4-6** — the Persian royal patronage of the Jerusalem Temple rebuilding.

## Works
**The Behistun Inscription (DB)** — the principal Darius-authored text. The inscription is **carved into the limestone cliff of Mount Behistun in western Iran**, 100+ meters above the road from Babylon to Ecbatana. The text proclaims Darius's legitimate kingship + his suppression of the nine rebel-kings + his religious-political legitimation under Ahura Mazda. The accompanying **rock-relief** depicts Darius standing over the prone Gaumata-pretender + nine bound rebel-kings standing in line awaiting punishment.

**The Behistun Inscription is the foundational text for the modern decipherment of cuneiform.** The trilingual Old Persian + Elamite + Akkadian text served the same function for cuneiform-decipherment that the Rosetta Stone did for hieroglyphic-Egyptian-decipherment: it provided a known starting-point (the Old Persian text was the first to be partially deciphered by Henry Rawlinson in the 1830s-50s) from which the Elamite + Akkadian could be reconstructed.

## Role in the tradition

### Zoroastrian imperial-religious systematization
Darius is **the first Achaemenid king to explicitly invoke Ahura Mazda systematically** in royal inscriptions — every major Darius-inscription begins with "Ahura Mazda is a great god; he created this earth; he created that sky; he created man; he created peace for man; he made Darius king." The systematic invocation of Ahura Mazda is **stronger and more theologically-systematic in Darius's inscriptions than in Cyrus's** — many scholars treat Darius as the principal Zoroastrian-imperial-religious systematizer.

### The *arta-vs-drauga* cosmic-political framing
Darius's Behistun Inscription **explicitly frames the suppression of the rebel-kings as the cosmic-religious defeat of *drauga* (the Lie, [[druj]]) by *arta* (the Truth, [[asha-vahishta]])**. The royal-political-religious deployment of cosmic-metaphysical-dualism for imperial-legitimation is **one of the earliest known cases** of this pattern — and a foundational template for subsequent imperial-religious legitimation across history (Sasanian, Byzantine-Christian, medieval-Christian Crusader, modern political-religious uses).

### Persian-Jewish relations: continuation of Cyrus
Darius continues Cyrus's pro-Jewish policy. Ezra 6 records Darius's **royal decree (DH = the Darius "Memorandum") authorizing the completion of the Second Temple rebuilding** (which had been delayed by local opposition). The Second Temple is dedicated in -515, under Darius's protection. The Persian-Jewish religious-cultural exchange that produces the foundational theological transformations of Second-Temple Judaism (cosmic dualism, angelology, demonology, resurrection-of-the-dead, future-Messiah figure) operates institutionally under Darius's administrative-religious patronage.

### The Magophonia and the Magi-Achaemenid relationship
After the Gaumata-the-Magus crisis (-522), Darius institutes an annual festival commemorating the killing of the Magi conspirators — Herodotus 3.79 calls it the **Magophonia** ("Magus-slaying"). The festival has been interpreted as evidence of substantial Median-Magi opposition to the Persian-Achaemenid Zoroastrian reform (the Magi being Median; the Achaemenid kings Persian); the Magophonia ritualizes the suppression of the Median priesthood and the consolidation of Achaemenid royal-religious authority. However: the [[the-magi|Magi]] subsequently emerge as the institutional priesthood of the Achaemenid Empire, so the religious-political conflict is more complex than a simple suppression.

### Egyptian and Indian patronage
Darius rebuilds the temple of Ptah at Memphis (his Egyptian pharaonic role); he commissions the Suez Canal connecting the Nile to the Red Sea; he extends the empire into the Indus Valley (the easternmost satrapies). His **religious-tolerance pattern** (continuing Cyrus's policy) is documentable across multiple religious-cultural-political contexts.

### Battle of Marathon (-490)
Darius's punitive expedition against Athens (in retaliation for Athenian support of the Ionian Revolt of -499-494) ends in **the Persian defeat at Marathon (-490)** — one of the most-famous battles in history, foundational to subsequent Greek self-identity. Darius dies before he can mount a second invasion; his son Xerxes I leads the second (and also defeated) invasion in -480.

## Disputes
- **Darius's relationship to Cyrus's family.** Darius is *not* directly descended from Cyrus the Great. His own genealogical claim (Behistun Inscription) traces him to Achaemenes through a side-branch (his father Hystaspes was a noble but not a king). Whether this represents (a) legitimate genealogical claim through a parallel-Achaemenid line, (b) opportunistic-fictional claim retroactively constructed to legitimate his usurpation, or (c) somewhere in between, is debated. Most modern scholarship treats the claim as substantially-constructed-after-the-fact; the practical-political legitimation succeeded regardless of genealogical accuracy.
- **The Gaumata-the-Magus narrative.** Whether Gaumata was actually a Magus impersonating the dead Bardiya (Darius's account), or actually the real Bardiya (whom Darius then murdered and slandered post-hoc), is one of the most-disputed cases in ancient history. Some modern scholars (Olmstead 1948 and others) favor the second reading. The Behistun Inscription is, after all, written by the winner.
- **Zoroastrian content of Darius's religion.** Whether Darius was a **strict Zoroastrian** (in the sense of Zarathustra-prophetic-tradition Zoroastrianism) or a **broader-Iranian-religious king** with eclectic religious policy is debated. Boyce 1982 argues substantially Zoroastrian; revisionists (Skjærvø, Kellens) argue more eclectic.
