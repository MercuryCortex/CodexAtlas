# Zhuangzi (Zhuang Zhou)

## Identity

**Zhuang Zhou** (莊周), known as **Zhuangzi**, "Master Zhuang," is the author of the seven Inner Chapters of the book that bears his name and the most formally inventive philosopher of the Warring States. Where the [[phase-2-014-daodejing]] is gnomic, impersonal and addressed to rulers, Zhuangzi writes fable, dialogue, tall tale, joke and lyric — a giant fish that becomes a bird whose back covers the sky, a butcher whose knife has not been sharpened in nineteen years, a butterfly that may be dreaming the man who dreamed it. His subject is what happens to knowledge, language and the self when you stop assuming your own point of view is the view.

He is not, in the Warring States, a "Daoist." That label is a Han-dynasty bibliographic invention (*daojia*, in the *Shiji* and the *Hanshu* catalogues) that retroactively bundled him with [[laozi]]. The pairing has held for two thousand years and is not misleading, but it is a later classification, and the Inner Chapters show no awareness of belonging to a school.

## Historical evidence

Sima Qian's *Shiji* 63 gives a short biography: Zhuang Zhou was a native of **Meng**, in the state of Song; he held a minor post at the lacquer grove (*qiyuan*) there; he was a contemporary of King Hui of Liang (r. -370 to -319) and King Xuan of Qi (r. -319 to -301); he wrote in over 100,000 words, mostly fable; and he refused an offer of high office from King Wei of Chu, saying he would rather play in the mud than be the pampered sacrificial ox.

The biography is thin, but the historicity is generally accepted, and for a specific reason: the Inner Chapters presuppose a real intellectual environment they do not have to explain. Zhuangzi argues against the **Mohists** ([[phase-2-025-mozi]]) and the Confucians ([[confucius]] appears constantly, sometimes as buffoon and sometimes as unexpected mouthpiece), and above all he argues with **Hui Shi** (Huizi), the logician and minister of Wei, who appears in the Inner Chapters as friend, foil and permanent irritant — the giant gourd and the useless tree in chapter 1, the debate over whether a human being has an "essential nature" in chapter 5. A fabricated sage does not get a specific, named, philosophically distinctive sparring partner whose own paradoxes are separately recorded (in chapter 33, *Tianxia*).

**A caution about the famous anecdotes.** The best-known Zhuang Zhou stories are *not* all in the chapters attributed to him. The **butterfly dream** (end of ch. 2) and **Cook Ding** (ch. 3) are Inner Chapters. But the debate with Hui Shi about the happiness of fish on the bridge over the Hao is chapter 17 (*Qiushui*), the drumming on a tub at his wife's death and the conversation with the roadside skull are both chapter 18 (*Zhile*), and the instruction to leave his corpse for the birds is chapter 32 — all **Outer or Miscellaneous** chapters, i.e. school lore about the master rather than the master's own writing. They are excellent evidence for how his followers understood him; they are not autobiography.

## The text and its strata

The received *Zhuangzi* has **33 chapters** in the redaction of **Guo Xiang** (d. 312 CE), who cut it down from a 52-chapter version recorded in the *Hanshu* bibliographic treatise and supplied the commentary through which almost all later readers met the book. The tripartite division is:

- **Inner Chapters (1–7)** — *Xiaoyaoyou* (Free and Easy Wandering), *Qiwulun* (Discussion on Equalizing Things), *Yangshengzhu* (The Principle of Nurturing Life), *Renjianshi* (In the Human World), *Dechongfu* (Signs of Fullness of Power), *Dazongshi* (The Great Ancestral Teacher), *Yingdiwang* (Fit for Emperors and Kings). Consistent in vocabulary, style and argument; attributed to Zhuang Zhou himself.
- **Outer Chapters (8–22)** and **Miscellaneous Chapters (23–33)** — accretion over the following 100–150 years, by several hands with different agendas.

**Graham 1981/1989** is the foundational stratification: alongside the Inner Chapters he distinguishes a "School of Zhuangzi," a **Primitivist** stratum (chs. 8–11, sharply anti-civilizational and datable by internal reference to the end of the Qin), a **Yangist** stratum (chs. 28–31, concerned with preserving one's own life and nature), and a **Syncretist** stratum (including ch. 33, *Tianxia*, the earliest surviving map of Warring States philosophy). **Liu Xiaogan 1994** reaches a broadly similar division on independent linguistic grounds. Both are mainstream; the disagreements are over allocation of particular chapters, not over the principle that the book is layered.

## The thought

**Perspective, and the collapse of shi/fei.** The core argument of chapter 2 is that every judgement of "this" (*shi* 是, also "right") is made from somewhere, and generates its "that" (*bi* 彼) and its "not-so" (*fei* 非) as a matter of grammar. Monkeys, fish, humans and deer disagree about the right place to sleep, the right thing to eat and what counts as beauty; there is no species-neutral seat from which to adjudicate. Disputation therefore cannot settle disputation: if you and I argue and you win, are you right? — bring in a third party, and they are either partisan or as ignorant as we are. The conclusion is not "nothing is true." It is that the fixed standpoint from which one *pronounces* is the problem, and that the sage "illuminates by the light of Heaven," using judgements without being owned by them.

**Wandering, uselessness, and the great tree.** *Xiaoyao you* — free and easy wandering, roaming without a fixed destination — is the positive counterpart. Its recurring image is **uselessness**: the enormous gnarled tree survives precisely because no carpenter wants it; the crippled man is exempted from conscription and fed by the state. Utility is a frame imposed from outside, and being unavailable to it is a form of freedom.

**Skill as the model of action.** **Cook Ding** (ch. 3) has cut up oxen for nineteen years without sharpening his blade, because he goes where the joints already are — "I meet it with my spirit and don't look with my eyes." The craft parables (also the swimmer in the cataract, the cicada-catcher, the wheelwright) are Zhuangzi's account of what expertise feels like from inside: deliberation drops away and responsiveness takes over. This is *wu-wei* made concrete and non-mystical — not doing nothing, but doing without the interference of the doer.

**Techniques of the self.** *Xinzhai* 心齋, "the fasting of the mind" (ch. 4): stop listening with the ears, then with the mind, and listen with *qi*, which is empty and waits on things. *Zuowang* 坐忘, "sitting and forgetting" (ch. 6): limbs and body dropped away, perception discarded, "identical with the Great Thoroughfare." These are among the earliest descriptions of contemplative practice in Chinese literature and are the textual root that later Daoist meditation and [[alchemy-chinese-neidan]] claim.

**Death and transformation.** The *zhenren* (true person) of chapter 6 does not know how to be pleased at life or hate death. Death is *wuhua* 物化, the transformation of things — the same process that produced you continuing to work. Chapter 6's dying men speculate cheerfully about what they will be made into next: a rooster's leg, a cartwheel.

**The butterfly.** At the end of chapter 2, Zhuang Zhou dreams he is a butterfly, entirely a butterfly, and wakes not knowing whether he is a man who dreamed a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming a man. The passage closes with the words *wuhua* — "this is what is meant by the transformation of things." It is not a puzzle about scepticism regarding the external world (the Cartesian reading). It is the perspectivism of the rest of the chapter applied to the perspective-holder: the "I" is one more standpoint among the things that transform.

## Reception and transmission

**Wei-Jin xuanxue.** From the third century the *Zhuangzi* is one of the "Three Mysteries" of [[philosophy-xuanxue]] (Dark Learning), alongside the [[phase-2-014-daodejing]] and the [[phase-2-042-yi-jing-i-ching]]. Guo Xiang's commentary — which reads *ziran* as each thing's self-generated, self-sufficient nature — becomes the standard metaphysics of the medieval Chinese elite.

**The Buddhist encounter.** Zhuangzi's vocabulary — *wu* (absence), *ziran*, *wang* (forgetting), the critique of names — was the conceptual clothing in which Indian Buddhist ideas were first heard in China, and Chinese Buddhists wrote in it before they wrote past it. The older textbook name for this is *geyi* ("matching concepts"), but **Mair 2010** argues that the standard account of *geyi* as a general translation method is a modern reconstruction rather than a medieval practice; the vault states the influence and flags the terminological caution rather than repeating the textbook story.

**Chan/Zen.** Chan's anti-textual rhetoric, its encounter dialogues, its skill-stories, its taste for the paradoxical, and its valorization of spontaneity over graduated cultivation are all continuous with the Zhuangzi, and the continuity is visible from the [[phase-5-004-platform-sutra-huineng]] onward. The honest form of the claim is *conditioning influence*, not derivation: Chan's doctrinal content is Buddhist (emptiness, buddha-nature, the Laṅkāvatāra and Prajñāpāramitā lines), while its **idiom, literary form and sensibility** are heavily Zhuangzian. John McRae and others have warned against the older tendency to describe Chan as "Daoism in Buddhist dress." See [[philosophy-chan-zen]], [[bodhidharma]], [[huineng]], [[dogen]].

**Canonization.** In 742 CE Emperor Xuanzong of Tang conferred on the book the title *Nanhua zhenjing* (True Scripture of Southern Florescence) and on Zhuang Zhou the title *Nanhua zhenren* (True Person of Southern Florescence). A philosopher who spent his life mocking official honours received the highest one the state could give.

**Literati self-image.** For the next millennium the Zhuangzi is the charter text of the eccentric recluse — the poet who will not serve, the drunk, the mountain hermit, the official who resigns. It is the private half of the Chinese elite's double life, the Confucian classics being the public half; the [[tradition-sanjiao]] settlement institutionalizes the arrangement.

**To the modern West.** Herbert Giles (1889) and James Legge (1891) put it into English; **Oscar Wilde** reviewed Giles in 1890 with delight at finding an ancient Chinese anarchist; [[martin-buber]] published German selections in 1910, and his reading fed into *I and Thou*; Thomas Merton's *The Way of Chuang Tzu* (1965) carried it into Catholic contemplative circles (see [[consciousness-merton-contemplative-zen-christian]]); Graham, Watson and Ziporyn made it a standard text in Anglophone philosophy departments.

## Disputes

- **Historicity.** Generally accepted. The *Shiji* notice is brief but coherent, and the Inner Chapters' engagement with named contemporaries (above all Hui Shi) is hard to explain as invention.
- **Dates.** The conventional -369 to -286 is a reconstruction, not a record; the *period* — late Warring States, contemporary with Mencius, older than Xunzi — is secure.
- **Which chapters are his.** Graham 1981 and Liu 1994 both isolate the Inner Chapters as authorial; the exact boundaries of the later strata remain argued.
- **Mystic or skeptic?** The long-running interpretive split. **Hansen** and **Graham** read the *Qiwulun* as a rigorous argument about language and perspective, closer to a therapeutic scepticism than to mysticism. Others (Roth, and the whole received tradition) read the *xinzhai* and *zuowang* passages as descriptions of an attained contemplative state that the perspectivism serves. The text sustains both, and the Kjellberg & Ivanhoe volume (1996) is the standard place where the argument is conducted.
- **Relativist or not?** A further split within the skeptical reading: whether Zhuangzi holds that no perspective is better than any other (hard relativism), or that the *sage's* perspective — the one that has stopped absolutizing — is genuinely better, which would make the position self-undermining if pushed. Most current readers take the second horn and treat the asymmetry as deliberate.

## Cross-tradition position

### MASSIVE WIN 1 — Zhuangzi ↔ Nāgārjuna: two independent dismantlings of the fixed position

[[nagarjuna]] (c. 150–250 CE), five centuries later and a subcontinent away, executes structurally the same manoeuvre by different means. The *Qiwulun* argues that every fixed standpoint generates its own opposite and cannot ground itself; the *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* ([[phase-4-075-mulamadhyamakakarika]]) argues that every posited inherent nature (*svabhāva*) collapses under analysis. Both refuse to replace the demolished position with a new one; both insist the result is not nihilism; both take the outcome to be liberation from a way of holding views rather than the acquisition of a better view.

The differences are as instructive as the match. Nāgārjuna's instrument is formal *prasaṅga* — a dialectical machine; Zhuangzi's is fable and ridicule. Nāgārjuna's resolution is the Two Truths and dependent origination ([[doctrine-pratitya-samutpada-dependent-origination]], [[emptiness-sunyata]]); Zhuangzi's is *you* — roaming, going along with the transformation of things. This is a **Parallel** edge, not Transmission: there is no contact mechanism. Its real historical consequence came later, when Chinese readers who already knew the Zhuangzi met Madhyamaka in translation and found it legible — which is a genuine Transmission effect *downstream* of the parallel. The reciprocal wire is carried on [[nagarjuna]].

### MASSIVE WIN 2 — Zhuangzi ↔ Pyrrho: skeptical strategy, East and West

[[pyrrho-of-elis]] (c. -360 to -270) is Zhuangzi's near-exact contemporary. Both deploy arguments from the disagreement of perceivers (what is beautiful to one animal is repellent to another; what tastes right to one creature poisons another), both use the impossibility of a neutral criterion, and both take the *practical* payoff to be the release of anxiety rather than a doctrine — Pyrrhonian *ataraxia* and Zhuangzi's freedom from being ruled by right-and-wrong. Lisa Raphals and others have set the *Qiwulun* beside Greek skeptical strategy in detail (Kjellberg & Ivanhoe 1996).

The relationship must be tiered carefully. The **structural parallel is T1 and secure**. A **transmission** claim is not: the suggestion that Pyrrho's Indian encounter during Alexander's campaign carried Indian sceptical or śramaṇa material west has serious academic defenders (Christopher Beckwith's *Greek Buddha*, 2015, argues it strongly) but is a **minority position (T2)**, and no scholar proposes a route from *China* to Pyrrho. Wire the parallel; do not wire a transmission. See [[philosophy-pyrrhonian-skepticism]].

### MASSIVE WIN 3 — Cook Ding and the annihilated agent

The craft parables describe an agency in which the practitioner acts with total responsiveness while the deliberating, crediting self has dropped out of the way. The same shape recurs in traditions with no contact with China: Sufi *fanāʾ* (the passing-away of the self, after which the act is God's act), Eckhart's *Gelassenheit* and his "living without why" ([[meister-eckhart]], [[the-godhead-eckhart]], [[phase-5-028-meister-eckhart-sermons]]), and — downstream and by genuine transmission — the Zen accounts of practice as non-instrumental activity, most rigorously in [[dogen]]'s *shushō ittō*.

Two honest distinctions. Zhuangzi's cook is not annihilated *before God*; there is no God in the picture, and the "spirit" he meets the ox with is his own. And the parable is about **acquired skill** — nineteen years of oxen — not about grace. The parallel is at the level of the phenomenology of expert action and the disappearance of the self-monitoring agent, and should be wired as **Parallel**, with the theological asymmetry stated. See [[consciousness-zazen-shikantaza-just-sitting]].

### The butterfly and the states-of-consciousness traditions

The butterfly dream is regularly set beside Indian analyses of waking, dreaming and dreamless sleep (the Māṇḍūkya scheme and its *turīya*, the "fourth"), and the pairing is illuminating — both destabilize the assumption that the waking standpoint is privileged. But the destinations differ sharply: the Indian schemes posit a witnessing consciousness that persists through all three states and is the real self, whereas Zhuangzi's passage ends on *wuhua*, transformation, with no persisting witness at all. On this point Zhuangzi is closer to Buddhist *anātman* than to Vedāntic *ātman*. See [[non-duality]], [[doctrine-reincarnation-samsara]].
