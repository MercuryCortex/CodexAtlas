# Antoine Court de Gébelin

## Identity

**Antoine Court de Gébelin** (b. Nîmes, c. 1725 — some sources give 1719; d. Paris, 10 May 1784) was a Huguenot pastor, Enlightenment comparative philologist, Freemason and man of the Parisian salons. He was the son of **Antoine Court**, the pastor who rebuilt French Protestantism underground after the Revocation of the Edict of Nantes, and he inherited both his father's institutional work and a scholar's ambition of a very different kind.

That ambition was ***Le Monde primitif, analysé et comparé avec le monde moderne*** (9 vols., Paris 1773–1782): an attempt to reconstruct, from comparative grammar, myth, heraldry, coinage and calendar, a single lost primeval civilisation whose wisdom he believed survived in fragments across every later culture. It is a characteristic Enlightenment project — universalist, encyclopedic, and methodologically catastrophic. Its etymologies are free-associative; its comparative method has no controls; its conclusions were reached before its evidence.

In **volume 8 (1781)** Court de Gébelin included a short essay, ***Du Jeu des Tarots***. It is the reason he is in this vault.

## The 1781 claim

Court de Gébelin reports that at a friend's house he was shown a pack of tarot cards, and recognised in them — immediately, by his own account — a survival of ancient Egypt. His thesis:

1. The 78-card pack is the lost **Book of Thoth**, an Egyptian priestly book preserved in card form after the temples fell.
2. The 22 trumps encode Egyptian religious doctrine; the Popess is the high priestess of Isis, the Chariot is Osiris triumphant, and so on.
3. The word *tarot* derives from Egyptian *tar* ("way", "road") and *ro / rog* ("royal") — the **"royal road"** of initiation.
4. The pack was carried into Europe by the people then called Bohemians (the Roma) out of Egypt.

Every one of these is false, and their falsity is not a matter of interpretation:

- **The Egyptian script was undeciphered.** Champollion's *Lettre à M. Dacier* is 1822 — **41 years after** the essay. Court de Gébelin's Egyptian etymologies were not weak; they were unavailable. He invented them. See [[jean-francois-champollion]].
- **The documentary record runs the other way.** Playing cards enter Europe in the 1370s from the Mamluk sultanate; the trump suit is added in northern Italy in the 1420s–1440s; the earliest secure reference to *carte da trionfi* is the Ferrara court ledger of **1442** (Dummett 1980). See [[visconti-sforza-tarocchi-deck]].
- **There is no divinatory tarot before the 18th century.** Roughly three hundred years separate the invention of the pack from the first evidence of anyone reading fortunes with it (Decker, Depaulis & Dummett 1996).
- **The Roma transmission claim is chronologically impossible and ethnographically unsupported.** Roma arrival in western Europe is documented from the early 15th century — after cards were already circulating — and the sources that do record Roma divination in the 16th century record **palmistry**, not cards (Decker, Depaulis & Dummett 1996; and see [[palmistry-chiromancy]]).

## Why the claim mattered anyway

Court de Gébelin was not a charlatan and was not selling decks; he was a serious, credulous, systematically wrong scholar of a kind the 18th century produced in quantity. What made his essay decisive was **timing and vacancy**.

[[isaac-casaubon]]'s 1614 redating of the *Corpus Hermeticum* had demolished the Renaissance belief that Hermetic wisdom predated Moses (see [[event-casaubon-redates-hermetica-1614]]). Western esotericism had spent the intervening century and a half looking for a new foundation of claimed antiquity — one that philology could not reach. Court de Gébelin supplied one: **an object with no texts attached to it**, whose meaning therefore could not be philologically dated. A deck of cards cannot be redated by its Greek.

Within two years [[etteilla-jean-baptiste-alliette]] — already a professional cartomancer working with ordinary playing cards — adopted the Egyptian frame and built the first divinatory tarot system on it. Within seventy-five years [[eliphas-levi]] had bolted the pack to the Hebrew alphabet and the sefirotic tree. The Egyptian story never acquired any evidence, and it never needed any.

He died in Paris on 10 May 1784, while undergoing treatment from Franz Anton Mesmer, whose animal magnetism he had publicly endorsed.

## Role in the tradition

- **Originator of the tarot's false antiquity.** Every esoteric tarot lineage — French (Etteilla → Lévi → [[papus]]) and English ([[samuel-liddell-macgregor-mathers]] → [[arthur-edward-waite]] → [[aleister-crowley]]) — descends from the 1781 essay.
- **A case of an invented tradition with a fully documented invention.** The vault holds many contested-origin claims. This one is not contested: the inventor is named, the date is fixed, the publication survives, and the counter-evidence is a court account book.
- **A negative anchor for methodology.** Court de Gébelin illustrates the vault's working distinction between a *practice* (real, living, meaningful to practitioners) and an *origin claim* (documented false). Holding both at once, without collapsing either, is the discipline the CODEX exists to enforce.

## Disputes

- **Birth year.** 1719 and 1725 both circulate; the Nîmes parish record is not decisive. Death date (10 May 1784, Paris) is secure.
- **How much of the essay is his.** The tarot material in volume 8 is *two* essays: Court de Gébelin's own, and a second by [[comte-de-mellet]] which he printed with it. The Hebrew-alphabet correspondence — the seed of the entire later Kabbalistic tarot — is **de Mellet's, not his**. Popular accounts routinely merge them and credit Court de Gébelin with both. Decker, Depaulis & Dummett 1996 separate them.
- **Whether he believed it.** Nothing in the record suggests otherwise. His whole nine-volume project rests on the same method; the tarot essay is not an aberration within it but a specimen of it.
