# Inês de Castro (~1325–1355)

## Historical life
Inês de Castro was born in Galicia (~1325) to a noble Castilian-Galician family. She came to Portugal as a lady-in-waiting to Constança Manuel, who married Prince Pedro (the future Pedro I of Portugal) in 1340. Pedro fell in love with Inês — and the relationship continued even after Constança's death (1345), with Inês bearing Pedro four children.

The relationship was a political problem: Inês was a foreigner, and her brothers were powerful Castilian nobles with political ambitions in Portugal. King Afonso IV, Pedro's father, ordered her removal from court and eventually — after a failed attempt at clemency — signed her death warrant. On **January 7, 1355**, three royal councillors entered the Quinta das Lágrimas ("Estate of Tears") outside Coimbra and stabbed Inês to death.

Pedro rose in revolt. Civil war lasted two years until his father died (1357). When Pedro became king, he:
1. Had two of the three assassins extradited from Castile and executed with particular cruelty (he personally participated)
2. **Declared that he had secretly married Inês** (whether this was true is disputed; witnesses came forward)
3. **Had her corpse exhumed and coronated**, dressing it in royal robes and forcing the court to kiss her lifeless hand as their queen
4. Erected an elaborate Gothic double tomb at the Monastery of Alcobaça — where both bodies lie to this day, feet facing each other, so they will see each other first at the resurrection

## Camões's treatment (Os Lusíadas, Canto III)
Camões narrates the murder of Inês within the historical survey of Canto III. He gives her a speech before her executioners that became one of the most quoted passages in Portuguese literature — "Quem pode ser no mundo tão cruel / Que contra uma fraca mulher se arme?" ("Who in the world can be so cruel / As to arm himself against a weak woman?"). The flowers, the children, the innocent landscape of Coimbra — all brought as witnesses to her plea for mercy.

For Camões, Inês represents **sacrificial love destroyed by power** — the dark underside of the imperial glory narrative. The same king who began the line that produced the Discoveries also ordered the murder of the woman his son loved. The epic holds both truths without resolving the tension.

## Mythological dimension
Inês's story accumulated mythological qualities rapidly:
- The **Quinta das Lágrimas** ("Estate of Tears") in Coimbra preserves a spring said to be reddened by her blood — a toponym that literalizes the body-becomes-landscape motif (cf. [[adamastor]], [[boann]])
- The **Fonte dos Amores** (Fountain of Love) at the same site became a pilgrimage destination
- The **coronation of the corpse** — Pedro forcing the court to pay homage to a dead woman — is one of the most extreme acts of posthumous love in documented European history; it directly parallels the mythology of the **undying beloved** (Alcestis, Eurydice, the sovereign who cannot accept death as permanent)
- Her tomb at Alcobaça became one of the sacred sites of Portuguese identity; during the Napoleonic invasion (1811), French troops desecrated the tomb — an act treated by Portuguese contemporaries as sacrilege against the nation itself

## The structural myth
Inês de Castro is the Portuguese instance of the universal **sacred-love-destroyed-by-power** archetype — the beloved murdered not for her own fault but as a political sacrifice. Like Alcestis (died for love), like Iphigenia (sacrificed for the political-military mission), like the many queens of medieval romance who die because their love threatens the political order, Inês is the price Portugal paid for becoming what it became. Camões makes this explicit: the Discoveries have a hidden cost, written in the blood of Coimbra.
