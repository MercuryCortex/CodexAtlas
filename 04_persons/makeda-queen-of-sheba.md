# Makeda, Queen of Sheba

## Identity
**Makeda** is the name given by Ethiopian tradition to the **Queen of Sheba** of biblical fame — the southern monarch who, in 1 Kings 10 and 2 Chronicles 9, travels to Jerusalem to test Solomon's wisdom, exchanges gifts and questions, and returns to her own land. In the Ethiopian *[[phase-8-008-kebra-nagast]]* (~1314–1322 redaction of much older traditional material), she is Queen of Aksum, conceives a son by Solomon, and gives birth on her return journey to **Menelik I**, who as a young man returns to Jerusalem, is acknowledged by Solomon, and brings the **Ark of the Covenant** back to Aksum, where it remains. This narrative is the foundational national-religious-genealogical myth of Ethiopia.

The same queen figures across major Mediterranean and Near Eastern traditions under different names:

- **Hebrew Bible** — unnamed; "the Queen of Sheba." (1 Kings 10, 2 Chr 9.)
- **Targum Sheni to Esther** — elaborated wisdom-test narrative with riddles.
- **New Testament** — *the Queen of the South* (Mt 12:42 / Lk 11:31): Jesus's positive reference to her seeking wisdom.
- **Josephus, *Antiquities* 8.165–175** — *Nikaule*, "Queen of Egypt and Ethiopia."
- **Origen, *Commentary on the Song of Songs*** — christianizes the queen typologically as the Gentile-church seeking Christ.
- **Quran 27 (Surah an-Naml, vv. 15–44)** — anonymous in the Quran itself; called *Bilqis* in tafsir tradition (al-Tabari, Ibn Kathir).
- **Ethiopian tradition** — *Makeda*, Queen of Aksum-Saba.

The Sheba name itself maps onto two ancient kingdoms: **South Arabian Saba** (Sabaean kingdom in Yemen, well-attested archaeologically and epigraphically from at least the 8th c. BCE) and the **Aksumite-Ethiopian highland polity** (which derives its Semitic linguistic culture from Sabaean immigration across the Red Sea; the South Arabian and Ethiopian Sabas are linguistically and culturally continuous). Modern scholarship is comfortable describing the original Queen-of-Sheba traditions as referring to the South Arabian Sabaean kingdom; Ethiopian tradition appropriates and re-locates the narrative onto Aksum via the Solomonic-genealogical claim.

## Historical evidence
Makeda is a **legendary** figure: no contemporary 10th-c. BCE evidence attests her specifically. The Sabaean kingdom of which she would be queen is archaeologically attested but the queen's historicity is undocumentable. The narrative is best understood as a foundational-mythic complex around which multiple traditions (Hebrew, Sabaean, Aksumite, Ethiopian-Christian, Islamic) cluster.

None.

## Role in the tradition
Makeda is, with Menelik I, the foundational figure of Ethiopian dynastic-religious self-understanding. The Solomonic dynasty (restored 1270 under Yekuno Amlak) — and through 1974 with [[wolega-tafari-makonnen-haile-selassie]] — claimed direct genealogical descent from Solomon through Menelik I (her son). The *[[phase-8-008-kebra-nagast]]* makes the Ark-translation from Jerusalem to Aksum the constitutive event of Ethiopia's identity as the *new* chosen people. In Ethiopian iconography Makeda appears in royal-portraiture style with attributes of queenly authority; her image circulates in modern Ethiopian national-historical imagery and is iconic.

The Rastafarian movement, drawing on the Kebra Nagast genealogy, treats the Solomon-Makeda-Menelik lineage as authenticating Haile Selassie's messianic role — Makeda functions in Rastafarian theology as the Black-African foundational matriarch of the Davidic-Solomonic line.

## Disputes
- **Historicity.** Legendary; no contemporary attestation. The underlying historical core may reflect Sabaean-Israelite trading-diplomatic contact in the 10th c. BCE — Sabaean inscriptions name queens (e.g., the Sabaean queens of the *Annals of Sennacherib* and other ANE sources) and trade between Saba and the Levant is archaeologically attested — but the named individual is not recoverable.
- **South Arabian Saba vs. Aksumite Saba.** Modern Sabaean studies generally treats the historical Sheba as South Arabian; Ethiopian tradition treats it as Aksumite-trans-Red-Sea. Both readings have some basis: the Sabaean-Aksumite cultural-linguistic continuum is real, and the Aksumite polity's South-Arabian origins place it within a broader "Sabaean" cultural world.
- **Identity with the Quranic Bilqis.** Identification is by Islamic tafsir tradition, not by Quranic text itself; the Quranic queen of Saba is anonymous.
