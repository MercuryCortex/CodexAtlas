# Josiah

## Identity
Josiah was the 16th king of Judah (~640–609 BCE), brought to the throne at age 8 after the assassination of his father Amon. His reign coincided with the collapse of the Neo-Assyrian Empire (Ashurbanipal died ~627; Nineveh fell 612), opening space for a Judahite religious-political restoration. In his 18th regnal year (~622 BCE), a "Book of the Law" was reportedly discovered during temple repairs (2 Kings 22); Josiah responded with the most thoroughgoing religious reform in Judah's recorded history — centralizing all sacrificial worship at the Jerusalem Temple, destroying the *bāmôt* ("high places") and outlying sanctuaries (including the major northern sanctuary at Bethel), suppressing the cults of Baal, Asherah, the host of heaven, child-sacrifice at Topheth, and the Asherah-image in the Jerusalem Temple itself. He died at Megiddo at age 39, killed by Pharaoh Necho II as Egypt marched to support the rump Assyrian state at Carchemish.

## Historical evidence
2 Kings 22–23 and 2 Chronicles 34–35 give detailed narrative; Jeremiah praises his justice (Jer 22:15–16); Zephaniah's prophetic activity is dated to his reign (Zeph 1:1). The Egyptian campaign against Carchemish that resulted in his death is confirmed by the Babylonian Chronicle.

## Works
None authored. Patron of the discovery and promulgation of what is widely identified as the core of Deuteronomy ([[phase-2-011-hebrew-bible-d-source]]).

## Role in the tradition
The Deuteronomistic Historian (compiling Joshua–2 Kings in something like the form we have it) treats Josiah as the high point of Judahite kingship, the second David — explicitly: "Before him there was no king like him, who turned to YHWH with all his heart and with all his soul and with all his might, according to all the Law of Moses; nor did any like him arise after him" (2 Kings 23:25). His sudden death at Megiddo posed a theological crisis: the reform did not save Judah, and within 23 years the Babylonians destroyed Jerusalem.

## Disputes
- **Scope and historicity of the reform.** Some scholars (Lemche, Davies in the minimalist school) doubt the reform's depth or even historicity; most accept a substantial reform.
- **Date and identity of the "Book of the Law".** Mainstream view: a form of Deuteronomy 12–26 ("Ur-Deuteronomium"). Whether composed for the occasion (de Wette 1805 onward) or genuinely recovered remains disputed.
