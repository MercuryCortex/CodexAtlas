# Tiresias

## Identity
Tiresias (Greek **Τειρεσίας**, *Teiresias*) is the blind seer of Thebes — arguably the most famous *mantis* (seer) of Greek legend. He is the son of Everes and the nymph Chariclo, descended through Oudaios from the Spartoi, the "Sown Men" who sprang from the dragon's teeth at Thebes' founding, and the mythological tradition stretches his life across **seven generations** of the Theban saga, from the city of Cadmus down to its fall to the Epigoni. He has no historicity; he is a figure of the epic and tragic tradition, fixed for us in two great literary surfaces — the *Odyssey*'s Nekyia (c. 700 BCE) and fifth-century Athenian tragedy — with a myth-complex (blinding, gender-change, prodigious lifespan) elaborated around him from the pseudo-Hesiodic *Melampodia* to Callimachus and Ovid. In the Atlas he is placed among the legendary persons (on the model of [[orpheus]] and [[odysseus]]) and matters to two hubs at once: he is the consulted shade at the centre of the vault's Greek necromancy material, and the Greek instance of the prophet-vs-king pattern.

Two aetiologies of his blindness circulated in antiquity, and they cannot be harmonised. In the version Callimachus tells (*Hymn* 5, "The Bath of Pallas"), the young Tiresias accidentally saw [[athena]] bathing; the goddess blinded him, then — for his mother Chariclo's sake — compensated him with the seercraft, the understanding of birds, a staff to walk with, and the privilege of keeping his mind among the dead. In the version going back to the pseudo-Hesiodic *Melampodia* (and told by Apollodorus 3.6.7 and Ovid, *Met.* 3.316-338), Tiresias struck a pair of coupling snakes and was changed into a woman; seven years later, striking them again, he was changed back. Called to arbitrate the quarrel of [[zeus]] and [[hera]] over whether man or woman has the greater share of love's pleasure, he answered from experience of both — against Hera — and the goddess blinded him, while Zeus gave him prophecy and long life in recompense. Both stories turn on the same exchange: the loss of outward sight purchased inward sight — the pattern that makes "the blind seer" a fixed figure of the Western imagination.

## In the Odyssey — the shade who keeps his wits (Od. 10-11)
Tiresias' epic role is unique: he is the reason for the Nekyia. Circe tells Odysseus he cannot sail home before he has consulted "the Theban Tiresias, the blind seer" — for to Tiresias alone, even in death, Persephone has granted an unimpaired mind (*noos*), while the other dead flit about as shadows (Od. 10.490-495). This grant is the hinge of the whole episode: Homeric shades are witless until they drink sacrificial blood, but Tiresias remains a *seer* on the far side of death — the one point of intact prophetic consciousness in the Homeric underworld.

At the edge of Ocean, Odysseus digs the pit (*bothros*), pours the libations, and bleeds the black sheep into it, holding the crowding shades off with his sword until the seer arrives, bearing a golden staff (Od. 11.23-96). Tiresias drinks and prophesies (11.90-151): the wrath of [[poseidon]] for the blinding of Polyphemus; the fatal temptation of the cattle of Helios on Thrinacia (11.104-115); the suitors in his hall; and beyond the homecoming, the strange inland penance — Odysseus must carry an oar until he reaches a people who mistake it for a winnowing-fan, plant it there, and sacrifice to Poseidon (11.119-131) — before a gentle death comes to him "away from the sea" in sleek old age (11.134-137; the phrase *ex halos* was disputed already in antiquity). Tiresias also states the mechanics of the rite: whichever shade Odysseus allows to the blood will speak truth to him (11.146-149). The scene is the ritual template of Greco-Roman necromancy (Ogden 2001), and Tiresias is its paradigm: the dead prophet whose knowledge outlasts death and must be ritually enabled to speak.

## In Theban tragedy — the seer against the king
Fifth-century Athens made Tiresias the recurring counter-figure to Theban royal power. In Sophocles' *Oedipus Tyrannus* (300-462) the king summons the seer to name Laius' killer; Tiresias at first refuses to speak, and when Oedipus' taunts loose his tongue, he names the king himself as the land's pollution. Oedipus answers with the standing accusations against the mantic craft — that the seer is a paid conspirator, that his art is fraud — and Tiresias exits on the riddle of the killer who will be found brother and father at once. In *Antigone* (988-1090) he comes to Creon with a technical report of failed divination — birds screaming unintelligibly, burnt offerings that will not catch — as evidence that the gods reject a city polluted by the unburied dead; Creon repeats the money-taunt, and Tiresias prophesies the death of the king's own son. In both plays the pattern is identical: the seer is summoned or arrives, is abused by the throne, speaks the unwelcome divine truth, and is vindicated by the catastrophe. Euripides keeps the figure in the same posture — warning Pentheus, beside old Cadmus, to receive [[dionysus]] (*Bacchae* 170 ff.), and requiring Menoeceus' sacrifice from Creon (*Phoenician Women* 834 ff.).

The tradition rounds off his legend at the fall of Thebes: fleeing the Epigoni, he drank from the spring Tilphussa and died (Apollodorus 3.7.3); his daughter Manto was dedicated at Delphi and became the mother of the seer Mopsus. Plutarch records an oracle of Tiresias at Boeotian Orchomenos that had fallen silent after a plague (*De defectu oraculorum*) — a minor cult-trace, not a major cult, which is why he is typed here as a legendary person rather than a divinized hero. His afterlife in reception is long: Dante places him among the twisted diviners of *Inferno* 20, and T.S. Eliot made him the presiding consciousness of *The Waste Land*, expressly because he had been both man and woman — Eliot's note quotes Ovid's sex-change episode — and, implicitly, because he had walked among the dead.

## Cross-tradition significance
Tiresias completes two comparative slots, and the honest framing differs for each.

**The consulted dead prophet.** The Nekyia's Tiresias stands structurally beside the raised **Samuel** of 1 Samuel 28 ([[samuel-prophet]]): in both scenes a leader in extremity, directed by a ritual intermediary (Circe; the medium of En-dor), summons a dead prophet who must be enabled to speak and who answers with the consulter's fate. The parallel is old, striking, and — on the necromancy hub's own verdict — **typological rather than genetic**: the premise that the dead know hidden things is a near-universal inference from the cult of the dead, and no textual chain binds En-dor to the *Odyssey*. The narrow transmission window is real but modest: ritual ghost-summoning is attested in Mesopotamia centuries before Homer (Finkel's *eṭemmu* rituals), West (1997) sets the Nekyia within a broadly West Asiatic literary ambit, and the pit-evocation has Anatolian ritual analogues — so a diffuse Near-Eastern-to-Greek current of necromantic *technique* is plausible without being demonstrable. What is distinctively Greek is the Persephone-grant: only in the Homeric scene is the consulted prophet singled out as the *one* dead mind still intact.

**The prophet against the king.** Tiresias-vs-Oedipus and Tiresias-vs-Creon supply the Greek member of the [[prophet-and-king]] pattern whose sharpest form is Hebrew (Samuel-Saul, Nathan-David, Elijah-Ahab) and whose Iranian form is Zarathustra against the *kavi*-kings. Here no one argues transmission at all: the parallel is purely **structural convergence** — independent dramatisations of the collision between claimed divine knowledge and royal will. And the differences matter as much as the fit: the Greek *mantis* is a sign-reading religious craftsman, an interpreter of birds and burnt offerings embedded in a polytheist civic order (Flower 2008), not a covenant prosecutor announcing a jealous God's judgment; the tragic seer wins the argument through plot, not through a rival institution. The hub's own hedge — the seer is "not exactly a prophet in the Hebrew sense" — is correct, and this node keeps it.

## Disputes
- **The two blinding aetiologies.** The Athena-bath version (Callimachus, following Pherecydes) and the snakes-and-Hera version (*Melampodia*, Apollodorus, Ovid) are rival ancient variants, not stages of one story; Brisson (1976) reads the complex structurally — blindness, double sex, and mantic knowledge as linked figures of mediation — rather than seeking an original.
- **Does Odyssey 11 reflect real necromantic practice?** Whether the Nekyia mirrors an existing Greek (or imported Near Eastern) rite of consulting the dead, or whether the later Greek *nekromanteia* grew up partly in imitation of the famous epic scene, is a live question Ogden (2001) treats directly; the direction of influence between text and practice cannot be fixed.
- **Seer vs prophet.** How far the Greek *mantis* may be assimilated to the Hebrew *nabi* for comparative purposes is debated; Flower (2008) warns against modernising the seer into a "prophet" of conscience, while the structural parallel of the confrontation scenes remains the reason the prophet-and-king hub lists him.
- **Historicity.** None; nor is the seven-generation lifespan a datum to rationalise — it is the tradition's device for making one seer available to every layer of the Theban saga.
