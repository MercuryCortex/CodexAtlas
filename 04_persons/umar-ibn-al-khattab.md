# ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb

## Identity
**ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb al-ʿAdawī al-Qurashī (~584–644 CE)** — known by the canonical Islamic honorific **al-Fārūq** ("the Distinguisher between right and wrong"), and conventionally called *Amīr al-Muʾminīn* ("Commander of the Believers" — a caliphal title that ʿUmar himself instituted) — is the **second of the four Rashidun ("Rightly Guided") caliphs** (r. 634-644), the architect of the **first Arab-Muslim imperial expansion** (the conquest of Sasanian Persia, Byzantine Syria, Palestine, and Egypt within his ten-year caliphate), and one of the most consequential individual figures in the founding period of Islam. A late convert (~616), initially a fierce opponent of Muhammad before his conversion narrative (one of the canonical Sira-tradition conversion-stories — ʿUmar approaches his sister's house to attack her for converting, hears Quran recitation, converts on the spot), ʿUmar became one of Muhammad's closest companions in Medina, the father of Muhammad's wife Ḥafṣa, and the **principal architect of Sunni political-administrative practice** in the immediate post-Muhammad period.

Under his caliphate ʿUmar **instituted the Hijri calendar** (c. 638, with 622 = 1 AH as Year 1), **founded the *dīwān*-administration** (the central register-system for the conquered territories — fiscally and administratively organizing the conquered Sasanian and Byzantine territories on Sasanian-Byzantine bureaucratic precedents), **established the Amīr al-Muʾminīn title**, organized the first Arab-Muslim garrison-cities (Baṣra, Kūfa, Fusṭāṭ) in the conquered territories, and presided over the **transformation of the early Muslim community from a Hijazi tribal-religious movement into an inter-continental imperial polity**. He was **assassinated in Medina in 644** by Abū Luʾluʾa, a Persian Christian slave, in the Masjid al-Nabawī during morning prayer — the first of the major political assassinations that culminate in [[event-first-fitna-656-661|the First Fitna]] under his successors. (Kennedy 2007; Donner 2010.)

## Historical evidence
Documented extensively. ʿUmar appears in massive volume in the Sunni hadith, Sira, and historical-biographical literature (Ṭabarī, Ibn Saʿd, Balādhurī). Independent non-Muslim contemporary attestation: **the Doctrina Iacobi (Christian apocalyptic, 634)** mentions Arab conquests under "ʿUmar" by name — one of the earliest non-Muslim attestations of a named figure in early Islamic history. Inscriptional and numismatic evidence: ʿUmar's caliphate is securely datable; the conquered-territory administrative-archaeological evidence is extensive.

## Works
- **No written works** in his own hand. ʿUmar is foundational for the **institutional shape of Sunni political tradition** rather than for textual production. His sayings and judgments are preserved as hadith and as *āthār* (companion-reports) in the Sunni hadith corpus and in later genres like the *Kitāb al-Kharāj* of Abū Yūsuf (~795, Ḥanafī treatise on land-tax based partly on ʿUmar-precedents).

## Role in the tradition
- **The Saqīfa succession-meeting, 632.** Immediately after Muhammad's death, ʿUmar **personally drove the selection of [[abu-bakr|Abū Bakr]] as caliph** at the Saqīfa Banī Sāʿida meeting in Medina, against the Anṣār's preference for a Medinan-helper candidate and (per Shīʿī tradition) before [[ali-ibn-abi-talib|ʿAlī]] could be considered. The Saqīfa is foundational for the Sunni-Shīʿī divergence: Sunni tradition treats the Abū Bakr-selection as legitimate consultative choice; Shīʿī tradition treats it as illegitimate sidelining of ʿAlī (whom Muhammad had — per Shīʿī reading of the Ghadīr Khumm declaration — designated as successor). ʿUmar's personal role in the Saqīfa makes him a **load-bearing figure in the foundational Sunni-Shīʿī fault-line**.
- **The first Arab-Muslim conquests, 634-644.** Under ʿUmar's caliphate the Arab-Muslim armies conducted the **astonishing first phase of conquest**: Battle of Yarmūk (636) defeated the Byzantine field-army and gave the Arabs Syria/Palestine; Battle of Qādisiyya (636) defeated the Sasanian field-army; Battle of Nihāwand (642) ended the Sasanian Empire as a political entity; Jerusalem surrendered to ʿUmar in person (638 — the celebrated *al-ʿuhda al-ʿUmariyya*, ʿUmar's Pact, granting religious-freedom protections to the Christian population); Egypt conquered under ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ (639-642). The speed and scope of conquest is one of the great historical puzzles; Kennedy 2007 surveys the demographic, military, and political factors.
- **The *dīwān*-administration.** ʿUmar's organizational innovation: a **central register (*dīwān al-ʿAṭāʾ*) of all Muslims entitled to a share of the conquest-revenues**, ranked by precedence (priority of conversion + tribal status + military participation). This is the foundational fiscal-administrative institution of the Islamic state; subsequent caliphates extend and elaborate the *dīwān* system.
- **The Hijri calendar.** Instituted c. 638; ʿUmar's choice to start the calendar at the Hegira (622) rather than at the first revelation (610) or Muhammad's birth (~570) is **politically meaningful**: the Hegira marks the founding of the Muslim *umma* as a political community, not merely the beginning of revelation. The calendar choice encodes the umma-centric political theology.
- **The 'Umariyya Pact' (Jerusalem 638).** ʿUmar's personal receipt of Jerusalem's surrender from the Patriarch Sophronius and the granting of the *al-ʿuhda al-ʿUmariyya* (the Pact of ʿUmar) is the foundational text of **dhimmi-status legal arrangements** for Christian populations under Muslim rule: protected status with religious-freedom and property-rights guarantees, subject to *jizya* (poll-tax) and various status-restrictions. The historical authenticity of the precise text of the Pact is contested (the surviving Pact-of-ʿUmar text shows signs of later-tradition editing; the historical core of a 638 ʿUmar-Sophronius arrangement is secure).
- **The assassination, 644.** ʿUmar was killed by Abū Luʾluʾa, a Persian Christian slave, in the Masjid al-Nabawī during morning prayer (Dhū al-Ḥijja 26, 23 AH / November 3, 644). The assassination is treated in Sunni tradition as a Persian-Christian act of personal grievance; some Shīʿī polemical traditions treat Abū Luʾluʾa positively, leading to occasional intra-Muslim sectarian tension.

## Disputes
- **The Saqīfa and the Sunni-Shīʿī origin-story.** Sunni: ʿUmar's role in selecting Abū Bakr is legitimate political consultation; ʿAlī accepted Abū Bakr after some delay. Shīʿī: the Saqīfa is an illegitimate sidelining of ʿAlī's designated succession; ʿUmar (with Abū Bakr) bears principal responsibility. The classical Sunni-Shīʿī divergence on ʿUmar runs through every aspect of his post-632 political role.
- **The 'Pact of ʿUmar.'** The surviving text is partly later-tradition reconstruction; the historical core is secure but the specifics are contested.
- **Conquest-period administrative innovations.** How much of the *dīwān* + tax-administration + garrison-city pattern is ʿUmar's personal innovation vs. necessary adaptation of Sasanian-Byzantine precedents in the conquered territories is debated.
