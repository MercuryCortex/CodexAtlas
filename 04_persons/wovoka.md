# Wovoka

## Identity
Northern Paiute prophet, founder of the 1890 Ghost Dance movement (the second Ghost Dance, distinct from the 1870 movement by Wodziwob). On January 1, 1889, during a solar eclipse, Wovoka — a Paiute man working as a ranch hand in Mason Valley, Nevada — fell into a trance and reported being taken to heaven, meeting God, and receiving a message and a ceremonial dance: if Indigenous peoples performed the dance, lived peacefully and morally, did not steal or fight, the world would be renewed — the buffalo and the ancestors would return, the white settlers would be removed, and Indigenous nations would be restored. The Ghost Dance spread rapidly across Plains and Western nations; among the Lakota it was reshaped in more militant directions, leading to the Wounded Knee Massacre of December 29, 1890.

## Historical evidence
Extensively documented: James Mooney's 1892 government-ethnographic visit to Wovoka, published as *The Ghost-Dance Religion and the Sioux Outbreak of 1890* (1896, BAE 14th Annual Report), is the principal contemporary source — Mooney interviewed Wovoka and recorded his teachings firsthand. Wovoka's own dictated *Messiah Letter* survives. Photographs of Wovoka (1890s, 1916, 1924) exist. He lived in Mason Valley until his death in 1932, continuing to receive visitors from many Indigenous nations.

## Works
- *The Messiah Letter* (1890) — dictated by Wovoka to white acquaintances and sent to inquirers across Indigenous nations.
- Recorded teachings in Mooney 1896.

## Role in the tradition
Wovoka was simultaneously: a Paiute religious figure operating within Numic shamanic-prophetic tradition; the founder of what became a pan-tribal revitalization movement; explicitly *non-militant* in his own teaching (the Lakota militant reading was a downstream development); seen by some contemporary white Americans as a "Indian Christ" (the comparison was both his Indigenous followers' framing and his white opponents' demonization). After Wounded Knee (December 1890), the Ghost Dance lost momentum but persisted in modified form in some nations (Caddo, Pawnee, Kiowa traditions retained Ghost Dance elements for decades).

## Disputes
- **Christian influence on Wovoka's teaching.** Wovoka grew up in part with the David Wilson family (Mormon and Presbyterian-influenced), and his trance-vision incorporates Christian elements (heaven, God-the-Father, moral teaching). How much of his vision is Numic-shamanic, how much Christian-derived, and how much genuinely synthetic is intensively studied (Hittman 1990; Smoak 2006).
- **Responsibility for Wounded Knee.** Wovoka explicitly counseled non-violence; the Lakota Ghost Shirt tradition (bullet-proof shirts) and militant interpretation was a Lakota-specific reshaping that Wovoka neither sanctioned nor opposed publicly. The historiographic question of how to attribute responsibility is intensively debated.
