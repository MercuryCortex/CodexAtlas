# Maimonides (Moses ben Maimon)

## Identity

**Moshe ben Maimon (1138–1204)** — *Rambam* in Hebrew, *Mūsā ibn Maymūn* in Arabic, *Rabbi Moyses* to the Latin scholastics — is the pivotal figure of medieval Jewish thought and one of the few people in this vault who is simultaneously a first-rank jurist, a first-rank philosopher, and a working physician. He wrote the most comprehensive code of Jewish law ever composed (*Mishneh Torah*), the most influential work of Jewish philosophical theology ([[phase-5-019-maimonides-guide-for-the-perplexed|the *Guide of the Perplexed*]]), a substantial Arabic medical corpus, and several hundred surviving responsa; he led the Jewish community of Ayyubid Egypt; and he did nearly all of it in a language — Judeo-Arabic — that placed him inside the Islamic intellectual world rather than beside it.

The Hebrew adage *mi-Mosheh ʿad Mosheh lo qam ke-Mosheh* — "from Moses to Moses there arose none like Moses" — measures him against [[moses]]. That is the tradition's own estimate; the reception history bears it out in an unexpected direction, because his largest single downstream effect was on a tradition that was not his own.

## Life across the Islamic world

Born in **Córdoba** in 1138 (some older literature gives 1135), son of the dayyan Maimon ben Joseph. The **Almohad conquest of Córdoba in 1148** ended the relatively tolerant Andalusi settlement; the family left and spent roughly a decade moving through Iberia and the Maghreb, reaching **Fez** around 1160 — itself an Almohad capital, which is part of why the period is obscure. A brief stay in Crusader **Acre** in 1165, a visit to Jerusalem and Hebron, and then permanent settlement in **Fustat** (Old Cairo) from c. 1166.

The family lived on the gem trade run by his younger brother **David**, who drowned in the Indian Ocean around 1177 carrying the family's capital. Maimonides described the grief in a letter recovered from the Cairo Genizah and said he was bedridden for a year. He then took up **medicine professionally** — a decision with consequences, since he practised in the Ayyubid court circle, in the household of **al-Qāḍī al-Fāḍil**, [[saladin]]'s vizier, and later for Saladin's son **al-Afḍal**, for whom he wrote the *Regimen of Health*. The familiar description "Saladin's personal physician" is the popular compression and is not securely documented (Davidson 2005).

He twice served as **head of the Egyptian Jewish community** (*raʾīs al-yahūd*), c. 1171–77 and again from the mid-1190s; the formal extent of that office is debated. He died in Fustat in 1204; tradition places his burial at Tiberias.

## Historical evidence

Exceptional by medieval standards. The **Cairo Genizah** of the Ben Ezra synagogue preserved autograph letters, drafts of the *Mishneh Torah* and the Commentary on the Mishnah, responsa in his own hand, and community documents (Goitein 1967–93; Kraemer 2008). Independent Arabic notices come from Ibn al-Qifṭī and Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿa. Davidson (2005) is the standard corrective: he works through the biographical tradition item by item and shows how much of what "everyone knows" about Maimonides rests on late or single-source report.

## Works

- **Commentary on the Mishnah** (*Kitāb al-Sirāj*, Judeo-Arabic, completed 1168, begun at 23). Contains two free-standing philosophical texts: the **Thirteen Principles of Faith**, in the introduction to the tenth chapter of Sanhedrin, and the ***Eight Chapters*** (*Shemonah Peraqim*), an Aristotelian-Galenic virtue ethics with the doctrine of the mean, prefaced to Avot.
- **Sefer ha-Mitzvot** (Judeo-Arabic) — the enumeration of the 613 commandments, governed by fourteen explicit rules of inclusion. [[nachmanides]] wrote critical glosses on it.
- ***Mishneh Torah*** (Hebrew, c. 1170–1180). Fourteen books — hence *ha-Yad ha-Ḥazaqah*, "the strong hand", *yad* being fourteen in gematria. See below.
- ***Guide of the Perplexed*** (*Dalālat al-Ḥāʾirīn*, Judeo-Arabic, c. 1185–1190), addressed to his student Joseph ben Judah. Translated into Hebrew twice within a generation — literally by **Samuel ibn Tibbon**, who corresponded with Maimonides about it, and more freely by **Judah al-Ḥarizi** — and into Latin as the ***Dux neutrorum*** from al-Ḥarizi's version.
- ***Treatise on Resurrection*** (1191) — written under pressure, to answer the charge that he had denied bodily resurrection.
- **Epistles** — the *Epistle to Yemen* (c. 1172, to a community under forced conversion and a messianic claimant) and the *Epistle on Forced Conversion*, which argues that outward compliance under coercion is not apostasy.
- **Medical corpus** in Arabic — *Regimen of Health*, *On Asthma*, *On Poisons and Their Antidotes*, *Medical Aphorisms* (*Fuṣūl Mūsā*), *Glossary of Drug Names*, a commentary on the Hippocratic *Aphorisms*. Widely copied, translated into Hebrew and Latin, and read outside Jewish circles.

## The *Mishneh Torah*

Three decisions make it unlike anything before it, and each one caused trouble.

1. **It is complete.** It codifies the *whole* of halakhah, including Temple law, sacrifices, and priestly purity — inoperative for eleven centuries. The law is treated as a total system whose validity does not depend on its current enforceability.
2. **It is in Hebrew, and in clear Hebrew** — a lucid Mishnaic style, not the Aramaic of the Talmud and not the Arabic he used for almost everything else. It was meant to be usable by any literate Jew.
3. **It cites no sources.** No "Rabbi X said", no tractate references, no chain of reasoning — only the ruling. The intent was a book that made consulting the Talmud unnecessary. The reaction was immediate: **Abraham ben David of Posquières (Rabad)** attached critical *hassagot* rejecting rulings whose basis he could not check, and the objection — that a code without sources severs law from its argument, which is the thing the Talmud exists to preserve — was never really answered.

It opens with *Sefer ha-Madda*, the Book of Knowledge, whose first law is not ritual but metaphysical: the foundation of foundations is to know that there is a First Existent who brings all else into being. A legal code that begins with an ontological proposition is a statement of program. See [[moral-mishneh-torah-ethical-code]].

## The *Guide* — negative theology and its neighbours

The [[phase-5-019-maimonides-guide-for-the-perplexed|*Guide*]] is addressed to the reader who has both a religious formation and a philosophical one and finds them at war. Its central positions:

- **Negative theology** (I.50–60). No positive essential attribute may be predicated of God. Every scriptural attribute must be read either as an **attribute of action** (God is called merciful because the world exhibits what mercy would produce) or as a **negation** (God is "living" meaning *not inanimate*). The more one denies, the closer one comes; silence, on his reading of Psalm 65:2, is the highest praise. Anthropomorphism is not a harmless simplification but a category error that puts one outside belief. He does not originate this line — it descends from Plotinus, Pseudo-Dionysius, the Muʿtazila and [[ibn-sina]] — but nobody had pushed it this far. See [[via-negativa]] and [[doctrine-apophatic-via-negativa]].
- **Creation vs. eternity** (II.13–25) — the hinge of the whole book. Maimonides argues that [[aristotle]] never *demonstrated* the eternity of the world; both creation and eternity are unproven, and he chooses creation because scripture teaches it and because eternity would evacuate prophecy and law. Then II.25 says the quiet part aloud: *if* eternity were demonstrated, the scriptural texts could be reinterpreted, exactly as the anthropomorphic verses already are. That sentence is the seed of every esoteric reading of him.
- **Prophecy** (II.32–48) — prophecy as a *natural* perfection of the rational and imaginative faculties, which God may withhold but does not simply confer on the unprepared. Moses is placed in a separate category: prophecy without imagination and without intermediary.
- **Providence and evil** (III.8–24) — evil as privation rather than a positive creation; individual providence proportional to intellectual perfection; the Book of Job read as a philosophical dialogue in which each speaker holds a recognizable position.
- **Reasons for the commandments** (III.25–50) — every commandment has an intelligible purpose. The most provocative case (III.32): the sacrificial cult was a **divine accommodation**, a graduated weaning of Israel off the Sabian idolatry it was steeped in, rather than an end in itself. Historicizing the Temple cult from inside the tradition was, and remains, explosive.
- **Critique of kalām** (I.71–76) — a systematic dismantling of the Islamic dialectical-theological proofs, from a thinker who took Aristotelian demonstration as the only real standard.

## The Maimonidean controversies

Three waves, and they are the tradition's longest-running internal argument about the standing of philosophy.

1. **c. 1180–1200.** Rabad's glosses on the *Mishneh Torah*; then Samuel ben Ali, head of the Baghdad academy, attacked him on bodily resurrection — provoking the *Treatise on Resurrection* (1191), which affirms it while conceding that ultimate felicity is intellectual.
2. **c. 1230–1235.** The sharpest. Solomon ben Abraham of Montpellier and Jonah Gerondi obtained bans on the *Guide* and on *Sefer ha-Madda*; the Maimonideans of Provence and Iberia (David Kimḥi among them) issued counter-bans. A tradition reported by later medieval sources — and treated cautiously by modern historians, since the earliest witness is well after the fact — holds that the books were burned by Dominicans at Montpellier in 1232/33 after anti-Maimonideans denounced them. What is not in doubt is that the Talmud itself was condemned at the Paris disputation of 1240 and burned in 1242, which gave the whole episode a grim epilogue.
3. **1303–1306.** Abba Mari of Lunel against the Provençal allegorizers; **Solomon ibn Adret (Rashba)** issued a ban in 1305 forbidding the study of Greek philosophy and natural science before the age of twenty-five — medicine exempted. The bans held unevenly, and the argument never fully closed.

The outcome is a paradox: **the codifier won completely and the philosopher won partly.** The *Mishneh Torah* and the Thirteen Principles were absorbed into ordinary Jewish life — the Principles are recited liturgically in the *Yigdal* hymn — while the *Guide* remained the property of a minority and a permanent object of suspicion.

## MASSIVE win — the two-way transmission hinge

**Maimonides is one of the best-documented cross-tradition transmission nodes in the vault, and it runs in both directions.**

*Into Latin Christendom.* The Arabic *Guide* → al-Ḥarizi's Hebrew → the Latin ***Dux neutrorum*** (c. 1220s–40s) placed a rabbinic authority on the desks of the Paris and Cologne schools within forty years of his death. He was read by Alexander of Hales, William of Auvergne, [[albertus-magnus]], [[thomas-aquinas]], [[meister-eckhart]] and Duns Scotus. Two dependencies are specific and checkable:

- **[[thomas-aquinas]] on creation.** *Summa Theologiae* Ia q.46 a.2 holds that the world's temporal beginning is an article of faith and cannot be philosophically demonstrated — the Maimonidean position, adopted against Bonaventure's contrary view, and Aquinas cites "Rabbi Moyses" in the vicinity of the argument.
- **[[thomas-aquinas]] on the divine names.** *Summa Theologiae* Ia q.13 a.2 states and then rejects the purely-negative account of divine predication, attributing it to Rabbi Moses, and puts the doctrine of analogy in its place. Latin analogical theology is defined partly *against* Maimonidean negative theology — which is a form of dependence.
- **[[meister-eckhart]]** used Maimonides more than any other non-Christian author in his Genesis commentaries, for both the negative theology and the allegorical method.

*Out of the Islamic philosophical world.* The material he transmits is [[philosophy-falsafa]]: [[aristotle]] as received through [[al-farabi]] (whom he told Ibn Tibbon to read above all others) and [[ibn-sina]]. So the chain is **Greek → Arabic falsafa → a Jewish author writing Arabic in Cairo → Hebrew → Latin → scholasticism**, running alongside and partly independent of the Toledo Arabic-to-Latin route (see [[event-toledo-translations-1085-1150]] and [[event-toledo-school-of-translators]]). The most consequential intellectual transmission in Western history has a rabbi standing in the middle of it.

*Onward.* [[baruch-spinoza]]'s *Tractatus Theologico-Politicus* attacks Maimonides' allegorical hermeneutic by name — a hostile dependence, and the point at which biblical criticism turns against the method that made it possible. Then Mendelssohn, Hermann Cohen, Leo Strauss and Levinas, each reading a different Maimonides.

## Disputes

- **The esoteric reading.** Strauss (1952) argued the *Guide* deliberately conceals a radical Aristotelian teaching beneath an orthodox surface, using contradiction and dispersal as devices Maimonides himself describes in the introduction. Enormously influential; seriously contested. Davidson (2005) is the leading sceptic, holding that the contradictions are ordinary compositional artefacts and the professed positions are the real ones. **Tier 2 — an interpretive school, not the consensus.**
- **Resurrection.** He affirms bodily resurrection in the Thirteen Principles and defends it in the *Treatise*, while the *Guide*'s account of ultimate felicity is intellectual conjunction with the Active Intellect. Whether these cohere is a permanent question.
- **Forced conversion in the Maghreb.** Ibn al-Qifṭī reports that Maimonides outwardly professed Islam during the Almohad years. Kraemer treats it as plausible in context; Davidson finds the evidence insufficient. Unresolved; the *Epistle on Forced Conversion*, which argues that coerced outward profession is not apostasy, is read on both sides.
- **Ibn Rushd.** Exact contemporary, same city, same Almohad regime — and yet Maimonides tells Ibn Tibbon in 1199 that he had only lately obtained his Aristotle commentaries. How much [[ibn-rushd]] shaped the *Guide*, if at all, is debated.
- **Date of birth.** 1138 is now standard; 1135 appears in older literature.
- **"Physician to Saladin."** Court-circle physician, yes; personal physician to the sultan, not documented (Davidson 2005).
