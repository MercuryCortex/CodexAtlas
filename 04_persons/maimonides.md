# Maimonides (Moses ben Maimon)

## Identity
Moshe ben Maimon (1138–1204) — Maimonides, Rambam — was the towering figure of medieval Jewish thought: philosopher (the *Guide for the Perplexed*), jurist (the *Mishneh Torah*, the most comprehensive halakhic code ever composed), physician (court physician to Saladin and his successors), and *Nagid* of the Egyptian Jewish community. Born Córdoba; family fled Almohad persecution ~1148; sojourns in Morocco and Palestine; settled Fustat ~1166. The Hebrew adage *mi-Mosheh ʿad Mosheh lo qam ke-Mosheh* — "from Moses to Moses there has been none like Moses" — captures Jewish tradition's estimate of him.

## Historical evidence
Among the best-documented medieval Jewish figures — substantial surviving correspondence (Cairo Genizah), responsa, autobiographical references in his works, contemporary Arabic and Hebrew references.

## Works
*Guide for the Perplexed* (philosophical theology, ~1190); *Mishneh Torah* (halakhic code, ~1170–1180); *Commentary on the Mishnah* (~1158–1168, including the "Thirteen Principles of Faith"); *Treatise on Resurrection*; medical works on diet, asthma, poisons, sexual hygiene; extensive responsa and correspondence.

## Role in the tradition
Foundational; the Aristotelian philosophical synthesis the Guide represents triggered the multi-generation "Maimonidean Controversy" (his works publicly burned at Montpellier 1232 and Paris 1234). Despite controversy, Maimonides became canonical: the *Mishneh Torah* and the Thirteen Principles are central to subsequent Jewish life and thought. Cited in Latin scholastic theology by Aquinas and Albertus Magnus as "Rabbi Moyses"; cited by Spinoza, Mendelssohn, and every subsequent Jewish philosopher.

## Disputes
Whether the *Guide* is esoterically Aristotelian-rationalist behind an exoteric orthodox surface (Strauss 1952) is the central interpretive question. Whether Maimonides believed in bodily resurrection (officially yes, per the *Treatise on Resurrection*; many readers find this in tension with the *Guide*'s intellectualism) is a perennial debate.
