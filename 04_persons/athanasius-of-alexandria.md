# Athanasius of Alexandria

## Identity

**Athanasius of Alexandria** (Greek *Ἀθανάσιος*; Coptic *Abba Athanasius*; c. 296–373 CE) was the twentieth bishop (pope/patriarch) of Alexandria and among the most tenacious defenders of Nicene orthodoxy in the fourth century. He is remembered under the tag *Athanasius contra mundum* — "Athanasius against the world" — for holding to the full divinity of the Son of God through five exiles and roughly seventeen years away from his see, across the reigns of four emperors who variously favoured his Arian and semi-Arian opponents. He must not be confused with the seventeenth-century German Jesuit polymath **Athanasius Kircher** (a wholly different person, catalogued separately in the Atlas); this node is the fourth-century Church Father.

Present at the **Council of Nicaea (325)** as a young deacon in the retinue of Bishop Alexander of Alexandria, Athanasius succeeded Alexander in the see in 328 and spent the rest of his life making the Nicene *homoousios* ("of one substance / consubstantial") the non-negotiable test of orthodoxy. He is honoured as a saint and Doctor of the Church in the Catholic, Orthodox, and Oriental-Orthodox traditions, and is often titled the **"Father of Orthodoxy."**

## Works and the Arian controversy

Athanasius's achievement has two faces — the theologian and the ecclesiastical politician — and the modern literature (Barnes 1993; Gwynn 2012) insists on holding both together.

**The theology.** In the double work *Contra Gentes*–*De Incarnatione* he set out the logic that governs everything he wrote: only the Creator, not a creature, can re-create; therefore the Word who redeems must be fully God, and the redemption is a **deification** of humanity — *"He became man that we might become god"* (*De Incarnatione* 54). Against Arius's teaching that the Son was the first and highest **creature** — "there was when he was not" (*ēn hote ouk ēn*) — Athanasius argued in the three *Orations against the Arians* that a created Son could save no one, so the *homoousios* of Nicaea is not an optional philosophical term but the guarantee of salvation itself. In the *Letters to Serapion* he took the further step of arguing for the **full divinity of the Holy Spirit** against the *Tropici* / Pneumatomachoi, laying the groundwork the **Cappadocians** ([[gregory-of-nazianzus]], [[gregory-of-nyssa]], Basil) would complete at the Council of Constantinople (381).

**The politics.** Athanasius was deposed and exiled five times: to Trier under Constantine (335), twice under **Constantius II** (339, 356), under the pagan **Julian** (362), and under the Arian **Valens** (365). During the desert exiles he wrote his great polemical-historical defences (*Apologia contra Arianos*, *Historia Arianorum*) and drew on the monastic movement as a power base. He died in his see, restored, on 2 May 373 — eight years before the doctrine he defended was finally vindicated.

**The canon and monasticism.** Two of his acts reached far beyond the Arian dispute. His **39th Festal Letter** (Easter 367) gives the **earliest surviving list of exactly the twenty-seven books of the New Testament** (see [[phase-4-095-new-testament-canon]]), distinguishing books "canonised" from books merely "read." And his **Life of Antony** ([[antony-the-great]]) invented the literary form of the saint's *Life*; in Latin translation it carried Egyptian **monasticism to the West** — Augustine records in the *Confessions* (VIII.6–7) that hearing the story of Antony precipitated his own conversion.

## Cross-tradition significance

Athanasius's importance is in the first place **intra-Christian**: he is the hinge between the Council of Nicaea and the settled Trinitarian dogma of 381, and one of the most influential patristic authorities for the co-eternity and consubstantiality of the Son. His comparative reach in the Atlas runs through the doctrines he shaped — [[doctrine-trinity-nicene]], [[doctrine-incarnation]] — and the doctrine he refuted, [[doctrine-arianism]].

The most genuinely **cross-tradition** parallel is structural, and it is one the Arianism hub also flags. The controversy Athanasius won turned on the question **"is the divine 'second' co-eternal with God, or a creature?"** — the Son / Logos, in his case. The **Islamic controversy over the createdness of the Qur'an** (*khalq al-Qur'ān*), fought in the ʿAbbāsid *miḥna* of the ninth century, poses the *same structural question* of a different divine "second," the eternal Speech of God. In both, the party that made the divine second a **creature** (Arius; the Muʿtazila) lost, and "uncreated / co-eternal" became the orthodox confession — with Athanasius as the Christian analogue of the Ḥanbalī defenders of the uncreated Qur'an. **Harry Wolfson** (*The Philosophy of the Kalam*, 1976) pressed this into a transmission claim (Christian Logos-debate shaping Muslim *kalām*); most current scholarship (van Ess) reads it as **convergence** — two strict monotheisms independently forced to the same problem — not documented borrowing. Secure: the two debates are structurally the same. Not secure: that one caused the other.

A second, softer parallel is soteriological: Athanasius's axiom of **deification** (*theōsis*) — the human made "god" by participation — is often set beside the language of divinization in other traditions, but this is thematic resonance within a distinctively Christian, Incarnation-anchored frame rather than a cognate doctrine.

## Disputes

- **Athanasius as a source.** Because so much of what we know of Arius and the early controversy survives only in Athanasius's hostile quotation, the field has had to reckon with how far he is a *reliable witness*. **Rowan Williams** (2001) argues that "Arianism" as Athanasius presents it is a flattening polemical construct that obscures a range of fourth-century positions; **Lewis Ayres** (2004) makes the dissolution of the unified "Arian party" central. Athanasius is a shaper of the record, not a neutral reporter of it.
- **The bishop's methods.** **Timothy Barnes** (1993) documents the coercive and factional side of Athanasius's episcopate (charges of violence against Melitian rivals, the manipulation of imperial politics), reading the heroic "contra mundum" image partly as Athanasius's own successful self-construction. The theological greatness and the ruthless ecclesiastical politics are, on this account, the same man.
- **Dating of *De Incarnatione*.** The *Contra Gentes*–*De Incarnatione* double-work has been dated anywhere from before 325 (the traditional early dating) to the 330s (Barnes); the question bears on whether it is a pre- or post-Nicene statement.
- **Spuria.** Several works long circulated under his name are not his — most notably the so-called **"Athanasian Creed"** (*Quicunque vult*), a later Latin Western composition, not a work of Athanasius.
