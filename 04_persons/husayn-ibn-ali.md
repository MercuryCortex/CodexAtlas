# Husayn ibn Ali

## Identity
Second son of [[ali-ibn-abi-talib]] by Fatima al-Zahra, daughter of the Prophet [[muhammad-ibn-abdullah]]. Born in Medina ~626; raised in the Prophet's household; the grandson whom Muhammad — per multiple well-attested hadith preserved in both Sunni and Shi'i collections — held in special affection ("Husayn is of me and I am of Husayn"; "Hasan and Husayn are the masters of the youth of Paradise"). For Shi'i Islam, the **Third Imam**, succeeding his older brother [[hasan-ibn-ali]] in 670. Killed at Karbala on 10 Muharram 61 AH (10 October 680 CE) by the Umayyad force of 'Umar ibn Sa'd, on orders from the governor 'Ubayd Allah ibn Ziyad of Kufa, ultimately reaching Caliph Yazid I in Damascus. The killing of Husayn and ~72 of his companions — beheaded, his head sent to Damascus; the surviving women and the gravely-ill son [[ali-zayn-al-abidin]] taken captive — is the foundational catastrophe of Shi'i religious history.

## Historical evidence
Documented across the full early-Islamic corpus, Sunni and Shi'i, with extensive elaboration in the dedicated *maqtal al-Husayn* genre (martyrology-narratives of Husayn's killing). The earliest *maqtal*, by Abu Mikhnaf (d. 774), survives in extensive quotation in [[al-tabari]]'s *Ta'rīkh al-rusul wa-l-mulūk*. Sunni hadith ([[phase-5-007-sahih-al-bukhari]], Muslim, Tirmidhi) and Shi'i hadith both preserve traditions about Husayn's birth, childhood, the Prophet's affection for him, and his death. The narrative core of the Karbala event (the killing, the date, the principal actors) is uncontested across Sunni and Shi'i sources. The literary–devotional elaboration (the specific dialogue, the precise battle sequence, the mourning narratives of Zaynab) is heavier in the dedicated *maqtal* tradition, lighter in the early historiographers.

## Works
- ***Du'a Arafa*** — supplicatory prayer of the Day of Arafa attributed to Husayn; a central piece of Shi'i devotional liturgy, recited on the 9th of Dhu al-Hijja (the eve of the Hajj day-of-standing-on-Arafat).
- Various sermons, letters, and aphorisms preserved in Shi'i compilations (notably the *Tuhaf al-'Uqul* of ibn Shu'ba al-Harrani, 10th c.).

## Role in the tradition
**The single most important figure in Shi'i Islam after Ali — arguably, in cultic-devotional terms, *more* central than Ali.** Husayn's killing is not just a historical event but a perpetually-present religious reality in the Shi'i liturgical year: Ashura (10 Muharram) is the central Shi'i ritual, with parallel rites on the 40th day (Arba'in, 20 Safar), and a mourning calendar that fills two full months (Muharram and Safar) with weeping-gatherings (*majlis*), processions, breast-beating (*latmiyya*), and re-enactments (*ta'ziya*).

Mature Twelver theology — articulated most fully by Ayoub 1978 — assigns to Husayn's death a redemptive-intercessory function: weeping for Husayn becomes salvific; Husayn intercedes on the Day of Judgment for those who have mourned him. The doctrine is not Qur'anic and is not present in its mature form in the earliest *maqtal* layer; it develops through the 8th–12th c. and is institutionalized under the Buyid dynasty (10th–11th c.) when public Ashura ritual becomes Baghdad's annual urban event. Under the Safavids (1501+) it becomes Iranian state ritual; the *ta'ziya* passion-play tradition crystallizes in the Qajar period (18th–19th c.).

The **Karbala paradigm** (Hussain 2005) — the structural opposition Husayn / Yazid = righteous-oppressed / illegitimate-tyrant — becomes a permanently re-applicable template for Shi'i political-religious self-understanding. Every Shi'i revolt of the early period (the *Tawwabun* movement 685, the Mukhtar revolt 686, the Zaydi uprisings, the Abbasid revolution itself — though the Abbasids betrayed the Husayni line — the Fatimids, etc.) cites Karbala. In modernity, Ali Shariati's pre-Revolution reading of Husayn as revolutionary archetype, and Khomeini's *"Every day is Ashura, every land is Karbala,"* explicitly redeploy the paradigm. The 1979 [[event-iranian-revolution-1979]] is unintelligible without this template.

In Sunni Islam, Husayn is honored as the grandson of the Prophet and a martyr; Yazid is widely (though not universally) condemned; but the centerpiece-of-religious-life status that Husayn holds in Shi'ism does not transfer. The Sunni hadith corpus preserves Husayn-related traditions but the Umayyad-Abbasid political pressure on the early Sunni hadith collection process means that traditions valorizing Husayn against the Umayyads are unevenly preserved.

## Disputes
- **Husayn's strategic intent.** Was Husayn marching toward Kufa for a confrontation he believed he could win politically (responding to the letters from Kufan supporters)? to negotiate from a position of moral authority? or in a knowing self-sacrifice? Twelver devotional tradition has tended toward the third reading (Husayn knew his fate and went willingly as redemptive sacrifice — predicted, in some Shi'i hadith, even by Muhammad himself). Critical historiography (Madelung 1997; Wellhausen) reads Husayn as a political claimant who expected at minimum a major political concession; the willing-redemptive-martyr framing is read as post-event theological elaboration.

- **Crystallization of the cosmic-redemptive theology.** Modarressi 1993 (*Crisis and Consolidation*) and Hussain 2005 argue the intercessory–redemptive theology around Husayn develops gradually over the 8th–12th c., consolidating under the Buyids. The earliest *maqtal* layer is grief-narrative without yet the fully developed soteriology.

- **The structural parallel to Christian Passion theology.** Ayoub's reading — that Husayn occupies in Shi'i devotion a religious-structural position comparable to that of Christ's Passion in Christian devotion (weeping for the righteous-killed becomes salvific; the killed-one intercedes for the mourner) — is influential and contested. Whether the parallel implies *historical transmission* (via the substantial Aramaic-Syriac-Christian milieu of Iraqi Shi'i formation, with its Holy Week and Good Friday lamentation liturgies, its *qenoth* mourning hymns for Christ) or *structural parallel* (independently-generated religious responses to the killing of a holy figure) is genuinely open. Both positions register in scholarship. The Iraqi geographical–religious matrix of the 7th–10th c. (Aramaic-Christian, Mandaean, Manichaean, Mazdean-Zoroastrian, Jewish, and Muslim) is the substrate. The parallel is real; the historical-transmission claim is undecided.

## Cross-tradition connections
- ↔ [[event-karbala-680]] — the defining event; Husayn's killing is the structural center of Shi'i religious life.
- ↔ [[ali-ibn-abi-talib]] (father), [[hasan-ibn-ali]] (older brother), [[ali-zayn-al-abidin]] (son, sole surviving male; Fourth Imam), [[muhammad-al-baqir]] (grandson, Fifth Imam) — the genealogical-charismatic spine of Shi'ism after Karbala runs through Husayn.
- ↔ [[muhammad-ibn-abdullah]] — maternal grandfather; the Prophet's affection for the child Husayn is foundational for the later devotion.
- ↔ [[martyrdom-theology]] — Husayn is the canonical Islamic martyr; the *shahīd* terminology applies preeminently to him; the Karbala-event is the central Islamic-martyrology referent.
- ↔ [[passion-narrative]] — structural parallel to Christ's Passion in religious–devotional function (Ayoub 1978; debated above).
- ↔ [[apocalyptic-cosmic-warfare]] / [[messianism]] — the *return of Husayn* tradition in Shi'i eschatology (Husayn returns with the Mahdi at end-times to defeat injustice).
- ↔ [[tradition-shia-islam]], [[tradition-ismailism]] — universally venerated across all Shi'a branches.
- ↔ [[event-iranian-revolution-1979]] — the Karbala paradigm is the explicit ideological structure of Khomeini's revolutionary discourse.
- ↔ [[dumuzi-tammuz]] / [[dying-rising-god]] — Husayn is *not* a dying-rising god (rigorous Islamic monotheism; Husayn is fully human, not deified) but the *ritual form* of his commemoration draws on the broader Near Eastern lamentation-for-the-killed-righteous repertoire.
