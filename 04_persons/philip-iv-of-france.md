# Philip IV of France

## Identity
King of France 1285–1314. Grandson of Saint Louis (Louis IX). Architect of the early-14th-c. French royal-fiscal-juridical-ecclesiastical extension over corporate institutions: the Jews (expelled 1306, property confiscated), the Lombard bankers (expelled 1311), the [[tradition-knights-templar|Knights Templar]] (mass-arrested 1307, suppressed 1312, last leadership burned 1314). Forced the relocation of the papacy to Avignon (1309). Politically defeated Pope Boniface VIII at Anagni (1303) — Nogaret's commando attack on the elderly pope, who died a month later.

Married to Joan I of Navarre; their three sons (Louis X, Philip V, Charles IV) all became kings of France, each died without surviving male heir, ending the direct Capetian line in 1328 and precipitating the Hundred Years' War.

## Historical evidence
Massive. French royal administrative records survive in substantial volume from his reign — the *Trésor des Chartes*, the *Olim* (Parlement records), the *Comptes* (financial). Chronicle attestation across Christendom (mostly hostile outside France).

## Works
None authored personally. Royal ordinances and letters were drafted by his jurists.

## Role in the tradition
- **Architect of the Templar suppression.** The 1307 mass-arrest was a coordinated coup-de-main against a papal-direct institution by a secular crown — unprecedented. The motive-mix (fiscal recovery, demonstration of royal supremacy, ideological alliance with the mendicant inquisitorial apparatus) is debated; consensus position: layered motives. Barber 2006.
- **Royal-supremacist precedent.** Philip's defeat of Boniface VIII at Anagni (1303), his coercion of Clement V (1305–1314), and his suppression of the Templars (1307–1312) collectively demonstrated the political subordination of the late-medieval papacy to royal power. The pattern fed the Avignon Papacy structural French-dependency and the eventual Conciliar Movement (Pisa 1409, Constance 1414–1418).
- **Mass-expeller of corporate communities.** Jews (1306), Templars (1307), Lombard bankers (1311) — each expulsion accompanied by property confiscation. Read together as a fiscal-corporate policy rather than as religious policy.

## Connections
- **→ [[pope-clement-v]]** *coercer*: structural dependence is the political dynamic of Clement's pontificate.
- **→ [[jacques-de-molay]]** *executioner*: the burning of 18 March 1314 was a royal-arm execution of a pope-disposed prisoner.
- **→ Boniface VIII** *politically-defeated*: the Anagni outrage of 1303.
- **→ [[tradition-knights-templar]]** *suppressor*: the institutional end of the Order is Philip's project.

## Disputes
- **Did Philip *believe* the Templar charges?** Probably not — Barber 2006 ch. 2. The prepared formulary of the 1307 arrests reflects political opportunism rather than sincere conviction, though Philip's own piety (described in some sources as obsessive) may have provided psychological cover.
- **How much Templar treasure did Philip actually recover?** Less than the legend says. Substantial cash and land but most Western European Templar property went to the [[tradition-hospitallers|Hospitallers]] (*Ad providam*) rather than the French crown.
- **Was Philip mad?** No — political-historical caricature. But his last years (1313–1314) show isolation, paranoia, and severe political stress.
