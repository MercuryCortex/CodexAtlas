# Nammalvar

## Identity
The most theologically and poetically important of the twelve Tamil Āḻvārs and the *de facto* primary author of the Tamil Vaishnava canon. Per tradition: born to a Vēḷḷāḷa family at Kurukai (modern Āḻvārtirunagari) in the Pandyan country; refused to eat, speak, or open his eyes for sixteen years; meditated under a tamarind tree; finally taught by Madhurakavi, his elder disciple. The traditional lifespan is 35 years. Nammāḻvār's compositions — especially the 1102-verse *Tiruvāymoḻi* — became the foundational text of the Sri Vaishnava tradition. The Tamil Vaishnava community has historically called the four works the "Drāviḍa-Veda" (Tamil Veda); the *Tiruvāymoḻi* alone is called the "Sāma-Veda among them" in tradition.

## Historical evidence
- Continuous Sri Vaishnava textual transmission from Nāthamuni (~9th–10th c.), who claimed to have received the lost *Tiruvāymoḻi* from Nammalvar through mystical encounter.
- Pillan's *Āṟāyirappaḍi* commentary (c. 1100; written under Ramanuja's direction) is the first surviving sustained exegesis.
- Inscriptional evidence for Alvar cult worship at Āḻvārtirunagari and across Tamil temple country from at least the 10th c.

## Works
- ***Tiruvāymoḻi*** ("Sacred Word of the Mouth"): 1102 Tamil verses in 100 decades of 10 verses each + a 11th "tan-ucca" cap-verse. The principal canonical Tamil Vaishnava scripture. Each decade is a unified meditative-emotional cycle; the whole is held together by a sophisticated *antādi* (last-foot-as-first) linking pattern.
- ***Tiruviruttam*** ("Sacred Surveying"): 100 verses; the female-voice yearning-for-the-beloved cycle.
- ***Tiruvāciriyam***: 7 verses in *āciriyappā* meter; cosmological-meditative.
- ***Periya Tiruvantāti***: 87 verses; meditations on the divine.

## Role in the tradition
- **Sri Vaishnavism**: Nammāḻvār is the single most authoritative voice in the tradition — the Tamil Veda's principal author, the foundational *jñāni* (knower) recognized as inheritor of the canon and conduit of its meaning. Ramanuja's school developed a formal hierarchy of Alvar commentary culminating in the *Bhagavad-Viṣaya* — five layered commentaries on the *Tiruvāymoḻi* from Pillan (~1100) through Vedanta Desika (~1300) to Periyavāccāṉ Piḷḷai. The work is considered as authoritative as the Sanskrit Veda in Sri Vaishnava soteriology — a major early-medieval theological claim about vernacular scripture.
- **Tamil literature**: the *Tiruvāymoḻi* is one of the supreme works of pre-modern Tamil poetry.
- **The bhakti diffusion thesis**: Hardy (1983) identifies Nammāḻvār's *viraha-bhakti* — yearning erotic-meditative devotion — as the deep South Indian root of the later pan-Indian bhakti tradition (the *Bhāgavata Purāṇa*'s emotional Krishna-bhakti has been argued to derive from Tamil Alvar models).

## Cross-tradition connections
- **Tamil-Sanskrit canonical equality**: Sri Vaishnavism's claim that the *Tiruvāymoḻi* is canonically equal to the Sanskrit Veda is, comparatively, one of the most explicit pre-modern South Asian assertions of vernacular-scripture authority — a structural parallel to (much later) Protestant vernacular Bible movements, though the doctrinal grounds differ.
- **South Indian bhakti as source of North Indian bhakti**: the standard "bhakti movement spreads from Tamil South to North" thesis (canonical in 20th-c. Indology; critiqued by Hawley 2015 as partly a modern construction) — Nammāḻvār is the foundational poet from which Hardy 1983 traces the diffusion through the *Bhagavata Purana* into medieval North Indian Krishna-bhakti (Surdas, Mirabai, Chaitanya).
- **Krishna-bhakti's bridge to Sufi devotional vocabulary**: by the time North Indian bhakti reaches its full development (12th–16th c.), it shares with Indo-Persian Sufi poetry a vocabulary of *viraha* / *firāq* (separation), *prema* / *‘ishq* (passionate love), and erotic-mystical bridal yearning — a cross-tradition lexical-thematic affinity whose distal South Indian root is Nammāḻvār's *Tiruvāymoḻi*.

## Disputes
- **Dates**: 8th c. (traditional) vs. 9th c. (Hardy, Cutler) vs. early 10th c. (more recent inscriptional reassessment) — unresolved.
- **Caste**: the tradition's claim that Nammāḻvār was Vēḷḷāḷa (i.e., a "śūdra" by orthodox Brahmanical categorization) and yet became the supreme Vaishnava theological voice is itself part of the tradition's deliberate caste-pluralism — a feature subsequently elaborated in Sri Vaishnava theology as the *paratantra* (utter dependence) of all souls on the Lord.
