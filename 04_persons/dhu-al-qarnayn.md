# Dhū al-Qarnayn

Quranic figure appearing exclusively in **Surah 18:83–101** (al-Kahf, the Cave surah). The name means "He of the Two Horns." He is a divinely guided king who travels to the western and eastern limits of the earth, then builds an iron-and-copper wall to contain Gog and Magog (*Yājūj wa-Mājūj*) until the Day of Judgment (18:99).

## Quranic narrative

- **Surah 18:83–88** — Dhū al-Qarnayn reaches the western limit (a spring of muddy water); judges between the wrongdoer and the righteous
- **Surah 18:89–98** — travels east; then reaches the wall-builders who ask him to contain Gog and Magog; builds the iron-and-copper barrier
- **Surah 18:99–101** — eschatological frame: when God's promise comes, He will level the wall, and Gog-Magog will pour forth

## Alexander identification (dominant but debated)

Islamic exegesis from Ṭabarī onward identifies Dhū al-Qarnayn with **Alexander the Great**. The "two horns" iconography matches Alexander's depiction on coins as the "Two-Horned" (Greek: *ὁ δίκερας*), and the Alexander Romance tradition (*Iskandarname*) circulated extensively in pre-Islamic Arabia. The Syriac Alexander Legend (~629 CE) contains the Gog-Magog wall motif almost identical to the Quranic version, suggesting the Quran is engaging with a specific branch of the Alexander Romance tradition.

The Cyrus identification (proposed by some modern scholars) and a purely legendary-figure reading both have scholarly advocates. Reynolds 2018 gives the most careful assessment of the evidence.

## Gog and Magog (Yājūj wa-Mājūj)

The Gog and Magog motif derives from Ezekiel 38–39 and appears in Revelation 20:7–9. The Quranic version is the most elaborated: the wall is a physical structure that will be demolished at the End of Days. The parallel-tradition chain (Hebrew Bible → apocalyptic Judaism → New Testament → Quran) represents one of the clearest examples of sequential eschatological development across traditions.
