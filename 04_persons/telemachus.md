# Telemachus

## Identity
Telemachus (Greek **Τηλέμαχος**, *Tēlemachos*, usually explained as "fighter far away" — a name commonly connected — in ancient and modern readings alike — to the father fighting far away at Troy) is the legendary prince of Ithaca: son of [[odysseus]] and Penelope, an infant when his father sailed for Troy and a young man of about twenty when the *Odyssey* opens. He is the protagonist of the poem's first four books — the **Telemachy** — and the *Odyssey*'s coming-of-age figure: the sheltered son who, roused and escorted by [[athena]] in disguise, journeys to Pylos and Sparta in search of news of his father and returns capable of standing beside him in the slaughter of the suitors. There is no historical evidence for an individual Telemachus; he is a creation of the oral-formulaic epic tradition stabilised in the 8th-7th centuries BCE. In the Atlas he is placed among the legendary persons (on the model of [[odysseus]] and [[theseus]]) because his story is among the vault's clearest ancient instances of the coming-of-age / [[initiation]] arc, the source-line of the modern "mentor" figure, and the *Odyssey*'s showcase of correct hospitality (*xenia*).

## In the Odyssey (the Telemachy, Books 1-4, and the return)
The poem opens not with Odysseus but with his house in disorder: the suitors of Penelope feast daily on the absent king's substance while the son, "sitting among the suitors, grieving," can do nothing. From the divine council, Athena descends in the shape of **Mentes**, chief of the Taphians, is received hospitably by the boy, and plants the journey in him (Od. 1); she holds up **Orestes** — the son who avenged his father Agamemnon — as the explicit paradigm of what a grown son does (Od. 1.298-302, repeated by Nestor at 3.195-200). Telemachus then does three things a man does in epic: he calls and addresses the Ithacan assembly (Od. 2), he takes a ship in secret, and he goes abroad — accompanied by Athena in the borrowed shape of the Ithacan elder **Mentor** (Od. 2.267-268, 2.399-401).

The two guest-scenes that follow are the *Odyssey*'s panels of hospitality done right, in deliberate counterpoint to the suitors' perverted feasting at home. At **Pylos** (Od. 3) Nestor's household receives the strangers at a sacrifice on the shore: they are seated, fed, and questioned only after the meal; Telemachus is bathed by Nestor's daughter Polycaste, and sent on by land with a chariot, an escort (Nestor's son Peisistratus), and provisions. At **Sparta** (Od. 4) Menelaus rebukes a servant who hesitates over uninvited strangers, feasts them amid a double wedding, and only then seeks their names; Helen recognises the boy by his likeness to Odysseus; guest-gifts are given, and Menelaus relays Proteus's news that Odysseus lives, held on Calypso's island. The suitors, meanwhile, lay an ambush for the returning son (Od. 4.669-672) — anti-*xenia* aimed at a guest-cycle's homecoming.

Telemachus's return (Od. 15) threads past the ambush; father and son are reunited and recognised at the hut of the swineherd Eumaeus (Od. 16); and the maturation arc closes in deeds: at the trial of the bow Telemachus would have strung his father's bow on his fourth attempt had Odysseus not signalled him to stop (Od. 21.128-130), and he fights at his father's side in the killing of the suitors (Od. 22). In the post-Homeric epic cycle, the *Telegony* (known from Proclus's summary) carries the family further — there Telemachus ends as husband of Circe — a continuation the Atlas records as reception, not as Homeric substance.

## Cross-tradition significance
Three distinct threads run through this node, and they carry different kinds of evidence.

**The coming-of-age arc (typology within Greek literature).** Homeric scholarship since the early 1960s (Eckert 1963; Clarke 1963) has read the Telemachy against van Gennep's rites-of-passage schema: separation from home, liminal testing abroad among the father's peers, return with new standing. This makes Telemachus the natural ancient anchor for the [[initiation]] hub — with the honest caveat that what is secure is the *literary* maturation arc; whether an actual Greek ritual pattern (an ephebic or initiatory institution) underlies the text is an interpretive claim, argued but not provable from Homer alone. The same interpretive lens is applied to [[theseus]]'s journey to claim his father's tokens (Jeanmaire's line of initiation-reading), which is why the two are paralleled here — parallel *readings*, not an ancient kinship.

**The mentor line (documented transmission).** The guide of Telemachus's journey is Athena in the shape of the elder **Mentor** — in Homer, the human Mentor himself does almost nothing; the guidance is the goddess in his skin. The modern English word "mentor" descends from this figure by a documented, two-step route: François **Fénelon's** *Les Aventures de Télémaque* (1699), the most-read European reworking of the Telemachy, promoted Mentor into the sage tutor of its hero, and the common noun enters English in the eighteenth century from Fénelon's book (OED s.v. "mentor"), not directly from Homer. Downstream, Vogler's screenwriting "Mentor" archetype (*The Writer's Journey*, 1992) explicitly takes its name from this line, while Campbell's "supernatural aid" stage is the broader typological slot the archetype was folded into — which is why this node wires into [[theme-monomyth]]: a secure reception lineage, Homer → Fénelon → the modern word → the archetype, and one of the vault's cleanest cases of a named literary transmission rather than a typological guess.

**The hospitality panels (institution with linguistic depth).** The Pylos and Sparta episodes are the fullest positive hospitality type-scenes in Homer (Reece 1993), enacting the doctrine that strangers and suppliants stand under [[zeus]] Xenios (Od. 6.207-208, 9.270-271) — the *Odyssey* even has the suitors warned that gods walk the earth as strangers testing men (Od. 17.483-487), the Greek form of the theoxeny type met independently in Genesis 18-19 and in Ovid's Baucis and Philemon (structural convergence; no transmission claimed). The institution itself, however, has genuine comparative depth: Benveniste's analysis of Greek *xénos* beside Latin *hostis/hospes* treats guest-friendship as an inherited Indo-European institution of reciprocity, engaging Thieme's case that the Vedic [[aryaman]] is the divine guarantor of the same guest-bond. The careful framing: the *institution* is comparably old and linguistically traceable; the *Telemachus narrative* is Greek and travels only by reception.

## Disputes
- **Was the Telemachy originally a separate poem?** The nineteenth-century Analysts (classically Kirchhoff, *Die homerische Odyssee*, 1879) held that Books 1-4 were an independent composition stitched into the *Odyssey*. The prevailing modern view (Heubeck, West, Hainsworth 1988; narratological scholarship since) treats the Telemachy as integral design — the son's small nostos built to prepare and mirror the father's — though the seams the Analysts pointed to are real features of the text's history.
- **Initiation ritual or literary coming-of-age?** Eckert's rites-of-passage reading is attractive and widely cited, but Homer describes no ritual; the initiatory pattern is an interpretive grid laid over a narrative arc. The Atlas wires the theme while flagging the claim's status: secure as literary shape, hypothetical as ritual survival.
- **The "mentor" etymology's popular form.** Popular accounts derive the word straight from Homer's Mentor; the documented route runs through Fénelon's *Télémaque* (1699), where alone Mentor is actually the wise, active tutor the modern word implies. In Homer the effective guide is Athena in Mentor's shape.
- **Historicity.** None; the figure is legendary, and no cult of Telemachus is attested (hence type: person, not deity).
