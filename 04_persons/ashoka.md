Ashoka Maurya (r. c. 268–232 BCE) was the third emperor of the Maurya dynasty, which controlled most of the Indian subcontinent. His conversion to Buddhism following the devastating conquest of Kalinga (c. 261 BCE, in which approximately 100,000 people died in battle and 150,000 were deported) is one of the most documented transformations in ancient history. Following his conversion, Ashoka used his imperial infrastructure to propagate dharma — Buddhist-inflected ethical law — through an unprecedented mass-inscription campaign.

## The Edicts — Writing as Political-Theological Technology

The Ashokan edicts are the earliest large corpus of datable Brahmi inscriptions and represent the first use of mass writing as political-theological communication in India. Unlike Mesopotamian cuneiform (primarily administrative records) or Egyptian hieroglyphs (primarily royal propaganda and religious ritual), Ashoka deployed writing as *moral instruction* addressed directly to the population.

The edicts were inscribed on:
- Natural rock faces at major road junctions
- Specially polished sandstone pillars (now among the most recognizable monuments of ancient India; the Sarnath lion capital is India's national emblem)
- Cave walls in Bihar (dedicated to non-Buddhist ascetics — an ecumenical gesture)
- Boulders at Buddhist pilgrimage sites

Content: prohibitions against animal sacrifice; instructions for the planting of shade trees and medicinal herbs along roads; medical facilities for humans and animals; religious tolerance ("One should not honor only one's own religion and condemn the religions of others"); periodic releases of prisoners.

See [[alphabet-brahmi-origin]] for the significance of these edicts in establishing the earliest datable Brahmi inscriptions and the question of how Brahmi itself originated.

## Ashoka and Buddhist Expansion

Ashoka sponsored Buddhist missions to Ceylon (Sri Lanka), the Hellenistic kingdoms (Egypt, Macedonia, Epirus, Cyrene), and Central Asia. The Theravada Buddhist tradition of Sri Lanka traces its founding to Ashoka's son Mahinda. The Ashokan dharma-infrastructure (monasteries, stupas, wells, rest-houses) created the physical network along which Buddhism spread across Asia.
