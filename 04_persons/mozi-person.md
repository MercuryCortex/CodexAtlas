# Mozi (Mo Di)

## Identity
Mo Di, conventionally known as Mozi ("Master Mo"), was the founder of Mohism — the first articulated rival to Confucianism in classical China and, in the late Warring States, Confucianism's principal competitor. Mozi was born in the state of Lu around -470 (just after Confucius's death) and died around -390. He was the first known organized leader of an ideological fraternity in Chinese history: the Mohists wore distinctive simple black garb, lived ascetically, and traveled in disciplined bands to defend cities under aggressive siege — an applied embodiment of the doctrines of *anti-aggression* (*fēi gōng*) and *universal love* (*jiān ài*).

## Historical evidence
Sima Qian's *Shiji* ch. 74 gives Mozi a brief notice at the end, expressing uncertainty about whether he was a contemporary of Confucius or "after him by some time." This already-old uncertainty (by ~-90 BCE) reflects the rapid decline of Mohism after the Qin-Han transition. The *Mozi* text itself attests Mo Di's name and contains references to his specific historical contemporaries (Duke Mu of Lu, the Duke of Zhao, the Lord of Lu Yang). Mengzi's well-known polemic ("Mozi's universal love is to be without a father") is the earliest external attestation of Mozi's doctrinal positions.

## Works
- The *Mozi*: 53 received chapters (originally 71). Three layers: (1) Core Doctrines — ten triadic essays on the ten characteristic Mohist topics; (2) Mohist Canons — six chapters of dense logical-epistemological-scientific writing (Later Mohist work, ~-300 BCE); (3) Dialogues — narrative anecdotes about Mozi. Only the Core Doctrines layer derives plausibly from Mozi's own teaching; the Canons are unambiguously Later Mohist; the Dialogues mix the two.

## Role in the tradition
Founder of Mohism — the principal Warring States rival to Confucianism, the second of the Two Reigning Schools (alongside Confucianism) according to Han Fei (*Xian Xue*, ch. 50). The movement collapsed during the Qin unification and the early Han; by mid-Han Mohism had effectively ceased to be a living tradition. The Mozi was preserved as a Daozang anomaly through the medieval period (it was included in the Daoist canon despite not being Daoist), kept in libraries but not actively read. Modern rehabilitation: Sun Yirang's 1894 *Mozi Jiangu* edition; Hu Shih's 1919 doctoral thesis rehabilitating Mohism as proto-utilitarian and proto-scientific; the 20th-c. translations (Mei, Watson, Johnston) and Graham 1978's reconstruction of the Mohist Canons brought Mozi back into the philosophical conversation. In modern Chinese self-understanding, Mozi has become the *lost alternative tradition* — the road not taken.

## Disputes
- **Date.** Conventional ~-470 to -390. Sima Qian's uncertainty reflects the genuine difficulty of placing him precisely.
- **Mozi's life and biography.** Beyond founding Mohism and the doctrinal positions, very little is securely known. The traditional account of Mozi as a craftsman (a maker of military defensive engines, a *gongren*) reflects Mohism's pro-artisanal stance but is not securely attested.
- **Authorship of the corpus.** Only the Core Doctrines plausibly derive from Mozi's own teaching; the Canons are Later Mohist; the Dialogues are mixed.
- **Was Mohism "religion" or "philosophy"?** The active-Tian theology, the spirit-belief, and the organized fraternity-form push toward "religion"; the systematic-argumentative form pushes toward "philosophy." Same debate as Confucianism, sharper.
