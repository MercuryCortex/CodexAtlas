# Bodhidharma

## Identity
Bodhidharma is the traditional founder of Chan / Zen Buddhism, said to have transmitted a *separate transmission outside the scriptures* (*jiao wai bie chuan* 教外別傳) from India to China in the early 6th century CE. He is identified as the 28th Patriarch of Indian Buddhism (in the lineage descending from Mahākāśyapa, who received the wordless transmission from the Buddha by smiling at a flower) and the First Patriarch of Chinese Chan. Tradition: a south Indian prince who became a monk; traveled to China; had a famously brusque audience with Emperor Wu of Liang ("Vast emptiness, nothing holy!" — when asked the meaning of the Buddha's holy truth); crossed the Yangtze on a reed; spent nine years gazing at a wall in a cave at the Shaolin Temple on Mount Song; transmitted the Dharma to his disciple Huike (who, according to legend, cut off his own arm to demonstrate his sincerity).

## Historical evidence
A historical Bodhidharma figure almost certainly existed but the surviving record is thin and the legendary biography is largely constructed. The earliest reference is a single passing mention in Yang Xuanzhi's *Luoyang Qielanji* (~547 CE) — describing a foreign monk called "Bodhidharma," originally Persian (波斯 *Bosi*), who admired the Yongning Temple in Luoyang. Daoxuan's *Xu Gaoseng Zhuan* (~645) gives the first short biography, identifying Bodhidharma as a south Indian (rather than Persian) and attributing the Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra transmission. The famous biography — the encounter with Emperor Wu, the reed-crossing, the nine-year wall-gazing at Shaolin, Huike's arm-amputation — emerges progressively in 8th-9th c. Chan literature and is fully developed only in Daoyuan's 1004 *Jingde Chuandeng Lu*. Faure 1986 reconstructs the layers: each successive Chan tradition added biographical detail aligned with its own theological program.

## Works
- *Two Entrances and Four Practices* (二入四行論, *Erru Sixing Lun*) — a short treatise on the two entrances to the Dharma (by principle, *li ru*; by practice, *xing ru*) and the four practices (accepting karmic recompense, adapting to conditions, seeking nothing, acting in accord with the Dharma). The only work plausibly by Bodhidharma himself; the attribution is contested (some scholars assign it to his disciple Tanlin) but the text is unambiguously early-6th-c. and consistent with what minimal evidence exists for Bodhidharma's teaching. Broughton 1999 translates.
- Most other works ascribed to Bodhidharma (the *Six Gates*, the *Treatise on the Bloodstream*, etc.) are later Chan compositions retroactively assigned.

## Role in the tradition
The legendary founder of Chinese Chan (Zen / Seon / Thien) — and one of the most iconographically recognizable Buddhist figures in East Asia (the Daruma doll in Japan; the bearded patriarch with intense glare in Chinese painting). The story of Bodhidharma transmitting the Dharma "outside the scriptures" became the constitutive Chan-self-narrative — Chan is *the* Buddhist tradition that does not depend on scripture but on direct mind-to-mind transmission, with Bodhidharma the founding figure of that lineage. The Laṅkāvatāra-transmission narrative anchors Chan's relation to a specific Mahāyāna sūtra (the Laṅkāvatāra) and was a key textual claim of the early "Laṅkāvatāra Masters" tradition before the Southern School's strategic shift to the Diamond Sūtra (codified in the [[phase-5-004-platform-sutra-huineng]]).

## Disputes
- **Historicity.** A real Bodhidharma figure (or some figure named "Bodhidharma") almost certainly existed; the *biography* is largely constructed by successive Chan generations. Faure 1986 is the foundational critical study. *[claim: contested]*
- **National origin.** Yang Xuanzhi (the earliest reference) calls him Persian (波斯). Daoxuan (the next earliest) calls him south Indian. Later tradition unanimously: south Indian prince. The Persian-origin reading has occasional modern defenders.
- **Dates.** Conventional floruit ~470-540, but the wide variation in the early sources makes precise dating impossible. The Emperor Wu of Liang audience (if historical) would place an event ~527.
- **The Laṅkāvatāra transmission.** Whether Bodhidharma actually transmitted the Laṅkāvatāra to Huike is doubted by most modern scholars; it appears to be an early-Chan self-positioning narrative.
- **The Shaolin tradition.** The story that Bodhidharma created Shaolin kung-fu while teaching martial-discipline exercises to enfeebled meditation monks is widely cited but historically baseless (a 17th c. invention).
