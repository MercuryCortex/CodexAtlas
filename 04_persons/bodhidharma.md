# Bodhidharma

## Identity

Bodhidharma is the traditional founder of Chan / Zen Buddhism — the Indian master who, in the standard Chan genealogy, transmitted a *special transmission outside the scriptures* (*jiao wai bie chuan* 教外別傳) from India to China in the early 6th century CE. He is the 28th Patriarch of Indian Buddhism (in the lineage descending from Mahākāśyapa, who received the wordless transmission from the Buddha via the flower sermon) and the First Patriarch of Chinese Chan. The subsequent patriarchal line runs through Huike, Sengcan, Daoxin, [[hongren]], and [[huineng]] (the Sixth Patriarch), whose Southern School defined Chan as it has been practiced ever since.

## Historical layer vs. legendary layer

Methodological principle: the historical and legendary Bodhidharma must be distinguished. Chan's authority rests on the legendary figure; the historical figure is far thinner.

**The historical Bodhidharma** (attested evidence only): A meditation master of foreign origin — Yang Xuanzhi's *Luoyang Qielanji* (~547 CE), the earliest surviving reference, describes a foreign monk the text calls Persian (波斯 *Bosi*) who was astonished by the Yongning Temple's beauty. Daoxuan's *Xu Gaoseng Zhuan* (~645 CE) gives the first short biography, identifies him as south Indian, and describes his teaching as centered on the Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra and a method called *biguan* (壁觀, "wall contemplation"). He had at least one significant disciple, Huike. McRae (2003) identifies him as a plausible figure at the origin of the early Chan patriarchal lineage, though the biography is largely a construction of subsequent Chan generations rather than contemporary documentation.

**The legendary Bodhidharma** (textually 7th–10th c., fully elaborated 1004 CE): Nine years facing a wall in a cave at Shaolin Monastery. The famous audience with Emperor Wu of Liang. Crossing the Yangtze River standing on a reed. Cutting off his own eyelids to stay awake during meditation (whence, in the legend, the first tea plants grew from the discarded eyelids — the origin of the tea-meditation link in Chan). [[huike]] cutting off his own arm in the snow to prove sincerity and receive teaching. Most scholars treat these narratives as founding myths that Chan generated to authorize its own transmission claim — each successive Chan faction added biographical layers that served its own theological program (Faure 1997).

## The Emperor Wu koan — the first great Chan encounter

The most consequential scene in Bodhidharma's legendary biography: his audience with Emperor Wu of Liang (ruled 502–549 CE), a great Buddhist patron who had built temples, supported monasteries, and funded the copying of sutras on a vast scale.

Wu asks: "I have built temples, copied sutras, supported monks throughout the empire. What merit have I gained?"
Bodhidharma: "None whatsoever." (*méi yǒu gōngdé* 沒有功德)
Wu: "What is the highest truth of Buddhism?"
Bodhidharma: "Vast emptiness — nothing holy." (*kuòrán wúshèng* 廓然無聖)
Wu: "Who stands before me?"
Bodhidharma: "I don't know." (*bù zhī* 不知)

The exchange encodes Chan's entire pedagogical program in three moves:

1. **Refusal of conventional merit** — the accumulated pious acts that constitute institutional Buddhism's economy of merit are declared worthless. Not because they are bad, but because they rest on a subject who "gains" something — and that subject is precisely the illusion Chan aims to dissolve.
2. **Apophatic deflation** — when asked for the highest truth, Bodhidharma gives not a doctrine but an anti-doctrine: emptiness is vast, and holiness is a projection. The "highest truth" cannot be stated as a positive teaching.
3. **Final non-knowing** — when asked who he is, the master does not say "I am Bodhidharma, the 28th Patriarch." He says he doesn't know. The self that would know itself as a patriarch is not present.

This encounter became the archetype of the Chan *mondo* (問答 *wèndá*, question-and-answer) format — encounter dialogue in which the master refuses conventional religious expectations, deflates doctrinal questions, and models non-knowing as the highest cognitive stance. Every subsequent koan collection (the *Blue Cliff Record*, the *Gateless Gate*) descends from this template.

## Wall-gazing (*biguan* 壁觀)

Bodhidharma's foundational meditation method as described by Daoxuan (~645 CE): *biguan*, "wall contemplation" or "wall-gazing." The "wall" in question is not a stone wall but the mind's own unmoving ground — the practitioner rests attention in the mind's natural stillness, neither grasping after thoughts nor pushing them away, as steady and impassive as a wall faces space.

The *Two Entrances and Four Practices* (the only text plausibly by Bodhidharma, translated by Broughton 1999) describes the "entrance by principle" (*li ru* 理入): "Abandon the false, return to the true; in a wall-gazing (*biguan*) state, there is no self or other, ordinary person and sage are one and the same; firmly abide without wavering, not following written teachings." This is the contemplative substrate of all subsequent Chan and Zen meditation.

### MASSIVE WIN — Biguan ↔ hesychia ↔ khalwa ↔ hitbonenut

The structural convergence of contemplative stillness traditions across world religion:

| Tradition | Practice | Core structure |
|---|---|---|
| Chan Buddhism | *biguan* 壁觀 (wall-gazing) | Attention withdrawn from outer objects; resting in the mind's unmoving ground |
| Christian hesychasm | *hesychia* (stillness) | Desert Fathers and [[tradition-eastern-orthodoxy]] Hesychast movement; inner silence as the condition for divine encounter; Gregory Palamas's 14th-c. systematization |
| Neoplatonism | Plotinian withdrawal (*epistrophē*) | Withdrawal from sensation and discursive thought to rest in the undivided One (*Enneads* I.6, VI.9) |
| Sufism | *khalwa* (retreat) | Forty-day or extended isolation from outer stimulation; the standard Sufi initiation retreat form in multiple orders |
| Judaism | *hitbonenut* (contemplative absorption) | Chabad Hasidic practice of sustained contemplation of a divine concept until the conceptual structure dissolves into direct apprehension |

All five traditions converge on the same structure: the practitioner withdraws attention from the external world, from discursive thought, and from the constructed self, and rests in the source. The methodological parallel is stronger than mere analogy — each tradition developed a formal practice, a pedagogical lineage to transmit it, and a theological argument for why this stillness opens access to ultimate reality.

## MASSIVE WIN — Transmission outside scriptures ↔ Sufi *silsila* ↔ apostolic succession

Bodhidharma is credited with establishing Chan's foundational self-description: *"A special transmission outside the scriptures (*jiao wai bie chuan* 教外別傳); not depending on words and letters; directly pointing to the human mind; seeing into one's own nature and attaining Buddhahood."* This claim — that authentic spiritual transmission is embodied, personal, lineage-dependent, and not reducible to textual study — is structurally identical to two major institutions in other traditions:

**Sufi *silsila*** (سلسلة, "chain"): The unbroken chain of initiation from the Prophet Muhammad through a sequence of masters to the living teacher to the student. No text, however authoritative, can substitute for the living transmission. The *silsila* list (recited at initiation) is a formal genealogy of transmission events, exactly analogous to the Chan patriarchal list from Mahākāśyapa to the current master. In both cases, the lineage *is* the credential.

**Christian apostolic succession**: The unbroken chain from the apostles to the current bishop; ordination within the chain validates teaching authority and sacramental power. A priest ordained outside this chain cannot validly celebrate the Eucharist, regardless of personal holiness or scriptural knowledge.

The convergence across all three:

| Feature | Chan transmission | Sufi *silsila* | Apostolic succession |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chain structure | Bodhidharma → Huike → … → living master | Prophet → Companion → Shaykh → murid | Apostles → bishop → priest |
| What transmits | Mind-to-mind awakening; *dharma* and *bowl-and-robe* | *Baraka* (divine blessing), the Path | Holy Orders; sacramental power |
| Requirement for validity | Living teacher in the lineage | Living shaykh with *silsila* | Bishop ordained in apostolic chain |
| Role of scripture | Subordinate ("outside the scriptures") | Subordinate (Quran is the ocean, the shaykh is the boat) | Subordinate (the Church authenticates scripture) |
| What fails without it | Intellectual Buddhism without awakening | Book-learning without transformation | Protestant schism (from Catholic perspective) |

This is among the vault's strongest cross-tradition convergences in **transmission epistemology**: three major world traditions independently developed the claim that the highest spiritual realities require a living, lineaged teacher, and that texts alone are insufficient. The claim is itself a theological argument about the limits of textual transmission.

## Works and textual legacy

**Two Entrances and Four Practices** (*Erru Sixing Lun* 二入四行論): The only text plausibly attributable to Bodhidharma himself. Short, cogent, and philosophically dense. The "two entrances" are *li ru* (entrance by principle — direct apprehension of one's own nature) and *xing ru* (entrance by practice — four behavioral practices: accepting karmic recompense, adapting to conditions, seeking nothing, acting in accord with the Dharma). Broughton (1999) provides the definitive translation and critical apparatus. Some scholars attribute the text to Bodhidharma's disciple Tanlin rather than Bodhidharma directly.

**Texts retroactively assigned**: The *Treatise on the Bloodstream* (*Xuemai lun*), *Six Gates*, *Breakthrough Sermon* (*Poxiang lun*) and others were composed by later Chan authors and attributed to Bodhidharma. This is standard Chinese textual practice for founding figures — the biography fills in; the corpus expands. Broughton (1999) distinguishes the authentic from the pseudepigraphical.

**The [[phase-4-103-lankavatara-sutra]] transmission**: Bodhidharma is said to have given the Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra to his disciple [[huike]] as the sole textual companion needed for Chan practice. Whether this transmission historically occurred is doubted by most modern scholars (it appears to be an early-Chan self-positioning narrative to anchor the movement's Mahāyāna credentials). The early Chan school was sometimes called the "Laṅkāvatāra masters" (*Léngqié zōng*) in reference to this transmission claim. The later Southern School under [[huineng]] strategically shifted the textual anchor to the [[phase-5-002b-diamond-sutra]].

## Historical disputes

- **Historicity**: A real Bodhidharma figure almost certainly existed; the *biography* is largely constructed by successive Chan generations. Faure (1997) is the foundational critical study. *[claim: the biography is a layered construction; a historical figure underlying it is probable]*
- **National origin**: Yang Xuanzhi (~547) calls him Persian (波斯). Daoxuan (~645) calls him south Indian. Later tradition unanimously: south Indian prince. *[claim: contested — earliest source says Persian; subsequent tradition says Indian]*
- **Dates**: Conventional floruit ~470–540 CE. The Emperor Wu of Liang audience (if historical) would place an event ~527. The wide variation in early sources makes precise dating impossible. *[claim: approximately 470–540, margin of decades either way]*
- **The Laṅkāvatāra transmission**: Whether Bodhidharma transmitted the Laṅkāvatāra to Huike is doubted by most modern scholars. *[claim: probably a legitimating narrative rather than a historical transmission]*
- **The Shaolin kung-fu tradition**: The story that Bodhidharma taught martial-discipline exercises to monks at Shaolin and thereby founded Shaolin martial arts is historically baseless — a 17th-century invention. *[claim: fabricated; no pre-17th-c. textual basis]*

## Role in the tradition

Bodhidharma is the constitutive figure of the Chan self-narrative: Chan is the Buddhist tradition that does not depend on scripture but on direct mind-to-mind transmission, and Bodhidharma is the Indian master who brought that transmission to China. The story — the Emperor Wu encounter, the nine-year wall-gazing, the transmission to Huike in the snow — functions as Chan's founding myth, authorizing the tradition's distinctive claims about where awakening comes from and what teachers are for.

His iconographic form — the bearded, wide-eyed foreign patriarch with an intense stare — is one of the most recognizable images in East Asian art. The *Daruma doll* in Japan, weighted to return upright when knocked over, is a popular artifact of the nine-year sitting legend.

The lineage he is credited with founding: Bodhidharma → [[huike]] → Sengcan → Daoxin → [[hongren]] → [[huineng]] (Southern School) / [[shenxiu]] (Northern School). The Southern School's victory in the 8th century defined Chan/Zen/Seon/Thiền as we know it, with [[d-t-suzuki]] as the modern transmitter of that inheritance to the West.
