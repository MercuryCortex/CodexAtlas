# Sargon of Akkad

## Identity
Sargon of Akkad (r. ~-2334 to -2279 BCE) was the founder of the **Akkadian Empire**, the first explicit multi-city-state empire in human history. From his (still archaeologically unlocated) capital Akkad — somewhere in the region between Babylon and Kish — he overthrew Lugal-zage-si of Uruk (the last hegemon of the Sumerian Early Dynastic IIIb period), unified Sumer under a single ruler, and extended Akkadian power on his own claim "from the Upper Sea to the Lower Sea" — i.e., from the Mediterranean coast to the Persian Gulf. His reign (and that of his grandson [[naram-sin]]) defined the *Sargonic* period (~-2334 to -2154), the first imperial epoch of Mesopotamian history. He is the father of [[enheduanna]], whom he installed as *en*-priestess of Nanna at Ur — the first identifiable author in human history — and through her his Akkadian dynastic claim was inscribed into the Sumerian religious establishment of the south. The Akkadian language, previously a secondary Semitic dialect in a Sumerian-dominant world, becomes administratively standard under Sargon, beginning its multi-millennium career as the dominant written language of Mesopotamia.

## Historical evidence
Robust though more limited than Ur III rulers:
1. **Royal inscriptions** preserved in Old Babylonian copies of the original Akkadian monuments — the originals stood at Nippur and were copied centuries later by scribal-school students. Frayne RIME 2 collects them.
2. **The Akkadian period administrative archives** — though substantially smaller than the Ur III archives, the Akkadian state did produce administrative texts at Nippur, Adab, Lagash, and elsewhere.
3. **Sumerian King List** entry: 56-year reign (which closely matches the inscriptional record of ~55 years).
4. **Enheduanna's own inscriptions and hymns** ([[phase-1-003-enheduanna-hymns]]) name him as her father.
5. **The Disc of Enheduanna** (Penn Museum) names him.
6. **The Legends of the Kings of Akkad** (Westenholz 1997) — later (OB and NA) literary tradition; *legendary* rather than primary-historical, but transmitting some likely historical kernel.

The Sargon Birth Legend — a later first-person legendary text in which Sargon is born in secret, placed in a reed basket, set on the Euphrates, found and raised by Akki the drawer-of-water — is generally **not** treated as primary historical evidence. It is the prototype of the foundling-king motif that would resurface in the Moses birth narrative of Exodus 2 (parallel-motif; not direct dependence).

## Works
None directly authored. Year-names and royal inscriptions (Frayne RIME 2). The Sargonic legendary cycle is later literary tradition.

## Role in the tradition
Sargon is the founder-figure of empire as a concept in Mesopotamian historical memory. His name and his deeds became the template against which subsequent rulers measured themselves; Naram-Sin extends his model and overreaches; Old Babylonian and Neo-Assyrian rulers consciously echo Sargonic titulature (Sargon II of Assyria, eighth century BCE, takes his name as deliberate imitation). The Sargonic legends — pseudo-autobiographical compositions — were standard scribal-school texts in the OB period.

In modern reception, Sargon has become the archetype of the empire-founder and the foundling-king. The parallels with the Moses birth narrative (Exodus 2:1–10) — secret birth, basket on the river, found and raised by a stranger — have been extensively debated since the 19th century; current consensus treats this as *parallel-motif* (a recurring ANE foundling-king motif) rather than direct literary dependence.

## Disputes
- **Location of Akkad**. The capital has not been archaeologically located; proposals include sites near the Sippar–Kish region and east of the Tigris. The unfound capital is a major Assyriological mystery.
- **Date**. Middle Chronology gives -2334 to -2279; Low Chronology shifts somewhat later.
- **Authenticity of the Birth Legend**. The legend is universally treated as later literary fiction; no scholar treats it as primary biography. Its parallels with the Moses narrative are *parallel-motif* in the strict sense.
- **Sargon and Lugal-zage-si**. The Sumerian King List's account of Sargon defeating Lugal-zage-si is reasonably anchored; archaeological-historical sequence is secure.
