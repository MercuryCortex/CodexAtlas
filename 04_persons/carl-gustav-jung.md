# Carl Gustav Jung

## Identity
Swiss psychiatrist (1875–1961); founder of analytical psychology; close collaborator and then antagonist of Freud (1907–1913); developed the concepts of the collective unconscious, archetypes, individuation, introversion/extraversion, the Shadow, anima/animus, the Self. Late period (1930s–1950s) focused on alchemy, Gnosticism, and the symbolic history of Christianity. The Bollingen Series and the C.G. Jung Institute (Zürich, 1948 onward) institutionalized his school as a quasi-religious as well as therapeutic tradition.

## Historical evidence
Extensively documented; the Bollingen Foundation archives (Library of Congress and Princeton), Jung's voluminous correspondence (8-vol *Letters* Bollingen series), Aniela Jaffé's *Memories, Dreams, Reflections* (1962, with Jung-collaboration questions), the Pauli–Jung correspondence (Meier ed.), and the recently published *Red Book / Liber Novus* (2009) — Jung's illustrated record of his 1913–1930 confrontation-with-the-unconscious period.

## Works
See texts-authored. The *Collected Works* in 20 volumes (Princeton/Bollingen 1953–1979) is the standard reference. The *Red Book* (composed 1914–1930, published 2009) opened a major new scholarly field on Jung's mystical-experiential basis.

## Role in the tradition
Jung is treated in this vault as a **hybrid scholar / quasi-religious-founder** because: (a) he produced rigorous psychiatric and clinical work; (b) he simultaneously functioned as the founder of a comprehensive worldview (Jungian depth psychology) with religious-quasi-religious practices (analysis as initiation; the *opus*); (c) his late writings (especially *Answer to Job*) are confessional-theological as much as academic. The Jungian movement post-Jung has had both scientific-clinical and spiritual-religious wings.

## Disputes
- **Politics 1933–1934.** Jung's editorship and Zentralblatt period; "Jewish vs. Aryan psychology" 1934 essay; his postwar denials and his contemporary public statements have been the subject of sustained debate. Bair 2003 is the balanced standard; Noll 1994, 1997 are sharply critical; Lammers and others defend.
- **Scientific status of analytical psychology.** Mainstream academic psychology generally does not consider Jungian analytical psychology empirically substantiated in the way it considers, e.g., cognitive-behavioral therapy; Jungian institutions maintain a clinical-and-spiritual hybrid status.
- **Red Book interpretation.** Whether the visionary content of 1913–1917 is best read as creative imagination, psychotic episode, mystical experience, or scientific self-experiment is itself a Jungian-studies debate.
