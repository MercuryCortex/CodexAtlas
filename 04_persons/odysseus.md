# Odysseus

## Identity
Odysseus (Greek **Ὀδυσσεύς**; Latin **Ulysses**) is the legendary king of Ithaca and the protagonist of the *Odyssey* — son of Laertes and Anticleia, husband of Penelope, father of Telemachus. He is the Homeric exemplar of *mētis* (cunning intelligence) and *polytropos*, "the man of many turns": the deviser of the Trojan Horse in the *Iliad*-cycle, and in his own epic the wanderer who endures a ten-year *nostos* (homeward return) from Troy to Ithaca, beset by the wrath of [[poseidon]] and aided by [[athena]]. There is no historical evidence for an individual person Odysseus; he is a heroic-legendary figure, the creation of the oral-formulaic epic tradition stabilised in the *Odyssey* in roughly the 8th-7th centuries BCE. In the Atlas he is placed among the legendary persons (modelled on [[orpheus]], the other Greek living-hero who crosses into the realm of the dead) because his defining episode — the **Nekyia of *Odyssey* 11** — is the foundational Greek *katabasis* narrative.

## In the Odyssey (the Nekyia, Book 11)
The eleventh book of the *Odyssey*, the **Nekyia** (Νέκυια, "the rite of the dead"), is Odysseus's consultation of the underworld. On Circe's instruction (Od. 10.487-540) he sails to the far edge of Ocean, to the land of the Cimmerians wrapped in perpetual mist, beaches at the grove of Persephone, and there performs the summoning: he digs a pit a cubit square, pours libations of milk-and-honey, wine, and water, scatters barley, and cuts the throats of a black ram and ewe so that the dark blood fills the trench. The shades of the dead gather to the blood, and Odysseus — sword drawn to keep them back until he has spoken with the seer — questions the Theban prophet **Teiresias** about his return.

The episode establishes, for the entire later Western tradition, the canonical features of the underworld visit: the crossing of water to the world's edge, sacrificial blood that restores speech to the dead, and encounters with specific named figures. Odysseus meets the shade of his own unburied companion Elpenor; his mother **Anticleia**, who died of grief in his absence (and whom he tries three times to embrace, his arms passing through her like a shadow, 11.204-222); the heroines and heroes of the past; **Agamemnon** and **Achilles** (who utters the famous judgement that he would rather be a living serf than king over all the dead, 11.488-491); and, near the close, the phantom (*eidōlon*) of **[[heracles]]** (11.601-627) — a detail the present Atlas hub on katabasis miscredits as *Heracles's* own descent, when in the text it is Heracles encountered *within Odysseus's* Nekyia. Crucially, Odysseus's descent is conducted **without a divine guide**: he is briefed beforehand by Circe and consults the dead on arrival, which sets the Homeric *nekyia* against the later *guided* katabasis of Virgil's Aeneas (led by the Sibyl) and Dante's pilgrim (led by Virgil).

A long-standing philological question is whether *Odyssey* 11 is, strictly, a *nekyia* (a summoning of the dead to the upper world, *necromancy*) or a *katabasis* (a bodily descent into Hades), since the text contains seams that point both ways; this is the classic problem the book has posed since antiquity (see Disputes).

## Cross-tradition significance
Odysseus is the **Greek anchor of the cross-tradition katabasis-and-anabasis motif**. *Odyssey* 11 is the foundational Western literary underworld-visit, and the explicit model for the two later texts that fix the type for European literature: **Virgil's *Aeneid* 6** (Aeneas's descent, which deliberately reworks the Nekyia) and, through Virgil, **Dante's *Inferno***. The episode therefore stands at the head of the Western branch of the motif catalogued by this hub.

Upstream of Homer, the closest analogues are Mesopotamian. The living hero who crosses the waters of death to consult one who knows the secret of return is the structure shared by Odysseus-to-Teiresias and **Gilgamesh-to-Utanapishtim** (*Gilgamesh* XI); the broader "wandering hero meets the divine ale-wife / island-goddess" scene links Odysseus's Circe-and-Calypso cycle to Gilgamesh's Siduri (*Gilgamesh* X). **Martin West** (*The East Face of Helicon*, 1997) and **Walter Burkert** (*The Orientalizing Revolution*, 1992) make this the strongest single case for Bronze Age Near Eastern literary transmission into Greek epic, probably via Anatolian intermediaries. Further upstream still lies the Sumero-Akkadian descent prototype — the [[inanna-sumerian|Descent of Inanna]] / Descent of Ishtar — though there the parallel is structural (the underworld-journey type) rather than a demonstrable line of dependence.

The honest framing matters here. The **Aeneid-and-Dante line is secure transmission**: Virgil and Dante demonstrably read and reworked Homer. The **Gilgamesh parallel is argued, not proven**: the resemblances are striking and West's transmission case is the mainstream view, but it remains a reconstruction, and cautious scholars (e.g. Ken Dowden) treat the Odyssey-Gilgamesh overlap as suggestive rather than settled. The **Inanna/Ishtar connection is typological** — a shared underworld-descent pattern widespread enough that independent convergence cannot be excluded. Odysseus thus belongs in the hub as the documented Western fountainhead of the motif, with a firm downstream lineage and a debated upstream one.

## Disputes
- **Nekyia or katabasis?** Whether *Odyssey* 11 depicts the dead summoned to Odysseus at the rim of the world (a *nekyia* / necromancy) or Odysseus actually descending into Hades (a *katabasis*) has been debated since the Alexandrian scholars. The text shows seams — the "Catalogue of Heroines," and the scenes of Minos judging and of Tantalus, Sisyphus, and Tityus undergoing punishment deep in Hades (11.568-600), sit awkwardly with a summoning conducted at a pit by the Ocean. Many analytic critics (and the Homeric scholia) treated parts of Book 11 as later interpolation; unitarian readers defend the book's coherence. The Atlas follows the standard practice of treating Odyssey 11 as the Western *katabasis* template while noting that it is, in its mechanics, primarily a *nekyia*.
- **Historicity.** None. Attempts to locate a "real" Odysseus or to pin the topography of the wanderings to a specific Mediterranean itinerary (a tradition going back to Eratosthenes' dismissal of such efforts) are not taken seriously by mainstream scholarship; the figure is legendary and the geography is poetic.
- **Direction and security of the Near Eastern parallel.** As above: West's transmission thesis is mainstream but is a reconstruction; the strength of the Gilgamesh-Odyssey link, versus independent convergence on a common underworld-journey type, remains an open question.
