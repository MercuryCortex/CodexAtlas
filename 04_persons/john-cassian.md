# John Cassian

## Identity
Born around 360, probably in Scythia Minor (modern Dobrudja, Romania) into a Latin-speaking but Greek-cultivated provincial family. Entered the monastery in Bethlehem around 380; spent the next 15 years (~385–400) in the Egyptian desert (Skete and elsewhere), absorbing the teaching of the great monastic abbas — including, controversially, Evagrius Ponticus. In Constantinople in the early 400s, he was ordained deacon by John Chrysostom and was sent to Rome to plead Chrysostom's cause after his deposition. Settled around 415 in Marseille, where he founded a double monastery (St Victor for men, St Salvator for women) and wrote his two great works of monastic theology, the ***Institutes*** and the ***Conferences***. Died at Marseille around 435.

## Historical evidence
His own works are the primary source. Gennadius of Marseille's *De viris illustribus* gives a brief biography. Prosper of Aquitaine's *Contra Collatorem* (~435) is the hostile contemporary witness to the semi-Pelagian controversy.

## Works
- ***De institutis coenobiorum*** (*Institutes*, 12 books) — the external practices of monastic life (dress, prayer cycle, fasting, communal rule) and the eight principal *logismoi* / vices (the original of what became the Western Seven Deadly Sins).
- ***Conlationes*** (*Conferences*, 24 books) — staged conversations with the great Egyptian abbas; the internal / spiritual side of monastic practice. The most influential monastic-spiritual text of the medieval West.
- ***De incarnatione Domini contra Nestorium*** (430) — anti-Nestorian Christology, commissioned by Leo the Great.

## Role in the tradition
The principal transmitter of **Egyptian desert monastic teaching to the Latin West**. Benedict's *Rule* explicitly enjoins reading Cassian (RB 73.5). The Eight Logismoi / Seven Deadly Sins of medieval Catholicism descend (via Gregory the Great) from Cassian's transmission of Evagrius. The Cassian-Augustine debate over grace and human free will (semi-Pelagianism: Cassian held that the initial movement toward God can be from the human side) was finally resolved against Cassian's position at the Council of Orange (529) — but Cassian's broader monastic theology was untouched and Orthodox East still honors him as a saint.

## Connections — substrate and influence
- **Cassian is the bridge**: Egyptian desert ([[antony-the-great]], [[pachomius]], [[evagrius-ponticus]], Macarius, Moses the Ethiopian) → Latin monasticism. His *Conferences* IX–X transmit Evagrian psychology and meditation technique (*ergasia*, the eight thoughts, *apatheia* renamed *puritas cordis* to dodge Stoic-Pelagian connotations) to the Latin tradition. Stewart 1998 traces this in detail.
- His **Origenist heritage via Evagrius**: Cassian's psychology is Origenist-Evagrian, structurally Platonic — the soul's progress from *praktike* (practical asceticism) to *theoria* (contemplation) to mystical union. After the 553 anti-Origenist condemnations, the Eastern transmission of this material became fraught; Cassian's Latin form survived intact because the West did not enforce 553.
- The eight-vices framework (gluttony, fornication, avarice, anger, sadness, *acedia*, vainglory, pride) drives Western moral psychology for a millennium. The framework itself is partly Stoic (catalogues of passions) reorganized through Christian ascetic experience.

## Disputes
- **Semi-Pelagianism.** Cassian's *Conference* 13 became the principal target of Prosper of Aquitaine's polemic; the question of whether Cassian was genuinely "Pelagian" or just careful about human cooperation with grace remains a theological debate.
- **Cassian's Evagrian dependence.** Cassian never names Evagrius (already a controversial figure in Cassian's late lifetime); the textual borrowings are extensive but quietly absorbed. Stewart 1998: 32–56.
