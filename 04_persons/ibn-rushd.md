# Ibn Rushd (Averroes; Abū al-Walīd Muḥammad ibn Aḥmad ibn Muḥammad ibn Rushd)

## Identity
**Abū al-Walīd Muḥammad ibn Aḥmad ibn Muḥammad ibn Rushd (1126–1198 CE)** — known throughout the Latin Middle Ages as **Averroes** and addressed by Aquinas as **"the Commentator"** (*Commentator* tout court — the unmodified noun means Averroes) — is the third of the four canonical falsafa philosophers (after [[al-kindi|al-Kindī]], [[al-farabi|al-Farabi]], [[ibn-sina|Ibn Sīnā / Avicenna]]) and the architect of the late-falsafa Andalusi peripatetic synthesis. Born to a Córdoban family of Mālikī jurists, Averroes served as **qāḍī (Mālikī judge) of Córdoba** under the Almohad caliphs Abū Yaʿqūb Yūsuf (r. 1163-1184) and Abū Yūsuf Yaʿqūb al-Manṣūr (r. 1184-1199), and was court physician to both. His **commentaries on Aristotle in three forms (Short / Middle / Long)** are the canonical Arabic Aristotelian commentary corpus; **all five Long Commentaries (*Posterior Analytics*, *Physics*, *De Caelo*, *De Anima*, *Metaphysics*) were translated into Latin in the 13th century** and became the standard medieval European Aristotelian commentary tradition. He **fell from favor c. 1195** (al-Manṣūr's anti-philosophy political turn under jurist pressure), with his philosophical books briefly burned and his presence banished from Córdoba; restored shortly before his death in Marrakesh in 1198. His most consequential reception, paradoxically, is **post-mortem and in Latin Europe** — Latin Averroism at Paris (Siger of Brabant, Boethius of Dacia) is condemned by Bishop Étienne Tempier in the **219 Propositions of 1277** (the largest condemnation of philosophical theses in medieval Europe), but the Aristotelian commentary tradition Averroes founded remains the European Aristotelian curriculum through the 16th century. (Leaman 1988; Hasse 2016; Butterworth 2001.)

## Historical evidence
Documented. Andalusi / Maghribi biographical sources (Ibn al-Abbār, al-Marrākushī, Ibn al-Khaṭīb) provide the biographical entries; the Latin-Christian reception is independently extensive. His extant manuscripts in Arabic, Hebrew (substantial — some works survive only in Hebrew translation), and Latin are well-catalogued. The fall-from-favor episode c. 1195 is documented in al-Marrākushī's *Muʿjib*.

## Works
- **Aristotelian commentaries** — three commentary-forms on most of the Aristotelian corpus: Short Commentary (*talkhīṣ* / *epitome*; paraphrastic), Middle Commentary (*tafsīr* / *paraphrase*; longer), Long / Great Commentary (*sharḥ kabīr* / *expositio*; lemmatized verse-by-verse Aristotelian exegesis). **The five Long Commentaries are on the *Posterior Analytics*, *Physics*, *De Caelo*, *De Anima*, and *Metaphysics*** and are the canonical medieval European Aristotelian commentary set. Translated into Latin in the 1230s-1240s primarily by Michael Scot at the Hohenstaufen court of Frederick II in Sicily; later by Hermannus Alemannus.
- **[[phase-5-046-ibn-rushd-tahafut-al-tahafut|*Tahāfut al-Tahāfut* (The Incoherence of the Incoherence)]]** — Averroes's point-by-point reply to al-Ghazālī's *Tahāfut al-Falāsifa*; the defense of falsafa against Ghazālian anti-philosophy. The bookend of the **[[tahafut-controversy|Tahafut controversy]]** — together with [[phase-5-045-al-ghazali-tahafut-al-falasifa|Ghazali's *Tahāfut al-Falāsifa*]], it forms a dialectical pair on whether revealed religion and demonstrative philosophy are compatible.
- ***Faṣl al-Maqāl* (Decisive Treatise on the Harmony of Religion and Philosophy)** — Averroes argues, **as a Mālikī jurist**, that demonstrative philosophical inquiry is a religious obligation (the Quran's *iʿtibār* — reasoned reflection on God's creation), and that revealed religion and demonstrative philosophy cannot truly conflict because both are different modes of approach to the same truth. Companion treatise *Kitāb al-Kashf ʿan Manāhij al-Adilla*.
- **Commentary on Plato's *Republic*** — Averroes's reading of the *Republic* as a treatise of Islamic political philosophy (the imam-philosopher-king as Almohad model; the city of the prophet-lawgiver). Surviving only in Hebrew (Samuel ben Judah of Marseille's 1320 translation); English from the Hebrew by Lerner 1974.
- ***Kitāb al-Kulliyāt fī al-Ṭibb* (Latinized *Colliget*)** — medical *summa*; secondary to Avicenna's *Canon* in medieval European medical education but influential as a complementary text.

## Role in the tradition
- **"The Commentator."** Aquinas's habitual designation of Averroes — *Commentator* tout court — signals Averroes's canonical status as the Arabic Aristotelian commentator par excellence. Aquinas engages Averroes more than any other non-Christian philosopher (503 citations in the *Summa Theologiae* and *Summa contra Gentiles*); the engagement is often critical (against the unicity-of-intellect doctrine) but proceeds at the highest philosophical level Aquinas knows.
- **Latin Averroism at Paris.** Siger of Brabant, Boethius of Dacia, John of Jandun develop a strong reading of Averroes at the Paris Arts faculty in the 1260s-1280s — most controversially, **the unicity of the material intellect** (the rational soul as a single cosmic intellect, individuated only in imagined-phantasms — implying that personal immortality is theologically problematic). Aquinas writes *De Unitate Intellectus contra Averroistas* against this reading. Bishop Étienne Tempier issues the **Condemnation of 1277** (219 propositions, many Averroist), the largest single condemnation of philosophical theses in medieval Europe; the Condemnation is the principal political-philosophical crisis of late-13th-c. Latin scholasticism. Some Tempier-condemned theses (e.g., proposition 27 on the eternity of the world, proposition 38 on God's knowledge of particulars) **track directly with the Ghazālī indictment of Avicenna in the *Tahāfut* a century earlier** — the same falsafa-theological tensions resurface across the Mediterranean transmission.
- **The 'double-truth' charge.** Latin Averroists were accused (by Aquinas, Bonaventure, Tempier) of holding the 'double-truth' doctrine — that philosophical and theological propositions can both be true even when they contradict each other. **Whether Averroes himself held this** is contested: Averroes's *Faṣl al-Maqāl* explicitly *rejects* contradiction between philosophy and revealed religion (both are different routes to one truth). The 'double-truth' charge appears to be the Latin-Averroist extension or polemical caricature rather than Averroes's own position.
- **Jewish-Andalusi reception.** Maimonides (the *Guide for the Perplexed* — [[phase-5-019-maimonides-guide-for-the-perplexed]]) is the younger Andalusi-Jewish contemporary; Maimonides arrived at Cordoba just before the Almohad anti-Jewish persecutions forced his family to Egypt; his philosophical theology is in dialogue with Averroes (cautious citations). Subsequent Jewish-Andalusi philosophy (Gersonides, Levi ben Gerson; Isaac Abravanel) is substantially Averroist. **Averroes survives in Hebrew translation** even where the Arabic is lost (Commentary on Plato's *Republic*, several Short Commentaries) — the Jewish-Provençal philosophical-translator tradition (Samuel ben Judah of Marseille, Moses ibn Tibbon, Kalonymus ben Kalonymus) is the principal preserver of Averroes outside Latin Europe.
- **Renaissance Aristotelianism through 1600.** Averroes's commentaries remain in the European Aristotelian curriculum through the Padua-Bologna Renaissance Aristotelianism (Pomponazzi, Zabarella, Cremonini); the late-medieval Latin printings of Aristotle conventionally include Averroes's commentaries on the facing pages.

## Disputes
- **Unicity of the material intellect.** Did Averroes hold one cosmic material intellect shared among humans (per the Latin-Averroist reading), or did he develop the position more carefully through his three commentary forms (Short Commentary unitarian; Middle Commentary individuated; Long Commentary apparently re-unitarian)? Modern critical scholarship (Davidson 1992 *Alfarabi, Avicenna, and Averroes on Intellect*) reads the position as evolving and the Long Commentary's mature view as a sophisticated unicity-position that the Latin Averroists then took as their banner.
- **Position on Ghazālī.** Averroes's *Tahāfut al-Tahāfut* concedes some Ghazālī points (e.g., that Avicenna's emanational cosmology requires correction toward stricter Aristotelianism) while defending falsafa against the broad anti-philosophy charge. Whether Averroes's defense is a re-defense of Avicenna or a re-foundation of falsafa on stricter Aristotelian lines is partly contested.
- **The 'double-truth' attribution.** Averroes's own *Faṣl al-Maqāl* rejects contradiction; the 'double-truth' charge is the Latin-Averroist extension. Modern scholarship is largely settled against attributing 'double-truth' to Averroes himself.
- **Fall-from-favor c. 1195.** Whether al-Manṣūr's anti-philosophy turn was a genuine theological-juridical decision under pressure from the Mālikī establishment, or a tactical political move during a campaign against Castile, is debated.
