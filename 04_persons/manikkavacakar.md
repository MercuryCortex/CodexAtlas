# Manikkavacakar

## Identity
9th-century Tamil Shaiva poet-saint, author of the *Tiruvācakam* — the *summa* of Tamil Shaiva devotional poetry. Per tradition: brahmin minister to the Pandyan king Varaguna II (or Arimarthana Pandyan in some versions) at Madurai; sent to purchase horses, he gave the king's gold instead to a Shaiva master who appeared to him; abandoned his ministerial career and traveled the Tamil country composing devotional poetry; finally merged with Śiva at Chidambaram. Unlike the canonical 63 Nayanars whose lives are recorded in Sekkizhar's *Periya Purāṇam* (~1135), Manikkavacakar is not formally among the 63; but his works are canonized in the *Tirumurai* (Book 8) and his place in Tamil Shaiva devotional life is supreme. The traditional saying: "*Mānikkavācakarukku urugātār yārukku urugu vārkaḷ*" — "Whose heart will melt, if not for Manikkavacakar's words?"

## Historical evidence
- The *Tiruvācakam* and *Tirukkōvaiyār* themselves, transmitted in the Tirumurai canon from at least the 10th c. (Nampi Aṇṭār Nampi's late-10th-c. canonization of the Tirumurai is the key transmission moment).
- Chola-period inscriptions referencing Manikkavacakar's hymns in temple liturgy from the 11th c. onward.
- Madurai and Chidambaram temple traditions with continuous association.

## Works
- ***Tiruvācakam*** (Sacred Utterances): 51 sections, 656 stanzas. Combines metaphysical exposition (the *Civapurāṇam*, the opening section, is the Tamil Shaiva creed in verse) with intense personal devotional outpouring. The third section, *Tirucātakam*, is a 100-verse meditation on Śiva's grace; the *Tiruvempāvai* (twenty verses on the girls' Margazhi vow) is the Tamil Shaiva counterpart to Andal's *Tiruppāvai* (the two are festival-paired).
- ***Tirukkōvaiyār*** (Sacred Garland): 400 verses; a virtuosic *akam* (interior, love-themed) cycle in classical Tamil love-poetics, mapped onto the meditative journey of the devotee-soul toward Śiva.

## Role in the tradition
- **Tamil Shaivism**: Manikkavacakar is the supreme devotional voice of Tamil Shaivism; the *Tiruvācakam* is the central liturgical-devotional text recited in Tamil Shaiva temples and homes daily.
- **Saiva Siddhanta**: the medieval Tamil Shaiva theological system (Meykanḍar 13th c., onwards) treats the *Tiruvācakam* as foundational scripture; the school's distinctive doctrines of *pati* (Lord), *paśu* (souls), *pāśa* (bondage), and grace-as-liberation are first poetically articulated in Manikkavacakar.
- **Bhakti's southern source**: alongside [[nammalvar]] (Vaishnava) and the Nayanars, Manikkavacakar is one of the deep Tamil-South sources for the pan-Indian bhakti tradition.

## Cross-tradition connections
- **Tamil Vaishnava-Shaiva festival parallel**: the *Tiruvempāvai* (Manikkavacakar) and the *Tiruppāvai* ([[andal]]) are festival-paired in the Tamil month of Margazhi; the parallel performance of Shaiva and Vaishnava women's-vow texts is a striking case of intra-Hindu sectarian parallelism preserved liturgically.
- **Tamil-Southeast Asian transmission**: the *Tiruvempāvai* was carried with the Tamil Shaiva ritual diaspora to Cambodia (the Khmer empire's substantial Shaivism) and especially to Thailand, where it became the *Triyampavai-Trippavai* state ritual recited at the coronation of Thai kings (continuing into the 20th century) — a remarkable case of medieval Tamil Hindu liturgy preserved in Buddhist Southeast Asian royal ritual.
- **Christian/Sufi mystical parallel**: G.U. Pope's 1900 translation explicitly framed Manikkavacakar's *Tiruvācakam* as comparable to the Hebrew Psalms and the Christian mystical bridal poetry tradition — a Victorian Anglican comparative-religion reading whose framing is dated but whose structural observation (intense personal devotional address to a transcendent-immanent God) remains apt for cross-tradition analysis.

## Disputes
- **Dates**: 9th c. is the standard placement; revisionist proposals span the 7th–10th c.
- **Pandyan patron**: which Pandyan king is the historical referent is unclear.
- **Canonical status**: why Manikkavacakar is not among the 63 Nayanars (per *Periya Purāṇam*) but is canonized in the Tirumurai is unresolved; possibly the *Periya Purāṇam* preserves an earlier list before Manikkavacakar's full canonization.
