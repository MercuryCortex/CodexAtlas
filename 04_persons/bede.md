# Bede

## Identity
Northumbrian Anglo-Saxon monk. Entered the twin monastery of Wearmouth-Jarrow at age 7 (around 680); ordained deacon at 19, priest at 30; spent the entirety of his life within the monastic enclosure. Author of the *Ecclesiastical History of the English People* — the foundational text of English historiography, completed 731. Died at Jarrow in 735. He is the only Englishman in Dante's *Paradiso* (X.131).

## Historical evidence
Documented through his own works (which include a brief autobiographical note at the end of the *Historia ecclesiastica*) and the *Epistola de obitu Bedae* by his pupil Cuthbert (a primary account of his death). His library at Wearmouth-Jarrow is the best-attested early-medieval English monastic library, supplied by Benedict Biscop and Ceolfrith from their Roman book-buying trips.

## Works
- ***Historia ecclesiastica gentis Anglorum*** (731) — 5 books covering Britain from Caesar's invasion to 731 CE, with the Augustinian mission of 596–597 as the pivot. Source for most of what is known of the conversion of England.
- ***De temporum ratione*** (725) — comprehensive treatise on the reckoning of time: computus, the Christian calendar, the Easter cycle, world chronology. Codified the AD / *Anno Domini* dating system across Latin Christendom (the system itself was Dionysius Exiguus's 6th-c. innovation; Bede popularized it).
- ***De natura rerum*** — natural-philosophy compendium.
- Extensive biblical commentaries — the bulk of his output.
- ***Vita Cuthberti*** — two lives of the Lindisfarne saint.
- ***De arte metrica*** — Latin metrical theory.

## Role in the tradition
Bede is the principal Latin scholar of the early Middle Ages between Isidore (d. 636) and Alcuin (d. 804). His chronological system, his historiographical method, and his exegetical commentaries became standard Carolingian-and-after reading. Without Bede, our understanding of early English Christianity would be reconstructed from charters and archaeology.

## Connections — substrate and influence
- Bede's *Historia ecclesiastica* deliberately uses **Eusebius's *Ecclesiastical History* as its model** (the dependence is explicit) — transmitting Eusebian Christian historiography to the medieval West.
- The Easter computus he codifies in *De temporum ratione* is itself a Christian solution to a problem inherited from the **Jewish lunisolar calendar** and Greco-Roman astronomical learning. The 19-year Metonic cycle that the Christian computus depends on derives from 5th-c. BCE Athenian astronomy (Meton of Athens, 432 BCE).
- His acceptance and dissemination of the *Anno Domini* dating system (Dionysius Exiguus, 525) puts the framework of Western chronology onto a Christ-centric framework — replacing the older Roman *Anno Urbis Conditae*, Diocletian-era, and consular dating.
- Bede preserves substantial information about Anglo-Saxon paganism (Easter from the goddess Ēostre, *HE* I.30 on Gregory's instruction to convert temples; pre-Christian poetry and naming) — making him one of our principal sources for what was being Christianized.

## Disputes
- **Historicity of certain *HE* miracles.** Bede's hagiographic register is part of his historiographical method; the basic political-religious chronology is generally accepted.
- **Bede's sources for *HE* I.** What was Bede's textual basis for the period before the Augustinian mission? Gildas, Orosius, and oral / written church sources from Canterbury.
