# Valerian

## Identity
Roman emperor 253–260, elevated during the crisis-decade of the mid-3rd century. Issued two edicts (257 and 258) escalating Christian persecution, targeting **clergy** (bishops, presbyters, deacons) and **senatorial- and equestrian-class** Christians — a deliberate strategy of decapitating the Christian leadership rather than the mass persecution attempted by Decius. Captured by the Persian king **Shapur I at Edessa in 260** — the first Roman emperor taken alive by an enemy; he died in Persian captivity. Christian writers (Lactantius, *On the Deaths of the Persecutors*) treated his fate as divine retribution.

## Historical evidence
**Cyprian's correspondence** (especially *Ep.* 76–80) provides detailed contemporary documentation of the edicts' application in Africa. **Eusebius HE 7.10–13** preserves Dionysius of Alexandria's reports. Shapur I's victory inscriptions at Naqsh-e Rustam (Iran) confirm the imperial capture.

## Works
- First edict (257): exile of bishops; prohibition of Christian assembly; confiscation of Christian property.
- Second edict (258): summary execution for clergy and high-class Christians who continued in the faith; loss of status for matronae; enslavement for imperial-household Christians who would not recant.

## Role in the tradition
- **Decapitation strategy**: a more targeted, sophisticated, and dangerous persecution than Decius's universal-sacrifice approach. It killed bishops including Cyprian, Sixtus II of Rome, and Fructuosus of Tarragona.
- **Cyprian's martyrdom (258)**: the most documented martyrdom of the period, with multiple recensions of the *Acta Cypriani* preserving the trial dialogues.
- **End of persecution**: Valerian's capture; his son Gallienus (260–268) issued a rescript ending persecution and restoring confiscated Christian property — a turning point in Christian-imperial relations.

## Disputes
- **Strategy vs. accident**: the targeted-clergy policy may have been deliberate strategy (Frend 1965) or pragmatic response to the inefficiency of Decian universal-sacrifice.
- **Manner of death in Persian captivity**: Christian sources (Lactantius) give a gruesome death (flayed and stuffed); Persian sources less clear.
