# al-Kindi (Abū Yūsuf Yaʿqūb ibn Isḥāq al-Kindī)

## Identity
**Abū Yūsuf Yaʿqūb ibn Isḥāq al-Kindī (~801–873 CE)** — known in Latin as **Alkindus** — was the **first major philosopher in the Arabic language** and is conventionally identified as the founder of the falsafa tradition. Patronized by the Abbasid caliphs al-Maʾmūn (r. 813-833), al-Muʿtaṣim (r. 833-842), and al-Wāthiq (r. 842-847) during the caliphal sponsorship of the **Graeco-Arabic translation movement at the Bayt al-Ḥikma ("House of Wisdom") in Baghdad**, al-Kindi supervised a "circle" of translators rendering Greek philosophical, scientific, and medical works into Arabic. His own corpus (approximately 270 attributed treatises, of which ~40 substantially survive) spans **metaphysics, epistemology, optics, music theory, astrology, pharmacology, cryptography, and meteorology**, in a fundamentally **Neoplatonist-Aristotelian synthesis** typical of the late-antique-into-Arabic philosophical inheritance. He was theologically aligned with the Muʿtazilī rationalist school that the Abbasid caliphate officially supported during the *miḥna* period; his fortunes declined after the Sunni-Hanbali traditionalist restoration under al-Mutawakkil (r. 847-861). (Adamson 2007; Gutas 1998.)

## Historical evidence
Documented. Al-Kindi is named in the standard early Islamic biographical-bibliographical literature: **Ibn al-Nadīm's *Fihrist*** (~988 CE) provides the principal catalog of his works; biographical entries appear in al-Qifṭī, Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿa, and the Latin-Christian bio-bibliographical sources. His extant manuscripts (mostly in Arabic; some via medieval Hebrew or Latin translation) are catalogued and edited in the modern critical-editions literature (Adamson-Pormann 2012; Ivry 1974). His authentic-vs-attributed corpus is partly settled, partly contested.

## Works
- **Authentic.** *Fī al-falsafa al-ūlā* (*On First Philosophy* — metaphysics; foundational treatise. Ivry 1974), *Risāla fī al-ʿaql* (On the Intellect — Latinized as *De Intellectu*; foundational for Avicennian and Latin-scholastic intellect-theory), *Risāla fī ḥudūd al-ashyāʾ wa-rusūmihā* (On Definitions of Things), *Fī kammiyyat kutub Arisṭū* (On the Quantity of Aristotle's Books — a propaedeutic to the philosophical curriculum). Numerous treatises on optics (*De Aspectibus* via the Latin), music theory (foundational for medieval Arabic and Latin music theory), pharmacology, astrology.
- **Translated under his supervision** (al-Kindi-circle production). Major Neoplatonist Greek works including the **[[phase-5-048-theology-of-aristotle-arabic|*Theology of Aristotle*]]** (pseudo-Aristotelian paraphrase of Plotinus *Enneads* IV-VI — a foundational text of Arabic Neoplatonism that, via the misattribution, gave the falsafa tradition a substantial Plotinian core *thinking it was reading Aristotle*) and the **Liber de Causis** (Arabic paraphrase of Proclus *Elements of Theology*; later Latin-medieval staple). These al-Kindi-circle translations are arguably more historically consequential than al-Kindi's own original treatises.

## Role in the tradition
- **Founder of falsafa.** The conventional designation "Philosopher of the Arabs" (*faylasūf al-ʿArab*) — al-Kindi inaugurates the Arabic-language philosophical tradition that will run through [[al-farabi|al-Farabi]], [[ibn-sina|Avicenna]], [[ibn-rushd|Averroes]], and into Latin scholasticism.
- **Theological-philosophical synthesist.** Al-Kindi defended the compatibility of philosophy with revealed religion — Greek metaphysics with Islamic monotheism. His Aristotelian-Neoplatonist demonstration of the createdness of the world (against the Aristotelian eternity-of-the-world doctrine) was an attempt to reconcile philosophy with the Quranic creation-doctrine — the same problem that [[ibn-sina|Avicenna]] and then [[al-ghazali|al-Ghazālī]] and [[ibn-rushd|Averroes]] will rework in different directions, culminating in the [[tahafut-controversy|Tahafut controversy]].
- **Patronage and the Bayt al-Ḥikma.** Al-Kindi's career exemplifies the Abbasid-caliphal patronage of Greek-into-Arabic translation that made falsafa possible. Gutas 1998 makes the case that this translation movement is **the single most important intellectual transfer of the medieval Mediterranean** — Greek philosophy, science, medicine, and astronomy translated systematically into Arabic, preserved and developed, and then transmitted into Latin Europe via the 12th-c. Toledo school.
- **Latin reception as Alkindus.** Al-Kindi's optical treatise (*De Aspectibus*) is translated into Latin in the 12th c. and influences medieval Latin optics — Roger Bacon, Witelo, John Pecham. His *De Intellectu* is translated into Latin and engaged by Albert the Great and Aquinas. The Latin reception is narrower than that of Avicenna or Averroes but real and load-bearing in medieval Latin optics and intellect-theory.

## Disputes
- **Authenticity of attributed treatises.** Ibn al-Nadim's *Fihrist* attributes ~270 treatises to al-Kindi; ~40 substantially survive; ~30 are firmly authentic; the remainder is contested.
- **Theological alignment with the Muʿtazila.** Adamson 2007 argues that al-Kindi's theological positions overlap substantially with Muʿtazilī rationalism (createdness of the Quran, divine-attribute reductionism, free will) but al-Kindi is not narrowly a Muʿtazilī partisan; he's a philosopher engaging the theological-philosophical questions on his own terms.
- **Originality vs. translation-supervisor.** How much of al-Kindi's intellectual contribution is original work versus expert framing of the Greek translation enterprise? Modern critical scholarship leans toward viewing him as a substantive original thinker engaging a Greek inheritance, not merely a translator-supervisor.
