# Al-Hallaj

## Identity
Manṣūr al-Ḥallāj (858–922) — the most famous Sufi martyr — was an early Sufi ecstatic whose public utterance "*Anā al-Ḥaqq*" ("I am the Real" / "I am the Truth") brought trial, execution, and 1000 years of Sufi commentary. Born in Persia (al-Bayḍāʾ in Fars); spent much of his career in Baghdad and traveled to Khurasan and India; subjected to long Baghdad imprisonment (911–922) on charges of public scandal, doctrinal heresy, and (perhaps the real charge) political associations with critics of the Abbasid regime; tortured and crucified in 922. The execution made him a foundational figure of Sufi self-understanding: the martyr-witness whose ecstatic utterance was either heresy (orthodox Sunni reading) or the genuine speech of the Real through the annihilated self (later Sufi reading, esp. Akbarī).

## Historical evidence
Substantially documented through contemporary Abbasid court records (al-Ṭabarī and other historians cover the trial), through al-Ḥallāj's surviving works, and through the extensive Sufi memorial tradition. Louis Massignon (1883–1962) devoted 50 years to the source-criticism, reconstructing al-Ḥallāj's life and trial in monumental detail.

## Works
*Kitāb al-Ṭawāsīn* (~22 short ecstatic treatises on Islamic mystery-symbols); fragments of a *Dīwān* of poetry; reports of teaching preserved in early Sufi collections.

## Role in the tradition
The paradigmatic Sufi martyr. The *Anā al-Ḥaqq* utterance is the locus classicus of ecstatic-utterance (*shaṭḥ*) — Sufi-orthodox reception understands it as God speaking through the annihilated self; Sunni orthodoxy generally regards it as heretical. Al-Ghazālī defended al-Ḥallāj cautiously; Ibn ʿArabī integrated his theology fully; Rūmī cites him repeatedly. Modern Iranian and Pakistani Sufi reception treats him as the foundational martyr of ecstatic Sufism.
