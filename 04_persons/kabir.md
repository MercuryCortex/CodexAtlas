# Kabir

## Identity
15th-century North Indian poet-mystic of Varanasi, a Muslim weaver by social location who composed devotional-iconoclastic verse in Hindi/Bhojpuri vernacular synthesizing Hindu and Sufi-Islamic monotheistic-devotional currents. Kabir polemicized against Brahmanical ritualism *and* against Islamic external practice, calling instead for direct devotion to an unnameable, formless, ungendered divine reality (*Rām*, *Hari*, *Allah* — names indifferent). His verse is preserved in the *Bijak* (the principal Kabir-Panth recension), the *Kabir-granthavali* (Rajasthani manuscripts), and substantial selections in the *Guru Granth Sahib*, the Sikh scripture compiled by Guru Arjan in 1604.

## Historical evidence
Documented through near-contemporary references; the *Kabir Parachai* (Ananta-das, late 16th c.) is the earliest hagiography. Substantial Muslim and Hindu hagiographic traditions both claim him; the most-cited dates are 1440–1518 (long-life tradition) though substantial earlier and later dates have been argued.

## Works
- *Bijak* (Kabir-Panth recension; the foundational text of the Kabir-Panth sect that grew up around his memory).
- *Kabir-granthavali* (Rajasthani recension).
- Selections in the *Guru Granth Sahib* (Sikh canonization).

## Role in the tradition
- **Sant tradition**: foundational figure of the *nirguni* (formless-divine) North Indian sant lineage; precursor to Dadu, Nanak, Ravidas.
- **Sikhism**: a major Bhagat included in the Guru Granth Sahib; Kabir's verse is part of Sikh canonical liturgy.
- **Kabir-Panth**: a distinct sectarian community that grew up around his memory.

## Cross-tradition connections
- **The most-documented Hindu-Sufi synthesis figure in pre-modern South Asia**: Kabir's verse explicitly addresses the divine as *Rām* / *Hari* / *Allāh* / *Khudā* / *Sain* / *Sahib* interchangeably; the rhetorical position is that all religious externals (Brahmanical caste, Islamic shariʿa, Yogic siddha asceticism) are equally inadequate to the formless divine reality. Vaudeville 1993 *A Weaver Named Kabir* and Hess & Singh 1983/2002 *The Bījak of Kabir* are the foundational comparative studies. The polemical model — using shared scriptural-rationalist methods to argue against multiple religious traditions simultaneously — has structural cousins in late-medieval Sufi vernacular poetry (Rumi, Hafiz, the Indo-Persian *bhakti-marfa* literature) and is one of the principal pre-modern documented cases of religious-boundary-transcending devotional poetics.
- **Wired to [[guru-nanak]] and Sikhism**: the Adi Granth (1604) incorporates ~270 Kabir hymns as canonical scripture for the Sikh tradition; the Sant lineage Kabir-Ravidas-Namdev → Nanak is one of the clearest pre-modern Hindu-to-new-religion transmissions.
- **Akbar-period *sulh-i kull* atmosphere**: Kabir died (1518) before Akbar (r. 1556–1605), but the Akbar-period state ideology that valorized cross-religious harmony was substantially built on the late-medieval Sant-Sufi shared devotional idiom of which Kabir is the most-cited single representative.
- **Modern Indian anti-caste politics**: B.R. Ambedkar invoked Kabir as a foundational anti-caste voice; modern Dalit-Bahujan political movements have repeatedly reclaimed Kabir.
- **20th-c. global reception**: Rabindranath Tagore's 1915 *Songs of Kabir* (a loose literary translation) inaugurated the modern global Kabir reception; Beat poets, the 1960s counterculture, and the contemporary "spiritual but not religious" idiom all read Kabir alongside Rumi.

## Disputes
- Hindu vs. Muslim identification (traditionally claimed by both); the modern scholarly consensus reads Kabir as transgressing the boundary.
- Dating; authenticity of attributed verse (Hess and others have demonstrated substantial later attribution).
