# Apollos

## Identity
An eloquent Alexandrian Jewish convert to Christianity, described by Acts 18:24 as *anēr logios* ("a learned/eloquent man") and *dynatos en tais graphais* ("powerful in the scriptures"). He arrived at Ephesus already preaching but knowing "only the baptism of John" — was instructed more fully by [[priscilla]] and [[aquila]] — then traveled to Corinth where his powerful preaching made him a rival pole to Paul in the Corinthian community.

## Historical evidence
Strong (Acts + Pauline letters). His **Alexandrian** background — explicit in Acts — is a critical data point: it suggests early Christianity reached Alexandria *independently* of the Pauline mission and that the Alexandrian-Jewish-allegorical exegetical tradition (associated with [[philo-of-alexandria]]) entered Christianity through figures like Apollos.

## Works
None genuine. **Luther** proposed (1537) that Apollos wrote the Epistle to the Hebrews — based on (a) Hebrews's anonymity, (b) its strikingly polished Greek, (c) its Alexandrian-Philonic exegetical method, and (d) Apollos's profile fitting all three. Modern critical scholarship treats this as plausible-but-unprovable; alternative candidates include [[priscilla]] (Harnack), Barnabas, and various unknown Alexandrian Jewish-Christians.

## Role in the tradition
**Corinthian factionalism (1 Cor 1–4)**: Paul names Apollos alongside himself and Cephas as the figures around whom Corinthian factions had formed. Whether Apollos *cultivated* a faction (Paul implies not) or it merely formed around him is debated.

**Alexandrian Christianity origin story**: Apollos as the *first attested* Alexandrian Christian, prefiguring the great Alexandrian theological school of [[clement-of-alexandria]] and [[origen]].

## Disputes
- **Hebrews authorship.** Luther's proposal is plausible but unprovable.
- **Corinthian faction.** Did he encourage or merely attract followers?
- **Earlier Alexandrian Christian community.** If Apollos was already a Christian preacher when he arrived at Ephesus, where did he learn? Suggests a pre-Pauline Alexandrian Christianity — but Acts says he knew "only the baptism of John," which complicates the picture.
