# Lactantius

## Identity
Lucius Caecilius Firmianus Lactantius (~250–325 CE) — Latin Christian rhetor, apologist, and imperial tutor. Born in Roman North Africa; educated by Arnobius the Elder. Appointed by Diocletian as a teacher of Latin rhetoric at Nicomedia (Bithynia), the Diocletianic eastern capital. Lost his post in the Great Persecution of 303 (he refused to sacrifice; he survived only by going underground). After Constantine's victory and the Edict of Milan (313), recalled to the Constantinian court at Trier as tutor to Constantine's son Crispus. Author of the *Divine Institutes* (*Divinae Institutiones*, ~311), the first systematic Latin Christian theology, and the *De Mortibus Persecutorum* (~314), the principal historical source for the Great Persecution and Constantinian religious policy. Style so polished that he was called by Renaissance humanists "the Christian Cicero."

## Historical evidence
Documented through his own surviving works (which give autobiographical and historical detail), through citations in Jerome (*De Viris Illustribus* 80, lengthy biographical entry) and Eusebius. Constantinian-court career externally documented.

## Works
- *Divine Institutes* (*Divinae Institutiones*, ~311) — seven books. The first systematic Latin Christian theology.
- *Epitome Divinarum Institutionum* (~314) — abridgment.
- *De Opificio Dei* (~303) — on creation.
- *De Ira Dei* (~313) — on divine providence.
- *De Mortibus Persecutorum* (~314) — historical narrative of the Great Persecution.
- *De Ave Phoenice* (poem, attribution disputed).

## Role in the tradition
**Lactantius is the load-bearing patristic-Hermetic engagement node — the single most important Christian author for the documentary-attested Hermetic-Christian transmission edge in the early 4th century.** He cites Hermes Trismegistus 21+ times across the *Divine Institutes*, treating Hermes as a pagan-prophet who anticipated Christian theological truth — the *prisca theologia* reading later canonized in Renaissance Hermeticism (Ficino, Pico, Steuco). Lactantius's reading is **the opposite polemic** to Augustine's *City of God* VIII.23–26 engagement with the same Hermetic Asclepius a century later. Augustine reads Hermes as a defeated pagan inadvertently prophesying his own religion's loss; Lactantius reads Hermes as a near-Christian whose pagan testimony confirms Christian doctrine. Both polemic readings are taken from the same Latin Asclepius (and possibly from Hermetic material now in the Coptic Asclepius / Armenian Definitions / Stobaean Excerpts).

In addition, Lactantius is the principal source for two foundational events of Christian political history:
1. **The Great Persecution (303–313)** — first-hand eyewitness account in *De Mortibus*.
2. **The Edict of Milan (313)** — preserves the text and the political-religious context.

His Constantinian-court position made him an architect of the *Christian-Roman political vocabulary* that would dominate the next millennium (Digeser 2000).

## Originator of (related)
Lactantius is not an *originator* of major Christian doctrine; his theology is borrowed from Cyprian, Tertullian, and Greek apologists. But he is a **major preserver and transmitter** of the Hermetic-as-pagan-prophet reading that becomes load-bearing in Renaissance Hermeticism: Ficino's 1471 Latin *Pimander* preface explicitly cites Lactantius's *Divine Institutes* as authority for taking Hermes Trismegistus seriously as a pre-Mosaic sage.

## Disputes
- **Date of *Divine Institutes*.** Earlier dating ~305–311; some place portions earlier. The "Constantinian addresses" inserted in the text are post-313.
- **Was Lactantius a heretic?** Jerome accuses him of subordinationism; the *De Ira Dei* has been read as Stoicizing. Modern consensus: orthodox by the loose 4th-c. standards but not strictly Trinitarian by post-Nicene standards.
- **Is the *De Ave Phoenice* his?** Disputed.
