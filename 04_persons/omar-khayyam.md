# Omar Khayyam

## The Mathematician Who Solved the Cubic

Omar Khayyam (1048–1131) is the Persian mathematician who solved every type of cubic equation — and simultaneously one of the great lyric poets in the Persian language. His mathematical work extended al-Khwarizmi's algebra to problems al-Khwarizmi could not solve; his poetry (*Rubaiyat*) became the most widely read Persian poetry in the Western world after FitzGerald's 1859 translation.

That the same person produced the most rigorous algebraic treatise of the 11th century and the most celebrated quatrains of Persian literature is not coincidence. Khayyam understood both as investigations into pattern, transience, and the nature of number.

## MASSIVE WIN — Completing al-Khwarizmi's Program

Al-Khwarizmi (825 CE) had solved linear and quadratic equations algebraically. Cubic equations (x³ + ax² + bx + c = 0) were beyond al-jabr's algebraic methods.

Khayyam's *Treatise on Demonstration of Problems of Algebra* (c. 1070) classifies all **14 types of cubic equations** and solves each geometrically using the intersection of two conic sections — a circle and a parabola, or a circle and a hyperbola, depending on the type. Where al-Khwarizmi completed the square, Khayyam completed the cube.

His solution is geometric rather than algebraic — he finds the roots as geometric lengths, not as numerical formulas. The algebraic formula for the cubic (the Cardano-Tartaglia formula of 1545 CE) would not arrive for 475 more years. But Khayyam's geometric approach proved that all cubics were solvable and classified every case.

## MASSIVE WIN — The Parallel Postulate and Non-Euclidean Geometry

Khayyam also investigated Euclid's Fifth Postulate (the Parallel Postulate). He constructed a quadrilateral (now called the Saccheri-Khayyam quadrilateral, though Khayyam did it 600 years before Saccheri) and proved that the angles of the quadrilateral must be right angles IF the Fifth Postulate is true — but he could not prove the right-angle case from first principles.

In effect, Khayyam identified the same logical problem that would eventually be resolved when Bolyai and Lobachevsky showed the Parallel Postulate is independent (1820s) and Einstein showed the actual universe is non-Euclidean (1915). Khayyam was 700 years early on one of the deepest questions in mathematics.

## The Jalali Calendar

In 1079, Khayyam led a team of astronomers commissioned by Seljuk Sultan Malik-Shah to reform the Persian calendar. The result — the Jalali Calendar — has a year length accurate to within **one day in 3,770 years**. The Gregorian calendar (introduced 1582 CE, still in use) accumulates one day of error every 3,226 years. Khayyam's 11th-century calendar is more accurate than the one the modern world currently uses.

## The Rubaiyat — Mathematics and Mortality

The *Rubaiyat* (quatrains, رباعیات) are short meditations on wine, love, fate, and the transience of all things. FitzGerald's 1859 English adaptation made Khayyam famous in the Victorian world — the poem's fatalistic sensualism resonated with an era questioning religious certainty.

"The Moving Finger writes; and, having writ, / Moves on: nor all thy Piety nor Wit / Shall lure it back to cancel half a Line, / Nor all thy Tears wash out a Word of it."

That this poet was also the mathematician who classified cubic equations is one of the vault's most unexpected cross-tradition facts: the same mind that analyzed irreversible mathematical transformations also wrote the most famous Western poem about irreversibility.
