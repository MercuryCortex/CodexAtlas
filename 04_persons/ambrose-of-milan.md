# Ambrose of Milan

## Identity
Roman aristocrat (son of a praetorian prefect of Gaul), provincial governor in Liguria-Aemilia when he was abruptly elected bishop of Milan in 374 — by acclamation, while still a catechumen. Baptized and consecrated within a week. Bishop until his death in 397. The most politically formidable Latin bishop of the fourth century: he confronted Emperor Theodosius after the Thessalonica massacre (390) and compelled public penance — the first time imperial power was made to bend to ecclesiastical discipline.

## Historical evidence
Voluminous Latin corpus, in continuous transmission. Augustine's *Confessions* (V.13, VI.3–4) is a major source for Ambrose's personal impact. Paulinus of Milan wrote the *Vita Ambrosii* shortly after his death.

## Works
- ***De officiis ministrorum*** — Christian ethics for the clergy, modeled directly on Cicero's *De officiis*.
- ***De fide*** and ***De Spiritu Sancto*** — anti-Arian Trinitarian works.
- ***Hexaemeron*** — six sermons on Genesis 1, dependent on Basil of Caesarea's *Homilies on the Hexaemeron*.
- ***De Isaac vel anima***, ***De bono mortis*** — allegorical-Platonist works on the soul.
- ***De mysteriis*** / ***De sacramentis*** — major early witnesses to Latin baptismal liturgy.
- **Hymns** — *Aeterne rerum conditor*, *Deus creator omnium*, etc. Established the Latin hymn (iambic dimeter, eight-stanza form). The "Ambrosian" liturgical tradition of Milan still bears his name.

## Role in the tradition
The most consequential ecclesiastical-political figure of the Latin 4th c. Baptizer of Augustine (Easter Vigil 387). Architect of the Latin allegorical sermon (which is what won Augustine over, *Confessions* V.13). Hymnographer who shaped Western liturgical music. Confronter of emperors who set the precedent for medieval church-state confrontations.

## Connections — substrate and influence
- His *De officiis* is methodologically a Christian rewriting of [[seneca]] / Cicero — Stoic ethics with Christian *officia*. McLynn 1994: 248–60; Ramsey 1997: 1–55.
- His allegorical exegesis (*De Isaac vel anima*, *De Cain et Abel*) is heavily dependent on [[philo-of-alexandria]] — directly read or via Origen / Didymus the Blind. The Philonic *Logos* and the soul's ascent reach the Latin West through Ambrose.
- His ascent-of-soul mysticism in *De Isaac vel anima* and *De bono mortis* draws on [[plotinus]]'s *Enneads*, which were already circulating in Latin translation (Marius Victorinus); Augustine read them on Ambrose's recommendation (*Confessions* VII.9). This is the proximate channel by which **Plotinian Neoplatonism enters Augustine's Christianity**, and hence Western theology.
- His hymn-form is partly inherited from the Greek-Syriac hymnographic tradition (Ephrem) but Latin-metricized — a transmission point for Eastern liturgical practice.

## Disputes
- **The "Ambrosian" liturgy.** Which elements truly go back to Ambrose vs. later Milanese practice is debated.
- **De Spiritu Sancto and Didymus the Blind.** Jerome accused Ambrose of plagiarism from Didymus; modern scholarship treats Ambrose's use as standard patristic adaptation.
