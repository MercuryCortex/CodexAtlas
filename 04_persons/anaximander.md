# Anaximander of Miletus

## Identity
Anaximander (c. -610 to -546) was [[thales]]'s student and successor in the Milesian school of Ionian natural philosophy. He is credited with **three foundational firsts**: (1) the **first known Greek prose treatise** (the lost *On Nature*, c. -550), inaugurating the prose-philosophical genre that runs through Heraclitus, Plato, Aristotle, and onward; (2) the **first known map of the world** (a circular ecumene with the Mediterranean at the center, Asia and Europe and Libya around it); (3) the **first cosmological doctrine of the *apeiron*** — the "boundless" or "indefinite" — as the primordial source of all things, a step *more abstract* than Thales's water. He is also among the earliest figures to propose an **evolutionary-style explanation of human origins** (humans descended from fish-like creatures in the primordial sea — astonishingly proto-Darwinian in suggestiveness, though obviously not in mechanism), and to propose a **rotating cylindrical earth** floating freely at the cosmic center, balanced not by support but by **equipoise** (cosmic symmetry — a remarkable physical-philosophical move).

## Historical evidence
Like Thales, Anaximander left no surviving continuous text. He is reconstructed from:
- **[[aristotle]]** *Physics* 3.4 (203b6 ff.) and *On the Heavens* — extensive engagement with Anaximander's *apeiron*.
- **Theophrastus** (Aristotle's successor) *Physical Opinions* — the principal lost doxographical source, partly preserved in later compilations.
- **Hippolytus** *Refutation of All Heresies* 1.6 — preserves what is conventionally treated as a verbatim Anaximander fragment (DK B1): **"The things that are perish into the things from which they have come to be, according to necessity, for they pay penalty and retribution to each other for their injustice according to the assessment of Time."**
- **Diogenes Laertius** *Lives* 2.1–2 — biographical entry.

His **fragment B1** is one of the most celebrated and disputed pieces of pre-Socratic text — possibly the earliest surviving verbatim Greek philosophical prose.

## Works
- ***On Nature*** (Περὶ Φύσεως, lost) — c. -550. Reconstructed from doxographical testimony. Treats cosmology, cosmogony, meteorology, biology, and geography. The first known Greek prose philosophical work.
- ***Map of the World*** (lost) — circular ecumene; the inaugural Greek cartographic project.
- ***Globe / Celestial Sphere*** (lost) — the first known Greek celestial model.

## Role in the tradition
Anaximander's foundational moves:

1. **The *apeiron*** (τὸ ἄπειρον, "the boundless / indefinite"). Anaximander rejects Thales's water-as-*archê* in favor of a more abstract principle: the **boundless / indefinite / infinite** as the source from which all definite things emerge and into which they return. This is a major philosophical step toward abstraction: the *archê* is not any specific stuff but **the substrate prior to definite stuff**. The *apeiron* anticipates Aristotelian **prime matter**, Plotinian **the One above being**, and Christian-Platonist **God beyond categories** (Pseudo-Dionysius's apophatic theology). The genealogical line from Anaximander's *apeiron* through Plato through Plotinus through Pseudo-Dionysius into Christian negative theology is one of the cleanest threads in Western philosophical-religious thought.

2. **Cosmic justice / *dikē* in nature.** Fragment B1's claim that things "pay penalty and retribution to each other for their injustice according to the assessment of Time" projects the **moral-juridical category of *dikē*** onto cosmic process. This is one of the earliest articulations of a **natural-law / cosmic-order theology** — the cosmos as itself a moral structure. Heraclitus, Stoicism, and later natural-theology traditions all build on this foundation.

3. **Free-floating earth.** Anaximander's claim that the earth floats freely at the cosmic center, balanced by symmetry rather than supported by anything, is a **major step in physical cosmology** — the move from "earth supported by something" (Egyptian Geb, Indian world-tortoise, Mesopotamian floating-on-Apsu, Atlas) to "earth balanced by cosmic symmetry." Karl Popper called this "the most courageous and revolutionary thought in the whole history of human thinking."

4. **Egyptian transmission.** Hahn (2001) argues Anaximander's astronomical-cosmological measurements drew on Egyptian architectural-engineering practice (the proportional measurements of pyramid construction, applied to celestial cosmology). The Greek-Egyptian transmission edge is more visible in the geometrical-architectural domain than in the mythological-theological domain proper to Plutarch and Herodotus.

## Disputes
- **Authenticity of B1.** Whether the famous fragment is genuinely Anaximander's words or a Theophrastean paraphrase later transmitted as quotation is debated. The standard view (Kahn 1960) accepts substantial Anaximandrean wording with possible Theophrastean reformulation.
- **The *apeiron*.** What did Anaximander mean by "boundless"? Spatially infinite? Temporally infinite? Indefinite as to its specific qualities? Probably all three, with the **indefinite-as-to-quality** reading dominant in modern scholarship.
- **Evolutionary-biology suggestion.** Anaximander's claim that humans descended from fish-like creatures in primordial water is genuinely his (multiple ancient sources confirm); whether this constitutes "proto-Darwinism" is a question of how seriously to read the analogy with modern evolutionary biology. Modern scholars are cautious — the *form* of explanation is suggestive, the *mechanism* (random variation + natural selection) is wholly absent.
