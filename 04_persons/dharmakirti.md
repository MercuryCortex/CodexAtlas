# Dharmakīrti

## Identity
7th-century (c. 600–660 CE) South Indian Buddhist philosopher, the great systematizer of the Buddhist *pramāṇavāda* — the "way of the means of valid cognition," the logic-and-epistemology school founded by [[philosophy-pramana-buddhist-epistemology|Dignāga]]. Where Dignāga laid the foundations in the *Pramāṇasamuccaya*, Dharmakīrti built the finished edifice: his verse *Pramāṇavārttika* is nominally a commentary on Dignāga's root text but so far outgrew it that the commentary became the classic and the root survives largely through it. He is, with the Naiyāyikas his opponents, the central figure of the classical age of Indian epistemology, and one of the most formidable Buddhist opponents of Nyāya realism.

Tradition makes him a Brahmin of the far South who converted, went to Nālandā, and studied logic under **Īśvarasena**, a direct pupil of Dignāga — only to conclude that Īśvarasena had misunderstood the master, and to reconstruct the whole system himself. A famous hagiographic topos has his contemporaries fail to appreciate the *Pramāṇavārttika*, so that (in the legend) the manuscript was tied to a dog's tail and dragged through the dust; Dharmakīrti is said to have predicted that his work, unrecognised in his lifetime, would in time flood the world — as, through the commentators and through Tibet, it did.

## Works — the seven treatises (*sapta-grantha*)
The corpus reconstructed by Frauwallner (1961) comprises seven works:

- ***Pramāṇavārttika*** ("Commentary on Valid Cognition") — the magnum opus, ~2,000 verses in four chapters (inference-for-oneself *svārthānumāna*; the *pramāṇasiddhi* chapter establishing the Buddha as the "means of knowledge" embodied; perception *pratyakṣa*; inference-for-others *parārthānumāna*). Dharmakīrti wrote his own auto-commentary (*svavṛtti*) only on the first chapter; the rest drew the great later commentaries.
- ***Pramāṇaviniścaya*** ("Ascertainment of Valid Cognition") — his own later, tighter restatement, mixing verse and prose.
- ***Nyāyabindu*** ("Drop of Reasoning") — a compact three-chapter primer, the school's standard textbook, glossed in the influential *Nyāyabinduṭīkā* of Dharmottara.
- ***Hetubindu*** ("Drop of Reasons"), ***Sambandhaparīkṣā*** ("Analysis of Relations"), ***Vādanyāya*** ("The Logic of Debate"), and ***Santānāntarasiddhi*** ("Proof of Other Minds") — the four shorter treatises, on the logical reason, on the unreality of relation between momentary particulars, on the conduct of formal debate, and on the inference to the existence of other mental continua.

## What he added to Dignāga
Dharmakīrti inherits Dignāga's framework — exactly **two** means of knowledge (perception *pratyakṣa* and inference *anumāna*), the **bare momentary particular** (*svalakṣaṇa*) as the sole ultimately real object, meaning as **exclusion** (*apoha*), and the **self-awareness** of cognition (*svasaṃvedana*) — and gives each a deeper metaphysical grounding:

- **The causal criterion of the real (*arthakriyā*).** For Dharmakīrti what makes the svalakṣaṇa ultimately real is that it is *causally efficacious* (*arthakriyā-samartha*) — able to do work, produce effects. This same efficacy is the test of a cognition's validity: a *pramāṇa* is a cognition that is *non-belying* (*avisaṃvāda*), that does not let you down when you act on it. Truth is cashed out as reliable causal purchase, an extrinsic (efficacy-tested) account of validity set against the Mīmāṃsā doctrine of *intrinsic* validity (*svataḥ-prāmāṇya*).
- **Everything real is momentary (*kṣaṇikatva*).** His celebrated **inference of momentariness from mere existence** (*sattvānumāna*: *yat sat tat kṣaṇikam*, "whatever exists is momentary") argues that to be causally efficacious at all is to be instantaneous; the enduring thing is a construction. This makes the svalakṣaṇa the epistemological face of the [[philosophy-abhidharma|Abhidharma]] *dharma*.
- **Inference grounded in a real "natural connection" (*svabhāvapratibandha*).** Dharmakīrti's deepest contribution to logic is his account of *why* the pervasion (*vyāpti*) that licenses an inference holds necessarily rather than by accident. A valid reason must be tied to what it proves by one of exactly two real relations: **identity/essential-property** (*tādātmya* → the *svabhāva-hetu*, e.g. "it is a tree because it is an oak") or **causation** (*tadutpatti* → the *kārya-hetu*, e.g. "there is fire because there is smoke"), with **non-apprehension** (*anupalabdhi*) grounding negative inference. Grounding necessary concomitance in real causal and identity relations, rather than in merely observed constant conjunction, is his attempt to solve what Western philosophy calls the problem of induction.
- **A causal (representational) theory of perception.** On the Sautrāntika level of his analysis, the external particular *causes* the perceptual cognition, which bears the object's "form" (*sākāra*); on the higher Yogācāra level the external object drops away and only self-aware cognition remains — the "sliding scale of analysis" (Dunne 2004).

## Cross-tradition significance
Dharmakīrti's primary place in the Atlas is **membership, not parallel**: he is the classical systematizer of the [[philosophy-pramana-buddhist-epistemology|Buddhist pramāṇavāda]] school — the node he most directly completes — and the author of its canon. That relation is documented intellectual history, not a cross-tradition resemblance.

**The Nyāya debate (documented intra-Indian polemic).** His most important cross-*school* relation is the named, centuries-long controversy with [[philosophy-nyaya-vaisheshika-realism|Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika]]. This is explicit mutual refutation, not loose likeness: Dharmakīrti argues against the Naiyāyikas; Uddyotakara's *Nyāyavārttika*, then Vācaspati Miśra, Jayanta Bhaṭṭa and Udayana answer the Dignāga–Dharmakīrti position by name; later Buddhists reply again. The two divide on every axis — two pramāṇas vs four, apoha-nominalism vs real universals, efficacy-tested vs intrinsic validity.

**Svabhāvapratibandha and the problem of induction (structural convergence).** Dharmakīrti's effort to ground the necessity of the reason–consequent connection in real causal and identity relations addresses the very problem — *what makes an inference from observed cases necessary?* — that David Hume would raise a millennium later and that modern philosophy of science calls the problem of induction. Steinkellner, Dunne and others draw the comparison, and it is illuminating precisely because Dharmakīrti's route (a metaphysically real *svabhāvapratibandha*) is one Hume rejects. This is **typological convergence**, not transmission: two independent traditions colliding with the same difficulty.

**Svasaṃvedana and self-awareness (convergence).** His defence of the intrinsic self-awareness of cognition runs parallel to Brentano's "inner perception" and to modern self-representational and higher-order theories of consciousness (Coseru 2012), again a structural parallel with no historical contact.

**Transmission to Tibet (documented descent).** In sharp contrast to those convergences, the passage of Dharmakīrti's entire system into Tibetan *tshad ma* — translated by rNgog Blo-ldan-shes-rab, systematised by Sa-skya Paṇḍita in the *Tshad ma rigs pa'i gter* — is documented, unbroken transmission, and it is in the Tibetan monastic curriculum that his logic remains a living discipline to this day.

## Disputes
- **His dates.** Frauwallner's c. 600–660 is the standard peg, but the chronology is genuinely contested; arguments from his relation to Kumārila Bhaṭṭa and Bhāviveka have been used to push him earlier into the mid-6th century, and the order of the Kumārila–Dharmakīrti exchange is unsettled.
- **Realist or idealist?** Whether Dharmakīrti is finally an external realist (the Sautrāntika reading — real, if imperceptible, external particulars) or an idealist (the Yogācāra reading — only self-aware cognition) is the central interpretive question. Dunne's "sliding scale of analysis" (2004) reads him as arguing on both levels; Dreyfus (1997) shows the Tibetan Sa-skya and Ge-luk schools splitting over exactly this.
- **The corpus and the order of the *Pramāṇavārttika*.** The reconstruction of the seven treatises, the authenticity of the ascription of the *svavṛtti*, and above all the internal order of the four chapters of the *Pramāṇavārttika* (which chapter is primary) rest on Frauwallner's philological argument and are still debated.
