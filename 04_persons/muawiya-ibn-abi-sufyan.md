# Muʿāwiya ibn Abī Sufyān

## Identity
**Muʿāwiya ibn Abī Sufyān ibn Ḥarb al-Umawī (~602–680 CE)** is the **founder of the Umayyad caliphate** (r. 661-680), the first major hereditary Arab-Muslim dynastic ruler, and one of the most consequential — and most contested — figures of early Islamic history. Born into the **Banu Umayya** clan of the Meccan Quraysh — the principal pre-conquest Meccan-aristocratic opposition to Muhammad — Muʿāwiya **converted to Islam at the conquest of Mecca in 630** alongside his father Abū Sufyān (per the canonical Sira-tradition conversion-narrative). He served as **governor of Syria from c. 640 to 661** under the Rashidun caliphs ʿUmar and [[uthman|ʿUthmān]] — a 20-year gubernatorial tenure that allowed him to build a uniquely strong personal-and-tribal power base in the Syrian-Arab military district. After ʿUthmān's assassination in 656, Muʿāwiya **led the anti-ʿAlī faction** in the [[event-first-fitna-656-661|First Fitna]] (656-661) — the foundational civil war — fighting [[ali-ibn-abi-talib|ʿAlī]] at the **Battle of Ṣiffīn (657)** and forcing the controversial *taḥkīm* (arbitration) that fractured ʿAlī's coalition and gave rise to the Khārijite secession. After ʿAlī's assassination in 661, Muʿāwiya assumed the caliphate from Damascus, founding the Umayyad dynasty and **transforming the Islamic political order from the Medinan-consultative Rashidun pattern to the Damascus-based Arab-imperial-monarchical pattern** that would dominate for the next 90 years (Umayyad caliphate 661-750). His **designation of his son [[husayn-ibn-ali|Yazīd I]] as successor c. 676** broke with the Rashidun consultative-selection precedent and led directly to the foundational Shīʿī catastrophe at [[event-karbala-680|Karbala (680)]], shortly after his death. (Keaney 2021; Kennedy 2007; Donner 2010.)

## Historical evidence
Documented extensively. Sunni Sira and historical literature (Ṭabarī, Balādhurī, Ibn Saʿd) treats Muʿāwiya comprehensively. Shīʿī polemical-historical literature is independent and substantial. **Non-Muslim contemporary attestation is unusually rich for the early Islamic period**: Byzantine Greek sources (Theophanes, Nikephoros), Syriac chronicles (the Syriac fragment of John bar Penkaye; Maronite Chronicle), and the early Sebēos Armenian chronicle (~660s) all reference Muʿāwiya by name and treat the post-Yarmūk Syrian-Arab political order he organized. **Numismatic and inscriptional evidence**: pre-Marwānid Umayyad coinage names Muʿāwiya; **the Greek inscription on the Hammat Gader bath-house** in Syria (dated 663) names Muʿāwiya as Arab ruler in a Christian-Greek context — one of the earliest non-Muslim inscriptional attestations of an Umayyad caliph.

## Works
- **No written works** in his own hand. Muʿāwiya is foundational for the **institutional shape of the Umayyad caliphate** rather than for textual production. His political-diplomatic style — *al-ḥilm* ("forbearance / political patience") — is preserved in subsequent Sunni political-literary tradition as a model of caliphal statecraft.

## Role in the tradition
- **Founder of the Umayyad caliphate.** The 661 succession (after ʿAlī's assassination) inaugurates the Umayyad period (661-750). Muʿāwiya's Damascus-based caliphate **establishes the institutional pattern** that subsequent Umayyad caliphs (Yazīd I, Muʿāwiya II, Marwān I, ʿAbd al-Malik, al-Walīd I, etc.) elaborate: Damascus as capital; Arab-tribal military aristocracy as ruling class; the *jund*-system of Arab garrison-districts in conquered territory; Arabic-language administration replacing Greek and Persian by ʿAbd al-Malik's reign; the Umayyad-monumental Islamic architecture (Dome of the Rock 691-692; Great Mosque of Damascus c. 705-715 under al-Walīd I).
- **Battle of Ṣiffīn and the *taḥkīm*, 657.** The principal military engagement of the First Fitna. After days of inconclusive battle, Muʿāwiya's army raised Qurans on lances calling for arbitration; ʿAlī accepted arbitration; the resulting *taḥkīm* arbitration deadlocked but its very acceptance by ʿAlī **split ʿAlī's coalition** — the Khārijite secession (the *Khawārij* "Goers-Out" who left ʿAlī's army declaring "no arbitration except God's") was the immediate consequence. The Khārijite faction assassinated ʿAlī in 661; the Umayyad path to caliphate was opened. **The Ṣiffīn / *taḥkīm* sequence is foundational for the formation of Sunni / Shīʿī / Khārijī (Ibādī) sectarian identity**.
- **Designation of Yazīd I as successor, c. 676.** Muʿāwiya's selection of his son Yazīd as designated successor (*walī al-ʿahd*) is the **first major break with the Rashidun consultative-selection precedent**. Sunni tradition treats this as understandable political pragmatism (the alternative being a new civil war on Muʿāwiya's death); Shīʿī tradition treats it as the foundational illegitimacy of the Umayyad regime, leading directly to Yazīd's killing of al-Ḥusayn at Karbala in 680 ([[event-karbala-680]]). The Yazīd-designation is one of the load-bearing events in Sunni-Shīʿī divergence.
- **Sunni-tradition ambivalence.** Mainstream Sunni tradition treats Muʿāwiya as a **sincere Companion of Muhammad** (he was secretary [*kātib al-waḥy*] to Muhammad recording revelations in the Medinan period) and **legitimate caliph**, but classical Sunni scholarship is willing to acknowledge moral-political criticisms (Muʿāwiya's opportunism in the First Fitna; the Yazīd-designation; the broader Umayyad-aristocratic re-Arabization that some Sunni traditionalists treat as a departure from the Rashidun-egalitarian ideal). The Sunni position on Muʿāwiya is therefore **respectful-but-critical**, while the Shīʿī position is **relentlessly condemnatory**.
- **Architect of the Mediterranean Arab-Muslim power.** Muʿāwiya organized the first Arab fleet (the *jund* of Egypt and Syria) and conducted the first Arab naval engagements: capture of Cyprus 649; raid on Rhodes; the Battle of the Masts (655) defeating a Byzantine fleet. The first Arab siege of Constantinople (674-678, partly under Muʿāwiya's reign) was repulsed by Byzantine Greek fire, but the **Mediterranean naval pattern** that Muʿāwiya inaugurated is the foundation for later Umayyad Mediterranean-power and the eventual 711 conquest of Iberia.

## Disputes
- **Sunni vs. Shīʿī assessment.** Sunni: respected-but-critical. Shīʿī: condemned. Modern critical scholarship: treats Muʿāwiya as a major political-organizational figure with both organizational achievements and serious moral-political costs (the foundational sectarian violence of the First Fitna).
- **The Yazīd-designation.** Mainstream Sunni: pragmatic necessity. Shīʿī: foundational illegitimacy. Modern scholarship: probably necessary for political stability but with deep long-term costs.
- **Historicity of the *kātib al-waḥy* role.** Muʿāwiya's role as one of Muhammad's secretaries recording revelations in the Medinan period is well-attested in Sunni hadith; some Shīʿī polemical traditions question or minimize.
- **Sufyānid vs. Marwānid Umayyad.** Muʿāwiya's Sufyānid Umayyad branch is briefly succeeded by Yazīd I and Muʿāwiya II; after the Second Fitna (680-692) the Marwānid Umayyad branch (descending from Marwān I) takes over. The dynastic split is significant for subsequent Umayyad history.
