# Lipit-Ishtar

## Identity
Lipit-Ishtar (r. ~-1934 to -1924 BCE) was the fifth king of the First Dynasty of Isin in the Isin-Larsa period — the period of competing southern Mesopotamian dynasties that followed the collapse of the Third Dynasty of Ur (Ur III) and preceded the rise of Hammurabi's Babylon. His 11-year reign is principally remembered for the **Code of Lipit-Ishtar** — a Sumerian-language law collection, promulgated ~-1930 BCE, which is the **second-oldest preserved law collection in human history** (after the Code of [[ur-nammu]] by ~150 years; ~150 years *before* the Code of [[hammurabi]]). The Code survives in Old Babylonian school-tablet copies, most extensively in the prologue and epilogue and ~38 of its laws. Lipit-Ishtar is also the subject of a substantial royal-hymn cycle (the *Lipit-Eshtar Hymns*) celebrating him as scribal patron and just ruler, in a style consciously continuous with the Ur-Nammu and Shulgi traditions.

## Historical evidence
Solid:
1. **The Code of Lipit-Ishtar** — Old Babylonian school copies from Nippur (the principal manuscript, published by Steele 1948).
2. **Royal hymns** in his name (the Lipit-Eshtar cycle).
3. **Royal inscriptions** on bricks and votive objects (Frayne RIME 4).
4. **Year-names** of his 11 regnal years.
5. **Edubba** (scribal school) exercise compositions naming him, transmitted as standard curriculum through the OB and beyond.

## Works
- **The Code of Lipit-Ishtar** (~-1930): prologue + ~38 surviving laws + epilogue, in Sumerian. The prologue establishes him as divinely commissioned to "establish justice in the land," echoing Ur-Nammu's prologue and preceding Hammurabi's. The laws cover marriage, family, property, slavery, oxen, boats. The epilogue invokes curses on anyone who alters the law text.
- **Lipit-Eshtar royal hymns** (ETCSL 2.5.5.01–05) — a cycle of Sumerian royal hymns.
- **Edubba'a exercise dialogues** — the "Examination Text" and "School Days" compositions, used as standard scribal curriculum into the late OB period, often invoke Lipit-Ishtar.

## Role in the tradition
Lipit-Ishtar belongs to the chain of Mesopotamian lawgiver-kings (Ur-Nammu → Lipit-Ishtar → the Laws of Eshnunna → Hammurabi) whose codes establish the law-text genre. The continuity is striking: the Sumerian prologue formulae of "establishing justice in the land" recur with minimal change through Hammurabi's Akkadian Code. The comparative study of these four collections is foundational for the history of ancient law. Lipit-Ishtar's hymns and his Code remained scribal-curriculum standards for centuries; he is one of the most-copied Mesopotamian royal figures in the OB scribal tradition.

## Disputes
- **Date**. ~-1934 to -1924 per the Middle Chronology; Low Chronology shifts later.
- **Order of legal compositions**. The Code of Eshnunna's relationship to Lipit-Ishtar (earlier? later? roughly contemporary?) is debated but the consensus is that Eshnunna is slightly later.
- **Authorship of the Code**. As with Ur-Nammu's and Hammurabi's, whether Lipit-Ishtar personally composed or commissioned the Code is unsettled — but the royal-authorship convention is treated as constitutionally meaningful.
