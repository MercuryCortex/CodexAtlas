# Jaan Puhvel

## Identity

**Jaan Puhvel** (1932–2026; b. Tallinn, Estonia, d. 25 April 2026) was an Estonian-American comparative mythologist, Indo-Europeanist, and Hittitologist, professor (from 2003 emeritus) of classics and Indo-European studies at the University of California, Los Angeles. He left Estonia as a wartime refugee in 1944 and was educated in North America (McGill; doctoral training at Harvard) before a career spent at UCLA. Two reference pillars carry his name: the multi-volume ***Hittite Etymological Dictionary*** (Mouton de Gruyter, 1984– ), a cornerstone reference of Anatolian philology (alongside Tischler's Glossar and Kloekhorst 2008), and ***Comparative Mythology*** (Johns Hopkins University Press, 1987; see refs). He was one of the two most prominent American comparative Indo-Europeanists of his generation, alongside Calvert Watkins — and, with C. Scott Littleton, among the principal bridges by which [[georges-dumezil|Georges Dumézil]]'s trifunctional comparatism reached Anglophone scholarship in disciplined, philologically hedged form.

## In the discipline

Puhvel's early work was technical Indo-European linguistics (*Laryngeals and the Indo-European Verb*, 1960). At UCLA he helped build the American institutional home of Indo-European studies — the campus that also housed the archaeologist [[marija-gimbutas|Marija Gimbutas]], whose steppe/Kurgan homeland hypothesis frames the vault's [[tradition-proto-indo-european-religion]] hub. The UCLA comparative-mythology conferences of the 1960s produced the volume Puhvel edited, ***Myth and Law Among the Indo-Europeans*** (University of California Press, 1970), which together with Littleton's *The New Comparative Mythology* (1966; 3rd ed. 1982) made Los Angeles the chief American beachhead of Dumézilian studies, as Littleton (1982) documents.

***Comparative Mythology*** (1987) is the mature synthesis: tradition-by-tradition surveys (Vedic and epic India, ancient and epic Iran, Greece, Rome, Celtic, Germanic, Baltic and Slavic) followed by thematic chapters reconstructing shared Indo-European structures — the twin cosmogony, the horse-and-sovereignty ritual complex, the war-of-the-functions, the thunderer and his adversary. Its character is best described as **sober Dumézilian**: it presents the trifunctional frame sympathetically but keeps the weight on linguistic cognates and named texts, and is candid about where reconstruction shades into conjecture.

The ***Hittite Etymological Dictionary*** (1984– , more than ten volumes across four decades) is the other half of the life's work: Anatolian evidence is the oldest attested Indo-European material, and Puhvel's dictionary is part of the philological infrastructure under nodes like [[tarhunna]] and [[illuyanka]] — the control-evidence against which any claim about "original" Indo-European religion must be checked.

## Cross-tradition significance

Puhvel is a **Phase-7 transmitter node**: not an ancient endpoint but a modern one — where the deep Indo-European comparisons land in the English-language academy. The vault already leans on *Comparative Mythology* as a Tier-1 source on several live edges, and this node is the endpoint those citations point at:

- **The twin cosmogony.** arguably Puhvel's best-known single contribution ("Remus et Frater," 1975; the twin-cosmogony chapter of 1987): the Proto-Indo-European first-man pair *Manu- ("Man") and *Yemo- ("Twin"), reflected in Vedic [[manu-vedic|Manu]] and [[yama-vedic|Yama]], Avestan [[yima-jamshid|Yima]], Germanic [[ymir|Ymir]] — and, on Puhvel's argument, Roman [[romulus|Romulus]] and [[remus|Remus]], with Remus etymologically continuing *Yemo-. This is a **linguistic-cognate claim**, stronger in kind than a structural parallel, though contested (see Disputes). It is distinct from the [[divine-twins]] horsemen complex ([[the-dioskouroi]], [[the-ashvins]]), which Puhvel also treats as inherited.
- **The thunderer and his adversary.** The Baltic-Slavic chapters transmit and consolidate the Ivanov-Toporov reconstruction: [[perkunas|Perkūnas]]/[[perun|Perun]] (with [[thor]] and [[indra]] as sister-reflexes of [[perkwunos|*Perkʷunos]]) against the chthonic cattle-thief [[velinas|Velnias]]/[[veles|Veles]] — what Ivanov and Toporov argued to be the most completely preserved Indo-European mythic narrative, feeding the vault's [[chaoskampf]] and [[apocalyptic-thunderer-vs-serpent]] wiring. Typological (non-IE) extensions of the storm-god cluster, as on [[raijin]], cite Puhvel for the comparative frame while remaining parallel-form, not transmission.
- **Horse and sovereignty.** The comparison of the Vedic aśvamedha, the Roman October Equus, and the medieval Irish inauguration rite reported by Giraldus Cambrensis as reflexes of one Indo-European horse-sacrifice-and-kingship complex — argued in *Myth and Law* (1970) and *Comparative Mythology* (1987).
- **Method.** Within [[comparative-religion-method]], Puhvel represents the philological wing: comparison anchored first in secure cognates (Remus~Yama-type etymological claims, the *Perkʷunos reflex-set) and only then in structural parallels (the three functions). This is the discipline's internal counterweight to phenomenological comparatism of the [[mircea-eliade|Eliade]] type — and it is the same cognate-vs-parallel discipline this vault enforces on its own edges. The trifunctional frame itself is elaborated at [[dumezil-trifunctional]].

Transmission-vs-convergence, stated honestly: Puhvel's synthesis concerns **sister traditions descended from a common source** — among the strongest kinds of cross-tradition claim, because it rests on genetic linguistic relationship rather than resemblance. But the individual reconstructions vary in security: the *Perkʷunos reflex-set and the Velnias~Veles cognate are linguistically secure; the Remus etymology is a serious but contested proposal; the full trifunctional architecture is a structural hypothesis whose elasticity has been under attack since Brough (1959).

## Disputes

- **How much of the Dumézilian frame survives?** Brough (1959) famously "found" the three functions in the Hebrew Bible, arguing the schema is elastic enough to fit non-Indo-European data; Jan Gonda pressed similar doubts from Vedic philology. *Comparative Mythology* inherits these objections to the degree it is Dumézilian; later syntheses (West 2007; Watkins 1995) deliberately rebuild the field on poetics and phraseology rather than the three functions. Puhvel's own hedged presentation is why the book has outlived stronger versions of the theory.
- **The politics of the discipline's history.** Momigliano and Ginzburg raised Dumézil's 1930s right-wing associations; Lincoln (*Theorizing Myth*, 1999) and Arvidsson (*Aryan Idols*, 2006) broadened the critique to the ideological history of "Indo-European mythology" as a scholarly formation. This dispute attaches to the school Puhvel transmits, not to his person — his own biography is that of a refugee from the Soviet occupation of Estonia — but any node that leans on the trifunctional frame inherits the obligation to know this literature.
- **The Remus thesis.** Wiseman (*Remus: A Roman Myth*, 1995) rejects the *Yemo- reading, arguing Remus is a comparatively late, inner-Roman political invention (4th century BCE), not an inherited cosmogonic twin. The vault's [[remus]] edge carries Puhvel's reading as a T1 scholarly parallel; Wiseman is the named dissent.
