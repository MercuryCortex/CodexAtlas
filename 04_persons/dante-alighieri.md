# Dante Alighieri

Dante Alighieri (1265–1321) was a Florentine poet, philosopher, and political figure whose *Divina Commedia* stands as the foundational monument of Italian literature and the most ambitious literary synthesis of medieval Christian thought. Born in Florence to a minor noble family, Dante received a thorough education in scholastic philosophy and the vernacular poetic tradition (*dolce stil novo*). He held public office in Florence but fell victim to the factional conflicts between the Black and White Guelphs: in 1302 he was exiled from his native city on trumped-up charges of corruption and never returned. The *Commedia* was composed entirely in exile, between approximately 1308 and 1321, and was dedicated to Can Grande della Scala, the lord of Verona who offered Dante refuge. He died in Ravenna the year the poem was completed.

The *Commedia* — later called *Divina* by Boccaccio — is structured as a journey through the three realms of the Christian afterlife: *Inferno* (9 concentric circles of hell, from the vestibule of the uncommitted to the frozen pit of Satan), *Purgatorio* (7 terraces of the mountain of purgation, topped by the Earthly Paradise), and *Paradiso* (9 celestial spheres, from the Moon through the Empyrean where God dwells). The Roman poet Virgil serves as Dante's guide through hell and purgatory — reason conducting the soul as far as it can go — while Beatrice, Dante's idealized beloved, guides him through the heavenly spheres, representing divine revelation. The architecture synthesizes Thomistic scholasticism, Augustinian interiority, Boethian fortune, Neoplatonic emanation, and the Aristotelian cosmological scheme of the spheres.

The question of the *Commedia*'s non-Christian sources is among the most consequential debates in medieval literary scholarship. Miguel Asín Palacios in 1919 proposed that Dante drew directly on Islamic eschatological literature — particularly the elaborated *Mi'rāj* narratives describing Muhammad's Night Journey and Ascent through the Seven Heavens. Asín Palacios documented structural parallels too systematic to be coincidental: the seven-heaven ascent with a guide, graduated punishment matched to sin categories, a narrow bridge over hell (the Sirat/Chinvat bridge), and the final beatific vision. The debate intensified when Enrico Cerulli in 1949 published a critical edition of the *Liber Scalae Machometi*, a Latin translation of a Spanish/Arabic *Mi'rāj* text produced in 1264 for Alfonso X of Castile. Cerulli demonstrated that this text — documenting Muhammad's ascent with precisely the structural features Asín Palacios identified — was in circulation in Dante's Italy and that Brunetto Latini, Dante's own teacher, had worked in Alfonso's court and had direct access to such material. The transmission chain is thus not merely suggestive but demonstrably possible: the *Mi'rāj* literature itself descends structurally from the Iranian afterlife journey tradition, especially the [[phase-4-086-arda-viraf-namag]] (Ardā Wirāz Nāmag), where a righteous man guided by divine figures traverses graduated heavens and a hell of sin-matched punishments. The *Commedia* is not a plagiarism of these sources but stands as the heir to a continuous cross-tradition imagination of the afterlife that runs from Zoroastrian Iran through Islamic expansion to the Christian high Middle Ages.

Dante's influence on Western literature is total. The afterlife geography his poem established — hell as a place of categorized moral punishment, purgatory as a place of gradual purification, heaven as a Neoplatonic ascent to the divine — entered European consciousness so completely that later readers often mistake it for official Christian doctrine. Every subsequent Western engagement with the afterlife (Milton's *Paradise Lost*, Blake's illustrations, Rodin's *Gates of Hell*, T.S. Eliot's *Waste Land*) is shaped by the *Commedia*.
