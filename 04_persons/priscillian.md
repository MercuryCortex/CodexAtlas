# Priscillian

## Identity
Wealthy Hispano-Roman aristocrat who from around 370 became the head of a Christian ascetic movement in northwestern Spain and southwestern Gaul. Ordained bishop of Avila around 380. His followers — including women on equal footing with men, an ascetic discipline including reading non-canonical Christian and possibly esoteric texts (the *Memoria Apostolorum*, certain apocryphal Acts), and a pneumatic / charismatic style of prophecy and biblical interpretation — drew opposition from neighboring bishops. Accused (probably falsely) of Manichaeism, of using apocryphal scriptures, of Gnostic dualism, and of magical practices. After complex appeals to Damasus at Rome, to Ambrose at Milan, and to imperial authorities, he was tried before the secular usurper Emperor Magnus Maximus at Trier and **executed by the sword in 385** — making him **the first Christian executed for heresy by Christian state authority**. Ambrose and Martin of Tours both objected to the use of secular execution for a doctrinal matter.

## Historical evidence
- **Sulpicius Severus**, *Chronicle* II.46–51 (~403) — primary narrative source; ambivalent but mostly hostile.
- **The Würzburg manuscript** (M.p.th.q.3, 5th–6th c.) discovered by Georg Schepss in 1885, containing eleven *Tractates* (probably Priscillian's own; some scholars attribute them to his disciple Instantius).
- **Canons on the Pauline Epistles** — Priscillian's own arrangement of Pauline material into a thematic canon.
- Council acts (Saragossa 380, Bordeaux 384) preserve some accusations against him.

## Works
- ***Eleven Tractates*** — pastoral / ascetic / doctrinal writings. Contents include defenses against charges of Manichaeism; ascetic instruction; biblical exegesis. Do not show overt Gnostic or Manichaean teaching, though they invoke apocrypha and emphasize ascetic dualism more sharply than mainstream Latin Christianity of the time.
- ***Canons on the Pauline Epistles*** — thematic-systematic compilation of Pauline texts.

## Role in the tradition
Priscillian's movement survived his execution; *Priscillianism* persisted in Galicia for two centuries, condemned at multiple regional councils (Toledo 400; Braga 561). The medieval Galician shrine at Santiago de Compostela, founded around 813, has been argued (Chadwick 1976; Sánchez Albornoz) to be **on the site of Priscillian's tomb** — i.e., the cult of "St James the Apostle" that becomes one of the great pilgrimage destinations of medieval Christendom was built on (and effectively replaced / co-opted) the cult of an executed "heretic." This is a startling, contested, but seriously argued instance of Christian site / cult substrate-Christianization within Christianity.

## Connections — substrate and influence
- Priscillian's ascetic movement, on the evidence of the *Tractates*, draws on:
  - Early Christian apocrypha (the *Acts of Andrew*, possibly the *Memoria Apostolorum*).
  - Strong **dualist asceticism** of body / spirit — the *Tractates* defend against the Manichaean accusation but display an ascetic intensity that lay on the spectrum where the charge had purchase.
  - Possibly **astrological** and esoteric materials — the synods accuse him of teaching the influence of the stars on the body; the *Tractates* deny it but do not entirely refute the charge.
- The Manichaean accusation against him is part of the broader **late-antique pattern of catholic Christianity defining itself against Manichaean and Gnostic ascetic alternatives** (Augustine vs. the Manichaeans is the parallel case from this same generation). Whether Priscillian was actually a crypto-Manichaean (Chadwick 1976 is cautious; modern scholarship leans toward "no, but his asceticism made the charge plausible") remains the central historical question.
- **The first execution of a Christian by Christians for heresy** is itself a historical pivot — making Priscillian a clear instance of the [[executed-divine-claimant]] pattern (authority transgression → execution by the dominant power → subsequent memorial cult). [[augustine-of-hippo]]'s later cautious endorsement of state coercion against Donatists (and his uneasy reading of Priscillian's case) marks the transition to the Christian-imperial repression model that will characterize medieval Christendom. Both [[ambrose-of-milan]] and Martin of Tours objected at the time to using secular execution for doctrinal matters — a rare early Christian dissent on the [[orthodoxy-vs-heresy]] coercion question.

## Disputes
- **Was Priscillian a Manichaean / Gnostic?** Chadwick 1976 inclines to "no, an ascetic with apocryphal interests." Some Spanish scholarship has read the surviving texts as more compromised.
- **The Santiago / Priscillian tomb identification.** Chadwick 1976 and others take seriously the proposal; the Catholic / Compostelan tradition (St James the Apostle) is the orthodox alternative.
- **Authorship of the *Tractates*.** Schepss attributed them to Priscillian himself; some have argued for Instantius (a disciple) as author or co-author.
