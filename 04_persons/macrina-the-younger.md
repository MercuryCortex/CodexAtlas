# Macrina the Younger

## Identity
Eldest of the ten children of Basil the Elder and Emmelia, of the Cappadocian Christian aristocracy; older sister of Basil of Caesarea, Gregory of Nyssa, and Peter of Sebaste. Founder, with her mother, of a community of ascetic women (and, on the opposite bank of the Iris, a community of men) at the family estate of Annisa in Pontus — one of the foundational cenobitic foundations of the Greek-speaking East. After Basil's death in 379, on her own deathbed, she figures in Gregory of Nyssa's *On the Soul and Resurrection* as "the Teacher" (*hē Didaskalos*) — a female philosophical interlocutor consciously modeled on Socrates and Diotima.

## Historical evidence
We know Macrina almost entirely through Gregory of Nyssa: the *Vita Macrinae* (a saint's life) and her starring role as the dialogue-partner in *On the Soul and Resurrection*. Basil of Caesarea mentions her ascetic foundation; Gregory of Nazianzus, the third Cappadocian, was a frequent correspondent of the family.

## Works
None directly written; her teaching survives only as Gregory of Nyssa transmits it.

## Role in the tradition
Pioneering female monastic founder. As Gregory's "Teacher" she is the literary device through which the *On the Soul and Resurrection* delivers a Christian Platonist account of the soul, the resurrection, the *apokatastasis* (universal restoration), and the *epektasis* (the soul's endless stretching into God). Burrus 2000 argues she is also a deliberate Christian-feminist counter-figure to the Socratic-male philosophical tradition.

## Connections — substrate and influence
- *On the Soul and Resurrection* is explicitly a Christian *Phaedo* — Plato's death-bed dialogue on the immortality of the soul, with Socrates replaced by Macrina (and her brother Gregory as Phaedo / Cebes). The structural dependence is acknowledged and intentional (Roth 1993: 7–13).
- Diotima from Plato's *Symposium* is the other direct model — a female teacher of philosophical *eros* / mystical ascent.
- Her ascetic community is part of the broader 4th-c. monastic explosion taking impetus from Egypt ([[antony-the-great]], [[pachomius]]); Cappadocian monasticism (Basil's rule, Macrina's community) domesticates the desert mode into family-property cenobitism.

## Disputes
- **How much of "Macrina's" philosophy is really Gregory's.** Burrus 2000, Wilson-Kastner 1981 read her as a literary cipher; Silvas 2008 argues for a genuine intellectual influence from Macrina on Gregory.
