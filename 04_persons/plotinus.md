# Plotinus

Founder of Neoplatonism. Born in Lycopolis in Egypt ~205; studied under Ammonius Saccas in Alexandria (~232–243); accompanied Emperor Gordian III's failed Persian expedition (intending to study Persian and Indian philosophy); after Gordian's assassination fled to Antioch and ultimately Rome (~245), where he taught for the next quarter-century until his death in 270. His pupil Porphyry collected and organized the 54 surviving treatises into the *Enneads* and prefaced them with the *Life of Plotinus* — one of our richest ancient philosophical biographies.

Plotinus's metaphysics articulates a triadic descent — the One → Nous → Soul → cosmos — that becomes the canonical framework of late-antique Platonism and shapes Christian apophatic theology via Augustine, Pseudo-Dionysius, and the Cappadocians. His Ennead II.9 "Against the Gnostics" is a polemical engagement with what we can now identify as specific Sethian Gnostic texts ([[phase-4-057-allogenes|Allogenes]], [[phase-4-056-zostrianos|Zostrianos]] — preserved at Nag Hammadi). [[porphyry|Porphyry]]'s *Vita Plotini* 16 records the documentary detail: Plotinus had his students refute these texts directly, Porphyry himself writing a forty-book refutation of *Zostrianos* and Amelius writing against *Allogenes*. This is the cleanest documented Christian-Gnostic / Platonist-philosophical confrontation in late antiquity (Turner 2001, Burns 2014). Plotinus reports four mystical unions (henōsis) with the One during his lifetime.

## Cross-tradition position

**The One ↔ Dao ↔ Ein Sof ↔ Nirguna Brahman**
Plotinus's One is the archetype for the Western tradition's apophatic absolute. Its structure — ineffable, beyond being, source of all by overflow, goal of mystical return — is structurally identical to the Daodejing's Dao, the Kabbalistic *Ein Sof*, and Advaita's *nirguna* Brahman. Plotinus predates the relevant Kabbalistic formulations by ~1,000 years and appears to have no knowledge of the Daodejing or Upanishads, despite Porphyry's report that he tried to join Gordian's Persian expedition to reach Indian sages. The fourfold parallel is therefore primarily a case of independent structural convergence — the same logical conclusion reached from different starting points. See [[tradition-neoplatonism]].

**Plotinus → Augustine → Western Christian apophatic tradition**
Augustine's *Confessions* explicitly credits his reading of "the books of the Platonists" (almost certainly Plotinus and Porphyry in Marius Victorinus's translation) as the decisive intellectual breakthrough before his conversion. Augustine's God is Neoplatonically structured: the highest being, beyond predication, the source of all, known by interior ascent. Christian Neoplatonism in the West runs primarily through this Augustinian channel.

**Plotinus vs. the Gnostics: the vault's key polemical node**
Ennead II.9 ("Against the Gnostics") is Plotinus's sustained critique of what we can now identify (via Nag Hammadi) as Sethian Gnosticism. The specific texts targeted — *Allogenes*, *Zostrianos* — were found at Nag Hammadi, confirming Plotinus's Gnostic circle had access to these documents. Plotinus's critique: the Gnostics introduce too much cosmic negativity (the Demiurge as evil), too little logical structure, and too much theatrical mythology where clean metaphysics suffices. Porphyry reports that Plotinus had his students write systematic refutations of these texts. This is the cleanest documented Platonist-Gnostic confrontation in late antiquity — both traditions were circulating in the same Rome milieu, attending some of the same seminars.

**The henōsis accounts**
Porphyry records that during the time he knew Plotinus (about six years), Plotinus achieved union (*henōsis*) with the One four times. These brief accounts of mystical union without subject-object distinction — "the flight of the alone to the Alone" (Enn. VI.9.11) — are among the most quoted passages in comparative mysticism. They parallel the Sufi *fanāʾ* (annihilation in the divine), the Upanishadic *samādhi* (absorption in Brahman), the Daoist sage's merging with Dao, and the Christian contemplative's *unio mystica*. Whether these parallels reflect a universal structure of mystical experience or independent literary conventions is the central debate of the perennialist vs. constructivist schools in religious studies.
