# Priscilla and Maximilla

## Identity
Two Phrygian Christian women who, together with [[montanus]], founded the New Prophecy movement ~+156/+172. **Priscilla** (or Prisca — to be distinguished from [[priscilla|Pauline Priscilla]] of Rom 16) and **Maximilla** prophesied in ecstatic trance, claimed Spirit-given authority equal to or greater than the institutional bishops', and (per the New Prophecy claim) received and transmitted revelations from the Paraclete. They are co-treated in this entry because the sources treat them as a unit and discrete biographical material is sparse for each.

## Historical evidence
Hostile but plentiful. The "Anonymous" anti-Montanist source in **Eusebius HE 5.16–18** is the principal narrative source. Maximilla's death is dated ~+179 ("after Maximilla there was no more prophetess" — though this is anti-Montanist polemic; subsequent Montanist prophetesses are attested epigraphically into the 4th c.). **Tertullian** preserves a verbatim Priscilla / Prisca utterance: "Christ came to me in the form of a woman" (*De anima* 9) — important data for early-Christian female-Christ imagery.

## Works
None directly. Short prophetic fragments survive (Priscilla / Prisca: the female-Christ vision; Maximilla: "I am word and spirit and power" — *I am chased away like a wolf from the flock; I am not a wolf*).

## Role in the tradition
- **Founding female-prophetic authority figures of a major 2nd-c. movement**: rare and consequential.
- **Catalyst for the proto-orthodox decision against women's ministerial-prophetic authority**: the anti-Montanist polemic explicitly attacks women's authority on Pauline grounds (1 Cor 14:34; 1 Tim 2:12).
- **Pepuza expectation**: per multiple sources they prophesied that the New Jerusalem would descend at Pepuza; Tabbernee's archaeological work locates the probable Pepuza site (a remote valley in Phrygia).

## Disputes
- **The post-Maximilla prophetic succession.** Hostile sources claim it ended; epigraphic and patristic evidence show Montanist prophetesses through the 4th c. (Quintilla, etc.).
- **Distinguishing Priscilla / Prisca from earlier and other women of the same name**: a recurring confusion.
- **Whether the "female-Christ" Priscilla-utterance is authentic**: Tertullian preserves it sympathetically; Epiphanius hostilely. Likely authentic.
