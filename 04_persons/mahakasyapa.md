# Mahākāśyapa

## Identity
Mahākāśyapa (Pali Mahā-Kassapa) was one of the principal disciples of the Buddha, distinguished by his extreme ascetic discipline (*dhutaṅga* practices: forest-dwelling, refuse-rag robes, eating only alms-food, etc.). After the Buddha's parinirvāṇa, Mahākāśyapa took *de facto* leadership of the monastic community and convened the **First Buddhist Council at Rājagṛha** (~-400), the assembly that established the foundational recitation of the Buddha's teaching: the Vinaya recited by Upāli and the Sutta-piṭaka recited by [[ananda-disciple]]. In the much later Chan / Zen lineage-story (canonized in the *Jingde chuandeng lu* of 1004 CE, with earlier roots), Mahākāśyapa becomes the **First Indian Patriarch**: at Vulture Peak (Gṛdhrakūṭa), the Buddha silently held up a flower, and only Mahākāśyapa smiled in recognition — the Buddha said, "I have the eye of the true dharma, the marvelous mind of nirvana, the formless reality... entrusted to Mahākāśyapa," inaugurating the "special transmission outside the scriptures" lineage that runs through 28 Indian patriarchs to [[bodhidharma]] and into China.

## Historical evidence
- **Pali canonical**: the Kassapa-saṃyutta (SN 16) is a series of suttas explicitly framed around Mahākāśyapa; he appears as a senior interlocutor and authority-figure in many additional suttas. The Cullavagga 11 (Vinaya-piṭaka) preserves the narrative of his convening the First Council.
- **Multi-recensional attestation**: Mahākāśyapa appears with consistent characterization in Pali, Chinese Āgamas, Sanskrit Mūlasarvāstivāda Vinaya, and Tibetan transmissions — he is one of the most robustly multi-attested early disciples.
- **The flower-sermon transmission story**, by contrast, is **not** attested in the Indian Pali or Sanskrit canonical sources and first appears in fully developed form in Song-dynasty Chinese Chan texts (the *Liandeng huiyao* of 1183 cites a now-lost *Da-fan tian wang wen fo jue yi jing* / *Sūtra in which the Mahābrahmā King asked the Buddha to resolve his doubts*; McRae 2003 treats this text as a Chan school's apocryphal composition).
- The First Council itself is broadly historical in outline (the recitation event); the precise institutional details are partly reconstructive by the Theravāda tradition.

## Works
None. His role is institutional and biographical, not literary.

## Role in the tradition
- **In Theravāda**: Mahākāśyapa is the post-parinirvāṇa convener and chief institutional figure who ensures the canonical-recitation event of the First Council. His ascetic stringency is the model for forest-monastic (*āraññaka*) discipline. The 25th-century-old Sri Lankan *bhāṇaka* (canonical-reciter) tradition treats him as the institutional founder of canonical transmission.
- **In Chan / Zen**: Mahākāśyapa is the **First Patriarch**. The "flower-sermon" transmission narrative is the foundational charter of the Chan school's "special transmission outside the scriptures" self-understanding. McRae 2003 demonstrates that the narrative is a Song-dynasty Chan composition retroactively attached to the Buddha-Kāśyapa pair, but it became — and remains — the central iconographic and pedagogical reference for the Chan/Zen lineage-claim. The Mahākāśyapa-smile is depicted in countless Chan/Zen paintings and koan-collection prefaces.
- **In Mahāyāna more generally**: Mahākāśyapa appears as a major figure in the Lotus Sūtra (chapter 4 the parable of the prodigal son), the Sukhāvatī-vyūha, and the Vimalakīrti-nirdeśa — usually as a senior śrāvaka (early-Buddhist disciple) whose understanding is contrasted with the more expansive bodhisattva understanding (the Mahāyāna sūtras frequently use senior śrāvakas as foils to make the bodhisattva-ideal case).

## Disputes
- **The flower-sermon transmission narrative**: as noted, this is a Chan school composition, not an Indian-canonical event. The narrative's pedagogical function — establishing the wordless transmission as Chan's authoritative starting point — is uncontested; its historicity as a Buddha-event is rejected by modern scholarship.
- **Mahākāśyapa's relationship to [[ananda-disciple]]**: the Pali Vinaya records substantial friction between Mahākāśyapa and Ānanda during the First Council, including Mahākāśyapa's formal accusations against Ānanda (failure to ask the Buddha to extend his life-span; allowing women into the saṅgha; failure to ask the Buddha to clarify minor rules; etc.). Some modern scholarship (Sujato, Anālayo) reads this as historical institutional tension; others as later literary construction.
- **Death and relics**: Pali tradition records Mahākāśyapa entering a meditation-cave at Mount Kukkuṭapāda (Cock-Foot Mountain) and remaining there in suspended *nirodha-samāpatti* until the future Buddha Maitreya, when he will revive and transmit the Buddha's robe to Maitreya. This tradition (preserved also in Xuanzang's pilgrimage account) gives Mahākāśyapa a special trans-aeonic role.
