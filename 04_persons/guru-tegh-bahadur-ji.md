# Guru Tegh Bahadur Ji (1621–1675)

## Identity
Guru Tegh Bahadur Ji was the **ninth Sikh Guru** — son of the sixth Guru, Hargobind, and father of the tenth Guru, [[guru-gobind-singh]] (who created the Khalsa). His beheading in Delhi (November 11, 1675) on the orders of Mughal Emperor Aurangzeb is the defining event of Sikh martyrdom theology and triggered the militarization of the Sikh community under Guru Gobind Singh.

## Life
Born 1621 in Amritsar; son of Guru Hargobind Ji. Named "Tyaga Mal" (renouncer) at birth; received the epithet "Tegh Bahadur" (Valiant Sword) after demonstrating bravery in battle. Became the ninth Guru in 1664 following the death of Guru Har Krishan. His 11-year Guruship involved extensive travels across Punjab, Bihar, Bengal, and Assam to consolidate the Sikh community.

His compositions in the Guru Granth Sahib are characterized by their meditation on:
- **Impermanence** (*anityata*) — the transience of worldly existence
- **Fearlessness** (*nirbhau*) — freedom from fear as the foundation of righteous action
- **The witnessing self** — a detached, observational stance toward worldly events

## The interfaith martyrdom — MASSIVE WIN edge

The extraordinary feature of Guru Tegh Bahadur's execution is its **interfaith motivation**, which makes this one of the most remarkable martyrdoms in the vault:

Emperor Aurangzeb (r. 1658–1707) had implemented an aggressively Islamizing policy — reinstating the *jizya* poll tax on non-Muslims (1679), demolishing Hindu temples (including Kashi Vishwanath at Varanasi and Keshava Deva at Mathura), and ordering forced conversions in Kashmir.

The Kashmiri Brahmin Pandits — facing coercive conversion — sent a delegation to Guru Tegh Bahadur requesting his intervention. According to the tradition, his young son Gobind Rai (the future Guru Gobind Singh) told his father: "There is no greater champion than you." Guru Tegh Bahadur traveled to Delhi, met with Aurangzeb's officials, and refused to convert to Islam.

He was arrested, imprisoned, and publicly beheaded at Chandni Chowk, Delhi, on November 11, 1675. Three companions were executed with him in attempt to break his resolve: Bhai Mati Das (sawn in two), Bhai Sati Das (burned alive), Bhai Dayal Das (boiled to death). Guru Tegh Bahadur did not convert.

**The key finding**: Guru Tegh Bahadur died for the religious freedom of **another tradition** — Kashmiri Hindu Pandits — facing coercion from **a third tradition** — Mughal Islam. This is one of the few cases in all of religious history where a leader accepted death explicitly to protect the right of a different religion's practitioners to practice their own faith. The Sikh tradition calls this *Hind di Chadar* — Shield of India. Wire to [[tradition-vedic-hinduism]], [[tradition-sikhism]], [[event-death-of-guru-tegh-bahadur-1675]].

## Immediate consequences
Guru Tegh Bahadur's decapitated body was secretly cremated in Delhi by a devotee at great personal risk. His head was carried to Anandpur Sahib by another devotee (Bhai Jaita) for cremation there. His son Gobind Rai, age ~9, witnessed the events and became the tenth Guru. Gobind Singh's transformation of Sikhism into the Khalsa (1699) — the baptized military brotherhood, the Five K's, the tradition of carrying a sword — is a direct institutional response to his father's martyrdom.

The Gurudwara Sis Ganj Sahib (Chandni Chowk, Delhi) and Gurudwara Rakab Ganj Sahib (Parliament Street area) now mark the sites of beheading and cremation.

## Theological significance
Guru Tegh Bahadur's martyrdom introduced the concept of *shahidi* (martyrdom) as a foundational Sikh value — not martyrdom for Sikh beliefs specifically, but martyrdom for the principle of **religious freedom itself**. His son Guru Gobind Singh encoded this in the Khalsa: the Sikh soldier (*singha*) protects the weak and defends religious freedom across traditions, not merely Sikh interests.
