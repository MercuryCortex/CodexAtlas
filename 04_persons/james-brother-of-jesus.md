# James the Just

## Identity
James (Ya'akov), called "the Just" (*ha-Tzadik* / *Iustus*), was a brother of Jesus (initially skeptical of his mission), who after a resurrection appearance (1 Cor 15:7) became the head of the Jerusalem Christian community. He led a Torah-observant Jewish-Christian movement in Jerusalem that contrasted with Paul's Gentile-mission. He was killed in +62 by stoning at the order of the high priest Ananus II during a brief vacancy in the Roman procuratorship, occasioning a public protest and the high priest's removal.

## Historical evidence
**Among the best-attested figures of mid-1st-century Judea**: Paul personally met him (Gal 1:19) and engaged with him repeatedly; Josephus describes his death with notable sympathy and frames it as a Jewish-internal scandal (*Antiquities* 20.200) — this passage is the **undisputed Josephan attestation of Jesus's brother**, and incidentally of Jesus himself; Hegesippus (~+170, preserved by Eusebius) preserves a detailed traditional account; Acts depicts him as the decisive authority at the Jerusalem Council (Acts 15:13–21). For this vault, James matters as: (a) the *Jewish-Christian* alternative to Pauline Christianity, eventually overwhelmed by it; (b) historical anchor for Jesus's existence (his biological brother is a non-mythicizable datum); (c) the source-figure of the Jerusalem Jewish-Christian *Ebionite* and *Nazarene* movements of the 2nd–4th centuries; (d) the namesake of the Epistle of James, with its distinctively practical-wisdom, anti-Pauline-sounding (Jas 2:14–26) theology. Eisenman's (1996) controversial proposal to identify James with the Qumran Teacher of Righteousness is heterodox (Tier 4); the mainstream rejects it but James's Torah-zeal and Qumran-resonant atmosphere are recognized.
