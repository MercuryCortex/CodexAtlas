# Aeneas

## Identity

Aeneas (Latin *Aenēās*, Greek Αἰνείας *Aineías*) is the Trojan prince — son of the mortal Anchises and the goddess Venus (Greek Aphrodite) — who escapes the sack of Troy and, after long wanderings, settles in Latium, where his descendants are destined to found Rome. He is the protagonist of Virgil's *Aeneid* and the national founder-hero of Rome, defined throughout that epic by his standing epithet *pius* — **pietas**, the dutiful subordination of self to gods, father, and fate. In the Atlas he is filed as a **person** (a legendary mortal hero, like [[orpheus]]) rather than a deity: although he received a thin local Italian hero-cult as **Aeneas Indiges**, this is far slighter than the state apotheosis of [[romulus]] as Quirinus, and his significance is overwhelmingly that of an epic and ancestral figure. He is wired here for one reason above all: **Aeneid VI is the Roman entry in the cross-tradition katabasis-and-anabasis motif.**

## In the Aeneid (and the prior tradition)

Aeneas is already present in Homer: in the *Iliad* he is a secondary Trojan champion, rescued in turn by Aphrodite, Apollo, and Poseidon, the last of whom prophesies that "the might of Aeneas shall reign over the Trojans, and his sons' sons that shall be born in days to come" (Il. 20.307–308) — the seed from which the later Roman foundation-claim grew. Greek mythographers and the iconographic *Tabula Iliaca* tradition carried him westward, and by the time of Dionysius of Halicarnassus (*Roman Antiquities* 1.45–64) and Livy (*Ab Urbe Condita* 1.1–2) the legend of Aeneas in Italy was the established prehistory of Rome. Augustus's family, the gens Iulia, traced its descent through Aeneas's son **Ascanius / Iulus** to Venus herself — the dynastic claim that Virgil's epic monumentalises.

The **descent to the underworld** occupies **Book VI**, the structural and thematic centre of the poem. At Cumae Aeneas consults the **Cumaean Sibyl**, who sets the conditions for the journey: he must first find and pluck the **golden bough** (6.136–148). Pleading the precedent of earlier living descenders — Orpheus, Pollux, Theseus, and Heracles (6.119–123) — he is granted passage. Guided by the Sibyl, he crosses the **Acheron** with the ferryman Charon, passes the borderland of the unburied, the fields of mourning where he meets the shade of **Dido**, and the quarters of the war-dead, before reaching **Elysium**. There he is reunited with the shade of his father **Anchises**, who expounds a doctrine of the soul — its imprisonment in the body, its purgation after death, and its rebirth (6.724–751) — and then reveals the **parade of unborn Roman heroes** (the *Heldenschau*, 6.756–886), the future Romulus, the kings, the Republican worthies, and finally Augustus, whose age is foretold as the return of a golden age. Aeneas then returns to the upper world through the **Gate of Ivory** (6.893–898), the gate of false dreams — a famous interpretive crux. Late in the poem's afterlife-frame, **Ovid** (*Metamorphoses* 14.581–608) narrates his deification as the local god **Indiges**, his mortal part washed away by the river Numicius at Venus's petition.

## Cross-tradition significance (the Roman katabasis)

Aeneas is the **Roman member of the katabasis-and-anabasis cluster** — the trans-traditional motif of the hero or soul that descends into the underworld and returns. Virgil's Book VI stands in an explicit and acknowledged literary lineage: it is the Latin **imitation and transformation of the Homeric *Nekyia*** (Odyssey 11), and it places Aeneas alongside the Greek living-descenders Orpheus, Heracles, and Theseus, whom the text itself names as precedents. The relationship to the Greek tradition is therefore a case of **direct literary transmission and emulation**, not of independent convergence: Virgil is consciously writing a Roman *katabasis* in dialogue with Homer and with the Orphic-Pythagorean eschatology that Eduard Norden (1916) reconstructed behind Anchises' speech on the soul.

What is distinctive about the Roman handling is its **dynastic and political** turn. Where Odysseus descends to learn his way home and Orpheus to recover a wife, Aeneas descends to receive a **revelation of the Roman future** — the *Heldenschau* makes the underworld a stage for Augustan ideology, and the descent-and-return becomes the hinge between the Trojan past and the imperial present. Within the wider Mediterranean motif, Aeneas thus belongs to the **literal underworld-descent** variant (with Inanna, Persephone, Orpheus, Heracles, and Christ's *descensus*), as distinct from the soul-ascent-through-the-spheres variant of the Hermetic, Mithraic, and Hekhalot traditions. The continuity from the Homeric *Nekyia* through Virgil to **Dante** — for whom Virgil-as-character is the guide of the *Inferno*, precisely because of Aeneid VI — makes this one of the best-documented descent-lineages in the Western literary tradition.

## Disputes

Aeneas is legendary, and there is no question of his historicity; the scholarly debates concern interpretation rather than fact. (1) **The Gate of Ivory.** Aeneas exits through the ivory gate of *false dreams* (6.893–896) rather than the gate of horn through which *true* shades pass — a long-debated crux variously read as a chronological marker (the journey ends after midnight), as a comment on the fictionality of the vision, or as a subtle Virgilian irony undercutting the triumphal Augustan prophecy. (2) **The sources of Anchises' eschatology.** Norden (1916) argued for a coherent Orphic-Pythagorean (and Stoic) background to the doctrine of the soul in 6.724–751; the precise blend of Platonic, Pythagorean, Orphic, and Stoic elements remains discussed (Horsfall 2013). (3) **The "Augustan" reading.** Whether the Aeneid, and Aeneas's *pietas*, straightforwardly endorse the Augustan order or contain a darker counter-voice (the "Harvard school" pessimistic reading) is among the central debates of modern Virgilian scholarship — and bears directly on how the Book VI prophecy is to be taken.
