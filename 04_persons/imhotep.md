# Imhotep

## Identity
Imhotep (c. -2680 to -2620; Egyptian *Ii-em-Ḥtp*, "the one who comes in peace") was the **vizier, royal architect, and high priest** of the 3rd-Dynasty pharaoh Djoser (Netjerikhet, r. ~-2670 to -2640). He designed and built the **Step Pyramid at Saqqara** — the **first known monumental stone building in human history** and the prototype for all subsequent pyramid construction. Over the following millennia he was progressively **deified**: revered first as a sage and scribe (Middle Kingdom), then as a healing god (Late Period), and from the Ptolemaic period identified with the Greek [[asclepius-greek]] in the **Greco-Egyptian healing cult of Asklepios-Imhotep**. He is one of only a handful of non-royal Egyptians ever to be fully deified, and his cult is **the single most consequential individual Greek-Egyptian theological syncretism**.

## Historical evidence
- **Contemporary 3rd-Dynasty attestation**: Imhotep's titles inscribed on the base of a statue of Djoser at Saqqara — "Chancellor of the King of Lower Egypt, First after the King of Upper Egypt, Administrator of the Great Palace, Hereditary Lord, High Priest of Heliopolis, Imhotep" — the earliest documentary attestation of him during his own lifetime.
- **Step Pyramid complex at Saqqara** — built ~-2650 under Imhotep's direction; the **first monumental stone construction**, an architectural revolution that establishes the Egyptian pyramidal tradition.
- **Middle Kingdom**: Imhotep referenced as a sage in literary texts (*Instruction of Hardjedef* and others); not yet divine.
- **Late Period (664–332 BCE)**: progressive divinization; cult-temples and votive offerings to Imhotep at Saqqara and Memphis. Imhotep is one of only a few non-royal Egyptians ever deified — Amenhotep son of Hapu (18th Dynasty vizier) is the other major case.
- **Ptolemaic-Roman period**: full deification with major cult-temples at Saqqara, Karnak, Deir el-Bahari, and Philae. **The Greek-Egyptian Asklepios-Imhotep cult flourishes** with bilingual votive inscriptions, dream-incubation practice modeled on Greek Asclepieia, and a substantial literary corpus including the Greek-language Asklepios-Imhotep papyri (P. Oxy. 1381 etc.).

## Works
- **Step Pyramid of Djoser at Saqqara** (~-2650) — the first monumental stone building in history; the pyramidal complex includes the first known instance of free-standing stone columns, the first known use of fluted columns, and several other major architectural innovations.
- No surviving authored texts can be confidently attributed to Imhotep himself. Late tradition attributes various medical and architectural treatises to him; none survive in form that can be authenticated as his.

## Role in the tradition
Imhotep's role unfolds in five distinct phases — a unique trajectory in Egyptian religious history:

1. **3rd Dynasty (life)**: vizier, architect, high priest of Heliopolis. Major historical figure but a mortal one.
2. **Old Kingdom posterity**: legendary architect and sage, still mortal.
3. **Middle Kingdom**: revered as a sage of antiquity; the *Instruction of Hardjedef* lists Imhotep among the famous sages whose writings survive.
4. **Late Period (especially Saite, 26th Dynasty)**: progressive deification as healing-god and patron of scribes. Cult-temples at Saqqara and Memphis.
5. **Ptolemaic-Roman**: full divine status, identified with [[asclepius-greek]] in the Greek-language religious world. The **Asklepios-Imhotep cult is the principal Egyptian healing-cult in the Hellenistic world** and produces its own substantial literature.

For the Codex Atlas's mission this trajectory is **load-bearing**: Imhotep represents the clearest single case of **Egyptian religious tradition flowing into Greek religious tradition through cult-syncretism**. The Greco-Egyptian healing temple at Saqqara (the Asklepieion of Memphis) operated as a Greek-Egyptian dream-incubation sanctuary into the Roman period; its papyrological archive (the Asklepieion archive) preserves dream-narratives, votive inscriptions, and the *Aretalogy of Asklepios-Imhotep* (P. Oxy. 1381) — a first-person Greek-language hymn to the Egyptian god.

The Hermetic dialogue [[phase-4-012-asclepius]] (Latin translation of a Greek original, c. 2nd–3rd c. CE) features Asclepius as one of Hermes Trismegistus's interlocutors — almost certainly the Asklepios-Imhotep of Greco-Egyptian temple religion rather than purely the Greek Asclepius. The Hermetic tradition is in this sense **partly an Imhotep tradition**, reflecting the religious culture of Greco-Egyptian priestly intellectuals.

## Disputes
- **Was Imhotep historical?** Yes — the Saqqara statue-base inscription names him by his lifetime, securing his historicity beyond reasonable doubt. (For decades after the inscription's discovery in 1926, some scholars treated Imhotep as wholly legendary; this is now decisively rejected.)
- **Date of Imhotep's first divinization.** Conventionally placed in the Late Period (~-650 onward); some scholars argue for earlier (New Kingdom) divinization on indirect evidence. The Ptolemaic-Roman full divinity is uncontested.
- **Authorship of medical texts.** Late tradition attributes the *Edwin Smith Surgical Papyrus* (Old Kingdom medical knowledge) to Imhotep; this is unprovable but not implausible given his vizier-court role.
- **Imhotep-Asklepios direction of identification.** Greek-led (Greek travelers identifying Imhotep with Asclepius) or Egyptian-led (Egyptian priests presenting Imhotep to Greek inquirers as their Asclepius). Moyer (2011) substantially defends an Egyptian-led version.
