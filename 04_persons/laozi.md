# Laozi

## Identity
"Laozi" — literally "Old Master" or "Old Child" — is the name traditionally attached to the Daodejing and, by extension, treated as the founder of philosophical Daoism. Whether the name refers to a single historical individual, two or more figures conflated by tradition, or is essentially a literary persona for the composite Daodejing, is unresolved. The most influential traditional biography is Sima Qian's *Shiji* 63 (~-90 BCE), which itself records three alternative identifications and concludes uncertainly.

## Historical evidence
The Shiji biography reports: Laozi's personal name Li Er, courtesy Boyang or Dan; from the state of Chu; served as keeper of the Zhou archives; met Confucius (a famous traditional anecdote in which the elder Laozi instructs the younger Confucius); foresaw the decay of Zhou, headed west, was persuaded at the western pass by the gatekeeper Yin Xi to write down his teaching; departed and vanished. Already in Sima Qian this account is given with hedging; no contemporary inscription, document, or independent witness exists.

## Works
Traditionally: the Daodejing (Tao Te Ching). Modern scholarship treats this work as a composite school anthology stabilizing through the 4th–3rd c. BCE (Mawangdui silk mss ~-200 BCE; Guodian bamboo-slip partial version ~-300 BCE).

## Role in the tradition
Foundational sage of philosophical Daoism. In Religious Daoism (from ~Han period onward), Laozi is divinized as Taishang Laojun ("Most High Lord Lao"), one of the Three Pure Ones. The Buddhist-Daoist polemical text *Huahujing* claimed Laozi traveled west and became (or instructed) the Buddha — a polemic strategy to subordinate Buddhism to Daoism, formally banned by imperial edicts at various times.

## Disputes
- **Historicity.** Mainstream modern view: the Daodejing is a composite text; "Laozi" is at best a fictional or composite author-name; possibly there is a historical kernel (a real "Lao Dan" archivist) but he cannot be reliably linked to the received text. Some scholars (Graham 1989, Sivin) decisively reject the historical Laozi; others (Boltz, Henricks) leave the question open.
- **Date.** Traditional: contemporary of Confucius (~-550). Modern: the *text* is later (Warring States, 4th–3rd c. BCE); any historical Laozi is therefore at best the namesake or earliest contributor.
- **Relation to Confucius.** The traditional meeting is generally regarded as legendary.
