# Laozi

## Identity

**Laozi** (老子) is not a personal name. It is a title — "Old Master," or, reading *zi* literally, "Old Child" — attached in Chinese tradition to the ~5,000-character text now called the [[phase-2-014-daodejing]] and, by extension, to the origin of Daoism itself. Whether a man of that name wrote that text is one of the genuinely open questions in the history of philosophy, and it has been open since the first person tried to answer it.

That first person was the Han court historian **Sima Qian**, whose *Shiji* ("Records of the Grand Historian," ~-90) chapter 63 contains the earliest connected biography. It is worth being precise about what that chapter actually does, because it is routinely quoted as though it settled the matter. Sima Qian offers **three candidates** and declines to choose between them:

1. **Li Er** (courtesy name Dan, hence *Lao Dan* 老聃), from Quren village in Ku county, state of Chu; keeper of the archives of the Zhou court.
2. **Lao Laizi**, also of Chu, author of a work in fifteen sections, said to be a contemporary of Confucius.
3. The **Grand Historian Dan** (太史儋) of Zhou, who is recorded meeting Duke Xian of Qin some 129 years after Confucius's death.

Sima Qian then writes that no one in the world knows whether these are one man or several. He also relays that Laozi lived 160 years, or perhaps over 200, by cultivating the Dao. A biography that reports three mutually exclusive identifications and a 200-year lifespan is not a hostile witness we are choosing to doubt; it is a friendly witness telling us he does not know.

## Historical evidence

**What the *Shiji* narrative contains.** Laozi served as archivist at the Zhou court; Confucius came to him to ask about the rites and left shaken, comparing him to a dragon; seeing the Zhou decline, Laozi went west; at the frontier pass the Keeper of the Pass, **Yin Xi**, would not let him through until he wrote his teaching down; he produced a work in two parts, on *dao* and on *de*, in something over five thousand characters, and departed, and no one knows where he ended. Every element of this — the archive, the meeting with Confucius, the pass, the gatekeeper, the disappearance — is a narrative motif before it is a datum.

**What is missing.** No contemporary inscription names him. No Warring States bronze or bamboo document identifies an author of the Daodejing. The [[phase-2-015-analects-of-confucius]] — the text that would most obviously record a formative encounter between Confucius and a great elder sage — does not mention him. The earliest substantial appearances of a "Lao Dan" are in the [[phase-2-022-zhuangzi]], a book that puts words into the mouths of historical and invented figures with equal freedom, and which uses Confucius himself as a mouthpiece and a joke.

**What the manuscripts show.** The text has a physical history that is far better evidenced than its author:

- **Guodian** (Jingmen, Hubei; excavated 1993; tomb sealed c. -300). Three bundles of bamboo slips carrying roughly 2,000 characters — around two-fifths of the received text, in a quite different order, with no chapter divisions and no *Dao*/*De* two-part structure. This is the oldest witness.
- **Mawangdui** (Changsha, Hunan; excavated 1973; tomb sealed -168). Two silk manuscripts, A and B. Substantially the whole text — but with the *De* section placed **before** the *Dao* section, i.e. a *Dejing–Daojing*.
- **Peking University Han bamboo *Laozi*** (acquired 2009; Western Han). A further early witness, again with the *De* section first.
- The **received text** in 81 chapters comes down through the commentaries of **Wang Bi** (226–249) and **Heshang Gong**, both post-dating the manuscripts above by centuries.

The manuscript record therefore documents a text that was still moving — in order, in extent, and in internal arrangement — for at least three hundred years. That is the profile of a school anthology, not of a single authorial act at a mountain pass.

**The Guodian variant that matters most.** The received chapter 19 opens with a frontal attack on Confucian values: cut off sageliness, discard wisdom; cut off benevolence (*ren*), discard righteousness (*yi*). The Guodian slips carry a differently-worded opening that targets cleverness, disputation and artifice — and **does not attack *ren* and *yi***. Scholars differ on the reading of individual graphs, but the substantive point is broadly accepted: the sharpest anti-Confucian polemic in the Daodejing may be a later stratum, laid over a text that was not originally a factional weapon (Henricks 2000; Csikszentmihalyi & Ivanhoe 1999). This is one of the most consequential things excavated Chinese manuscripts have told us, and it directly weakens the traditional picture of Laozi as the elder who put Confucius in his place.

## Works

Traditionally, one book: the **Daodejing** (*Tao Te Ching*), "Classic of the Way and its Power." Modern scholarship treats it as a **composite anthology** of aphoristic and rhymed material, drawn from a shared stock of proverb, verse and political counsel, stabilizing through the 4th–3rd centuries BCE and reaching something like its received form only in the early Han. Attribution to a single author is a tradition about the text, not a finding about it.

Later Daoism attributes a large body of revealed scripture to Laozi in his divine form — most importantly the *Xiang'er* commentary of the Celestial Masters and, polemically, the *Huahujing* (below). These are attributions to **Lord Lao the deity**, not claims about a Warring States man.

## The thought

The Daodejing's argument, insofar as an anthology has one, runs roughly as follows.

**The nameless.** The book opens by disqualifying its own vocabulary: *dao ke dao fei chang dao* — the way that can be "way-ed" (spoken, made a path of) is not the constant way. [[dao]] is prior to the distinction between things and therefore prior to names, which work only by cutting. Naming produces the ten thousand things; the nameless is where they come from.

**Reversal.** The characteristic move is inversion of the obvious. The soft overcomes the hard; water, the weakest thing, wears away stone and always seeks the low place; the useful part of a wheel is the hole, of a pot the emptiness, of a room the space. The valley, the female, the infant, the **uncarved block** (*pu* 樸) are the recurring images — all of them figures for undifferentiated potential that has not yet been spent on being something in particular.

**Wu-wei.** *Wu-wei* 無為 is not inaction. It is action without coercion, without forcing, without the assertion of a self against the grain of a situation — action that does not *add* anything. Its correlate is *ziran* 自然, "self-so": letting things be as they already are of themselves. Politically this becomes the most radical anti-interventionist argument of the ancient world: the more prohibitions, the poorer the people; the more sharp weapons, the more disorder; the more laws, the more thieves. The ideal ruler is the one whose subjects say, when the work is done, "we did it ourselves."

**Against cultivated virtue.** Where the Confucians propose to repair the world through ritual (*li*), benevolence (*ren*) and deliberate self-cultivation, the received Daodejing answers that these are symptoms, not cures: when the great Dao is abandoned, then you get benevolence and righteousness. (As noted above, this specific polemic is weaker in the oldest manuscript.)

## Reception and transmission

**Warring States and Han.** The [[phase-2-026-han-feizi]] chapters *Jie Lao* and *Yu Lao* are the earliest surviving commentary, and they read the text as **statecraft** — the Legalist appropriation of Daoist quietism into a theory of the concealed, unreadable ruler. Under the early Han, "Huang-Lao" (Yellow Emperor and Laozi) named a governing doctrine of non-interference that was court orthodoxy before Emperor Wu's Confucian turn.

**Deification.** In 165 CE, at the order of Emperor Huan, Bian Shao composed the *Laozi ming* (Laozi Inscription) — the earliest clear evidence of Laozi worshipped as a god, coeternal with the cosmos and repeatedly incarnated as adviser to sage rulers. In 142 CE **Zhang Daoling** reported a revelation from **Taishang Laojun**, "the Most High Lord Lao," and founded the Way of the Celestial Masters ([[tradition-zhengyi-taoism]]) — the beginning of organized, liturgical, communal Daoism. Lord Lao becomes one of the Three Pure Ones of the later pantheon. The distance between the archivist of the *Shiji* and the god of the Celestial Masters is the single clearest case in this vault of a **philosophical figure absorbed upward into a cult** (Seidel 1969; Kohn 1998).

**The conversion-of-the-barbarians polemic.** The *Huahujing* (化胡經, "Scripture of the Conversion of the Barbarians"), attributed to Wang Fu around 300 CE, claimed that when Laozi went west he continued to India and there became — or taught — the Buddha. The move subordinates Buddhism to Daoism by making it a foreign edition of Lord Lao's teaching. It was contested for a millennium, and after the Buddhist-Daoist court debates under the Mongol Yuan the text was condemned to burning in 1281. It is the vault's textbook example of the **Polemic** edge bucket: a genealogical claim manufactured to demote a rival (Zürcher 1959).

**Literati and syncretic reception.** From the Wei-Jin period the Daodejing is one of the "Three Mysteries" of [[philosophy-xuanxue]] (Dark Learning), alongside the Zhuangzi and the [[phase-2-042-yi-jing-i-ching]]; Wang Bi's commentary makes it a metaphysics of *wu* (nothing/absence) that then conditions how Chinese readers first heard Buddhist emptiness. Internal alchemy ([[alchemy-chinese-neidan]]) and the immortality traditions ([[theme-immortality-cultivation]]) claim him as patriarch.

**To Europe.** The Jesuit mission read the Daodejing early; a Latin translation reached the Royal Society in 1788. Stanislas Julien's 1842 French version and Legge's 1891 English version opened it to the West, and it has since become one of the most-translated books in any language. **Heidegger** attempted a German translation with Paul Shih-yi Hsiao in 1946, abandoning it partway — documented by Hsiao himself, and a rare instance of a major European philosopher working directly on the text rather than on a paraphrase of it.

## Disputes

- **Did he exist?** Three positions are live. (a) **No single Laozi** — the figure is a composite constructed from the Zhuangzi's Lao Dan and the Zhou Grand Historian Dan, retrofitted to a text that needed an author (Graham 1990; broadly Kirkland). (b) **A historical kernel** — a real archivist Lao Dan existed and taught, but cannot be securely linked to the received text (Boltz; the cautious middle). (c) **Traditional** — a single sixth-century master wrote the book; held within Daoist tradition and by a small minority of scholars. The vault carries (a)/(b) as the mainstream and marks the node `historicity: disputed`.
- **When?** The traditional date makes him Confucius's elder contemporary (~-550). The textual evidence puts the *book* in the 4th–3rd centuries BCE. These cannot both be true of the same authorial event; the usual resolution is that the figure has been pushed backwards in time to give the text seniority over the Confucian classics.
- **The meeting with Confucius.** Told repeatedly in the Zhuangzi, the *Liji* and the *Shiji*, almost universally regarded now as legend — and specifically as *partisan* legend, since in every version the Daoist is the senior and Confucius the chastened student.
- **One text or two traditions?** Because Mawangdui and the Peking University manuscript both place *De* before *Dao*, the very title *Daodejing* reflects a later editorial decision. What we call chapter 1 is not necessarily where the book began.

## Cross-tradition position

### MASSIVE WIN 1 — Laozi ↔ Heraclitus: the same century, the opposite end of Eurasia

[[heraclitus]] of Ephesus (fl. ~-500) and the Laozi of tradition are near-contemporaries with no possible contact, and they make a structurally matched move. Both posit a single ordering principle — *logos* / *dao* — that (i) governs all change, (ii) is not itself one of the things it governs, (iii) is continuously participated in and continuously missed by almost everyone, and (iv) can only be approached through paradox, because ordinary predication is exactly what it escapes. Heraclitus builds his account on the strife and unity of opposites; the Daodejing builds its account on the mutual generation of opposites (being and non-being generate each other; long and short give each other form). Both write in aphorism, both are famous for obscurity, and both were read by later traditions as the fountainhead of a cosmology they did not themselves systematize.

The parallel is **Parallel-bucket**, not Transmission: there is no contact mechanism, and the honest claim is independent convergence, not borrowing. Its interest is that it is one of the sharpest data points for the [[theme-axial-age]] hypothesis — see also [[event-axial-age-800-200bce]] and [[theme-three-independent-reinventions]]. It should be stated with the historicity caveat attached: it is a parallel between two *texts*, and only derivatively between two men, one of whom may not have existed.

### MASSIVE WIN 2 — the earliest sustained apophasis

"The dao that can be spoken is not the constant dao" is, as a datable text, among the earliest sustained **apophatic** gestures in the world's literature — the claim that the ultimate is missed precisely by being named. The same structure recurs, independently, in the Upaniṣadic *neti neti* (roughly contemporary), in [[plotinus]] on the One ([[phase-4-019-plotinus-enneads]]), in [[pseudo-dionysius]] ([[phase-4-030-pseudo-dionysius]]), in [[meister-eckhart]] on the *Gottheit* ([[the-godhead-eckhart]]), and in the Kabbalistic *Ein Sof*. See [[theme-apophatic-theology]], [[doctrine-apophatic-via-negativa]], [[philosophy-apophatic-mysticism]].

Two cautions the vault should keep visible. First, the resemblance is **structural, not doctrinal**: the Dao is not a transcendent God beyond being, and reading it as one is exactly the Christian-Neoplatonic overlay the Jesuits found convenient. Second, the direction of the borrowing in the modern era runs the *other* way — Western readers came to the Daodejing already holding a negative theology, and found what they brought. [[matteo-ricci]] and the later Jesuit sinologists mined the text for a Chinese natural theology; that reading is a datum about the Jesuit mission, not about the Warring States.

### MASSIVE WIN 3 — the Confucius foil, and why the pairing is structural even though the meeting is legendary

The traditional encounter with [[confucius]] is legend. The **opposition** is not. It is the load-bearing dualism of Chinese intellectual history and it survives every change of dynasty and every arrival of a foreign religion:

- **Confucian**: order is made. Ritual (*li*), benevolence ([[moral-confucian-ren]]), study, and deliberate cultivation build a person and therefore a society. See [[philosophy-confucianism]], [[phase-2-023-mengzi]], [[phase-2-024-xunzi]].
- **Daoist**: order is found. Cultivation adds artifice; the natural order is already competent; the sage's skill is to stop interfering. See [[philosophy-daoism-philosophical]], [[zhuangzi]].

The two were never simply rivals — from the Han onward Chinese elites held both, professionally Confucian and privately Daoist, and by the Song the *sanjiao* settlement ([[tradition-sanjiao]]) made the pairing official alongside Buddhism. [[philosophy-neoconfucianism-zhu-xi]] rebuilds Confucian metaphysics using materials the Daoists (and Buddhists) had developed.

### The Buddhist encounter — a genuine Transmission edge, running both directions

Laozi's downstream involvement with Buddhism is two distinct claims and they must not be collapsed:

- **False**: that Laozi went to India and became the Buddha. This is the *Huahujing*'s polemic, made for factional advantage, condemned by successive courts, and rejected by all scholarship. Wire it as **Polemic**, not Transmission.
- **True**: that Daoist vocabulary — *wu* (non-being), *ziran*, *wu-wei*, and the Wang Bi metaphysics built on the Daodejing — supplied the conceptual clothing in which Indian Buddhist ideas were first heard in China, and that Chinese Buddhism repaid the debt by pushing Daoism to organize its own scripture, pantheon and monasticism. This is a documented, reciprocal **Transmission** edge (Zürcher 1959). See [[philosophy-chan-zen]], [[emptiness-sunyata]].
