# Laozi

## Identity
"Laozi" — literally "Old Master" or "Old Child" — is the name traditionally attached to the Daodejing and, by extension, treated as the founder of philosophical Daoism. Whether the name refers to a single historical individual, two or more figures conflated by tradition, or is essentially a literary persona for the composite Daodejing, is unresolved. The most influential traditional biography is Sima Qian's *Shiji* 63 (~-90 BCE), which itself records three alternative identifications and concludes uncertainly.

## Historical evidence
The Shiji biography reports: Laozi's personal name Li Er, courtesy Boyang or Dan; from the state of Chu; served as keeper of the Zhou archives; met Confucius (a famous traditional anecdote in which the elder Laozi instructs the younger Confucius); foresaw the decay of Zhou, headed west, was persuaded at the western pass by the gatekeeper Yin Xi to write down his teaching; departed and vanished. Already in Sima Qian this account is given with hedging; no contemporary inscription, document, or independent witness exists.

## Works
Traditionally: the Daodejing (Tao Te Ching). Modern scholarship treats this work as a composite school anthology stabilizing through the 4th–3rd c. BCE (Mawangdui silk mss ~-200 BCE; Guodian bamboo-slip partial version ~-300 BCE).

## Role in the tradition
Foundational sage of philosophical Daoism. In Religious Daoism (from ~Han period onward), Laozi is divinized as Taishang Laojun ("Most High Lord Lao"), one of the Three Pure Ones. The Buddhist-Daoist polemical text *Huahujing* claimed Laozi traveled west and became (or instructed) the Buddha — a polemic strategy to subordinate Buddhism to Daoism, formally banned by imperial edicts at various times.

## Disputes
- **Historicity.** Mainstream modern view: the Daodejing is a composite text; "Laozi" is at best a fictional or composite author-name; possibly there is a historical kernel (a real "Lao Dan" archivist) but he cannot be reliably linked to the received text. Some scholars (Graham 1989, Sivin) decisively reject the historical Laozi; others (Boltz, Henricks) leave the question open.
- **Date.** Traditional: contemporary of Confucius (~-550). Modern: the *text* is later (Warring States, 4th–3rd c. BCE); any historical Laozi is therefore at best the namesake or earliest contributor.
- **Relation to Confucius.** The traditional meeting is generally regarded as legendary.

## Cross-tradition position

**Laozi and Heraclitus — the logos/dao parallel (~-540 to -480)**
The most striking chronological coincidence in the history of philosophy. Heraclitus of Ephesus and the historical Laozi (if he existed) were roughly contemporary (~-540 to -480 BCE), on opposite ends of the Eurasian landmass, with no known contact. Both articulate a universal governing principle (*logos* / *dao*) that:
- Is the basis of all change and opposition
- Remains itself unchanging and self-identical
- Is ignored by most people even though they participate in it continuously
- Requires paradox or anti-conventional language to approach

Heraclitus: "The Logos is common but most people live as if they have their own private understanding." Laozi: "The Dao is the mother of all things but is itself nameless." Both treat the opposition of contraries (Heraclitus's unity of opposites; Laozi's yin-yang complementarity) as the operating structure of the universal principle. The parallel is structural and chronological but no transmission is possible. It represents independent discovery of the *logos*-style principle by two cultures at approximately the same moment — one of the strongest cases for the "axial age" hypothesis.

**Laozi's apophaticism and Neoplatonic negative theology**
The Daodejing's opening gesture ("The Dao that can be named is not the eternal Dao") is the earliest sustained apophatic move in any text — predating Plotinus by ~600 years, predating the Upanishadic *neti neti* by at most a century or two (contemporaneous). The logical structure is identical to Plotinus, Pseudo-Dionysius, and Kabbalistic *Ein Sof*: the Absolute cannot be named because naming limits, and the Absolute by definition is beyond limit. Early Jesuit missionaries to China (Ricci, de Nobili) immediately noticed this and used it as a point of Catholic-Daoist theological dialogue.

**Laozi vs. Confucius — the structural foil**
The traditional Laozi-Confucius meeting is legendary, but the Confucius-Laozi opposition is structurally real and one of the greatest philosophical dualisms in history. Confucius: the good society requires proper ritual conduct (*li*), benevolence (*ren*), and active cultivation of virtue. Laozi: all this cultivation is artificial imposition on the natural order; *wu-wei* and spontaneous alignment with Dao is the path; the more laws, the more criminals; the more virtue-talk, the more corruption. This debate between the activist (Confucianism) and the quietist (Daoism) runs through all of Chinese intellectual history.
