# Athenagoras of Athens

## Identity
A philosophically trained Christian apologist of Athens who addressed his *Embassy for the Christians* to **Marcus Aurelius and Commodus** ~+177 (the joint reign — dating the work precisely). He defends Christians against the standard pagan charges — atheism (denial of the gods), Thyestean banquets (alleged ritual cannibalism), and Oedipean intercourse (alleged incest) — using arguments drawn from Greek philosophical convention, particularly Platonism. The treatise *On the Resurrection* is attributed to him but disputed.

## Historical evidence
The *Embassy* is extant and securely dated by its addressees. Athenagoras receives strikingly **no attestation from his near-contemporaries** — neither Irenaeus, nor Clement, nor Eusebius mentions him. This silence is a puzzle that has led some scholars (Barnes 1975) to question the traditional attribution.

## Works
- ***Embassy for the Christians*** — sophisticated philosophical defense of monotheism (drawing on Plato's *Timaeus* and on Stoic theology to argue for One God who is *kosmou poiētēs*, "world-maker"), and of the apostolic moral teaching against the trifecta of pagan slander.
- ***On the Resurrection*** — argues for resurrection of the body via natural-theological and philosophical premises. The attribution is contested (Grant 1954 challenged it; others defend).

## Role in the tradition
A philosophically polished apologist whose work was preserved by the Cappadocian-era manuscript tradition rather than by 2nd-c. quotation. He represents the *Athenian* strand of 2nd-c. Christian apologetic — Platonist, articulate, irenic. He is a key witness to how Christians presented themselves to the philosophically educated pagan elite.

## Disputes
- **Authenticity of *On the Resurrection*.** Grant 1954 challenged; Schoedel and others defended.
- **Absence from near-contemporary citation.** Genuinely puzzling; no fully satisfactory explanation.
- **Identity beyond the texts.** Nothing biographical is securely known.
