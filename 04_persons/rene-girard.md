# René Girard

## Identity

René Noël Théophile Girard (1923–2015) was a French-American literary critic, cultural anthropologist, and philosopher of religion. Born in Avignon, he trained as a historian of medieval documents (*École des Chartes*, Paris), emigrated to the United States in 1947, earned a PhD in history at Indiana University (1950), and spent his career at American universities — Indiana, Duke, SUNY Brockport, Johns Hopkins, SUNY Buffalo, and finally Stanford (1981–2008), where he held the Andrew B. Hammond Chair in French Language, Literature, and Civilization. He was elected to the Académie française in 2005 — unusual for a theorist working outside strictly French literary categories — and died in Stanford in November 2015.

## Mimetic theory: desire, rivalry, and the founding murder

Girard's theoretical project emerged from a close reading of great European novels (*Cervantes, Stendhal, Flaubert, Dostoevsky, Proust*) in his first book (*Mensonge romantique et vérité romanesque*, 1961). His initial discovery: **human desire is not autonomous**. We desire not objects for their own sake but objects because they are desired by a *model* or *mediator* — desire is always triangular (subject → model → object). Girard called this **mimetic desire**.

Mimetic desire is intrinsically rivalrous: when two people desire the same object through the same model, the model becomes the rival. As rivalry spreads through a community (each member imitating the desires of others), social tension escalates — what Girard calls the **mimetic crisis**. The crisis threatens to dissolve all social distinctions. The community's solution — discovered, in Girard's hypothesis, in pre-historic proto-human groups — is to concentrate all rivalry onto a single **surrogate victim**: a scapegoat. The unanimous collective violence against this victim (who is typically marginal: stranger, criminal, or paradoxically the sacred king) resolves the crisis by redirecting all aggression onto one target. Peace follows the murder. The community, not recognizing the mechanism, experiences the murdered victim as both guilty (cause of the crisis) and beneficial (the one whose death restored peace). The dead victim is **sacralized** — becomes a god. The [[scapegoat-mechanism]] is thus, for Girard, the origin of religion, sacrifice, and culture itself.

The three primary texts elaborating this theory: *Violence and the Sacred* (1972/1977) establishes the mechanism through readings of Greek tragedy, Dionysus, and Oedipus; *Things Hidden Since the Foundation of the World* (1978/1987) presents the full systematic statement; *The Scapegoat* (1982/1986) applies the hermeneutic to medieval persecution texts and the Gospels.

## The biblical trajectory: exposure of the mechanism

Girard's most distinctive and controversial claim concerns the relationship between his theory and the Judeo-Christian scriptures. He argues that:

1. **The Hebrew Bible begins to expose the scapegoat mechanism.** The prophets critique sacrifice (Psalm 51:16–17: "You do not desire sacrifice or I would bring it; you do not take pleasure in burnt offerings"; Isaiah 1:11; Amos 5:21–24). The stories of Cain/Abel, Joseph and his brothers, Job, and the Suffering Servant (Isaiah 53) all show the perspective of the innocent victim — which is absent from mythological texts that always justify the collective violence.

2. **The Gospels complete the exposure.** In the Passion narrative, Girard reads the same structure as the [[scapegoat-mechanism]]: the crowd demands a single man's death to preserve social peace (John 11:50 — "it is better for you that one man die for the people than that the whole nation perish" — Girard reads this as Caiaphas explicitly articulating scapegoat logic); Pilate confirms Jesus's innocence yet hands him over; the disciples flee (affirming collective complicity). But the Gospels tell this story *from the victim's perspective*, insisting on his innocence, and the Resurrection reverses the community's verdict. This makes the Gospels, for Girard, "the most subversive text in the history of religion" — the only text that unmasks the founding violence rather than concealing it under myth.

This Christological conclusion means Girard's theory of religion is simultaneously a vindication of Christianity's uniqueness — a position he held with increasing explicitness in his later work. He converted to Catholicism in the 1950s and regarded his theoretical conclusions as convergent with Christian revelation.

## Legacy and critique

Girard's theory has had substantial interdisciplinary impact. Walter Burkert (*Homo Necans*, 1972) independently reached strikingly similar conclusions about Greek sacrifice from a different disciplinary starting point — a convergence Girard himself noted as external confirmation. His framework has influenced literary criticism, anthropology of religion, conflict studies, and theology (especially Raymund Schwager's *Brauchen Wir Einen Sündenbock?* [1978] and James Alison's work on atonement theory).

Major critiques: (a) **Over-totalizing** — Girard's claim that *all* culture originates in founding violence is unfalsifiable and omits non-sacrificial traditions (Jain, Buddhist, Quaker) that are explicitly anti-sacrifice from the outset; (b) **Christocentric teleology** — the theory's arc culminates in Christianity as uniquely demythologizing, which critics read as question-begging; (c) **Historical evidence** — the founding murder is a hypothetical reconstruction, not an archaeological finding; (d) **Ivan Strenski** (*Theology and the First Theory of Sacrifice*, 2003) argues Girard confuses metaphorical with literal sacrifice and that his reading of Hebrew sacrifice as scapegoating misreads the texts.
