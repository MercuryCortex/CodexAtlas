# Silk Road (Overland Network)

## Origin and context

The term *Seidenstraße* was coined by the German geographer **Ferdinand von Richthofen** in 1877 to describe the network of overland routes linking Han-dynasty China (founded 206 BCE) with the eastern Mediterranean. The label is modern; the routes are far older. Bactrian camels were moving lapis lazuli from Badakhshan to Mesopotamia by 3000 BCE, and the *steppe corridor* north of the Iranian plateau was an active trade-and-migration zone throughout the Bronze and Iron Ages.

The conventional opening date of the *documented* Silk Road is **138-126 BCE**, when the Han envoy **Zhang Qian** completed his missions to the Western Regions and the Han court learned of the Yuezhi, the Wusun, the Dayuan (Ferghana), and the Parthian world beyond (Sima Qian, *Shiji* 123). The Han state opened the **Hexi Corridor** through the Gansu province in the 120s-110s BCE, garrisoning the four commanderies of Wuwei, Zhangye, Jiuquan, and Dunhuang, and the route was operational at imperial scale by 100 BCE.

The route's structural geography is a **chain of oasis cities** strung between two great deserts (the Taklamakan and the Gobi) and the high passes of the Pamirs, the Tianshan, and the Karakoram. There is no single road; rather, two arcs around the Taklamakan (northern via Turfan-Kucha-Aksu-Kashgar; southern via Miran-Khotan-Yarkand-Kashgar) reconverge at Kashgar before crossing the Pamirs into Sogdiana.

## Routes and commodity flow

**Eastward** along the road moved: Roman + Sasanian + Byzantine **glass** (Roman glass beads have been excavated from Han and Three Kingdoms tombs); **gold and silver coinage** (Roman *aurei*, Sasanian *drachms*, Byzantine *solidi* are found across the Tarim Basin); **horses** (the famous "blood-sweating" horses of Ferghana that Han Wudi went to war over in 104-101 BCE); **wool and woolen textiles**; **incense and aromatics** (cross-link to [[incense-trade-route-south-arabian]]); **lapis lazuli** from Badakhshan ([[lapis-lazuli-trade]]); and most importantly for cultural history, **religious texts and personnel** — Buddhist monks, Manichaean *electi*, Nestorian Christian missionaries, Zoroastrian priests.

**Westward** moved Chinese **silk** ([[silk-as-commodity]]) — the eponymous commodity, which by the 1st century CE was so prized in Rome that Pliny the Elder denounced the trade as draining the empire of gold (*Naturalis Historia* VI.54, XII.84); **porcelain** (from the Tang onward); **paper** (transmitted to the Islamic world via the [[battle-of-talas]] in 751 CE); **rhubarb**, **musk**, **cassia**, and from the Tang dynasty onward, **tea**.

The route's commercial peak is two-phase: **Han-Roman peak** (1st-2nd c. CE) and **Tang-Abbasid peak** (7th-9th c. CE), with a third great reactivation under the **Pax Mongolica** (1240-1350) when the unified Mongol khanates briefly made the entire overland network politically passable end-to-end (the journeys of Marco Polo, William of Rubruck, and Rabban Sauma date to this window).

## Cultural and religious transmission carried

The Silk Road is the principal vector by which **Buddhism reached East Asia**. The transmission is multi-wave:

- **Wave 1 (1st-3rd c. CE)**: Indian and Central Asian monks reach the Tarim Basin and the Han capital. The legendary Han Emperor Ming's dream and the dispatch of envoys to fetch Buddhist scriptures (recorded in the *Mouzi lihuolun*) is conventionally dated 67 CE. The **Kushan Empire** (1st-3rd c. CE, straddling Bactria, Gandhara, and the Tarim approaches) is the key intermediary: it is in Kushan-controlled Gandhara that the **Buddha image as we know it crystallises** (Greco-Buddhist statuary, drawing on Hellenistic sculptural conventions transmitted via the Seleucid-Bactrian Greek presence).
- **Wave 2 (4th-7th c. CE)**: The great translator-monks. **Kumārajīva** (344-413 CE), Kuchean by birth, translates the Lotus Sūtra and the foundational Mahāyāna corpus into Chinese at Chang'an. **Xuanzang** (602-664 CE) makes the reverse journey, travelling the southern arc through Turfan, Kucha, and over the Hindu Kush to Nalanda, returning with 657 Sanskrit texts whose translation under Tang state patronage establishes the **Yogācāra** school in East Asia. Xuanzang's *Great Tang Records on the Western Regions* (646 CE) is one of the principal primary sources on the 7th-century Silk Road.
- **Wave 3 (7th-10th c. CE)**: From the Tang Chinese context the transmission reaches **Korea** (Three Kingdoms / Unified Silla) and **Japan** (Asuka-Nara), in both cases via overland-and-maritime composite routes.

**Manichaeism** ([[tradition-manichaeism]]) — founded by Mani (216-274 CE) in Sasanian Mesopotamia — spreads east along the road, becomes the **state religion of the Uyghur Khaganate** in 762 CE, and persists in Chinese Fujian into the Ming. The Dunhuang library cave (sealed c. 1000 CE, rediscovered 1900) preserves the largest extant corpus of Manichaean texts in Chinese and Sogdian.

**The Church of the East** ([[tradition-church-of-the-east]], "Nestorian") establishes congregations along the entire route. The **Xi'an Stele** (781 CE), erected at the Tang capital, records 150 years of East-Syrian Christian presence under Tang patronage. Nestorian metropolitan sees existed at Merv, Samarkand, and as far east as Khanbaliq (Beijing) under the Mongols.

**Islam** ([[tradition-sunni-islam]]) reaches Central Asia with the Umayyad conquest of Transoxiana (705-715 CE under Qutayba ibn Muslim) and the Abbasid victory at the [[battle-of-talas]] in 751 CE. Islam then becomes the dominant religion of the road's central segment, displacing or absorbing Buddhism, Manichaeism, and Zoroastrianism between the 9th and 14th centuries.

**Zoroastrianism** ([[tradition-zoroastrianism]]) was the Sasanian state religion at the road's western terminus. Sogdian merchants ([[sogdian-trading-network]]) carried Zoroastrian practice as far as Tang Chang'an, where Han Chinese sources call it *xianjiao* and document state-registered temples in the foreign quarter (Forte 1996).

## MASSIVE-WIN clusters

**The Silk Road is the empirical proof that trade IS theology-transport.** Each of the road's four religious-transmission stories — Buddhism east, Manichaeism east-and-stays, Christianity east-and-recedes, Islam east-and-stays — runs on the **same merchant infrastructure** (Sogdian caravanserais, Khwarazmian credit instruments, oasis market quarters with foreign-religion permissions) that moves silk and lapis. The Sogdian merchant who carries a bolt of silk from Samarkand to Chang'an in 700 CE also carries a Manichaean *Kephalaia* or a Nestorian gospel-fragment in his saddle. The two transmissions are inseparable.

This is **cluster 2** from the [[28_exchange_networks/README|README]] cross-tradition list: the Silk Road as Buddhism corridor (and Manichaeism corridor, and Nestorian corridor, and Islam corridor). It also feeds **cluster 7** (Tyrian purple imperial-dye chain — Byzantine purple silk is woven from Chinese silk fibres on Constantinople looms; the Byzantine imperial purple silks that Charlemagne is buried in, and that line every great Western reliquary of the early Middle Ages, are the literal material output of this network).
