# Silk (as Trade Commodity)

## Origin and context

Silk is the **filament secreted by the larva of the silkworm moth** (*Bombyx mori*) when it spins its cocoon. A single cocoon yields a continuous filament of 600-900 metres of remarkable tensile strength and lustre. The systematic harvesting of this filament — **sericulture** — is a Chinese technological achievement of the Neolithic and Early Bronze Age (silk fragments from Yangshao-culture contexts c. 3500 BCE; the *Book of Rites* attributes the invention to the consort of the Yellow Emperor, **Leizu**, in a mythic framing).

Chinese sericulture was a **state monopoly** for approximately three thousand years. The export of silkworm eggs, mulberry seedlings, or sericulture knowledge was prohibited under the Han, Tang, and successor states on penalty of death. This monopoly is the structural fact that creates the [[silk-road-overland]] as a transcontinental economic phenomenon: finished silk could be exported, but the means of production could not.

The monopoly breaks in two great rupture events:
- **552 CE** — under Justinian, two **Nestorian Christian monks** smuggle silkworm eggs from China to Constantinople inside hollow bamboo staves (Procopius, *Wars* VIII.17.1-7). Byzantine sericulture begins, and silk is woven at the imperial *gynaikeia* in Constantinople from the 6th century. Byzantine purple silk becomes the imperial diplomatic gift par excellence for the next 600 years.
- **8th-12th centuries** — the technology diffuses to Sasanian-then-Islamic Persia, Andalusi Spain (Cordoba), Norman Sicily (Palermo's *Tiraz* workshops), and then medieval Italy (Lucca by the 11th c., Florence and Venice by the 13th c., Lyon under Louis XI in 1466).

## Routes / commodity flow + period

**Pre-Han (before 200 BCE):** Silk reaches the steppe pastoralists as luxury tribute and the Persian world as elite goods. Aristotle (*Historia Animalium* 5.19) mentions a silk-producing worm vaguely, possibly via Bactrian Greek intermediaries. Pazyryk burials in the Altai (5th-4th c. BCE) contain Chinese silk.

**Han-Roman (1st c. BCE - 3rd c. CE):** Silk becomes Rome's signature Eastern import. Pliny denounces it for draining 100 million sesterces per year from the empire (*NH* XII.84). Tacitus and Seneca condemn its diaphanous "shamelessness." Roman women wear *Coa vestis* (Coan silk, possibly wild silk dyed at Cos) and imported Chinese silk; the imported product is unravelled and rewoven in lighter Roman fashion. The transit of finished silk across the [[silk-road-overland]] is the principal cargo defining the route.

**Tang-Abbasid peak (7th-9th c.):** Silk becomes Eurasian currency. The **Tang state pays its armies in bolts of silk**; Sogdian merchants ([[sogdian-trading-network]]) carry it westward in bulk; Abbasid Baghdad receives it; Constantinople weaves its own. Silk is used as **diplomatic currency** between Tang, Abbasid, Byzantine, and steppe powers — a single high-quality bolt of patterned silk could substitute for substantial gold in elite gift exchange.

**Mongol Pax (13th-14th c.):** The unified Mongol empire makes silk one of the most-circulated commodities in Eurasia. Mongol robes of honour (*khil'at*) are silk; the chancery is conducted on silk; Marco Polo describes silk weaving in Yangzhou and Quinsai.

**Italian Renaissance peak (14th-16th c.):** Lucca, Florence, Venice, and Genoa become the great Western silk-weaving centres. **Velvet**, the new high-loft cut-pile silk fabric, emerges from Italian looms by the 14th c. and becomes the most coveted Western luxury textile.

**Atlantic-Pacific period (16th-18th c.):** The [[carreira-da-india-portuguese]] and Dutch VOC bring silk by sea around the Cape; the Spanish [[manila-galleon-trade]] brings Chinese silk across the Pacific to Acapulco and then by mule to Veracruz and by ship to Seville. By 1600 Chinese silk woven into ecclesiastical vestments is being shipped to Catholic missions on five continents.

## Cultural and religious transmission carried

**Silk IS the substrate of religious vesture across Eurasia.** Each major tradition encodes silk into its sacred-clothing system, and each encoding rides the trade.

**Buddhist *kasaya* (Pali *kāsāya*, Chinese *jiāshā* 袈裟, Japanese *kesa*).** The Buddha's prescribed monastic robe is rag-pieced cloth dyed in earth tones — but as Mahāyāna Buddhism moves east along the [[silk-road-overland]] and reaches the great Chinese monasteries, the *kesa* is woven of patterned silk. Tang and Song imperial gift-*kesa* survive in Japanese temple treasuries (most famously at Hōryū-ji and Tōdai-ji's Shōsōin) as the densest extant record of Tang silk weaving. Silk IS Buddhism's high-canonical vesture.

**Byzantine imperial silks.** The Byzantine imperial *gynaikeia* produced silks dyed in **Tyrian purple** ([[tyrian-purple-dye]]) reserved for the emperor's exclusive use. The most prestigious Byzantine diplomatic gifts in the 9th-12th centuries were patterned purple silks. The 6th-c. silk shroud of Charlemagne (now in Aachen) is Byzantine; the 8th-c. silk shroud of St Cuthbert (Durham) is Byzantine; nearly every great early-medieval Western relic-reliquary is wrapped in Byzantine silk.

**Catholic liturgical vestments.** From the early medieval period onward, the [[chasuble]], [[cope]], [[dalmatic]], [[stole]] of Catholic liturgy are woven of silk, embroidered with silk thread, often using the precise Byzantine and later Italian silks that the trade brought west. The famous **Opus Anglicanum** embroidered copes (13th-14th c.) are silk-embroidered on silk. Cardinal red — the colour of the College of Cardinals — is silk dyed in cochineal or scarlet kermes, and the very ecclesiastical hierarchy is colour-coded through dyed silk. This is the direct living chain from Roman senatorial purple to Byzantine imperial purple to Catholic priestly silks; the same fabric, the same dye-technology, the same prestige-grammar, **rebadged through three theological frames**.

**Imperial yellow silk** in China (forbidden to all but the Emperor and certain ranks of imperial clergy from the Tang forward), **Mughal *kamkhab* silks**, **Ottoman *kemha*** — every Eurasian sovereign tradition encodes a category of silk as the imperially restricted upper rank of textile.

**Sumptuary law.** Sumptuary legislation across medieval Europe (England 1336, France repeatedly, the German imperial cities) explicitly regulates who may wear silk. Silk-wearing IS social rank.

## MASSIVE-WIN clusters

This commodity is the **central case** for **cluster 7** of the README MASSIVE-WIN list (commodity monopoly and sacrality). Silk's Chinese-origin monopoly produces a global luxury substrate; Byzantine purple silk weaponises it for imperial-priestly status; the Catholic Church absorbs the colour-grammar; the system runs from Justinian's smuggling-monks (552 CE) through to the modern College of Cardinals (whose scarlet *biretta* and red silk *moiré* sash are continuous with this 1,500-year chain).

It is also the structural cargo of **cluster 2** (Silk Road as Buddhism corridor — the silk IS the Buddhism in many cases; Tang gift-*kesa* to Japanese temples ARE the transmission), **cluster 3** (Manila Galleon — Chinese silk crosses the Pacific to Acapulco, lining colonial Mexican churches and Andean processional vestments by the early 17th c.), and **cluster 5** (Spice Routes as Portuguese-imperial-theology vehicle — the *Carreira da Índia* carries silk westward; Portuguese-Goan silk vestments end up in every Padroado mission from Macau to Mozambique).
