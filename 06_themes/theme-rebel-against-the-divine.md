# Rebel Against the Divine

The rebel-against-the-supreme-divine-power is one of the most persistent and structurally durable patterns in the world's religious narrative traditions. Unlike the combat-myth's external chaos-monster, the rebel-within-the-divine pattern features a being who *belongs* to the divine order — a member of the divine court, family, or assembly — who chooses rebellion, is defeated, and is punished. The pattern is attested from Akkadian myth (2000 BCE) through Greek tragedy (5th c. BCE) through Jewish apocalyptic (3rd c. BCE) through Zoroastrian cosmology through Christian Satanology through Milton's *Paradise Lost* (1667) through Romantic reception (Blake, Shelley, Byron, Mary Shelley) through Nietzsche's transvaluation — and the rebel becomes, by progressive stages, the hero.

## The combat-myth substrate (Forsyth 1987)

Neil Forsyth's *The Old Enemy: Satan and the Combat Myth* (Princeton UP, 1987) establishes the deep substrate: the **combat myth**, in which a divine storm-god or champion defeats a chaos-dragon or sea-monster and creates (or restores) cosmic order through the victory. Instances:

- **Marduk vs. Tiamat** (Babylonian — [[phase-1-008-enuma-elish]], ~1800–1600 BCE): Marduk defeats the chaos-sea-dragon Tiamat and creates the ordered cosmos from her body. The definitive Mesopotamian instance.
- **Baal vs. Yam/Mot** (Canaanite — [[phase-1-013-baal-cycle]], ~1400 BCE): Baal defeats the sea-god Yam (chaos-waters) and the death-god Mot. The closest structural parallel to the Hebrew God's cosmic battles.
- **YHWH vs. Leviathan/Rahab** (Hebrew Bible — Psalms 74:14, 89:10; Job 26:12–13; Isaiah 27:1): YHWH defeats the sea-dragon Leviathan and the chaos-monster Rahab. These passages preserve the combat-myth substrate within Hebrew scripture.
- **Zeus vs. Typhon** (Greek — Hesiod *Theogony* 820–868 — [[phase-2-009-hesiod-theogony-works-and-days]]): Zeus defeats the hundred-headed monster Typhon after the Titanomachy. The Greek combat-myth.
- **Thor vs. Jörmungandr** (Norse): Thor defeats the World Serpent at Ragnarök. The Norse instance.
- **Indra vs. Vritra** (Vedic — [[phase-1-031-rigveda]]): Indra slays the dragon Vritra who has dammed the cosmic waters. The Vedic combat-myth.

The combat-myth pattern is pan-Near-Eastern and Indo-European. Its instances are structurally parallel but transmitted through documented cultural contact (Mesopotamian → Canaanite → Hebrew) and through independent instantiation (Norse, Vedic). Forsyth argues that the Christian Satan is the combat-myth's chaos-monster internalized into the divine court — the rebel-within.

## The rebel-within pattern (distinct from the combat myth)

The rebel-within pattern is structurally distinct from the external chaos-monster of the combat myth. The rebel is **a member of the divine order** — a court official, a member of the divine family, an angelic being. The rebellion is an act of will *within* an existing divine hierarchy, not an external threat. Instances:

**Anzu myth** (Akkadian, ~2000 BCE — [[ninurta]]): The divine eagle Anzu is a member of the divine court who serves Enlil. He steals the Tablet of Destinies — the source of Enlil's supreme authority over the cosmos — and claims divine power for himself. [[ninurta]] is commissioned by the divine assembly to defeat Anzu and recover the Tablet. Anzu is not an external chaos-monster but a court official who rebels. This is the earliest surviving instance of the rebel-within pattern: a divine being who belongs to the system, aspires to its summit, steals its authority, and is defeated by divine sanction. The structural parallel to Satan's rebellion in *Paradise Lost* is exact — Satan was the most exalted of the angelic beings (Lucifer, "light-bearer"), served God in the heavenly court, rebelled when God elevated the Son, and was defeated by divine commission.

**Semyaza and the Watchers** (Jewish apocalyptic, ~3rd c. BCE — [[phase-3-004-1-enoch]], [[semyaza]]): The Watchers are members of the divine court — angelic beings assigned to watch over humanity. [[semyaza]] leads two hundred of them in a sworn rebellion: descent to earth, union with human women, corruption of the antediluvian world. They are members of the divine order who choose transgression; they are bound underground as punishment until the eschatological judgment. The rebel-within pattern applied to the origins of evil in Second Temple Jewish theology.

**Lucifer/Helel** (Christian synthesis — [[lucifer]]): The Isaiah 14:12 "shining one" passage, read patristically as Satan's fall, creates the rebel-within at the highest possible level — the most exalted angelic being, the very brightest of heaven's court, who rebels through pride (*superbia*) and falls. The rebel is not an external enemy but the greatest of the divine servants.

**Prometheus** (Greek — [[prometheus]], Aeschylus *Prometheus Bound* ~460 BCE): A Titan — member of the original divine family (son of Iapetus, the pre-Olympian gods) — who acts against Zeus's explicit command to benefit humanity. Prometheus is not an external monster but a member of the divine generation whom Zeus has defeated and subordinated. His rebellion is an act of will within a kinship structure. The structural parallel to Lucifer is the Romantic identification: fire-theft :: forbidden knowledge; Zeus :: God; Caucasus chains :: Hell.

**Angra Mainyu** (Zoroastrian — [[angra-mainyu-ahriman]], [[phase-2-002-gathas-of-zarathustra]]): In the dominant non-Zurvanite reading, Angra Mainyu is not originally part of a divine family but an opposed independent principle; in the **Zurvanite variant** (heterodox Zoroastrian cosmology), Angra Mainyu and Spenta Mainyu are *twin sons of Zurvan* (Infinite Time) — brothers, one good and one evil — which is the most explicit rebel-within-divine-family instance in the Zoroastrian tradition. Even in the non-Zurvanite reading, Angra Mainyu's cosmic opposition to Ahura Mazda provides the structural template for the independent cosmic adversary that Jewish apocalyptic adapted. See [[theme-satan-angra-mainyu-transfer]].

**Milton's Satan** ([[phase-6-042-paradise-lost]]): The fullest literary elaboration of the rebel-within pattern in any tradition. Satan was Lucifer — "brighter once amid the host / Of Angels, than that star the stars among" (*PL* 7.132–133). His rebellion is triggered by God's elevation of the Son — an act of divine governance within the heavenly court that Satan reads as usurpation. The rebel is not the underdog but the greatest of the divine servants; his defeat is therefore not a combat-myth victory over an external threat but a tragic fall from within the divine order. Milton gives Satan the most rhetorically magnificent language in the poem precisely to make his self-deception — his inability to escape pride even when he can analyze it — more devastating.

## The Romantic inversion

The rebel-within pattern carries an inherent instability: a being who chooses autonomy over submission to supreme power is, by Enlightenment standards, the more *human* figure. The inversion was visible in Milton immediately.

Blake (*The Marriage of Heaven and Hell*, 1793): "Milton was of the Devil's party without knowing it." Satan's energy is Promethean creativity; God's law is Urizen's repressive ratio. The moral poles are reversed.

Shelley (*Defence of Poetry*, 1821; *Prometheus Unbound*, 1820): Milton's Satan is "the most heroic subject that ever was afforded to the contemplation of the poet" but marred by "ambition, envy, revenge." Shelley rewrites Satan as Prometheus, stripped of the retaliatory impulses — the pure rebel-for-humanity.

Byron (*Cain*, 1821): Cain meets Lucifer, who shows him the vastness of the cosmos and the smallness of the divine governance — the rebel becomes the educator of the human questioning spirit.

Mary Shelley (*Frankenstein*, 1818): The creature reads *Paradise Lost* and identifies with both Adam (created without consent) and Satan (rejected by the creator). Frankenstein-the-creator is the monster; the creature is the rebel-with-justice.

Nietzsche (*Thus Spake Zarathustra*, 1883–1885; *Beyond Good and Evil*, 1886): The transvaluation of the rebellion is complete — the will to power against the will of the "herd god" is the highest human affirmation. The rebel is now the Übermensch.

The Romantic inversion is not a misreading — it is the internal logic of the pattern revealed when the frame of divine authority is questioned. If the supreme power is not just, the rebel is not wrong. The entire subsequent history of Western secular culture's self-understanding as the rebellion against received authority — political, religious, epistemic — runs through this pattern.

## Epistemic framing

These instances are documented at different levels of scholarly confidence:

- The combat-myth cross-tradition transmission is well-documented with physical evidence (Mesopotamian tablets → Ugaritic texts → Hebrew scripture — Forsyth 1987, Wakeman 1973, Day 1985).
- The Anzu-Satan structural parallel is a scholar's inference from structural similarity; no direct documentary evidence of transmission exists.
- The Zoroastrian influence on Jewish Satanology is the best-documented doctrinal transmission (Russell 1977, Forsyth 1987, Shaked 1984) — timing, structure, and a direct etymological confirmation (Aeshma → Asmodeus) support it. See [[theme-satan-angra-mainyu-transfer]].
- The Prometheus-Satan identification is a Romantic-era literary and critical construction, not a claim about ancient transmission — but the structural parallel is genuine, and the Romantic reception tradition that made the identification is itself historically important.
- Milton's synthesis is documented: he read all of these sources and the connections are not interpretive inferences but direct literary engagements visible in the poem's text and his marginalia.
