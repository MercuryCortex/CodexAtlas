# Justification by Faith

The doctrine that the sinful human being is declared righteous before God (*iustificatio*) not on the basis of works of the Law or of moral merit but through faith in Christ's redemptive work — God's righteousness being either *imputed* (counted as) the believer's, or *imparted* (infused into) the believer's character, with the question of which became the Reformation's central theological earthquake. The motif is Pauline in origin, Augustinian in development, Lutheran in radicalization.

## Manifestations
- **Pauline origin:** [[phase-3-015-pauline-epistles]] — Romans 1:17 ("the righteous shall live by faith"), Romans 3:21–28, Galatians 2:16–3:14 — Paul argues that *erga nomou* (works of the Law) cannot justify; faith in Christ does. The locus classicus. Whether Paul meant a forensic-declarative justification (Luther's reading) or a relational-covenantal incorporation (the "New Perspective" of Sanders, Dunn, Wright) is the most contested question in late-20th-c. Pauline scholarship.
- **Augustinian deepening:** [[phase-4-028-augustine-confessions]], [[phase-4-029-augustine-city-of-god]] — Augustine's anti-Pelagian writings (411 ff.) make justification a work of unmerited grace alone (*sola gratia*); but Augustine reads "justification" as making-righteous (*iustum facere*), not merely declaring-righteous — a transformation, not a forensic verdict. This Augustinian-transformational reading is medieval and Catholic standard.
- **Medieval-scholastic apparatus.** *Habitus* of grace, sacraments as channels of infused grace, meritum *de congruo* / *de condigno*, treasury of merits, indulgences. Justification gets routed through sacramental and penitential machinery.
- **Luther's revolution:** [[phase-6-005-luther-95-theses]], [[phase-6-007-luther-bondage-of-will]] — Luther's 1513–17 tower experience reads Romans 1:17 *iustitia Dei* as *passive* righteousness — God's righteousness *given to* the believer, not demanded *of* him. The believer is *simul iustus et peccator* ("simultaneously righteous and sinner") — declared righteous extrinsically while remaining intrinsically sinful. This is the Reformation in conceptual nucleus. *Sola fide* — faith alone, not works — becomes a Reformation slogan.
- **Calvinist systematization:** [[phase-6-010-calvin-institutes]] — Calvin (1559 *Institutes* III.11ff.) tightens the forensic-imputational reading, integrates it with double predestination, and propagates it through the Reformed tradition.
- **Counter-Reformation:** Council of Trent's Decree on Justification (1547) affirms a transformational-imputational synthesis, anathematizing *sola fide* as Lutheran. The 1999 *Joint Declaration on Justification* between the Lutheran World Federation and the Vatican declares a non-condemnable consensus on the basics; whether real reconciliation has occurred is debated.

## Disputes
- **The "New Perspective on Paul."** Sanders 1977 argues that Second Temple Judaism was not the *works-righteousness* system Lutheran exegesis projected onto it; Paul was not anti-Torah-as-works but anti-Torah-as-ethnic-boundary-marker. Dunn, Wright, and others have developed this; conservative Lutherans (Westerholm) have defended the classical reading. The exegetical war continues.
- **Continuity with Augustine.** McGrath 1986 documents how Luther's *forensic-imputational* reading is a genuine departure from Augustine, not a recovery of him — Augustine's *iustum facere* is transformational. The Lutheran self-narrative ("we recovered Augustine") is partly retrospective.
- **Comparative analogs.** Functionally analogous doctrines of unmerited grace exist in [[bhakti-devotion]] (the *prapatti* of Śrīvaiṣṇavism — "let oneself be saved by the Lord"), in Pure Land Buddhism (*tariki*, other-power, in Shinran), and arguably in Sufi *fanāʾ*. Comparative theologians (Otto, Pannikar) have made much of this; reductionists demur.
