# Reason over Passion

The recurring Hellenistic ethical claim that *logos* (reason) ought to govern *pathē* (passions, emotions, appetites) — articulated by Plato (the chariot of *Phaedrus*; the tripartite soul of *Republic* IV), elaborated by Aristotle (virtue as habituation of the rational and non-rational parts), radicalized by the Stoa (passions are *judgments* of reason, so reason can master them entirely). 4 Maccabees gives the doctrine a programmatic Jewish formulation: "the question is whether *pious reason* (*eusebēs logismos*) is supreme over the passions" (4 Macc 1:1).

- [[phase-3-002-plato-dialogues]] — *Republic* IV, *Phaedrus* 246–254 (the chariot).
- [[phase-3-005-stoic-foundational-texts]] — the Stoic radicalization: passions as false judgments; the sage as *apathēs*.
- [[phase-3-019-4-maccabees]] — *eusebēs logismos*: reason informed by Torah-piety masters the passions in the martyrs Eleazar and the seven brothers.

The doctrine reappears in patristic asceticism ([[apatheia-and-virtue]]), in medieval scholastic faculty psychology (Thomas Aquinas, *ST* I-II qq. 22–48), and in early-modern moral philosophy (Spinoza, Kant). Modern critique: Hume's reversal ("reason is, and ought only to be, the slave of the passions"); 20th-century challenges from psychoanalysis and from feminist ethics of care which contest the rational-over-affective hierarchy.
