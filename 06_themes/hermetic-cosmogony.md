# Hermetic cosmogony

## Definition
The Hermetic account of how the cosmos came into being: an unknowable Father / Nous / Mind generates a second Mind (the Demiurge or Logos), which crafts the seven planetary spheres and the four elements; the primal Anthropos descends through the spheres and is captured by Nature, producing dual-natured humanity (mortal in body, immortal in essence); the cosmos thus constituted is itself a *living, divine, hierarchically-ordered being* — the visible god — whose elements participate in the divine Mind through the chain of being.

The diagnostic features:
1. **Apophatic supreme principle** — the Father is unknowable except through the revealing intermediary (Poimandres, Nous, Logos).
2. **Demiurgic intermediary** — a second Mind crafts the cosmos. Unlike Gnostic demiurges, the Hermetic demiurge is *good* and the cosmos is *good*.
3. **Anthropos-myth** — primal Man descends from the Father through the planetary spheres, sees his reflection in the lower waters, falls in love with it, descends, is embraced by Nature.
4. **Sevenfold planetary structure** — the soul descending acquires the qualities of the seven planets (vices); ascending, it sheds them.
5. **Cosmos as *deus visibilis* / second god** — the cosmos itself is divine; humanity is the third god (Asclepius §§7–8; Coptic Asclepius NHC VI,8 75.5–18).
6. **Egypt as *imago caeli***— the land of Egypt is the image of heaven (Asclepius §24, the opening of the Egyptian Apocalypse).

## Earliest attestations
- **Hermetic**: [[phase-4-011-corpus-hermeticum-i|CH I]] (Poimandres) is the locus classicus; ~100–200 CE. Variants in CH III (sacred discourse), CH X (the Key), CH XII (on common mind to Tat); the Asclepius §§7–17 cosmological exposition; the Coptic Asclepius NHC VI,8 75.5–18 closing metaphysics; **Kore Kosmou (Stobaean Excerpt XXIII)** is the most systematic single Hermetic cosmogony.
- **Egyptian background**: the **[[phase-1-027-memphite-theology-shabaka-stone|Memphite Theology of the Shabaka Stone]]** (~8th c. BCE redaction of much earlier material) — Ptah creates by thought + word; structurally the most direct Egyptian antecedent of the Hermetic Logos cosmogony. Hermopolitan Ogdoad cosmogony (8 primordial deities producing cosmos from chaos-waters) is also a substrate.
- **Greek background**: Plato's *Timaeus* (4th c. BCE) provides the demiurge + craft + ordered-cosmos vocabulary; Middle Platonist (Numenius, Plutarch) elaboration is the immediate philosophical intermediary.
- **Hellenistic Jewish background**: Philo of Alexandria's Logos cosmology shares vocabulary; **the LXX Genesis 1 "Let there be light" is directly echoed in CH I.5** (Logos descends on dark watery chaos and orders it).
- **Stoic background**: Stoic Logos-as-cosmic-rationality and pneumatic continuum.

## Variants
- **Greek Corpus Hermeticum** version (CH I, etc.): Hermes-Tat-Asclepius dialogue frame; Poimandres as revealing Nous.
- **Stobaean Kore Kosmou** version: Isis-Horus revelatory frame; more systematic; soul-grades and decan-astrology added.
- **Coptic Asclepius (NHC VI,8) / Latin Asclepius** version: emphasis on cosmos-as-image-of-heaven and Egypt-as-image-of-cosmos; humanity as third god.
- **Armenian Definitions** version: compressed gnomic propositions; the triadic God / cosmos / man hierarchy in maximally schematic form.

## Connections claimed
- **Egyptian substrate** (Fowden 1986, Mahé 1978-82): Hermetic cosmogony preserves Egyptian temple-cosmological tradition (Memphite, Hermopolitan, Heliopolitan) under Greek philosophical dress.
- **Platonist substrate** (Festugière 1944–54): Hermetic cosmogony is Middle Platonist philosophy with Egyptian vocabulary.
- **Modern consensus** (post-Fowden): both. Egyptian-priestly material rewritten in Greek philosophical categories.
- **Genesis 1 echoes** (Dodd 1935, Pearson 1990): the LXX Genesis 1 vocabulary is detectable in CH I.5 and the Coptic Asclepius's cosmology — evidence of Hellenistic-Jewish-Egyptian readership.
- **Christian engagement**: Lactantius cites Hermetic cosmology as confirming Christian creation doctrine; Augustine engages it polemically (City of God VIII.23–26); Renaissance Christians (Ficino, Pico, Steuco) read the cosmogony as harmonizable with Genesis 1.
- **Renaissance reception**: Ficino's 1471 *Pimander* preface treats the Hermetic cosmogony as Mosaic-prior wisdom; Casaubon 1614 redates it as post-Christian, ending its authority (but preserving its scholarly interest).
