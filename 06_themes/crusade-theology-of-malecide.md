# Crusade theology of malecide

## Definition
The theological move — articulated paradigmatically by [[bernard-of-clairvaux|Bernard of Clairvaux]] in [[phase-5-037-bernard-de-laude-novae-militiae|*De Laude Novae Militiae*]] (~1129–1136) — that killing-in-arms by a vowed [[tradition-knights-templar|Templar]] (or, by extension, by a crusader generally) against *malefactores* in defense of pilgrims and the Holy Land is theologically reclassified from *homicide* (requiring penance under standard Latin canon law) to ***malecide*** — sanctioned killing of evil-doers, soteriologically meritorious rather than penitentially-burdening.

The canonical Bernardine phrasing (*De Laude* ch. 3):
> *Sane cum occidit malefactorem, non homicida, sed (ut ita dixerim) malicida, et plane Christi vindex in his qui male agunt, et defensor Christianorum reputatur.*
>
> "When [the knight] kills a malefactor, he is not a homicide but, so to speak, a *malicide* — a clear avenger of Christ against those who do evil, and defender of Christians."

This reframing is the theological pivot point of medieval Western crusading ideology — the move that distinguishes *crusade* from *just-war* in the technical theological sense.

## Earliest attestations
- **Augustinian just-war substrate** — Augustine *De civitate Dei* and Letters provide the framework: certain wars are permissible (*bellum iustum*) when fought by legitimate authority, with just cause, with right intention, as last resort. *But*: Augustinian just-war provides *permission*, not *merit*. Augustine treats killing-in-just-war as *regrettable necessity*, not as meritorious act, and Augustinian-derived canon law (Gratian *Decretum* C.23) typically required *some* penance even for killing-in-just-war.
- **Urban II's plenary indulgence (1095, [[event-council-of-clermont-1095|Clermont]])** — the first major institutional shift. Crusade participation earns *remission of penance* — implying that the *killing involved* is either pre-emptively absolved or reclassified as meritorious.
- **Bernard's *De Laude Novae Militiae* (~1129–1136)** — the theological systematization. Killing-in-Templar-arms is reclassified as *malecide*, sanctioned, meritorious, contributing positively to the soul's progress rather than burdening it.
- **Bernard's *Quantum praedecessores* preaching for the [[event-second-crusade-1147-1149|Second Crusade]] (1146–1147)** — operationalizes the framework for the broader crusader (not just Templar) soldier.

## Variants
- **Augustine (just-war substrate):** Killing-in-just-war is *permitted* but not *meritorious*.
- **Urban II (1095):** Crusading carries plenary indulgence — the *penitential burden* is removed.
- **Bernard (1129–1147):** Templar / crusader killing is positively *meritorious* — soteriologically participating in the warrior's salvation rather than burdening it.
- **Innocent III (~1198–1216):** Crusading expanded — against heretics ([[event-albigensian-crusade-1209-1229|Albigensian Crusade]]), against political enemies of the papacy (the "political crusades" against the Hohenstaufen). The malecide-theology framework is applied with extreme breadth.
- **Aquinas (~1265–1273 *Summa Theologiae* II-II q.40, q.64):** Tightens the just-war framework back toward the Augustinian standard, but with the malecide-theological logic still operative for sanctioned-warrior cases.

## Connections claimed

This is one of the load-bearing edges where **Latin Christianity moves substantially beyond its Augustinian and earlier-patristic substrate**. The Augustinian just-war framework is *permission*; the Bernardine malecide framework is *merit*. Erdmann 1935 (the foundational treatment) identifies this as the inflection point that makes "crusade" a distinct theological category, not merely a heated case of just-war.

Polemical-critical reception:
- **Catholic moral-theological tradition post-Vatican II** — substantially distances itself from the malecide framework. The 1962–1965 [[event-second-vatican-council-1962-1965|Vatican II]] and post-conciliar Catholic teaching reframe the relation to violence; *Gaudium et Spes* (1965) §80 condemns total war and pushes back against the medieval framework.
- **Modern Christian pacifist traditions** (Mennonite, Quaker, Christian Anarchist) — treat the Bernardine malecide-theology as the paradigm-instance of Christianity's complicity with state violence.
- **Anti-Christian polemical literature** (Enlightenment and post-Enlightenment) — typically frames the Crusades through the malecide-theology framework as evidence of Christian violence.
