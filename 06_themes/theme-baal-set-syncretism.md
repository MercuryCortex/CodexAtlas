# Baal–Set Syncretism (Hyksos Period)

## Definition

The identification, under the Hyksos 15th Dynasty (c. 1650–1550 BCE), of the Egyptian god Set (*Sutekh*) with the West Semitic storm-god Baal-Hadad — producing a compound deity who served simultaneously as the Hyksos royal patron and as a culturally intelligible bridge between Egyptian and Levantine religious frameworks. This syncretism is the most archaeologically and textually documented case of direct Egyptian-Canaanite theological fusion in the second millennium BCE, and it is the primary mechanism by which Egyptian and Canaanite religion interpenetrate in the Abraham-Moses gap period.

## Egyptian side — Set as foreigner-god

Set was already Egypt's canonical deity of storm, desert, and foreigners before the Hyksos period, with a cult center at Ombos in Upper Egypt and Delta cult presence. His role as the murderer of Osiris and opponent of Horus positioned him as the principle of chaos and disruption within the Egyptian theological system — but also made him the natural patron of foreigners who disrupted Egyptian political order. The Hyksos 15th Dynasty kings took Set as their royal patron, a choice intelligible both as political claim to Egyptian legitimacy (Set is a legitimate member of the Egyptian Ennead) and as theological identification with their own West Semitic storm-god.

## Canaanite / West Semitic side — Baal Hadad

Baal-Hadad was the dominant storm- and weather-god of the Levantine Bronze Age, attested at Ugarit (the Baal Cycle, *KTU* 1.1–1.6) as lord of rain, lightning, and fertility. The Baal Cycle's central narrative — Baal's combat with Yam (Sea), his death at Mot's hands, and his resurrection — made him a dying-and-rising storm-god. The identification of Baal with Set thus brought a *narrative storm-god* mythos into contact with an Egyptian *storm-and-chaos deity* — two different theological traditions fused at the royal level.

Thomas Schneider (2008, *Ägypten und Levante*) demonstrated from Egyptian hieroglyphic orthography that the Egyptian scribal convention was to write "Baal" using the Set-determinative, confirming the equation was canonical in the Egyptian writing system itself, not merely a Greek-period inference.

## Long-term impact

The syncretism outlasted the Hyksos. The 19th Dynasty (Ramesside, founded c. 1295 BCE) traced its military origins to the Avaris garrison region. Ramesses I's family had deep connections to the Avaris Set-cult; Seti I ("Man of Set") and Setnakht ("Set is Strong") bear theophoric names. The **Year 400 Stela of Ramesses II** (discovered at Tanis, 1863; Cairo Museum) commemorates a 400-year Set-cult anniversary, possibly marking the founding of the Set temple at Avaris in the early Hyksos period — one of the few Egyptian state monuments explicitly memorializing a Hyksos-period religious foundation.

## Significance for Israelite tradition

The Baal-Set nexus is relevant to the question of early Israelite religion in two ways: (a) the Exodus narrative's Plague traditions include storm-and-darkness elements readable against a Baal-Set theological background — YHWH demonstrating superiority over the Hyksos/Canaanite storm-deity complex; (b) the [[tradition-canaanite-ugaritic]]'s Baal and the Egyptian Set share enough characteristics (storm, combat, power over death) that the Baal-Set equation also illuminates why the later polemic against Baal in the Hebrew prophets is so fierce — Baal was not a foreign irrelevance but the deity most closely associated with Egyptian-Canaanite political dominance.
