# El/Yahweh Convergence — The Canaanite Roots of Israelite Monotheism

## Definition

The thesis that the biblical God Yahweh emerged through a historical process of religious convergence with the Canaanite high god El — absorbing El's cosmic titles, shrine associations, divine characteristics, and consort (Asherah) — and that Israelite monotheism resulted not from a unique primal revelation but from the progressive theological concentration of what had been a polytheistic religious landscape. The convergence/differentiation model (Smith 2001, 2002) traces a two-phase process: Iron Age I convergence (Yahweh inherits El's identity), followed by Iron Age II differentiation (Baal and Asherah are polemically expelled from the Yahweh cult).

## The textual evidence

### 1. Biblical El-epithets

The most direct evidence is the cluster of El-epithets used freely of Yahweh throughout the Hebrew Bible, each of which designates a specific El-cult shrine in the Canaanite world:

| Epithet | Hebrew text | Location | Parallels |
|---|---|---|---|
| *El Shaddai* | Gen 17:1; Exod 6:3 | Bethel/patriarchal circuit | Northwest Semitic El-mountain theology |
| *El Elyon* | Gen 14:18–22; Ps 78:35 | Jerusalem (Melchizedek's cult) | Ugaritic *ʿly*, "the Most High" |
| *El Olam* | Gen 21:33 | Beersheba | "El the Eternal" |
| *El Bethel* | Gen 31:13; 35:7 | Bethel | Canaanite shrine deity |
| *El Roi* | Gen 16:13 | Negev (well near Kadesh) | "El who sees me" |
| *El Elohe Israel* | Gen 33:20 | Shechem | "El, God of Israel" |

Each of these patriarchal shrines is later attested as a Yahweh cult center. The conversion is total: every pre-Israelite El-sanctuary becomes a Yahweh-sanctuary. Cross (1973) and Smith (2002) argue this pattern cannot be coincidental: it reflects the institutional takeover by Yahweh of El's cultic geography.

### 2. Deuteronomy 32:8–9 (Dead Sea Scrolls / LXX text)

The most powerful single textual exhibit is Deut 32:8–9 in the Dead Sea Scrolls and LXX version (corrected from the Masoretic Text's anti-polytheistic alteration):

> "When Elyon divided the nations, when he separated the sons of man, he fixed the boundaries of the peoples according to the number of **the sons of God** [*bĕnê ʾĕlōhîm*; LXX: *angelōn theou*]. For Yahweh's portion is his people; Jacob is the lot of his inheritance."

The MT reads "sons of Israel" (*bĕnê yiśrāʾēl*) instead of "sons of God" — the Masoretic tradition changed a divine council reference to eliminate the polytheistic implication. The original text preserves a theology in which *Elyon* (= El) *distributed the nations among his divine sons*, and *Yahweh received Israel as his allotment* — meaning Yahweh was originally **one of El's divine sons**, not El himself. This is the most direct fossil of pre-merger Israelite theology surviving in the biblical text. Emanuel Tov identifies this as a deliberate MT alteration; the DSS text (4QDeut^j) has "sons of God."

### 3. Genesis 14 — the Melchizedek crux

Genesis 14:18–22 describes Melchizedek, king of Salem (Jerusalem), as priest of *El Elyon* ("God Most High, creator of heaven and earth"). Abraham pays him a tithe. When the king of Sodom offers Abraham spoils, Abraham swears "by Yahweh, El Elyon, creator of heaven and earth" — explicitly equating Yahweh with El Elyon. The equation is the redactor's; the underlying tradition preserves a separate El Elyon cult at Jerusalem (later the Temple site) that was subsequently identified with Yahweh.

The Ugaritic parallel confirms that "creator of heaven and earth" (*qōnēh šāmayim wāʾāreṣ*) is a Canaanite El epithet, attested on the Karatepe Phoenician inscription (8th century BCE) as part of El's title cluster. The formula was simply reassigned to Yahweh.

## The two-phase model (Smith 2001, 2002)

**Phase 1 — Convergence (Iron Age I, c. 1200–900 BCE):** The early Israelite league, coalescing from mixed highland Canaanite groups, Shasu nomads from the south (possibly the origin zone of Yahweh's theophany traditions — Sinai, Seir, Teman, Midian), and former Egyptianized Canaanites, adopted Yahweh as their tribal deity while simultaneously inheriting the El-cult shrines of the Canaanite landscape. El and Yahweh were identified: Yahweh received El's titles (El Shaddai, El Elyon, El Olam), El's consort (Asherah), and El's cosmic presidency over the divine council. The Ugaritic picture — El as elderly, enthroned patriarch; Baal as active storm warrior — maps onto the Hebrew Bible's composite Yahweh (wisdom/covenant = El; storm/warrior = absorbed Baal qualities).

**Phase 2 — Differentiation (Iron Age II, c. 900–586 BCE):** As Israelite monotheism hardened — particularly under the Deuteronomic reform tradition associated with Josiah (~620 BCE) and the prophetic tradition (Elijah, Hosea, Isaiah) — Baal was polemically expelled from the Yahweh cult, and Asherah was systematically removed from temples and condemned. The Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions (~810–750 BCE) document the transitional state: Asherah still paired with Yahweh in popular blessing formulae, regional Yahweh cults (Yahweh of Samaria, Yahweh of Teman) still functional, before the Josianic centralization erased them.

## The Yahweh origin problem — not El at all?

The convergence model does not mean Yahweh *was* El. The evidence for Yahweh's separate origin points south: the theophany traditions consistently locate Yahweh at Sinai, Seir (Edom), Teman, Midian, and Paran — not in northern Canaan. The Song of Deborah (*Judges 5:4–5*) and Deuteronomy 33:2 ("Yahweh came from Sinai, dawned from Seir, shone forth from Mount Paran") preserve an archaic memory of Yahweh as a storm deity from the southern wilderness, possibly associated with Kenite/Midianite groups and the Shasu nomads attested in Egyptian New Kingdom records (*Šʿsw yhw* in an Amenhotep III list ~1400 BCE — the earliest possible extra-biblical attestation of the divine name). Cross identifies Yahweh as originally an El-epithet that became hypostatized; others see Yahweh as an independent deity absorbed into the El-framework when Israelite highlanders occupied Canaanite shrine cities.

## Archaeological evidence

The [[document-kuntillet-ajrud-inscriptions]] (c. 810–750 BCE) and the Khirbet el-Qom inscription (c. 750 BCE) provide epigraphic evidence of "Yahweh and his Asherah" in both the Northern and Southern Kingdoms simultaneously — proof that the El-Asherah consort model had transferred wholesale to Yahweh before the Josianic reform eliminated it. This is the most cited archaeological evidence for the convergence thesis.
