# Mesoamerican Cosmogony

## Definition
The integrated complex of Mesoamerican creation narratives, cosmic structures, and time-cycles attested across Olmec, Maya, Aztec/Mexica, Mixtec, Zapotec, and related pre-conquest cultures (and partly continuing in post-conquest indigenous tradition). Diagnostic features distinguishing Mesoamerican cosmogony from other cosmological traditions: (1) **recurrent world-creation-and-destruction cycles** (the Aztec *Five Suns* schema, the analogous Maya creation-recreation in the *Popol Vuh* and the Chilam Balam books); (2) **maize as the substance of created humanity** (the Popol Vuh's gods make humans from maize-dough after failing with mud and wood; recurrent across Mesoamerican narratives); (3) **the World Tree (*Wakah-Chan* in Maya, often depicted as a Ceiba) as cosmic axis mundi** connecting the four directional quadrants, the underworld, and the heavens; (4) **dual-creator dynamics** with paired creator-rivals (Quetzalcoatl-Tezcatlipoca in the Nahua tradition; the Hero Twins Hunahpu and Xbalanque in the Maya); (5) **calendrical structuring of creation** (the 260-day *tzolk'in* / *tonalpohualli* and the 365-day *haab* / *xiuhpohualli* operate as both ritual and cosmogonic frameworks); (6) **sacrificial-cosmogonic motif** — gods sacrifice themselves to establish cosmic order (the Aztec myth of the gods at Teotihuacan throwing themselves into the fire to become Sun and Moon).

## Earliest attestations
- **Olmec (~-1500 to -400 BCE).** Iconographic precursors of the major Mesoamerican deity-complexes — the feathered serpent (proto-Quetzalcoatl / Kukulkan), the maize god, the rain god — appear in Olmec art (La Venta, San Lorenzo). Textual evidence at this depth is absent.
- **Classic Maya (~250–900 CE).** The earliest extensive textual-iconographic attestation. Maya hieroglyphic inscriptions at Palenque (the Cross Group inscriptions), Quiriguá (the great stelae), and elsewhere preserve creation-dates (the 4 Ahaw 8 Cumku creation of 11 August 3114 BCE in the proleptic Gregorian calendar — the start-date of the current Long Count Maya world), enthronement-of-the-gods narratives, and the cosmic-axis framework. The Vase of the Seven Gods (K2796) and the Vase of the Eleven Gods (K7750) depict the moment of Maya cosmic creation.
- **Postclassic Maya (~900–1500 CE).** The codices ([[phase-8-002-codex-borgia]] and especially [[phase-8-003-dresden-codex-maya]]) preserve extensive cosmogonic-calendrical material. The Dresden Codex's closing flood-scene (page 74) is the most striking pre-Columbian apocalyptic image.
- **Aztec / Mexica (~1325–1521 CE).** [[phase-8-002-codex-borgia]] (Mixtec-Aztec milieu) preserves pre-conquest cosmogonic imagery in its remarkable pages 29–46 — the most detailed visualization of Mesoamerican cosmic structure. [[phase-8-004-florentine-codex-sahagun]] (Sahagún's 1545–1590 Nahuatl-Spanish ethnographic encyclopedia) preserves the most extensive textual record of the Five Suns cosmogony, the Teotihuacan sacrifice myth, the Quetzalcoatl-Tezcatlipoca rivalry, and the creation of humans from bones from Mictlan.
- **Highland Maya (Postclassic / early-colonial).** [[phase-8-001-popol-vuh]] (the K'iche' Maya book of creation; surviving in mid-16th-century alphabetic transcription based on pre-conquest hieroglyphic original) is the most fully preserved Mesoamerican cosmogonic narrative — the Hero Twins, the failed creations, the maize-human, the descent to Xibalba.
- **Yucatec Maya (early-colonial).** [[phase-8-005-chilam-balam]] (the Books of Chilam Balam, 17th–19th c. Yucatec Maya manuscript tradition) preserve fragmentary creation passages and the prophetic-cyclical reading of Maya history within the cosmogonic framework.

## Variants
- **Maya vs. Aztec creation.** The Maya cosmogonic tradition (Popol Vuh, Classic-period inscriptions) emphasizes the Hero Twins, the descent to Xibalba (the underworld), and the maize-human creation. The Aztec tradition emphasizes the Five Suns schema and the Teotihuacan self-sacrifice of the gods. Both share the world-cycles-and-destruction framework and the world-tree axis mundi.
- **Number of world-cycles.** Aztec Five Suns (Nahui Ocelotl / Jaguar; Nahui Ehécatl / Wind; Nahui Quiahuitl / Rain; Nahui Atl / Water; Nahui Ollin / Earthquake — the current age). Maya creation-cycles are differently numbered across sources; the Long Count works with multiple *bak'tun* cycles, and the Popol Vuh narrates a three-attempt creation sequence (mud, wood, maize).
- **Identity of creator-pair.** Nahua: Quetzalcoatl and Tezcatlipoca (sometimes Quetzalcoatl alone in his cosmogonic-creator function; sometimes in rivalry with Tezcatlipoca; sometimes paired with Huitzilopochtli or with the dual creator Ometeotl). Maya: the divine pair Tepeu-Gucumatz / Itzamna and his consort Ixchel; the Hero Twins as agents within the creator-framework.
- **Sacrifice as cosmogonic.** The motif appears in Aztec form as the gods' self-sacrifice at Teotihuacan to create the Sun (Tonatiuh) and Moon. Maya parallel: the Hero Twins' self-sacrifice in Xibalba and their subsequent transformation into Sun and Moon.
- **The maize-human.** Constant across Maya and Nahua traditions; humans are made from maize-dough by the gods. This is the most diagnostic Mesoamerican cosmogonic-anthropological feature.

## Connections claimed
- **Cross-tradition Mesoamerican unity.** Schele & Freidel 1990, Freidel-Schele-Parker 1993, Carrasco 1982, León-Portilla 1963, and Read & González 2000 argue (with substantial archaeological-iconographic corroboration) that the major Mesoamerican civilizations share a substantially unified cosmogonic-religious framework descended from a common Olmec-era core, despite extensive cultural and linguistic variation. The world-tree, the four-quadrant cosmos, the underworld-realm, the maize-human, the calendar-as-cosmogony, and the world-cycles-and-destruction are pan-Mesoamerican.
- **Connections to other traditions (hyperdiffusionist claims).** Various 19th- and 20th-century hyperdiffusionist scholars (Donnelly 1882 on Atlantis as the source; Hancock 1995 on lost-civilization influence; Theosophical and modern-occult claims) have argued Mesoamerican cosmogony is descended from Old-World sources. **These claims are rejected by mainstream Mesoamericanist scholarship**, which treats Mesoamerican civilization as substantively independent in origin (cf. [[phase-7-036-hancock-fingerprints-of-the-gods]] for the modern-occult version; the academic mainstream rejects the hyperdiffusionist framework). The parallels (cosmic tree, flood, dying-rising gods) are universally distributed cosmological types, not evidence of trans-oceanic transmission.
- **Maya-Aztec inter-influence.** The substantial Postclassic-period interpenetration of Maya and Aztec cosmogonic traditions is uncontroversial; the Mixtec-Aztec [[phase-8-002-codex-borgia]] and the contemporaneous Maya codices share considerable iconographic vocabulary. Whether this reflects substrate-Olmec unity or later trade-and-political contact (or both) is the live question.
- **Pre-conquest continuity in post-conquest texts.** A central methodological dispute: how reliably do post-conquest alphabetic sources (Popol Vuh, Florentine Codex, Chilam Balam) preserve pre-conquest cosmogony, given that they are written in colonial contexts often by missionaries or indigenous Christians? Mainstream view (Tedlock, León-Portilla, Schele, Carrasco): substantial pre-conquest content is recoverable when post-conquest sources are checked against the pre-conquest codices, archaeological iconography, and ethnographic survivals.
