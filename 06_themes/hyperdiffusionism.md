# Hyperdiffusionism

## What it is
**Hyperdiffusionism** is the historiographical hypothesis, dominant approximately **1890–1940**, that most or all human civilizations derived from a single ancient origin. Its most influential version — **heliocentric hyperdiffusionism** — was promoted by British anatomist **Grafton Elliot Smith** (1871–1937) and his associate **W.J. Perry**, who argued that civilization originated in ancient Egypt and spread globally through migration and cultural diffusion. A German-centered variant located the origin in Mesopotamia (**Kulturkreislehre** / "culture circle" school — Frobenius, Schmidt, Graebner).

**[claim: methodologically discredited]** — Hyperdiffusionism as a scholarly framework was dismantled by mid-20th-century anthropology (Harris 1968 is the definitive critique). Its central claim — that independent invention of similar cultural forms is impossible or improbable — was refuted by extensive archaeological, genetic, and anthropological evidence for genuine independent invention across unconnected populations.

## Key figures
- **Grafton Elliot Smith** — *The Migration of Early Culture* (1915); *The Ancient Egyptians* (1911). Argued that mummification, sun-worship, megalithic monuments, and a cluster of associated cultural traits diffused from Egypt worldwide.
- **W.J. Perry** — *The Children of the Sun* (1923). Extended Smith's framework.
- **Lord Raglan** (*The Hero*, 1936) — argued that the hero myth pattern could only have one origin and diffused from a single source.
- **Thor Heyerdahl** — A *softer* diffusionist (not hyperdiffusionist), demonstrating the *possibility* of transoceanic contacts through practical voyaging experiments (Kon-Tiki, Ra expeditions). Heyerdahl did not claim monogenesis; he argued specific contacts were possible. Distinguished from hyperdiffusionism proper.

## Why it matters historically
Hyperdiffusionism had significant **ideological consequences**:
- It was often implicitly or explicitly **colonialist and racist** in application: non-Egyptian, non-European peoples were depicted as passive recipients of civilization rather than independent inventors.
- It paradoxically also had **diffuse Afrocentric parallels** (though different claims): both hyperdiffusionism and Afrocentrism challenged Eurocentric default narratives, though using very different frameworks.
- Its methodological legacy: the debate between hyperdiffusionism and the **independent-invention orthodoxy** forced anthropology to develop rigorous criteria for distinguishing genuine cultural contact from convergent cultural evolution. This methodological refinement is hyperdiffusionism's genuine (indirect) contribution.

## Soft diffusionism vindicated (partially)
The Polynesian-South American contact hypothesis — associated with Heyerdahl's voyages — was **partially vindicated** by Ioannidis et al. (2020, *Nature*): genetic evidence confirms pre-Columbian contact between Polynesian populations and South American (Zenu people of Colombia) populations, approximately 1200 CE. This vindicates *specific soft diffusionism* (contact occurred) while leaving hyperdiffusionism's broader monogenesis claims unjustified.

## MASSIVE-wins connections
- **Hyperdiffusionism ↔ modern genetics**: The specific Polynesian-South American contact now confirmed by ancient DNA (Ioannidis 2020) is exactly the kind of claim that was labelled "pseudoscientific diffusionism" for decades. The lesson is that not all diffusionist hypotheses are wrong — they require empirical testing. The methodological discipline required to distinguish valid contact hypotheses from hyperdiffusionist overreach is itself a research finding.
- **Hyperdiffusionism ↔ independent invention**: The debate produced the key methodological question of comparative religion: when two traditions share a motif, ritual, or narrative structure, is it evidence of historical contact or independent invention? This question underlies every `parallel-motif` edge in this vault.
