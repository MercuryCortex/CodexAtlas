# The Suitors of Penelope

## Identity

The suitors of Penelope (Greek **μνηστῆρες**, *mnēstēres*, "the wooers") are the collective antagonist of the *Odyssey*: **108 young nobles** — by Telemachus's own count, fifty-two from Dulichium, twenty-four from Same, twenty from Zacynthus, and twelve from Ithaca itself (*Od.* 16.245–253) — who, presuming [[odysseus]] dead, install themselves in his house to compete for his wife Penelope's hand, and for three years feast daily on his herds and wine while she holds them off (most famously by weaving and secretly unweaving Laertes's shroud, *Od.* 2.93–110; 19.137–156). Their leaders are **Antinous son of Eupeithes**, the brutal ringleader, and **Eurymachus son of Polybus**, his smooth-tongued double — a contrasted pair the poem uses systematically (Fenik's and de Jong's doublet analyses). The bare slug *antinous* is not used for this figure: the vault's Antinous is Hadrian's deified favourite (via [[fernando-pessoa]]), an unrelated Roman-era person.

The suitors are a **narrative collective typed as a theme** because their function in the comparative record is exemplary rather than biographical: they are Greek literature's paradigm of the **guest who violates hospitality** and is destroyed for it, the target of the locus classicus of the **chance-word omen** in Greek literature (Johnston 2008), and, as shades, the escorted party of what is standardly taken as the earliest full **psychopomp** scene in Greek epic. One collective completes three cross-tradition hubs.

## In the Odyssey

**The crime — xenia inverted.** Greek *xenia* binds host and guest in reciprocal obligation under Zeus Xenios (see [[moral-sacred-hospitality-cross-tradition]]). The *Odyssey*'s catalogue of hospitality is mostly a catalogue of **hosts** good and bad; the suitors complete it from the other side, as the definitive **bad guests**: uninvited consumers of an absent host's *oikos*, who plot the murder of his son (the ambush of Telemachus, *Od.* 4.669–674; 16.371–386) and abuse the disguised master begging at his own hearth — Antinous strikes the beggar with a footstool (17.462–465), Eurymachus hurls another (18.394–398), and Ctesippus throws an ox-hoof at him with the sneer that it is his *xeinion*, his "guest-gift" (20.287–302). The poem's theoxeny warning is voiced at this moment, by one of the suitors themselves: gods in the likeness of strangers roam the cities of men, watching their violence and their righteousness (17.483–487). Emily Kearns (1982) reads the whole return as a **Homeric theoxeny** with the master in the god's role: the household is tested by a disguised visitor, and the poem repeatedly insists the suitors perish "through their own reckless deeds" (*atasthaliai* — 22.317, 23.67, 24.458), the very theology Zeus announces programmatically at 1.32–43.

**The omens — a doomed party marked in advance.** Few victims in Greek epic are marked by so dense a sequence of omens. Halitherses reads the eagle-sign of Book 2 against them (2.146–176); Helen reads another (15.160–178); and on the eve of the slaughter comes the **kledon**: Odysseus prays for a *phēmē* (a sign in speech) and a *teras* (a sign in the sky), Zeus thunders from a clear sky, and a slave-woman at her mill — the weakest person still awake — prays that this feast be the suitors' last (*Od.* 20.98–121). This is the literary locus classicus of **cledonomancy**, the divination by chance-heard words catalogued in [[divination-molybdomancy-cledonomancy]] (Johnston 2008). Finally the seer Theoclymenus sees the hall filled with shrouded shapes, blood on the walls, and the sun perished from heaven (20.345–357) — the suitors laugh, and are dead within a day.

**The Mnesterophonia.** After the bow contest of Book 21 — the bow only the true husband can string — Odysseus's first arrow takes Antinous through the throat as he lifts his cup (22.8–21). Eurymachus blames the dead ringleader and offers restitution of twenty oxen per man; Odysseus refuses, and he is the second to die (22.44–88). Telemachus spears Amphinomus, the one decent suitor whom the disguised Odysseus had tried to warn away (18.119–157; 22.89–94). The killing is systematically keyed to xenia: the cowherd Philoetius kills Ctesippus with the taunt that the spear-thrust repays his "guest-gift" (22.285–291). Leiodes, the suitors' own sacrificing diviner (*thyoskoos*), pleads innocence and is beheaded (22.310–329); the singer Phemius and the herald Medon, compelled servants rather than guests, are spared (22.330–380). The aftermath — the hanging of the twelve slave-women (22.457–473) — is the scene modern ethics-of-the-poem debates centre on (see Disputes).

**The second Nekyia.** Book 24 opens with [[hermes-greek]] Kyllenios summoning the suitors' shades with the **golden wand** and leading them, squeaking like bats in a cave, down past the **White Rock**, the **gates of the Sun**, and the **district of dreams** to the asphodel meadow (*Od.* 24.1–14) — the only passage in Homer where Hermes performs the full office of *psychopompos*, and — on the standard dating of the Continuation — the earliest Greek attestation in the [[theme-guide-through-underworld]] hub. There the suitor Amphimedon retells the whole story to Agamemnon's shade — blaming Penelope's web-trick (24.121–190) — and Agamemnon pronounces the famous contrast of faithful Penelope with his own Clytemnestra (24.192–202). The poem ends with the suitors' kin, led by Antinous's father Eupeithes, marching for vendetta; Laertes kills Eupeithes, and Athena imposes the closing peace (24.412–548).

## Cross-tradition significance

The suitors are a Greek-internal creation with no cult and no independent existence outside the epic; their cross-tradition weight is as the **paradigm case** in three hubs, and the honest framing differs for each.

**Hospitality (structural completion, within one tradition).** The cross-tradition hospitality hub's punished violators are usually hosts — Sodom, the Cyclops, Baucis and Philemon's neighbours. The suitors supply the pattern's other arm: **inhospitality punished in the guest**. This is not a transmission claim but a structural one: the Pitt-Rivers/Derrida point that *hostis* means both guest and enemy is literalized in a party of guests who become the household's enemies and are slaughtered in the dining hall itself. Within Stith Thompson's motif grammar they sit under Q1.1 (inhospitality punished) as its guest-side realization, with the theoxeny motif (K1811) displaced onto the disguised mortal master (Kearns 1982).

**The returning husband (transmission vs convergence, genuinely debated).** The suitors' whole scenario — the long-absent husband returning in disguise to find his wife besieged by wooers, the recognition, the contest with the bow only he can master, the slaughter at the feast — is an **international tale-type**, ATU 974 "The Homecoming Husband" (Uther 2004), with its most striking non-Greek realization in the Turkic epic *Alpamysh*, where the hero likewise returns disguised to his wife's wedding-feast and wins the bow contest. Viktor Zhirmunsky's classic comparison (1967) weighed direct Greek-to-Central-Asian transmission (via Hellenistic Bactria) against independent convergence on a shared oral tale-type, and came down on the side of **typological kinship rather than borrowing** — though the dating of the *Alpamysh* tradition is itself contested and the transmission question remains open. The vault records this as a debated parallel, not a secured line of descent.

**The escorted dead (the hub's earliest Greek instance).** For the guide-through-underworld hub the second Nekyia matters because it is standardly taken as the **oldest full Greek psychopomp scene**: not a living visionary guided through the beyond, but the ordinary dead conducted to their place — the narrow threshold-function from which the fuller guide tradition (Virgil, Sraosha and Adar, Gabriel) elaborates. Its unique afterlife geography (White Rock, gates of the Sun, land of dreams) is discussed by Sourvinou-Inwood (1995) as a snapshot of Homeric afterlife belief in transition.

## Disputes

1. **Is the second Nekyia authentic?** Antiquity itself doubted the *Odyssey*'s ending: the Alexandrian critics Aristophanes of Byzantium and Aristarchus marked *Od.* 23.296 as the poem's "end" (*telos*/*peras*), and the scholia object that the suitors' shades enter Hades **unburied**, contradicting *Iliad* 23.71–74, where unburied Patroclus is barred. Denys Page (*The Homeric Odyssey*, 1955) pressed the analyst case that the whole Continuation (23.297–24.548) is a later addition; Heubeck's Oxford commentary (1992) mounts the standard defense of its authenticity and thematic necessity. The hub-relevant point survives either way: even if the second Nekyia is a later stratum, it remains an archaic Greek witness to Hermes psychopompos.
2. **Justice or atrocity?** Whether the poem presents the Mnesterophonia as clean divine justice is genuinely contested. The theodicy reading takes Zeus's programmatic speech (1.32–43) and the *atasthaliai* refrain at face value: the suitors are warned, omen-marked, and self-doomed. Recent work (Loney 2019, among others) argues the poem itself problematizes the revenge — the slaughter of the pleading Leiodes, the hanging of the slave-women, and the vendetta of Book 24 that only a goddess's intervention can stop. The node reports the debate; the hospitality hub's use of the suitors ("whose violation of xenia dooms them") reflects the poem's own stated theology, which is not the whole of its ethics.
3. **The "eclipse" of Theoclymenus.** The seer's vision that the sun has perished from heaven (20.356–357) was read as a solar eclipse already by ancient interpreters, and Baikouzis & Magnasco (*PNAS* 2008) used it to date the slaughter astronomically to 16 April 1178 BCE. Most classicists regard the line as a visionary image of doom, not an observational record, and the astronomical dating as an over-reading; it is recorded here as a curiosity of reception, not as evidence.
