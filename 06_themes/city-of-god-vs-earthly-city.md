# City of God vs. Earthly City (Augustine)

## Definition
Augustine's foundational political-theological schema in *De Civitate Dei* (~413–426): humanity is divided into *two cities* (*civitates*) constituted by *two loves*. The *Civitas Dei* — the City of God — comprises all those who love God to the contempt of self; the *Civitas Terrena* — the Earthly City — comprises those who love self to the contempt of God. The two cities are *intermingled* throughout history (no earthly political order, including the Christianized Roman Empire, coincides with the *Civitas Dei*); they will be definitively separated only at the eschaton. The *visible* church is not identical with the *Civitas Dei* (the wheat and tares of Matthew 13:24–30); the secular order is not identical with the *Civitas Terrena*.

Diagnostic markers: (a) two cities constituted by two *loves*, not by political-territorial boundaries; (b) intermingled in history (the *corpus permixtum*); (c) eschatological-not-political separation; (d) refusal to identify any historical political order — including a Christianized Rome — with the kingdom of God.

## Earliest attestations
- **Augustine, *De Civitate Dei*** (composed ~413–426 in 22 books): the canonical text. Books 1–10 are critical-polemical (against pagan religion and philosophy); Books 11–22 are constructive (the two cities from creation to eschaton).
- **Provoking event:** the sack of Rome by Alaric's Goths, 24 August 410. Pagan apologists blamed the abandonment of the traditional Roman religion; Christian apologists were embarrassed. *De Civitate Dei* responds: Rome's calamity does not impugn Christianity; the eternal city is the *Civitas Dei*, not Rome.
- **Augustine's earlier formulations:** the two-loves / two-cities distinction appears in nascent form in *Confessions* X, *De Genesi ad Litteram*, and the sermons of 410–412. *De Civitate Dei* is the systematic articulation.

## Variants
- **Strict Augustinian (the original):** the two cities are eschatological communities; the secular order is neutral pilgrim-territory in which the *Civitas Dei* makes its pilgrimage; no political identification with the kingdom of God.
- **Carolingian theocratic appropriation:** Charlemagne, Alcuin, and the medieval Holy Roman Empire read *De Civitate Dei* as *legitimating* the imperial-ecclesial union — the empire as visible-Civitas-Dei. This is widely held by historians (Markus 1970) to be a *misreading* of Augustine's actual position.
- **Papalist-hierocratic appropriation:** Innocent III, Boniface VIII (*Unam Sanctam* 1302) press a strong reading of the Church-as-Civitas-Dei with secular authority subordinated.
- **Lutheran two-kingdoms** (Luther's *Temporal Authority* 1523): the two-cities schema is *internalized*: every Christian belongs to both kingdoms (the spiritual kingdom of grace and the temporal kingdom of law), which operate by distinct rules. Closer to Augustine's anti-theocratic instinct than the medieval Carolingian appropriation.
- **Calvinist Reformed appropriation:** Calvin's Geneva and the New England Puritan project at points *identify* the visible covenanted community with the Civitas Dei (the "city on a hill" rhetoric); this is at significant distance from Augustine.
- **Modern political-theological retrievals:** Reinhold Niebuhr's *Christ and Culture* problem; John Howard Yoder's pacifist Augustinianism (*The Politics of Jesus* 1972); Oliver O'Donovan's *Desire of the Nations* (1996); Hauerwas's resident-aliens ecclesiology.

## Connections claimed
- **Platonic ancestry.** The *two-cities* structure has clear Platonic background: the *Republic*'s Kallipolis (see [[ideal-state-myth]]) is the city constituted by the love of the Good; the actually-existing oligarchic / democratic / tyrannical cities are constituted by lower loves. Augustine's *two loves* is a Christian transposition of Plato's *erōs ordered to the Good* vs. *erōs disordered*.
- **Pauline material.** Phil 3:20 ("our commonwealth is in heaven"), Eph 6:12 (the cosmic powers), and the Pauline emphasis on the eschatological body shape Augustine's account.
- **Roman political-theological context.** Augustine's anti-theocratic instinct is in significant part anti-Eusebian: Eusebius of Caesarea ([[phase-4-027-eusebius-ecclesiastical-history]]) had developed a strongly imperial-Christian theology — Constantine as God's chosen emperor inaugurating a Christian saeculum. Augustine rejects this; the *saeculum* remains neutral pilgrim-territory.

## Disputes
- **Augustine's actual political position.** Markus 1970 *Saeculum* is the major modern argument that Augustine's mature position is anti-theocratic and *secular* in the technical sense (the *saeculum* as pilgrim time). O'Donovan 1996 and the recent post-secular wave (Milbank's *Theology and Social Theory* 1990) press a less neutral reading; Cavadini and others mediate.
- **Whether the medieval Latin appropriation is *Augustinian* or *anti-Augustinian***. The Carolingian and high-medieval Christian-empire reading is sharply at odds with Augustine's own text but historically central to medieval political theology. Markus, Lubac, Sherwin-White treat this as appropriation-against-the-text.
- **The status of the Roman Empire.** Augustine's *De Civitate Dei* I–V deflates pagan claims for Rome's eternity but is also surprisingly *appreciative* of Roman political achievement. Whether Augustine should be read as ambivalent or fundamentally critical is a perennial question.
- **Continuing relevance.** Yoder 1972, Hauerwas, Cavanaugh treat *De Civitate Dei* as a permanent template for Christian political pluralism / non-identification with the state. Critics (Coles, Bretherton) see this as theocratic-by-stealth or as politically irresponsible.
