# Pseudoarchaeology

## Definition
Pseudoarchaeology is the contemporary scholarly category for **claims about the archaeological / human past that mimic the form of scientific argument but reject or evade the methodological constraints of the discipline of archaeology**. Diagnostic features (drawing on Feder 2020, Fagan 2006, Card 2018):

1. **Anomaly-hunting and selective citation.** Accumulating apparent anomalies without engaging the substantial published literature explaining them.
2. **Visual-similarity argument across disconnected cultures.** Treating shared visual or structural features (pyramids worldwide, flood myths worldwide, sun-symbols worldwide) as evidence of common origin, while ignoring the chronological-geographical-evidential obstacles to contact.
3. **Conspiracy explanation for academic rejection.** Treating the absence of professional acceptance not as evidence against the claim but as evidence of professional suppression — the "priesthood defending consensus" framing.
4. **Citation of pre-rejected academic work as current.** Often citing 19th- or early-20th-century academic work (Hapgood, certain Mayanists, certain Egyptologists) without engaging the subsequent rejection literature.
5. **Methodological asymmetry on standards of evidence.** Demanding extreme evidence for mainstream conclusions while accepting low-evidence claims supporting alternative frameworks.
6. **Lack of peer review and disengagement from professional venues.** Publication routed through trade press, documentary, podcast, and streaming platforms rather than professional journals; engagement with professional critique reframed as further evidence of suppression.

**Pseudoarchaeology is distinct from:**
- **Failed scientific hypothesis.** Charles Hapgood's crustal-displacement was a failed hypothesis but was advanced through normal scientific channels and engaged the rejection literature (within the limits of his pre-plate-tectonic moment). See [[charles-hapgood]] for the careful distinction.
- **Speculative historiography.** Genuinely-speculative-but-method-respectful historical hypotheses (e.g., the Thera-cultural-memory-kernel reading of Plato's Atlantis, advanced by Marinatos and Galanopoulos and evaluated seriously by Aegean archaeologists) are not pseudoarchaeology even when unconfirmed.
- **Religious tradition.** Catholic veneration of Marian apparitions, Hindu pilgrimage to Ramayana sites, Mormon Book-of-Mormon archaeology of the Americas — these are *religious-cultural* claims that the discipline of archaeology can investigate but that do not become pseudoarchaeology unless they assume the disciplinary form (claiming archaeological evidence) while evading disciplinary constraints.

## Earliest attestations (of the *category* and its principal exemplars)
- **Pre-disciplinary parallels.** Before archaeology professionalized (mid-19th century), every speculative claim about the past was on roughly equal footing. The category "pseudoarchaeology" presupposes a disciplinary archaeology to be pseudo *of*. The 18th-c. "British Druids built Stonehenge" tradition (William Stukeley) is a precursor.
- **19th-c. mass-market diffusionism.** [[phase-7-033-donnelly-atlantis-antediluvian-world]] (1882) is the foundational mass-market English-language pseudoarchaeological text. Augustus Le Plongeon's late writings on Mayan Atlantis are contemporary. The "Mound Builders" myth (a vanished pre-Indian race built the North American mounds) was definitively debunked by Cyrus Thomas at the Smithsonian Bureau of Ethnology 1894 — the canonical case-study of professional archaeology defeating a popular pseudoarchaeology in real time.
- **Esoteric pseudoarchaeology.** [[phase-7-006-secret-doctrine]] and [[phase-7-034-blavatsky-atlantis-root-race]] (1888) absorb Donnelly's diffusionism into a metaphysical-esoteric framework. James Churchward's *The Lost Continent of Mu* (1926) extends the genre. Lewis Spence's Atlantis books (1924, 1925, 1942) maintain the early-20th-c. tradition.
- **Mid-20th-c. cataclysmism.** Immanuel Velikovsky's *Worlds in Collision* (1950) is the classic catastrophist pseudoarchaeology / pseudoscience hybrid; influential and contested. The "ancient astronauts" tradition (Erich von Däniken *Chariots of the Gods* 1968) takes the visual-similarity-implies-common-source method and posits extraterrestrial source.
- **Late-20th and 21st-c.** [[phase-7-036-hancock-fingerprints-of-the-gods]] (1995) and the Hancock corpus, the Netflix *Ancient Apocalypse* series (2022, 2024), the Bosnian "Pyramid" (Semir Osmanagić 2005+), and a substantial podcast-and-YouTube ecosystem.
- **The scholarly category itself.** Wauchope 1962 *Lost Tribes and Sunken Continents* is a foundational systematic critique. Williams 1991 *Fantastic Archaeology* coins the field-name. Feder 1990 (now 2020 in 10th ed.) is the standard textbook. Fagan 2006 *Archaeological Fantasies* edited volume is the field's standard collection. Card 2018 *Spooky Archaeology* and Hoopes's ongoing public critique are the current generation.

## Variants
The form is stable; the content varies:
- **Lost-continent variant.** Atlantis, Lemuria, Mu, Kumari Kandam — see [[lost-continent]].
- **Ancient-astronauts variant.** Von Däniken, Sitchin's Annunaki, *Ancient Aliens* History Channel franchise.
- **Hyperdiffusionism without extraterrestrials.** Hancock's lost civilization, Donnelly's Atlanteans, Churchward's Mu.
- **Specific-monument variants.** Bosnian "pyramids," "Egyptian temple" in the Grand Canyon, Mayan-Hindu contact theories, Norse-presence-in-pre-Columbian-America-beyond-L'Anse-aux-Meadows.
- **Religious-archaeological variants.** Book of Mormon archaeology of the Americas; Bible-prophecy archaeology (Sun-Day, Ark-on-Ararat, Sodom-located-here-or-there); Hindu-nationalist saraswati-river revival.

## Connections claimed
- **The pseudoarchaeology category is itself a connection-edge type in this Atlas.** Documents are flagged `controversial` / `tier-4` / `pseudoarchaeology` *in the body and tags* when their substantive claims fall within this category. The Atlas's posture is *investigation, not endorsement*: pseudoarchaeological documents are catalogued as cultural-historical phenomena, not as sources for actual antiquity.
- **Racialized downstream.** The hyperdiffusionist argument-form has been repeatedly mobilized for racial-political purposes — see [[lost-continent]] for the careful framing. The Smithsonian Mound-Builder case (Thomas 1894) is the canonical 19th-c. example: the "vanished superior race built the mounds" claim served, in 19th-c. US political context, to justify Indigenous dispossession by suggesting the current Native population was not the heir of the visible cultural achievement.
- **Genre-genealogy.** The Donnelly → Blavatsky → Cayce → Hancock chain is also a *pseudoarchaeology* genealogy, not only an Atlantis genealogy — the same methods (visual-similarity, conspiracy, anomaly-chain, citation of rejected academic work) appear across the chain.

## Disputes
- **Boundaries of the category.** Where the line lies between *speculative-but-method-respectful* historiography and *pseudoarchaeology* is sometimes contested. Hapgood is the test case; the Thera-cultural-memory hypothesis is another (Marinatos was a credentialed archaeologist whose specific hypothesis is unconfirmed but is not pseudoarchaeology). The Atlas adopts the working distinction: *engages-the-rejection-literature* and *operates-within-disciplinary-venues* are the key markers for non-pseudoarchaeology even when the claims are speculative or wrong.
- **Public-engagement question.** Whether mainstream archaeology should engage pseudoarchaeological claims (SAA 2022 position: yes) or ignore them (a long-standing minority view that engagement gives credibility) is a live disciplinary-strategic debate.
- **Term itself.** Some authors prefer "fringe archaeology," "alternative archaeology," or "fantastic archaeology"; "pseudoarchaeology" is the most common in current academic use and the choice the Atlas adopts.
