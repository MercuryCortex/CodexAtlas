# Ethiopic Biblical Canon (81-book Broader Canon)

## Definition
The **broader Ethiopic biblical canon** — comprising approximately 81 books in the conventional Ethiopian count (46 Old Testament + 35 New Testament) — is the most inclusive Christian scriptural canon in any historic tradition. Distinctively Ethiopian additions to the OT side include:

- **[[phase-4-081-mashafa-henok-geez-1-enoch]]** (1 Enoch) — the only Christian tradition that retains it as full scripture.
- **[[phase-3-009-jubilees]]** (the Book of Jubilees) — same.
- **[[phase-5-040-meqabyan-ethiopian-maccabees]]** (the three Books of Meqabyan) — unique to Ethiopia entirely.
- **4 Ezra (the Apocalypse of Ezra)** — among the LXX/Catholic deuterocanon in Latin, but with broader-scriptural status in Ethiopian usage.
- **The Prayer of Manasseh, the Book of Joseph ben Gurion, etc.**

Distinctively-Ethiopian NT additions include:
- **[[phase-4-065-shepherd-of-hermas]]** — the Shepherd; canonical in Ethiopia long after the West dropped it in the 4th c.
- **The Sinodos** — a four-part church-order collection.
- **The Mashafa Kidan** (*Testament of Our Lord*) — a related early-Christian text.
- **The Didascalia.**
- **The Books of the Covenant.**

The total count of "81 books" was articulated by Abba Salama III (Andreyas, metropolitan 1841–1867) and is now conventional.

## Earliest attestations

- **The translation activity inaugurated under [[frumentius]] (4th c.)** and completed under the [[the-nine-saints]] (5th–6th c.) is the foundational translation event; the canonical breadth crystallizes in the same period via monastic-liturgical use.
- **Material witness:** the [[phase-4-080-garima-gospels]] (radiocarbon-dated 330–660 CE) attest to the NT-side textual stratum.
- **The canon was never formally promulgated by a council** in Ethiopian history; it crystallized through monastic-liturgical practice rather than conciliar decree.

## Variants

- **The "narrow" canon (~46 OT + 35 NT) vs. the "broader" canon** (~54 OT + 35 NT, including the uniquely-Ethiopian additions). The distinction is liturgical-practical rather than ecclesiastically-decreed.
- **Different book-grouping conventions** produce slightly different totals across Ethiopian traditions; "81" is conventional but variants exist.

## Connections claimed

- **The Ethiopian canon as preservation-of-Second-Temple-Judaism.** Ullendorff 1968 and successors argue that the Ethiopian canon's retention of pseudepigraphical-Jewish texts (Enoch, Jubilees) reflects a deeper continuity with Second-Temple-Jewish scriptural breadth than other Christian traditions managed.
- **The Ethiopian canon as continuation-of-pre-Nicene-canon-fluidity.** Bruce 1988 and others note that pre-Nicene Christian canonical boundaries were genuinely fluid; the Ethiopian situation can be read as the preservation of a pre-Nicene state of canon-formation across a tradition that never imposed a later, narrower canonical limit.
- **The Ethiopian canon and the Qumran discoveries.** Post-1947, the Qumran fragments of 1 Enoch (4Q201–4Q212), Jubilees (4Q216–4Q224), the Book of Giants (4Q203, 4Q530–4Q533), and other works confirm the Ge'ez Ethiopic textual tradition as a faithful witness to genuine Second-Temple-Jewish originals.
