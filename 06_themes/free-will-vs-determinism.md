# Free Will vs Determinism

## Definition

Are human actions **free** in a robust sense — genuinely up to the agent, such that the agent could have done otherwise — or are they **determined** by prior conditions: a divine decree, a cosmic fate, an unbroken causal series, the momentum of past deeds, or the agent's own settled character? Every tradition that holds *both* that the order of things is fixed *and* that persons are accountable is forced onto this field, and the moves available on it turn out to be remarkably few.

The three classical positions:

- **Hard determinism** — every action is necessitated by prior causes; libertarian freedom is an illusion, and (on the harsher versions) so is desert.
- **Libertarianism** — at least some actions are genuinely undetermined by prior causes; the agent is an originating source.
- **Compatibilism** — determinism may well be true, but "free" is redefined so that it survives: an act is free when it flows from the agent's own character and assent without external compulsion, whether or not the agent could have done otherwise.

**This node is one half of a pair.** Its sister, [[providence-and-fate]], holds the cross-tradition spread of the *governance and destiny* concepts themselves — the Moirai and the Norns, Stoic *heimarmenē*, deified Time, karma-as-allotment, Quranic *qadar*, the Christian decree — and the great reconciling syntheses. **This** node holds the dispute about the **will**: what its freedom would consist in, who argued what, and which of the resemblances across traditions are genuine chains and which are independent arrivals at the same small menu.

## When was "free will" invented?

A caution that governs the whole node. The *phrase* "free will" translates a family of terms that are not interchangeable — Greek *to eph' hēmin* ("what is up to us"), *autexousion* ("self-determination"), Latin *liberum arbitrium* ("free judgement"), Arabic *qadar* (measure, decree) and *istiṭāʿa* (capacity), Sanskrit *puruṣakāra* (human effort) against *daiva* (destiny), Chinese *ming* (allotment). They carve the field differently.

Susanne Bobzien (1998) argues that the free-will problem *in its recognizable modern form* — a metaphysical worry about whether universal causal determination is compatible with the ability to do otherwise — is a **late** arrival, crystallizing around Alexander of Aphrodisias (c. 200 CE) rather than in Aristotle or the early Stoa. Michael Frede (2011) locates the notion earlier, in Epictetus. The dispute is live and it matters here: **the Atlas should not read a 17th-century problem back into a 4th-century BCE text.** What is genuinely cross-cultural is the *pressure* — fixed order plus accountability — and the recurring set of answers, not a single shared concept.

## The Greek settlement — and why compatibilism keeps being reinvented

The Hellenistic schools produce the first fully explicit version of the argument, and the moves they make are the moves everyone makes afterwards.

**Epicurus** takes the libertarian route through physics. In a world of atoms falling through the void, everything would be fixed; so the atoms are given a minimal, uncaused *swerve*. Lucretius makes the purpose explicit — the swerve is what breaks "the decrees of fate" and makes *libera voluntas* possible — and Cicero, in *De fato*, ridicules it as an uncaused cause invented to order.

**Chrysippus** takes the compatibilist route, and does it so well that the argument has scarcely been improved on. Fate is a complete and unbroken causal series; nevertheless assent is *eph' hēmin*, up to us. The illustration, preserved by Cicero (*De fato* 41–43) and Aulus Gellius (*Noctes Atticae* VII.2), is the **cylinder and the cone**: an external push starts each rolling, but *how* each rolls is settled by its own shape. The push is fate; the shape is the agent's character; the rolling is the act, and it is the agent's own. Against the "lazy argument" — if all is fated, why call the doctor? — Chrysippus answers that the recovery and the calling of the doctor are *co-fated*: fate does not route around your deliberation, it routes *through* it.

That the Stoic answer recurs — in Ashʿarite *kasb*, in Aquinas's "God moves the will according to its nature," in Hobbes and Hume, in Frankfurt's severing of responsibility from alternate possibilities — is the strongest evidence on this node that the menu of solutions is short. Some of those recurrences are transmitted (Greek philosophy reached kalām and scholasticism alike through the Arabic translation movement); the Buddhist and Confucian versions are not.

## The Latin controversy — the one place this becomes a boundary of orthodoxy

Around 411 the ascetic teacher **Pelagius** and his associate Caelestius are accused at Carthage of denying that grace is necessary for the will's good acts. Pelagius's position is straightforwardly libertarian and moral: God does not command the impossible, so the human will must be able to obey. **Augustine**, who in his early *De libero arbitrio* had defended free choice against the Manichaeans, spends his last two decades arguing the opposite way — that the post-lapsarian will is *bound*, that it cannot even begin to will the good without prevenient grace, and (in *De praedestinatione sanctorum*, 429) that the elect are chosen before any merit of theirs. Pelagianism is condemned at Carthage in 418 and again at Ephesus in 431; the milder "semi-Pelagian" position of John Cassian is rejected at the Council of Orange in 529, which nonetheless also rejects any predestination to evil.

This settlement is a **transmission**, and it is easy to trace because the parties cite each other by name:

- **Boethius** (*Consolation* V, c. 524) supplies the reconciliation that the medieval schools run on: God does not foresee *in advance*, God sees all moments *at once* (*totum simul*), and a timeless knower necessitates nothing.
- **Aquinas** (*Summa theologiae* I q.83; I-II q.10) holds that God as first cause moves the will *according to its nature* — and since the will's nature is to be free, divine causation produces rather than destroys the freedom.
- **Erasmus** publishes *De libero arbitrio* in 1524; **Luther** answers in 1525 with *De servo arbitrio*, which he later called one of the few of his books worth keeping. The will, for Luther, is a beast ridden either by God or by Satan, with no third option.
- **Calvin** (*Institutes* III.21–24) formalizes double predestination: election and reprobation both by sovereign decree, both prior to any foreseen merit.
- **Luis de Molina**'s *Concordia* (1588) offers the Jesuit counter-move: *scientia media*, "middle knowledge" — God knows, prior to any decree, what any possible free creature *would* freely do in any possible circumstance, and providence works by actualizing the circumstances. The resulting **De auxiliis** controversy between Dominicans and Jesuits ran from 1598 to 1607 and was closed by Paul V without a verdict: each side was forbidden to call the other heretical. It remains undecided.
- The Reformed camp has its own version of the same split: the Remonstrants after Arminius are condemned at the **Synod of Dort** (1618–19), and Cornelius Jansen's posthumous *Augustinus* (1640) — a maximally Augustinian reading — is condemned by Innocent X in 1653.

Every one of these is a **Transmission** edge. They are one argument, conducted in one textual tradition, over twelve centuries.

## The Islamic controversy — a genuinely parallel formation

The *qadar* dispute begins in the Umayyad period, before any significant Greek philosophical influence, out of a scriptural tension: the Qurʾān both insists that God decrees all things and holds human beings answerable at judgement. The early **Qadariyya** affirm human power over acts; the **Jabriyya** affirm compulsion.

The **Muʿtazila** — "the people of justice and unity" — argue that divine *justice* requires that humans genuinely *create* their own acts (*khalq al-afʿāl*): a God who created the sin and then punished it would not be just. **Al-Ashʿarī** (d. 935), a Muʿtazilite who defected, produces the settlement that becomes Sunni majority doctrine: God creates every act, and the human being *acquires* it (*kasb* / *iktisāb*); responsibility attaches to the acquisition. **Al-Māturīdī** (d. 944) offers a variant with somewhat more room for the agent.

Two observations the vault should keep straight:

1. ***Kasb* is functionally a compatibilism** — and it is the same structural move as Chrysippus's, reached inside kalām on scriptural grounds. The two worlds are not wholly insulated (the Arabic translation movement carried Greek philosophy into Baghdad from the 8th century, and the falāsifa read Aristotle and the Neoplatonists closely), but the *qadar* dispute predates that influence and is driven by Qurʾānic exegesis. The right label is **parallel formation with partially shared vocabulary**, not a chain — and certainly not "Islam borrowed compatibilism from the Stoics."
2. **Occasionalism is the extreme end.** Al-Ghazālī's seventeenth discussion in the *Tahāfut al-falāsifa* denies that fire has any power to burn: what we call causation is God's habitual recreation of the world's accidents at every instant. This is a determinism far stronger than anything in the Latin debate — not merely that acts are decreed, but that nothing created has causal power at all. Ibn Rushd's *Tahāfut al-tahāfut* is the Aristotelian reply, and the exchange reaches the Latin West in translation.

## The Indic and Chinese debates — the strongest evidence for convergence

These are the rows on the grid with **no channel whatsoever** to the Mediterranean argument, and they are the reason this theme is flagged as a convergence rather than a diffusion.

**India, 5th–4th c. BCE.** Makkhali Gosāla's Ājīvika doctrine of *niyati* is a hard determinism of a purity rarely matched anywhere: there is no cause of defilement or purification, effort and human strength are nothing, and every being runs a fixed course of transmigrations, released automatically at its end. Both of the surviving śramaṇa traditions define themselves against it. **Jainism** relocates the determinism *inside* the agent — karma is subtle matter that the *jīva* binds to itself by its own acts, so the causal order is total and the responsibility is total at once. **Buddhism** takes the middle position explicitly: at *Aṅguttara Nikāya* 3.61 the Buddha rejects past-action determinism, creator-god determination, and pure acausality *together*, on the ground that each of the three leaves no reason to act. Conditioned arising is conditionality, not necessitation. And the **Bhagavad Gītā** states the antinomy in two verses without blinking: the Lord "whirls all beings, mounted as if on a machine, by his *māyā*" (18.61) — and then, four verses later, "having reflected on this fully, do as you wish" (18.63).

**China.** The seam runs differently. *Ming* (命) is the allotment or decree that fixes **outcomes** — lifespan, station, success — while moral effort remains entirely the agent's. Confucius: at fifty he knew the decree of Heaven (*Analects* 2.4), and without knowing *ming* one cannot be a *junzi* (20.3); the point of knowing it is to act rightly *regardless* of whether it succeeds. **Mozi** attacks fatalism head-on in the three "Fei ming" (Against Fatalism) chapters — not on metaphysical grounds but on consequential ones: the doctrine that outcomes are fixed makes rulers lazy and the people miserable, so it must be rejected. **Zhuangzi** goes the other way, counselling rest in what cannot be altered. The Neo-Confucians recast the problem again: for [[zhu-xi]] the moral nature (*li*) is universal and identical in all, obscured in each by the turbidity of one's *qi*, so self-cultivation is the reform of one's own material endowment; for [[wang-yangming]] innate moral knowing (*liangzhi*) is always already present and knowledge and action are one.

## MASSIVE-WIN — the convergence on compatibilism

The finding this node carries is not that one tradition taught another. It is that **at least five mutually unconnected traditions, facing the same pressure, invented the same middle answer.**

- **Chrysippus** (c. 240 BCE, Athens): fate pushes, the agent's own character determines the roll; assent is up to us.
- **The Buddha's middle position** (AN 3.61): conditions shape but do not necessitate; a doctrine that removes the point of acting is refuted by that fact alone.
- **Rabbi Akiva** (*Avot* 3:15): all is foreseen, and choice is given — the antinomy asserted rather than resolved.
- **Al-Ashʿarī** (10th c., Baghdad): God creates the act, the servant acquires it, and the acquisition is what is judged.
- **Confucian *ming***: the outcome is decreed, the effort is yours, and the *junzi* acts rightly without regard to the outcome.

The Athenian, Baghdadi and Latin versions of this move are partly connected — Greek philosophical vocabulary reached both kalām and scholasticism. The Indic and Chinese versions are not connected to any of them. What recurs is the **shape of the solution**: relocate freedom from the *origination* of the act to the *ownership* of it. The convergence is on the problem's structure and on the small number of ways out — the same combination of inheritance, diffusion and independent arrival the Atlas documents for [[providence-and-fate]] and [[chaoskampf]], and it earns the MASSIVE-WIN tag for the same reason: maximum span, honestly labelled link by link.

## The modern turn

The theological argument becomes a scientific one. **Spinoza** (*Ethics* II P48) denies any absolute or free will and compares the human sense of freedom to a stone that, thrown, believes it flies of its own accord. **Leibniz** softens this with *inclinant, non necessitant* — motives incline the will without necessitating it. **Laplace** (1814) states the mechanical picture in its cleanest form: an intelligence knowing every force and position would find the future as certain as the past. **Kant** relocates freedom out of the phenomenal order entirely, as a condition of practical reason rather than an event in nature. **Hume** and **Hobbes** recover the Chrysippean move in English.

In the 20th century, **Harry Frankfurt** (1969) severed moral responsibility from the ability to do otherwise, reviving compatibilism as the majority analytic position; the alternative camps — hard incompatibilism, event-causal and agent-causal libertarianism — are surveyed in Kane's *Oxford Handbook*. **Benjamin Libet**'s 1983 readiness-potential experiments, which appeared to show brain activity preceding the conscious decision to move, moved the argument into neuroscience; the interpretation is heavily contested (Schurger and colleagues re-read the readiness potential as stochastic accumulation rather than a decision signal), and the Atlas records the experiment as a live dispute rather than a result.

## Disputes

- **When the problem was born.** Bobzien 1998 dates the recognizable free-will problem to c. 200 CE (Alexander of Aphrodisias); Frede 2011 finds it already in Epictetus. Neither reads it back into Aristotle. Any Atlas claim that a pre-Hellenistic text "addresses free will" should be treated as anachronism until argued.
- **Was Pelagius a Pelagian?** The surviving Pelagian corpus is fragmentary and largely preserved through hostile quotation. Much of what "Pelagianism" names was systematized by Caelestius and by Augustine's polemic rather than asserted by Pelagius himself.
- **Is *kasb* a real solution or a verbal one?** Ashʿarite *kasb* has been criticized from within Islamic thought — most famously by the Muʿtazila and later by Ibn Taymiyya from another direction — as relabelling the problem rather than solving it. The same objection is made against Chrysippus's cylinder and against Frankfurt.
- **Does the Boethian move work?** Whether timeless knowledge genuinely avoids necessitation, or merely relocates the difficulty, has been disputed since the Middle Ages and is still argued in analytic philosophy of religion.
- **Is karma a determinism at all?** Because karma is generated by the agent's own prior acts, many interpreters insist it is the opposite of fatalism — a doctrine of radical responsibility. The Atlas lists it as an answer to the same structural problem while flagging the disanalogy. See [[providence-and-fate]], which makes the same qualification.
- **The neuroscience.** Whether pre-conscious neural activity bears at all on the metaphysical question is disputed on both empirical and conceptual grounds. Treat any "neuroscience has disproved free will" claim as contested.
