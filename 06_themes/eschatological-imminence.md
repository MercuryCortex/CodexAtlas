# Eschatological Imminence

The conviction that the end of the present age — judgment, resurrection, the in-breaking of God's kingdom — is **imminent**, expected within the speaker's generation. Distinct from the broader [[apocalyptic-revelation]] theme: imminence is the temporal claim that the eschaton is *near*, not merely that it will eventually arrive. The motif is diagnostic for Second Temple apocalyptic Judaism and earliest Christianity; later traditions either re-interpret the delay (Augustine, [[realized-eschatology]]) or recurrently revive the expectation (medieval Joachimism, Anabaptist Münster, Millerism, modern dispensationalism).

- [[phase-3-016-gospel-of-mark]] — "this generation will not pass away until all these things take place" (Mk 13:30); the temple-destruction generation as eschatological hinge.
- [[phase-3-015-pauline-epistles]] — 1 Thess 4:15–17 expects to be among the living at the parousia; 1 Cor 7:29 "the appointed time has grown short."
- [[phase-3-014-q-source]] — the proclamation "the kingdom of God has come near."
- [[phase-3-011-dead-sea-scrolls]] — War Scroll and Pesharim read prophetic texts as referring to the present, eschatologically-charged community.
- [[phase-3-004-1-enoch]] — the Animal Apocalypse and Apocalypse of Weeks place the writers in the final week.

Scholars debate how to handle the "delay of the parousia" — Schweitzer foregrounded it as the central interpretive problem; Dodd's [[realized-eschatology]] proposed the kingdom had already arrived in Jesus' ministry; Ehrman and Sanders defend the imminent-prophet reading as historically primary.
