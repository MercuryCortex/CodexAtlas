# Bhakti (Devotion)

## Definition

*Bhakti* — from the root √*bhaj-*, "to divide, to share, to partake in, to be devoted to" — is loving, personal, frequently passionate devotion of a worshipper to a chosen deity (*iṣṭa-devatā*). The etymology already carries the doctrine: bhakti is **participation**, a share taken in the deity, not merely an attitude held toward one.

As a soteriological category it stands alongside two others that the *Bhagavad Gītā* names explicitly and then subordinates to it: *karma-yoga*, the path of disinterested action, and *jñāna-yoga*, the path of knowledge. Bhakti's distinguishing commitments are three:

1. **The relation is real and irreducible.** Devotee and Lord are genuinely two, and remain so. This is a deliberate refusal of the Advaita position that the apparent distinction dissolves in realization — which is why bhakti theology and [[non-duality]] pull against each other for a thousand years, and why Rāmānuja and Madhva write commentaries against Śaṅkara.
2. **Emotion is epistemology.** *Bhāva* (mood) and *rasa* (savoured emotional essence) are not decoration on a cognitive path; they are the organ of religious knowing. Longing, and specifically longing-in-separation (*viraha*), gets closer to God than argument does.
3. **Access is not qualified by birth.** The path is open where Vedic ritual and Sanskrit learning are closed — the *Gītā* says so in as many words (9.32), and the poet-saints prove it: women, śūdras, untouchables, Muslims by origin.

**Scope note.** [[tradition-hinduism]] carries the historical narrative of the bhakti turn within the Hindu family, and [[event-bhakti-movement-c-700-1700]] carries the movement as an event. **This node carries the category** — what bhakti claims, how it was theorized, how it divides internally, and which of its cross-tradition cousins are related and which merely resemble.

## The textual spine

- **[[phase-2-021-shvetashvatara-upanishad]] 6.23** — the single verse in the older Upaniṣads to use *bhakti* in an unmistakably theistic sense ("for one who has the highest devotion to God, and to the teacher as to God, the matters declared here shine forth"). It is late, and often flagged as a possible addition, but it is the earliest anchor for the word in the sense this node uses.
- **[[phase-2-027-bhagavad-gita]]** — the fountainhead. Krishna teaches Arjuna that *bhakti-yoga* surpasses ritual and knowledge; that a leaf, a flower, a fruit, water offered with devotion is accepted (9.26); that those of any birth reach the highest goal by taking refuge in him (9.32); and, in the verse the Śrīvaiṣṇava tradition calls the *carama śloka*, "abandoning all dharmas, come to me alone for refuge; I will free you from all evils, do not grieve" (18.66). That verse becomes the scriptural charge of the entire theology of surrender.
- **[[phase-3-024-natyashastra]]** — not a devotional text at all, but load-bearing: Bharata's aesthetics of *rasa* supply the technical vocabulary that Gauḍīya theology will later convert into a soteriology.
- **Tamil canons, 6th–9th c.** — the twelve Vaiṣṇava **Āḻvārs** (including [[andal]], the one woman among them, and [[nammalvar]]), compiled as the *Nālāyira Divya Prabandham*; and the sixty-three Śaiva **Nāyaṉmārs** ([[tamil-three-nayanars]]), whose *Tēvāram* hymns enter the *Tirumurai*. Hardy 1983 argues that the *viraha-bhakti* of these poets — love-in-separation, sung in the vernacular, sung in the temple — is the matrix from which all later Krishna-devotion grows.
- **[[phase-5-001-devi-mahatmya]]** (c. 550–600 CE) — establishes the warrior-goddess [[durga]] as a supreme object of devotion, and with her the whole Śākta devotional stream that runs down to [[ramprasad-sen]]'s 18th-century Bengali songs to Kālī.
- **[[phase-5-050-bhagavata-purana]]** (c. 850–950, most likely South Indian) — after the *Gītā*, the most influential bhakti text ever written. Its tenth book, on Krishna's childhood and the *rāsa-līlā* with the *gopīs*, is the source-material for five centuries of poetry, painting, drama and dance.
- **The commentarial systematizations** — [[phase-5-016-ramanuja-sribhasya]] and [[phase-5-022-madhva-brahma-sutra-bhasya]] (below).
- **The vernacular corpora, 12th–17th c.** — [[basava]]'s Kannada *vacanas*; [[kabir]]'s Hindi *sākhīs* and *pads* ([[phase-6-031-bijak-kabir]]); [[mirabai]]'s Rajasthani *padas* ([[phase-6-013-mirabai-bhajans]]); [[surdas]]'s Braj *Sūrsāgar*; [[tulsidas]]'s Awadhi *Rāmcaritmānas* ([[phase-6-019-tulsidas-ramcharitmanas]]); the *Caitanya-caritāmṛta* ([[phase-6-020-chaitanya-charitamrita]]); and, in Marathi, the Vārkarī line of Jñāneśvar, Nāmdev, Ekanāth and Tukārām singing to Viṭṭhal at Pandharpur.

## The internal theology — where bhakti divides against itself

Bhakti is not one position. Its major fault lines are as consequential as the differences between Christian confessions, and the Atlas should not flatten them.

**Against Advaita: how much difference survives?** [[ramanuja]] (trad. 1017–1137) answers with *viśiṣṭādvaita*, "qualified non-dualism": the world and souls are the *body* of Brahman, really distinct as attributes are distinct from a substance, so the devotee never dissolves. [[madhva]] (1238–1317) goes further with *dvaita*: five eternal differences, God and soul never one, and — unusually for a Hindu system — a class of souls eternally unfit for liberation. [[nimbarka]] takes *dvaitādvaita* (difference-and-non-difference), [[vallabhacharya]] takes *śuddhādvaita* (pure non-dualism, in which the world is real and is Krishna's play), and the Gauḍīyas take *acintya-bhedābheda*, "inconceivable difference-and-non-difference" — a position that declines to resolve the paradox on principle. Each of these is a different answer to the same question: how do you keep the beloved from vanishing into the lover?

**Saguṇa and nirguṇa.** *Saguṇa* bhakti loves God *with* form and attributes — Krishna, Rāma, Śiva, Devī, with iconography, temple, festival and myth. *Nirguṇa* bhakti loves a formless divine without attributes, and is characteristically hostile to image, temple and priest: this is [[kabir]]'s register, and the Sant stream generally ([[tradition-bhakti-sant]]). Both are bhakti; both are emotional; they differ on whether the beloved can be depicted.

**Grace and effort — the Indian version of a very familiar argument.** After Rāmānuja, the Śrīvaiṣṇava community split over *prapatti* (surrender). The **Vaṭakalai** ("northern") school held the *markaṭa-nyāya*, the "monkey-hold": the infant monkey must cling to its mother, so the devotee must do something. The **Teṅkalai** ("southern") school held the *mārjāra-nyāya*, the "cat-hold": the kitten is carried by the scruff and does nothing at all, and so it is with grace. This is precisely the synergism-versus-monergism axis of the Latin grace controversies — arrived at independently, and settled no more decisively.

**Rasa as method.** Rūpa Gosvāmī's *Bhaktirasāmṛtasindhu* (16th c.) systematizes Gauḍīya devotion as a graded set of moods (*bhāva*) toward Krishna: *śānta* (peaceful), *dāsya* (servant), *sakhya* (friend), *vātsalya* (parental), *mādhurya* (amorous) — ascending in intimacy, with *mādhurya*, the gopīs' mode, at the summit. The devotee cultivates a chosen mood deliberately, following the model of one of Krishna's eternal companions, and thereby comes to *taste* (*rasa*) devotion itself. This is Bharata's dramatic aesthetics converted wholesale into a technique of salvation — see [[music-natya-shastra-rasa-system]] and Haberman 1988.

**Practice.** *Nāma-japa* and *nāma-saṃkīrtana* — the divine name repeated, and sung in call-and-response with drum and cymbal ([[kirtan-call-response-bhakti]]) — are bhakti's characteristic techniques, and their public, collective, ecstatic form is what made Caitanya's movement visible on the streets of Bengal.

## MASSIVE-WIN — the devotional-grace family, and its limits

Set the world's soteriologies side by side and three families emerge: **gnostic-realization** soteriologies (liberation by knowing — Advaita, Gnostic Christianity, Madhyamaka in one reading), **legal-ritual** soteriologies (liberation by right performance — Vedic ritualism, halakhic and sharia observance in their maximal forms), and **devotional-grace** soteriologies, in which the decisive movement is made by the deity and received by the devotee. Bhakti is the largest and most fully theorized member of the third family, and it has cousins that reached the same place with no contact whatsoever.

| Tradition | Term | Salvific agency | Characteristic move | Kind of claim |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hindu (Śrīvaiṣṇava) | *bhakti* / *prapatti* | the Lord, received by surrender | cat-hold vs monkey-hold | the node itself |
| Hindu (Gauḍīya) | *rāgānugā bhakti* | Krishna's grace, cultivated by mood | rasa aesthetics as soteriology | the node itself |
| Japanese Pure Land | *tariki* ("other-power") | Amida's vow; the *nembutsu* | abandon self-power entirely | **independent** — [[doctrine-pure-land-faith]]; [[shinran]] |
| Lutheran / Reformed | *sola gratia*, *sola fide* | God's unmerited election | works excluded from justification | **independent** — [[justification-by-faith]] |
| Sufi | *ʿishq*, *maḥabba*, *fanāʾ* | God's self-disclosure; the lover annihilated | love as knowledge; separation as the path | **shared milieu in North India** — [[fanaa-annihilation]]; [[tradition-sufism]] |
| Christian bridal mysticism | *sponsa Christi* | the Bridegroom's initiative | erotic figure for union | **independent** — [[bridal-mysticism]]; [[soul-as-bride]] |

**What the Atlas is and is not claiming.** The Pure Land and Reformation rows are **parallels**: Shinran (1173–1263) and Luther (1483–1546) had no channel to Rāmānuja or to each other, and Rudolf Otto's 1930 three-way comparison — the founding text of this whole discussion — is a *comparison*, not a genealogy. The Sufi row is different and needs stating carefully: bhakti is attested in Tamil three centuries before any significant Sufi presence in India, so **bhakti does not come from Sufism**; but from the Sultanate period onward North Indian bhakti and Chishtī Sufism occupied one social, linguistic and poetic field, and Burchett 2019 argues that bhakti's turn against tantric and magical religion was shaped in that encounter. The right edge type there is **shared milieu**, and the influence runs both ways.

What makes the convergence worth the MASSIVE-WIN tag is not that the traditions agree — it is that **they generate the same internal argument**. Once you say salvation comes by grace, you are immediately asked whether the recipient contributes anything, and the answer splits your community. Śrīvaiṣṇavas split over the kitten and the monkey; Latin Christians split over Pelagius, then over Erasmus and Luther, then over Molina; Pure Land split over whether a single utterance of the *nembutsu* suffices or many are needed. Three traditions, no contact, the same fracture — see [[free-will-vs-determinism]], where the same axis is worked through in its analytic form.

## Bhakti and caste — what the evidence supports

Bhakti's social record is genuinely radical and genuinely uneven, and both halves have to be said.

The radical half is not in doubt: the saints include weavers ([[kabir]]), leatherworkers (Ravidās), women of every rank from a Rajput princess ([[mirabai]]) to Tamil temple-singers ([[andal]]), and a Brahmin minister who organized a cross-caste assembly and paid for it politically ([[basava]]). They composed in Kannada, Tamil, Marathi, Braj, Awadhi, Bengali and Hindi rather than Sanskrit — which is itself the argument, since it removes the linguistic gatekeeping on which ritual authority rested. Novetzke 2016 treats this vernacularization as a political act constituting a premodern public.

The qualification is equally documented. Many bhakti communities re-institutionalized within a generation or two, acquiring their own hereditary lineages, temple economies and caste hierarchies; some *saguṇa* traditions were never anti-caste in the first place; and the low-caste saints were frequently absorbed as exemplary devotees without their social critique surviving the absorption. Hawley 2015 further warns that the picture of a single egalitarian "bhakti movement" sweeping India owes a great deal to 20th-century Hindi literary historiography and to the nationalist need for an indigenous reformation. The saints are historical. The movement is partly a frame.

## Disputes

- **Where bhakti began.** The older Indological consensus (Bhandarkar, Dasgupta) placed the origin in the *Gītā*. Hardy 1983 relocated *viraha-bhakti* to the Tamil south and read the *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* as the first northern, Sanskrit absorption of a southern vernacular phenomenon. Hardy's case is now the mainstream position; the *Gītā*'s priority as the first *theological* articulation is not thereby cancelled, and the two claims are often conflated.
- **Was there a "bhakti movement" at all?** Hawley 2015 is the sharp version of the doubt: the four-*sampradāya* scheme, the pan-Indian narrative, and the very phrase are substantially retrospective. Prentiss 1999 dissolves the problem differently, treating bhakti as a mode of embodied theology rather than a movement to be dated.
- **How much did Sufism shape it?** Burchett 2019 makes the strongest case for a formative encounter in North India; earlier scholarship ranged from denial to derivation. Neither pole is defensible: the chronology rules out derivation, and the shared poetic vocabulary of the Sultanate period rules out insulation.
- **Corpus attribution.** The Mīrā, Kabīr and Sūrdās corpora all grew by accretion; a large fraction of what circulates under each name is later. Any claim resting on a specific poem's authorship needs the recension named.
- **Is bhakti compatible with Advaita?** Śaṅkara's tradition makes room for devotion as a preparatory discipline for the unqualified; bhakti theologians reply that a preparatory devotion is not devotion at all, since it is designed to be superseded. The argument is nine centuries old and unresolved. See [[brahman-impersonal]] and [[tradition-advaita-vedanta]].
