# Bhakti (Devotion)

*Bhakti* (Sanskrit *bhaj-* "to share, partake in, be devoted to") is loving, personal, often passionately emotional devotion of the worshipper to a chosen deity (*iṣṭa-devatā*). It contrasts with *karma-yoga* (path of works) and *jñāna-yoga* (path of knowledge) as a third soteriological path; the *Bhagavad Gītā* makes the three explicit and elevates *bhakti* as supreme. Bhakti theologies typically affirm a real, irreducible relation between devotee and Lord (against monist Advaita absorption), and they typically valorize emotional / aesthetic states (*rasa*, *bhāva*) as religious epistemology.

## Manifestations
- **Bhagavad Gītā:** [[phase-2-017-mahabharata-ramayana-oral-layers]] — the *Gītā* (within the *Mahābhārata*, redacted c. 2nd c. BCE – 2nd c. CE) is the central textual fountainhead. Krishna teaches Arjuna that *bhakti-yoga* — loving devotion to Him — is the path open to all castes and genders, surpassing Vedic ritual and philosophical knowledge.
- **Tamil Alvars and Nayanmars** (6th–9th c.). The Alvars (12 Vaiṣṇava poet-saints, including Andal) and Nayanmars (63 Śaiva saints) inaugurate emotional-vernacular bhakti in Tamil; their hymns are canonized as the *Divya Prabandham* and *Tēvāram*. Hardy 1983 argues this Tamil *viraha-bhakti* (love-in-separation) is the matrix from which all later Krishna-bhakti grows.
- **Theological systematization:** [[phase-5-016-ramanuja-sribhasya]] — Rāmānuja (11th–12th c.) gives Śrī Vaiṣṇavism its theological backbone: a *qualified non-dualism* (*viśiṣṭādvaita*) that preserves the devotee-Lord relation against Śaṅkaran absorption. [[phase-5-022-madhva-brahma-sutra-bhasya]] — Madhva (13th c.) goes further into dualism (*dvaita*), maximally preserving the devotee-Lord distinction.
- **Goddess bhakti:** [[phase-5-001-devi-mahatmya]] — the *Devī Māhātmya* establishes the warrior-goddess Durgā as supreme object of devotion in Śākta traditions.
- **Vernacular North-Indian bhakti, 15th–17th c.:** the Sant tradition (Kabīr, Ravidās), *saguṇa* (with-form) bhakti to Krishna and Rāma, and *nirguṇa* (without-form) bhakti to the formless divine, coexist and cross-pollinate.
- **Mirabai:** [[phase-6-013-mirabai-bhajans]] — the Rajasthani princess-poet (~1498–1547) whose *padas* of erotic-mystical longing for Krishna are among the most beloved devotional songs in North India.
- **Tulsidas:** [[phase-6-019-tulsidas-ramcharitmanas]] — Tulsidas (1532–1623) writes the *Rāmcaritmānas* in Awadhi Hindi, making the Sanskrit *Rāmāyaṇa*'s Rama-bhakti accessible to a vernacular audience; the most influential devotional text in North India for four centuries.
- **Chaitanya and Gauḍīya Vaiṣṇavism:** [[phase-6-020-chaitanya-charitamrita]] — Caitanya (1486–1534) inaugurates the Bengali Krishna-bhakti movement; the *Caitanya-caritāmṛta* (Krishnadāsa Kavirāja, c. 1615) is its theological-biographical capstone. Gauḍīya theology systematizes the *rasa* (mood) theory of bhakti — *śānta* / *dāsya* / *sakhya* / *vātsalya* / *mādhurya* (peaceful / servant / friend / parental / amorous) as ascending modes of devotional intimacy.

## Disputes
- **Dating of bhakti's "origin."** Older Indological consensus (Bhandarkar, Dasgupta) placed bhakti's origin in the *Gītā*; Hardy 1983 decisively shifts the *viraha-bhakti* origin south, to Tamil-medium religion. The *Gītā* on Hardy's reading is the *first northern* / Sanskrit absorption of an originally southern *vernacular*-Tamil phenomenon.
- **Bhakti and caste.** Bhakti movements often (not always) had egalitarian and anti-Brahmin tendencies: low-caste saints, women saints, vernacular language, anti-ritualism. The "social radicalism" of bhakti has been variously emphasized (Hawley) and qualified (Burchett).
- **Comparative valence.** Bhakti's structural similarity to Christian *agape*, Sufi *ʿishq*, and Pure Land Buddhist *tariki* has been noted from Otto onward — a cross-tradition family of *devotional-grace* soteriologies, distinct from *gnostic-realization* soteriologies on one side and *legal-ritual* soteriologies on the other.
