# Memra

## Identity

**Memra** (Aramaic **מֵימְרָא**, *mêmrā*, "word, utterance"; from the root *ʾ-m-r*, "to say") is the periphrasis "**the Memra of the LORD**" that the Aramaic Targums — the synagogue paraphrase-translations of the Hebrew Bible — repeatedly substitute for the divine name, or for a too-bluntly anthropomorphic action of God, in their rendering of the biblical text. Where the Masoretic Hebrew says that *YHWH* did, said, created, or appeared, the Targum will frequently say that the *Memra of the LORD* did so. The construction is not a translation of any single Hebrew word; it is an interpretive insertion, one of a small family of Targumic reverential buffer-terms alongside *Yeqara* ("Glory") and [[shekhinah|*Shekhinta*]] ("Presence"). In the Codex Atlas, Memra is filed as the Jewish-Aramaic member of the [[logos-cosmic-reason|Logos]] complex: the term that stands, linguistically and conceptually, between the Hebrew Bible's *dabar* ("word of the LORD") and *ḥokhmah* ([[hokmah-hebrew|Wisdom]]) on one side and the Greek *Logos* of the [[logos-johannine|Johannine prologue]] on the other.

What is genuinely contested — and what makes Memra interesting rather than merely lexical — is *how much weight the term carries*. Is the Memra a mere stylistic device for avoiding anthropomorphism, a way of not saying "God" did something embarrassingly physical? Or is it a real theological mediator, a quasi-hypostasis: the Word of God as an agent distinct enough to act, yet not a second god? The whole cross-tradition payload of the node turns on that question (see Disputes).

## In the Targums

The Memra appears across the Aramaic Targum corpus, most densely in the **Palestinian Targums** (Targum Neofiti, the Fragment-Targums, Targum Pseudo-Jonathan) and present, more sparingly and with more reserve, in the official Babylonian Targum Onkelos. A representative sample of the usage:

- **At creation.** Targum Neofiti to **Genesis 1** has God create *by* his Memra: "By the Memra of the LORD the heavens were made" — folding the creation-by-word of the Priestly "and God said" into a standing agent, the Word.
- **In revelation and presence.** "The **Memra of the LORD** called to Adam" (Targum to **Gen 3:9**) where the Hebrew has simply "YHWH called." "I will set my **Memra** as a covenant between me and you" recasts the covenant formulae; the Memra is what is present *with* the patriarchs — "the Memra of the LORD was at his aid" (Targum to the Joseph and Exodus narratives).
- **As the object of faith and the agent of salvation and judgment.** The Targums speak of Israel "believing in the **Memra** of the LORD" (cf. the Targumic renderings of **Exod 14:31**, where the Hebrew has the people believing "in YHWH and in Moses his servant"), and of the Memra as the power that redeems at the Sea and that fights against the wicked.

The mechanism is consistent: wherever the unmediated divine name or a corporeal divine act would stand exposed in the Hebrew, the Targum interposes the Word. The same impulse produces the Glory (*Yeqara*) for what is *seen* of God and the [[shekhinah|Presence]] (*Shekhinta*) for where God *dwells*; Memra covers what God *says and does*. Michael Klein's studies of Targumic translation technique treat the three together as the standard anti-anthropomorphic apparatus of the meturgeman.

## Cross-tradition significance (the Jewish bridge to the Logos)

Memra sits at the hinge of one of the most consequential transmission problems in the Logos complex: **does the Word-theology of John 1 come out of Judaism, or out of Hellenistic philosophy?** The Atlas wires Memra to [[logos-cosmic-reason]] (the parent Logos theme), to [[logos-philonic]] (the Hellenistic-Jewish cousin), and above all to [[logos-johannine]] (the disputed daughter).

There are two well-defined positions, and they should not be collapsed:

1. **The deflationary / "buffer-word" reading (George Foot Moore, 1922).** The Memra is *not* a being, not an intermediary, not a hypostasis. It is a pious circumlocution — a verbal screen the Targumists drew across the bald anthropomorphisms of scripture, exactly as *Shekhinta* and *Yeqara* are. On this view the apparent parallel to the Logos is a mirage produced by Christian apologists eager to find the Word already in the synagogue; "the Memra of the LORD" means no more than "the LORD," reverently said. Moore's article was for two generations the decisive scholarly verdict, and it remains the necessary caution.

2. **The substantive-mediator reading (Martin McNamara; Daniel Boyarin).** Against Moore, McNamara argued that the Targumic Memra is a genuine *theological* usage with real bearing on the New Testament, and Boyarin (HTR 2001; *Border Lines*, 2004) pressed much further: a Jewish "**binitarian**" or [[theme-two-powers-in-heaven|two-powers]] theology — God and God's Word/Wisdom as a second divine principle — was widespread in late-antique Judaism, and **the Johannine prologue is a Jewish text drawing on this Jewish Word-theology (the "Gospel of the Memra"), not a Hellenistic graft from Philo**. On this reading Memra is the missing Aramaic middle term: Hebrew *dabar*/*ḥokhmah* → Aramaic *Memra* → Greek *Logos*, an internally Jewish development that Christianity then identified with a particular man.

The honest state of the question is that these are **structural and theological parallels argued from a shared Jewish matrix, not a demonstrated chain of textual borrowing**. The relationship of Memra to the Johannine Logos is best described as **common-source / convergence within Second-Temple and early-rabbinic Judaism** rather than direct literary dependence in either direction: John is not quoting a Targum, and no Targum is quoting John. What can be said securely is the *linguistic* fact (Memra is the Aramaic Word-of-the-LORD term, cognate in sense to *dabar* and *Logos*) and the *distributional* fact (Word-as-agent language is at home in the synagogue Aramaic of the period). Whether that licenses Boyarin's strong claim — that the Logos was already Jewish before it was Christian — is exactly where Moore's heirs still push back.

Memra also has near neighbours *within* Judaism that are not the Logos at all: the Philonic [[logos-philonic|Logos]] (a parallel Hellenistic-Jewish solution to the same mediation problem, but built from Plato and the Stoa rather than from Targumic translation practice), personified [[wisdom-personified|Wisdom]]/[[hokmah-hebrew|Ḥokhmah]] (Prov 8; Sirach 24; Wisdom of Solomon), the [[shekhinah|Shekhinah]] (the Presence-buffer that runs alongside Memra in the very same Targums), and the later angelic "[[metatron|Lesser YHWH]]" Metatron of the Hekhalot literature. These are not all the same figure, and the Atlas keeps them as distinct nodes; what unites them is the recurring late-antique Jewish work of placing *something* — Word, Wisdom, Glory, Presence, Angel — in the gap between the transcendent God and the world. Memra is the **translational** instance of that work.

## Disputes

- **Hypostasis or circumlocution?** The central dispute, set out above: Moore (1922) — reverential buffer-word, theologically inert; Boyarin (2001, 2004) and, more cautiously, McNamara — a substantive divine Word-agent with real continuity to the Logos. Most current treatments sit between the poles, granting Memra more theological seriousness than Moore allowed while resisting a fully personified "second god" reading of the Targumic evidence itself.
- **Did Memra feed John, or merely run parallel to him?** Even scholars who take Memra seriously divide over whether the Fourth Gospel's *Logos* is historically downstream of Targumic/Palestinian Word-theology (McNamara, Boyarin) or whether both are independent crystallisations of a broader Jewish Word/Wisdom tradition. The Atlas labels the John edge a **cross-tradition parallel / common-source convergence**, not a proven transmission.
- **Dating the Targums.** Because the strong "Memra was there before John" thesis depends on the Word-theology being demonstrably *early* (1st c. CE or before), the long-running uncertainty over when the Palestinian Targums and Onkelos reached their present form directly conditions how much weight the parallel can bear. Pre-Christian proto-Targumic Aramaic is inferred (and supported by Aramaic Bible-translation fragments from Qumran for other books) more than it is directly dated for the Pentateuchal Memra passages.
