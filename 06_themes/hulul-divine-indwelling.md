# Ḥulūl — Divine Indwelling / Incarnationism

## Identity

**Ḥulūl** (Arabic حلول, verbal noun of *ḥalla*, "to descend into, to alight in, to take up residence in") is the theological doctrine — in Islam almost always a *charge* rather than a self-description — that the divine can inhere in, descend into, or be infused into a created human being. In the standard vocabulary of Islamic theology (*kalām*) it is paired with **ittiḥād** (اتحاد, "union, becoming-one"): where ḥulūl names God *indwelling* a creature as water pervades a vessel, ittiḥād names God and creature *fusing* into a single reality. Both are classed by Sunni orthodoxy as forms of unbelief (*kufr*), because both violate the absolute creator–creature distinction that follows from *tawḥīd* (divine unity) — God is *bāʾin min khalqihi*, "wholly other than his creation." The heresiographers accordingly list a **ḥulūliyya** ("the incarnationists") among the deviant sects.

In the Codex Atlas, ḥulūl is placed among the doctrines of union and deification — beside [[apotheosis]], [[fanaa-annihilation]], and [[divine-immanence]] — because it names one of the sharpest boundary-cases in Islamic mysticism: the point at which the language of divine nearness tips over into the forbidden claim that God has *become present in a man*. Its paradigm exhibit is [[al-hallaj]], executed at Baghdad in 922, and its precise comparative value is that it is **the Arabic term Islamic theology uses for the Christian [[jesus-christ-deity|Incarnation]] it denies** (see Cross-tradition significance).

## In Islamic theology and Sufism

**The al-Ḥallāj paradigm.** Manṣūr al-Ḥallāj (858–922) is the figure around whom the category crystallised. His public ecstatic utterance *Anā al-Ḥaqq* — "I am the Truth / the Real," *al-Ḥaqq* being one of the ninety-nine names of God — was read by the Abbasid court and the jurists as a claim that the divine had taken up residence in him, i.e. ḥulūl, the capital transgression of *shirk*. After a nine-year imprisonment he was flogged, mutilated, crucified and beheaded in 922 (see [[event-execution-of-al-hallaj-922]]). Crucially, Louis Massignon — who devoted fifty years to the sources — argued that al-Ḥallāj's *own* theology was **not** crude ḥulūl: it was a doctrine of the transformative union of the human and divine wills in love (*ʿayn al-jamʿ*), in which the annihilated self becomes a transparent locus for divine speech, not a vessel physically inhabited by God. The ḥulūl label was the *classification imposed by his accusers*. This gap — between the ecstatic's meaning and the heresiographer's category — is the recurring structure of the whole topic.

**The "sober" guard.** Mainstream Sufism did not embrace ḥulūl; it built explicit defences against it. **Al-Junayd of Baghdad** (d. 910), the central figure of "sober" (*ṣaḥw*) Sufism, defined *fanāʾ* — mystical annihilation — as the passing-away of the self's *attributes*, not of its creaturely *existence*: the mystic does not *become* God and does not *contain* God. As the Atlas node [[fanaa-annihilation]] records, this formulation was designed precisely to immunise Sufism against the twin accusations of ḥulūl and ittiḥād. The distinction between a licit fanāʾ and an illicit ḥulūl is therefore one of the load-bearing joints of classical Sufi orthodoxy.

**The waḥdat al-wujūd distinction — why the Atlas keeps them separate.** It is a common error to equate ḥulūl with Ibn ʿArabī's [[wahdat-al-wujud]] ("unity of being"). The Akbarī tradition itself rejects the identification, and on a precise logical ground: **ḥulūl and ittiḥād both presuppose two pre-existing realities** — God and the creature — that then either indwell or fuse. Waḥdat al-wujūd denies the creature any independent being to begin with: there is only one *wujūd* (being), God's, of which all else is a transient self-disclosure (*tajallī*). There is no second thing for God to enter, and so, in the Akbarī analysis, ḥulūl is not radical enough — it is a crude error that still grants the creature its own existence. Nonetheless, **Ibn Taymiyya** (d. 1328) and his school polemically branded [[ibn-arabi]] and the Akbarīs as *ḥulūliyya* and *ittiḥādiyya* (Knysh 1999 is the definitive study of this polemical image), and modern Salafism inherits the charge. So ḥulūl functions in two registers at once: a genuine doctrinal position the tradition condemns, and a **polemical weapon** for tarring mystical metaphysics one disapproves of.

## Cross-tradition significance

The precise, non-trivial cross-tradition fact about ḥulūl is **terminological and structural at the same time**: *ḥulūl is the word Islamic theology uses for the Christian Incarnation.* When the *mutakallimūn* and heresiographers (al-Bāqillānī, al-Baghdādī, al-Shahrastānī) refute Christianity, they describe the claim that God *ḥalla* — indwelt — in Christ, and they file the Christian doctrine of the union of divine and human natures under exactly the categories of ḥulūl and ittiḥād that they use to condemn al-Ḥallāj. The Christian [[phase-4-042-athanasius-on-the-incarnation|Incarnation]] — "God became man so that man might become God" (Athanasius, *De Incarnatione* 54) — is, in Islamic eyes, the archetypal instance of the very error ḥulūl names. This makes ḥulūl the **Islamic boundary-term that maps onto the Christian doctrine Islam most sharply denies** (Qurʾān 5:72–75; 4:171 reject the divinisation of Christ).

This is a **shared vocabulary of condemnation, not a transmission**. There is no claim that the Christian Incarnation was borrowed from, or gave rise to, Sufi ḥulūl; rather, Islamic theology developed a single juridical category broad enough to cover *both* the internal ecstatic (al-Ḥallāj) *and* the external rival (Christianity). The convergence with the Atlas's wider clusters is structural:

- **With [[apotheosis]] / *theosis*.** Greek-patristic *theosis* — the deification of the human — is the mirror-image movement (man ascending to God) of ḥulūl (God descending into man); both press against the creator–creature line that monotheism guards, and the apotheosis node already flags "Sufi *fanāʾ*" as the Islamic variant that "approaches it." Ḥulūl is the register in which that approach is judged to have crossed the line.
- **With [[executed-divine-claimant]].** Al-Ḥallāj is the paradigmatic Islamic instance of the executed-divine-claimant pattern; ḥulūl is *the specific doctrinal content of the charge* on which that instance turns. Where the executed-divine-claimant hub tracks the recurring *social* pattern (claim → trial → theophanic inversion), ḥulūl names the *theological* proposition that the establishment reads into the claim. Massignon's own life's-work title, *The Passion of al-Ḥallāj*, consciously draws the Christ-parallel — the same structural crossing.
- **With [[divine-immanence]].** Ḥulūl is the point at which the immanence pole (God-within, indwelling, *Shekhinah*, *antaryāmin*) is pushed to the personal-incarnational extreme and, in Islam, judged heretical — the limiting case that shows where a given tradition draws its immanence/transcendence boundary.

The honest state of the matter: the **word and the category are secure**; what is genuinely contested is *who actually held the doctrine*. Almost no major Sufi affirms ḥulūl in propria persona — al-Ḥallāj's meaning is disputed (Massignon: not ḥulūl), Ibn ʿArabī explicitly rejects it, al-Junayd guards against it. Ḥulūl is thus better understood as **the name of a forbidden position that organises Islamic self-definition** — a boundary drawn to say what union may *not* mean — than as a school anyone straightforwardly professed.

## Disputes

- **Did al-Ḥallāj teach ḥulūl?** The court and the jurists said yes; Massignon (1982) and much subsequent scholarship say no — his was a union-of-wills / testimonial theology, not incarnationism, and the ḥulūl reading is a hostile classification of ecstatic *shaṭḥ* (Ernst 1985). The disagreement is not settled and partly turns on which fragments of the *Ṭawāsīn* and *Dīwān* are authentic.
- **Is waḥdat al-wujūd a form of ḥulūl?** Ibn Taymiyya and the Salafī tradition say yes (effectively pantheism/incarnationism); Chittick (1989), Corbin and Izutsu say emphatically no — the Akbarī doctrine is *acosmic monism* that abolishes the very duality ḥulūl requires. Knysh (1999) shows how much of the "ḥulūlī Ibn ʿArabī" is a constructed polemical image.
- **Ḥulūl vs. ittiḥād.** The heresiographies do not always distinguish the two cleanly, and later polemicists collapse them; scholars debate how far the medieval ḥulūliyya was a real sect or a heresiographical container-category assembled from disparate ecstatics.
- **The Christian equation.** That Muslim theologians describe the Incarnation as ḥulūl is well attested; the finer question — how far medieval *kalām* refutations of Christian ḥulūl and internal refutations of Sufi ḥulūl actually cross-reference one another — is less studied and is flagged here rather than asserted.
