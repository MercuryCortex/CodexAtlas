# Original Sin (Augustinian doctrine of inherited guilt)

## Definition
The doctrine that the sin of Adam transmits to every human being not merely *consequences* (mortality, disordered desire) but *guilt* and *liability to damnation* — such that infants come into the world already condemned, requiring sacramental rescue. Diagnostic marker: the inheritance of *guilt* (*reatus*), not just corruption. This distinguishes the Augustinian-Latin doctrine from Eastern *propatorikon hamartēma* (ancestral mortality) and from Pelagian frameworks (Adam as bad example only).

## Earliest attestations
- **Pauline seed:** [[phase-3-015-pauline-epistles]] — Romans 5:12 "through one man sin entered the world... and so death spread to all men, because all sinned" (eph' hō pantes hēmarton). The Greek is ambiguous; the Latin Vulgate's *in quo omnes peccaverunt* ("in whom all sinned") fixes the inherited-guilt reading and is decisive for the Latin tradition.
- **Augustine, against Pelagius:** *De Peccatorum Meritis et Remissione* (412), *De Natura et Gratia* (415), *De Nuptiis et Concupiscentia* (419–421), *Contra Julianum* (421–422), *Opus Imperfectum contra Julianum* (unfinished at death 430). The doctrine takes mature form here in the controversy with Pelagius, Caelestius, and Julian of Eclanum.
- **Council of Carthage 418** condemned Pelagianism and affirmed the necessity of infant baptism for original-sin remission.
- **Synod of Orange 529** definitively settled Latin doctrine in the anti-Pelagian Augustinian direction.

## Variants
- **Augustine's mature view:** Adam's *seminal* transmission of guilt via concupiscent procreation; original sin is both *guilt* and *corruption*.
- **Aquinas:** [[phase-5-024-aquinas-summa-theologiae]] *Ia–IIae* qq. 81–83 — refines the doctrine; original sin is the *absence of original justice* in the will, transmitted by physical descent.
- **Lutheran:** [[phase-6-007-luther-bondage-of-will]] — original sin is *total depravity* of the will; humans cannot will the good without grace.
- **Calvinist:** [[phase-6-010-calvin-institutes]] II.1–3 — original sin renders all of humanity a *massa damnata* apart from election.
- **Eastern Christian:** *Propatorikon hamartēma* — inherited mortality and corruption, but **not personal guilt**. Each person sins on their own account; baptism cleanses corruption rather than transferring guilt. The Latin–Greek divergence on this point is one of the longest-running theological splits.
- **Tridentine Catholic:** Council of Trent (1546, Session V) affirmed inherited guilt but allowed scholastic refinement.

## Connections claimed
- Augustine's anti-Pelagian polemic is widely seen (Brown 2000) as decisively shaped by his Manichaean past — the *substantial* dualism of Light/Darkness is renounced doctrinally, but the *anthropological pessimism* about embodied desire carries over.
- The doctrine's elaboration depends heavily on the Latin mistranslation of Romans 5:12 (*in quo* for *eph' hō*) — a textual contingency, but theologically irreversible by the time it was identified.

## Disputes
- **Pelagian counter-position.** Pelagius, Caelestius, and Julian of Eclanum explicitly refused inherited guilt. Pelagius held Adam's sin as bad example; humans retain libertarian free will and intrinsic capacity to obey God. Julian of Eclanum's late polemic (preserved in Augustine's *Opus Imperfectum*) is the most philosophically rigorous critique — accuses Augustine of crypto-Manichaeism. Condemned 418 / 431.
- **Eastern view.** The Greek Fathers (Athanasius, the Cappadocians, John Chrysostom, John of Damascus) read Romans 5:12 differently and never developed an inherited-guilt doctrine. Modern Orthodox theologians (John Romanides, Vladimir Lossky) treat Augustine's doctrine as a Latin innovation foreign to apostolic tradition.
- **Modern reception.** Liberal Protestant theology (Schleiermacher onward) reformulates original sin as solidarity in moral failure rather than transmitted guilt; Reinhold Niebuhr's *Nature and Destiny of Man* (1941) is the major 20th-c. retrieval. Modern Catholic theology (Rahner) reframes original sin as the "situation" into which humans are born.
