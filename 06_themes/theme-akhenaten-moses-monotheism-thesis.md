# Akhenaten–Moses Monotheism Thesis

## Definition

The thesis, in its broadest form, that the Mosaic monotheism of the Hebrew Bible derives — directly or culturally — from the Atenist religious revolution of Akhenaten (~1353–1336 BCE). The thesis has taken multiple distinct forms across scholarly history, ranging from Freud's historicist psychoanalytic claim (Moses *was* an Atenist priest) to Assmann's mnemohistorical argument (the *memory* of Akhenaten's religious revolution shaped the self-understanding of later Western monotheism). As a historiographical debate, the thesis is diagnostic: it is "held" by any scholarly or popular work that defends, qualifies, or refutes an Egyptian origin for Mosaic monotheism.

## Historical development of the debate

### 1. James Henry Breasted and the solar-hymn parallel (~1895–1934)

The American Egyptologist James Henry Breasted was the first to systematize the parallel between the [[phase-1-011-great-hymn-to-aten]] and Psalm 104 of the Hebrew Bible (~1895; synthesized in *The Dawn of Conscience*, 1934). Both hymns celebrate the solar cycle, the dependence of creatures on the creator-god's light, care for all peoples. Breasted treated the parallel as evidence of Egyptian influence on early Israelite theology. His framework became the entry point for subsequent debate.

### 2. Freud's *Moses and Monotheism* (1939) — Tier 4 claim

In his final book, Sigmund Freud proposed that Moses was not a Hebrew but an Egyptian nobleman who had been a priest or devotee of the Aten cult; that after Akhenaten's death and the suppression of Atenism, Moses transferred the monotheistic theology to a group of Hebrews he led out of Egypt; and that Moses was subsequently murdered by his followers (drawing on Ernst Sellin's then-current but later-retracted reading of Hosea). Freud further argued that "the return of the repressed" guilt over Moses's murder explains the later emergence of Mosaic law and Christianity's atonement theology.

The reception from professional historians and Egyptologists was uniformly negative: William Foxwell Albright dismissed it as "totally devoid of serious historical method." What Freud successfully did was place the Egypt-monotheism question at the center of 20th-century intellectual culture. His psychoanalytic method is Tier 4; his intuition that the Egypt connection matters is not unreasonable, though his specific mechanism is unsupported by archaeological evidence.

### 3. Assmann's reformulation: mnemohistory and the Mosaic distinction (1997–2014)

Jan Assmann's *Moses the Egyptian: The Memory of Egypt in Western Monotheism* (Harvard UP, 1997) is the scholarly pivot. Assmann explicitly separates two questions: (a) whether the *historical* Moses derived his religion from Akhenaten (he is agnostic, treating it as probably false); and (b) how the *memory* of Egypt — especially of Akhenaten's religious revolution as a type-case of monotheistic rupture — shaped Western monotheism's self-understanding. This is the distinction between "history" and "mnemohistory" (the history of cultural memory).

Assmann's core analytical contribution is the **Mosaic distinction**: the claim that the radical innovation of monotheism is not the *number* of gods but the *cognitive structure* — the introduction of a distinction between "true" and "false" religion that was absent from polytheistic systems. In polytheism, one could always translate one god into another ("Zeus = Jupiter = Baal"); monotheism makes such translation impossible and introduces religious intolerance as a structural feature. In *The Price of Monotheism* (Stanford UP, 2008), Assmann extended and defended this argument, clarifying that religious violence follows from *fundamentalism* (the political instrumentalization of the Mosaic distinction), not from monotheism per se.

### 4. Redford's archaeological alternative (1984, 1992)

Donald Redford's *Akhenaten: The Heretic King* (Princeton UP, 1984) and *Egypt, Canaan, and Israel in Ancient Times* (Princeton UP, 1992) separate the Akhenaten–Moses thesis at its historical base: (a) the Exodus tradition derives not from Akhenaten's revolution but from the *Hyksos expulsion* (c. 1550 BCE); (b) almost all toponymic details in Exodus reflect conditions no earlier than the Saite 26th Dynasty (~7th century BCE); (c) there is no archaeological evidence of any mass Exodus from Egypt corresponding to the biblical narrative. Redford thus severs the Akhenaten-Moses link at the source.

### 5. Hornung and the "religion of light" framing (1999)

Erik Hornung's *Akhenaten and the Religion of Light* (Cornell UP, 1999) treats Atenism as henotheistic rather than strictly monotheistic — the radicalization of Egyptian solar theology, without reaching the ontological "there is no other" of monotheism. Hornung was skeptical of direct Atenism–Yahwism connections. For Hornung, Atenism is the first historical attempt to explain all of nature from a single principle (light); it is not yet monotheism because it lacks Assmann's counter-religious structure.

## The Psalm 104 / Great Hymn parallel — current state

The structural parallel between the Great Hymn to the Aten and Psalm 104 (especially vv. 19–30) is universally acknowledged: both hymns celebrate the solar cycle, the dependence of creatures on the creator-god's light, the dangerous night giving way to human activity at dawn, the care of the creator for all peoples. The question is whether the parallel represents: (a) direct Egyptian influence on the Psalm; (b) Canaanite mediation — John Day (*Yahweh and the Gods and Goddesses of Canaan*, 2000) argues the first section of Psalm 104 reflects Canaanite storm-god material while the second section reflects Egyptian solar-hymn influence, perhaps mediated through Canaanite-Egyptian scribal interchange; (c) independent convergence on a shared ANE solar-hymn topos.

Mark S. Smith's assessment represents current mainstream caution: "enthusiasm for even indirect influence has been tempered in recent decades." Only 17 of the 149 lines of the Great Hymn show close parallels with Psalm 104; none are verbatim quotation. The channel of influence — if any — remains unresolved.

## What Assmann got specifically right

Assmann's lasting scholarly contribution is the separation of the mnemohistorical and historical questions. He does not claim Moses derived his religion from Akhenaten historically; he claims that the *cognitive structure* of later Western monotheism — particularly its counter-religious identity ("we are not Egypt, not polytheism, not idolatry") — is shaped by the *memory* of Egypt as the type-case of polytheism against which monotheism defined itself. This is a claim about the history of ideas that does not require the historical-derivation claim and is independent of the archaeological record's silence.
