# Covenant

A binding, formally-ratified relationship between two parties — paradigmatically between a deity and a people — that establishes mutual obligation. The Hebrew term is *berit* (בְּרִית); a covenant is not "signed" but **"cut"** — the idiom is *karat berit*, "to cut a covenant" — preserving the memory of the founding rite in which animals were divided and the parties passed between the pieces. The concept is the structural backbone of Israelite religion and, through it, of Judaism, Christianity, and Islam: the whole of "salvation history" is read as a sequence of covenants (Noahic, Abrahamic, Sinai/Mosaic, Davidic, New).

## The covenant-cutting rite (the self-curse oath)

The most archaic stratum is the ritual enacted in **[[document-genesis]]** at Genesis 15:9–18 (the J source, [[phase-2-005-hebrew-bible-j-source]]): Abram divides a heifer, a she-goat, a ram, a turtledove and a pigeon, laying each half "one against another," and after dark "a smoking furnace, and a burning lamp" — the divine presence — passes between the pieces. The act is a **self-imprecation**: to pass between the severed animals is to say "may I be made like these if I break this oath." [[document-jeremiah]] makes the self-curse explicit (Jeremiah 34:18: those who "cut the calf in twain, and passed between the parts thereof" and then broke the covenant will themselves be given over to death). The same dismemberment-and-passage rite is attested in the ancient Near East — in the Mari letters (the "killing of an ass" for a treaty) and the Alalakh and Sefire treaties — so the biblical rite belongs to a wider regional legal practice (McCarthy 1978; Speiser 1964 on Gen 15). At Genesis 15 the asymmetry is theologically decisive: **only God** (the furnace and lamp) passes through, binding himself alone — an unconditional grant.

## Two covenant types: grant vs. treaty

Moshe Weinfeld (1970) distinguished two forms drawn from two ANE legal institutions:

- **The covenant of grant** (unconditional). Modeled on the royal land-grant by which a king rewards a loyal servant in perpetuity. The **Abrahamic** ([[document-genesis]], the promise of seed and land) and **Davidic** ([[document-2-samuel]], Nathan's oracle of an everlasting throne; [[phase-2-005-davidic-psalms]], Psalm 89) covenants are grants: God obligates *himself*, the gift is irrevocable, and the human party's failure does not annul it.
- **The suzerain-vassal treaty** (conditional). Modeled on the Late Bronze Age Hittite treaties by which a great king bound a subordinate. George Mendenhall (1954) showed that the **Sinai/Mosaic** covenant ([[document-exodus]], [[document-deuteronomy]], the D source [[phase-2-011-hebrew-bible-d-source]]) mirrors the Hittite treaty structure: preamble and historical prologue ("I am the LORD thy God, which have brought thee out of the land of Egypt"), stipulations (the Decalogue and the law codes), deposition of the text, witnesses, and blessings-and-curses (Deuteronomy 27–28). Here obligation runs to the vassal-people, and the covenant *can* be broken.

The **Noahic** covenant ([[document-genesis]] 9, the Priestly source [[phase-2-018-hebrew-bible-p-source]], the rainbow as *'ot*/sign) is the most universal — made with "all flesh," not Israel alone — and likewise unconditional. The **New Covenant** ([[document-jeremiah]] 31:31–34, "I will write it in their hearts") reframes the conditional Sinai covenant as an interior, unbreakable one, and becomes the root-text of the Christian reading (Greek *diathēkē* → "New Testament").

## Ancient Near Eastern background

The covenant *form* is not a biblical invention but an adaptation of second-millennium international law. The Hittite suzerain-vassal treaties (Mendenhall 1954; later refined by treaties of the first-millennium Neo-Assyrian period, Weinfeld 1970), together with the treaty-rites at Mari, Alalakh, and Sefire, supply the legal grammar — oath, self-curse, witnesses, stipulation — that Israel transposes from the relation *king ↔ vassal* to the relation *God ↔ people*. This transposition is the theological move that makes Israelite religion distinctive: the deity is not merely the national god but a *treaty-partner* who has bound himself by oath.

## Tensions within the motif

- **Conditional vs. unconditional.** The grant (Abrahamic/Davidic — irrevocable) and the treaty (Sinai — breakable) coexist in tension throughout the canon; the Deuteronomistic History reads the monarchy's fall as covenant-breaking, while the prophets appeal to the unbreakable grant for hope of restoration.
- **Particular vs. universal.** The covenant binds *one* people, yet the Noahic covenant (all flesh) and the prophetic "new covenant" press toward universality — the same particular-vs-universal tension that runs through [[chosen-people]].

## Disputes

- **Date and direction of the treaty parallel.** Mendenhall's derivation of Sinai from the *second*-millennium Hittite treaties has been contested by those (e.g., following Weinfeld) who see the closer formal parallels in the *first*-millennium Neo-Assyrian treaties — which would push the covenant-treaty redaction later (Deuteronomic, 7th c.). The grant-vs-treaty typology itself remains broadly accepted.
- **Antiquity of the Gen 15 rite.** Whether the divided-animals rite is a genuinely archaic survival or a literary archaizing is debated (Speiser, Wenham), though the Mari and Sefire parallels favor a real legal background.
