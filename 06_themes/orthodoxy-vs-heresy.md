# Orthodoxy vs Heresy

The boundary-maintenance work by which a religious tradition defines an "inside" (orthodox; *catholic*; *sunna*) by simultaneously defining and excluding an "outside" (heretical; deviant). Walter Bauer's foundational 1934 *Orthodoxy and Heresy in Earliest Christianity* argued the chronological-priority inversion: in many regions (Egypt, Edessa, Asia Minor), what later became "heretical" was the *earlier* form of Christianity, and orthodoxy is the later imposition. The thesis has been substantially modified but its core insight — that orthodoxy and heresy are *retrospective* categories constructed in the act of polemical-boundary-drawing — is mainstream.

- [[phase-4-023-irenaeus-against-heresies]] — Irenaeus's *Adversus Haereses* (c. 180) is the foundational heresiological text of orthodox Christianity; the "Gnostic" category is largely Irenaeus's construction.
- [[phase-4-024-tertullian-apology]], [[phase-4-024b-tertullian-against-valentinians]] — Tertullian's polemical heresiologies follow Irenaeus.
- [[phase-7-014-gnostic-gospels]], [[phase-7-015-beyond-belief]] — Pagels's 1979 and 2003 monographs popularize the Bauer-style re-reading using the Nag Hammadi evidence: the "Gnostics" had their own scriptures, communities, and theological coherence; "heresy" was the *winner's* designation.

The pattern recurs across religious traditions: Sunni vs. Shi'a vs. Khariji in Islam (each calling the others *takfīr*/heretical); rabbinic Judaism vs. its Karaite, Samaritan, and other rivals; medieval Christian heresies (Cathars, Waldensians); intra-Buddhist sectarian designations (e.g., the Theravada / Mahayana naming of one another).
