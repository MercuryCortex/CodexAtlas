# Tzimtzum (Divine Contraction)

Isaac Luria's central cosmogonic innovation (Safed, ~+1570): before creation, Ein Sof — the Infinite — *contracted itself* (*tzimtzum*) to create an empty space (*tehiru*) in which finite existence could occur. Creation is not addition but withdrawal; the world exists in the negative space of a divine self-limitation. This solved a theological problem (how can finite reality coexist with infinite divinity?) by introducing a primordial divine self-restriction.

Tzimtzum is the first move in the Lurianic three-act cosmogonic drama: tzimtzum → [[shevirat-hakelim]] (breaking of the vessels) → [[tikkun-olam]] (cosmic repair). Together these form Lurianic Kabbalah's distinctive contribution to Jewish thought after 1492.

Scholem famously argued that tzimtzum is the theological mirror of the Spanish Expulsion: cosmic exile expressing historical exile. Modern process theology (Moltmann, Cobb) has retrieved tzimtzum as a model for divine self-limitation that grounds human freedom.

## MASSIVE WIN cross-tradition parallels

**Zoroastrian cosmogony** (Bundahishn): Ahura Mazda creates a bounded material world as an arena in which Angra Mainyu can be confined and defeated — both traditions require a divine "making-room" act before finite existence can occur. The logic is structurally identical: infinite divine reality produces finite space through a self-limiting act. See [[theme-zoroastrian-cosmogony]].

**Kashmir Shaivism āṇava-mala**: Trika's *āṇava-mala* ("impurity of the atom/individual") is structurally closer to tzimtzum than Vedantic māyā. In Trika, Śiva — the infinite self-luminous consciousness — *voluntarily contracts itself* into the perspective of a limited individual self. This contraction is not an illusion; it is a real act of Śiva's *svātantrya* (absolute freedom). The individual soul is Śiva contracted, just as Luria's finite world exists in the space of Ein-Sof contracted. Both posit: (1) the absolute is infinite, (2) the contraction is the absolute's own free act, (3) the contracted form is real (not mere illusion), (4) the path returns through recognizing the contraction as the absolute's self-expression. The Advaita Vedanta position (below) differs: māyā is a concealing superimposition, not a real voluntary self-limitation. The Trika position is structurally the closer match to tzimtzum. See [[tradition-kashmir-shaivism]].

**Vedantic māyā**: Brahman "contracts" into the appearance of finitude through its own creative power (*śakti* / *māyā*). The Advaita Vedanta position — the infinite presents as finite through a kind of divine self-concealment — parallels the Hasidic *metaphorical* reading of tzimtzum (God is still omnipresent; the tzimtzum is concealment, not genuine absence).

**Gnostic kenōma**: In Valentinian cosmogony, the *Pleroma* (fullness) produces a *kenōma* (emptiness / deficiency) when Sophia falls; the material world exists in this deficiency-space. Structurally: divine fullness → a void → creation in the void. Luria's tehiru (the empty space left by the tzimtzum) is the Kabbalistic cognate of the kenōma.

**Modern physics / Big Bang**: The universe begins from an infinitely compressed singularity that expands outward into space. Luria's metaphor of an infinite God withdrawing to create space for finite expansion is structurally homologous — noted by physicist-theologians including Gerald Schroeder (*The Science of God*, 1997, Free Press, T3 — speculative; use with caution). The structural parallel is striking regardless of its theological weight.

**Process theology** (Moltmann, Cobb, Hartshorne): Jürgen Moltmann (*The Trinity and the Kingdom*, 1981, SCM) explicitly uses tzimtzum as a model for God's kenotic self-limitation that grounds creaturely freedom. John Cobb draws on it in process theology's concept of God's "initial aim" that respects creaturely self-determination. Tzimtzum has become a live resource in contemporary Christian systematic theology.
