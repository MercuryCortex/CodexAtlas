# Critique of Idolatry

The polemical-theological category by which a (typically monotheist) tradition rejects the religious-cultic practices of an other (typically polytheist, image-using, or "foreign") tradition as worship of false gods, *avoda zara* (Hebrew: "strange worship"), *shirk* (Arabic: "association"), or *idolatria*. Halbertal & Margalit 1992 disentangle the multiple senses: idolatry as worship of nothing (a piece of carved wood), as worship of an intermediary mistaken for the supreme, as worship of the true God in unauthorized form, or as cognitive error about the divine. The category is internal to a tradition's self-definition: ancient Greeks didn't think they were idolaters, Hindus don't think they are; the term tracks polemic, not neutral phenomenology.

## Manifestations
- **Deuteronomic anti-Canaanite:** [[phase-2-011-hebrew-bible-d-source]] — Deut 4:15–28; 7:25–26; 12:2–4; 13:1–18 — the Deuteronomic Code's relentless campaign against high places, asherah-poles, "the work of human hands." The textual core of Hebrew anti-idolatry. Polemically directed at Israel's own popular religion as much as at foreign nations.
- **Exilic prophetic mockery:** [[phase-3-001-second-third-isaiah]] — Isa 40:18–20; 44:9–20: the most rhetorically savage anti-idolatry polemic in the Hebrew Bible. The blacksmith cuts down a tree, makes half into firewood and half into a god, prays to it: "Save me, for thou art my god." Mockery as theological method.
- **Pauline:** [[phase-3-015-pauline-epistles]] — Romans 1:18–25 — idolatry as the original sin of the Gentiles: they "exchanged the glory of the immortal God for images resembling mortal man and birds and animals and reptiles." Cognitive-moral pathology. Acts 17 (Paul on the Areopagus): a softer rhetorical approach in evangelistic mode.
- **Quranic *shirk*:** [[phase-4-034-quran]] — *shirk* (associating partners with God) is the cardinal unforgivable sin: 4:48 ("Allah forgives not that aught should be associated with Him; but He forgives anything else to whomever He wills"). Mecca's pre-Islamic Kaʿba-deities are the proximate target; doctrine of *tawḥīd* the positive correlate.
- **Christian iconoclasm.** Byzantine Iconoclasm (726–843) — the imperial-theological war over whether icons of Christ and saints are *eikōn* (proper image, theologically licit) or *eidōlon* (idol, prohibited). The Second Council of Nicaea (787) decides for the icons; the East celebrates the "Triumph of Orthodoxy." Eastern Orthodoxy is icon-affirming; Reform Protestantism is iconoclastic.
- **Reformation iconoclasm:** [[phase-6-005-luther-95-theses]], [[phase-6-010-calvin-institutes]] — Calvin's *Institutes* I.11 ("Of the Impiety of Attributing a Visible Form to God"): images of God are intrinsically idolatrous; the Mass-as-sacrifice is idolatry; the cult of saints is idolatry. Massive material iconoclasm in Zürich (Zwingli 1524), Geneva, Edinburgh — destruction of altarpieces, statues, stained glass. Lutheranism is more moderate; Reformed iconoclasm is the most material-destructive.

## Disputes
- **Internal-to-Hindu and Buddhist self-understanding.** *Mūrti-pūjā* (image worship) is theologically defended in Hindu philosophy: the image is a *focusing aid* for the formless deity, not the deity itself; the deity's *prāṇa-pratiṣṭhā* (life-installation) ritual specifies the ritual relation. Calling this "idolatry" mistranslates the internal grammar.
- **Halbertal-Margalit framework.** Their 1992 *Idolatry* distinguishes the *metaphysical* error (worshipping the wrong thing) from the *moral* error (false attachment, divided loyalty) — biblical and Quranic anti-idolatry-polemic mixes the two; modern theology often abandons the metaphysical accusation while keeping the moral concept (e.g. Tillich's "idolatry of finite things").
- **Political deployment.** Anti-idolatry-polemic functions historically as ethnic-religious boundary-marking and as legitimation of violence against other communities. The *Idolatry* category cannot be separated from the iconoclastic-violent-political contexts in which it operates.
