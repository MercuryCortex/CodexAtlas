# Scapegoat Mechanism

## Girard's core mechanism

René Girard's theory of the scapegoat mechanism (*le mécanisme victimaire*) identifies a structural sequence that he argues underlies the origin of human culture, religion, and sacrifice. The sequence runs:

1. **Mimetic desire** — humans desire what others desire (mediated, imitative desire), not autonomous objects
2. **Mimetic rivalry** — when desires converge on the same object, model becomes rival; rivalry spreads contagiously through the community
3. **Mimetic crisis** — all-against-all violence threatens to dissolve the social order; distinctions (family, rank, sacred/profane) collapse
4. **Surrogate victim** — the community's violence spontaneously concentrates onto a single target: a **scapegoat**, typically someone marginal (stranger, criminal, slave, or paradoxically the sacred king)
5. **Founding murder** — unanimous collective violence against the victim resolves the crisis; peace follows
6. **Sacralization** — the community, not recognizing the mechanism, experiences the victim as both guilty (cause of crisis) and beneficent (cause of restored peace); the victim is *sacralized* — becomes a god or founding figure
7. **Ritual repetition** — sacrifice re-enacts the founding murder in controlled form to prevent the crisis from recurring; this is the origin of religious ritual

The scapegoat mechanism is, in Girard's hypothesis, the origin of culture itself: the first shared act of unanimous violence produces the first shared object (the sacred victim), the first prohibition (mimetic rivalry that caused the crisis), and the first positive prescription (ritual repetition of the founding murder). Religion, sacrifice, and law all emerge from this single structural event.

Primary source: Girard, *Violence and the Sacred* (1977), *Things Hidden* (1987).

## Primary instances across traditions

The pattern appears across independent traditions — Girard's claim is that it is universal, not transmitted, because it solves the same social-coordination problem (ending mimetic crisis) in every human community.

### Greek pharmakos (Athenian Thargelia)

Athens expelled two human scapegoats annually at the **Thargelia** festival (late spring, honoring Apollo). Two individuals — *pharmakoi* (φαρμακοί) — typically criminals, the deformed, or social marginals, were selected, fed at public expense for a year, then led through the city, beaten with branches, and driven out beyond the city walls to carry away its accumulated pollution (*miasma*). Ancient sources (Helladius in Photius, *Bibliotheca* 279; Hipponax fragments) suggest some cities went further to execution, though this is contested. The *pharmakos* is the Greek mechanism in its clearest institutional form. See [[pharmakos]] for the full entry.

Source: Burkert, *Homo Necans* (1983), ch. 3; Bremmer, "Scapegoat Rituals in Ancient Greece," *Harvard Studies in Classical Philology* 87 (1983) — Tier 1.

### Levitical scapegoat / Yom Kippur (Leviticus 16)

On **Yom Kippur** (the Day of Atonement), the High Priest Aaron laid his hands on a live goat's head and confessed all Israel's sins, iniquities, and transgressions onto it; the goat was then driven into the wilderness "for **Azazel**" — a wilderness demon or toponym. A second goat was sacrificed to Yahweh. The English word "scapegoat" was coined by William Tyndale (1530) as a translation of the Hebrew *'ez 'ozel* (the "goat that departs" or "goat for dismissal"). The Hebrew text (Leviticus 16:8–26) clearly performs the transferal of communal pollution onto the expelled animal — structural identity with the Greek pharmakos mechanism is striking even without genealogical connection.

[[azazel]] — the demon/toponym receiving the scapegoat — is separately attested in 1 Enoch as a leader of the fallen Watchers (the tradition that gave him demonic weight).

Source: Milgrom, *Leviticus 1–16* (Anchor Bible, 1991) — Tier 1; Gerstenberger, *Leviticus* (OTL, Westminster, 1996) — Tier 1.

### Romulus kills Remus (founding violence of Rome)

[[romulus]] kills his twin Remus over the boundary of the new city — Remus mockingly leaps over Romulus's wall, and Romulus strikes him down with words attributed as "So perish whoever else shall leap over my walls" (Livy 1.7). Rome is founded on this fratricide. Girard reads Romulus/Remus as a structurally identical instance: founding violence → sacred city → the murdered twin is absorbed into the city's foundation myth. The **Cain/Abel** narrative (Genesis 4) is structurally parallel (first murder → city-building, Genesis 4:17) without transmission dependency.

See [[divine-twins]] for the broader fratricidal inversion pattern (Dioskouroi, Ashvins, Osiris/Set).

Source: Girard, *Violence and the Sacred*, ch. 3; Livy, *Ab Urbe Condita* 1.3–16 (Tier 1).

### Dionysus sparagmos (dismemberment)

The Orphic tradition narrates the **sparagmos** of [[dionysus-mystery]] / [[zagreus]]: the Titans tear the child Dionysus to pieces, consume him, and are struck by Zeus's lightning — from the Titans' ashes humanity is made. Dionysus is reconstituted. The ritual aspect: *sparagmos* ("tearing apart") and *omophagia* (eating raw flesh) in Dionysian cult reenact the founding dismemberment. Walter Burkert (*Homo Necans*, ch. 2) analyzes Greek tragedy — specifically the theatrical Dionysus — as a ritualized replay of the founding murder in which the tragic hero is the surrogate victim. Girard concurs: tragedy is the scapegoat mechanism aestheticized and institutionalized.

Source: Burkert, *Homo Necans* (1983) — Tier 1; Seaford, *Dionysos* (Routledge, 2006) — Tier 2.

### Christ crucifixion (Girard's reading: the mechanism exposed)

The Passion narrative has the same structure as every scapegoat event: social tension → unanimous community turning against a single victim → the victim's death restores peace (John 18:14: Caiaphas says "it is better that one man die for the people than that the whole nation perish" — Girard reads this as Caiaphas unwittingly articulating the scapegoat mechanism in its purest form). But the Gospels tell the story *from the victim's perspective*, repeatedly insisting on his innocence (Pilate three times: "I find no guilt in this man"), and the Resurrection reverses the community's condemnation.

Girard's argument: every founding myth *justifies* the collective violence (Romulus is right, Remus broke the sacred boundary; Oedipus *was* guilty of pollution). The Gospels *refuse* to justify it. This is the first text in world literature told systematically from the innocent victim's point of view — which is why, for Girard, Christianity constitutes a unique anti-sacrificial revelation rather than just one more mythological re-clothing of the mechanism.

The Pauline texts that come closest to the pharmakos / scapegoat logic: Galatians 3:13 ("Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us"), 2 Corinthians 5:21 ("God made him who had no sin to be sin for us") — Jesus as the one who *absorbs* the community's pollution, paralleling the pharmakos precisely.

Source: Girard, *Things Hidden Since the Foundation of the World* (1987), Part 2; *The Scapegoat* (1986), ch. 9–16 — Tier 1.

## The Girard–Burkert convergence

Walter Burkert's *Homo Necans* (German 1972, English 1983) was written independently of Girard's *Violence and the Sacred* (French 1972). Both appeared the same year in their original languages; neither initially knew the other's work. Both conclude:

- Greek sacrifice re-enacts a founding prehistoric violence
- The pharmakos/scapegoat is the mechanism's clearest institutional form
- Greek tragedy (Dionysus, Oedipus) reenacts the founding murder
- The sacred victim is simultaneously guilty and beneficial

Burkert's theoretical basis differs: he grounds the pattern in Paleolithic hunting rituals and anxiety around killing prey (the "hunting hypothesis"), whereas Girard grounds it in mimetic desire and social rivalry. The convergence from different starting points — classical philology vs. anthropological theory — is one of the strongest independent confirmations of the structural claim.

They later acknowledged each other and co-authored a discussion in *Violent Origins* (ed. Hamerton-Kelly, Stanford UP, 1987) — Tier 2.

## Cross-tradition pattern: independent vs. transmitted

The instances above are **structurally parallel without genealogical transmission**:

| Instance | Tradition | Period | Transmission to others |
|---|---|---|---|
| Yom Kippur pharmakos (Leviticus 16) | Israelite priestly | ~6th c. BCE redaction | None documented |
| Athenian Thargelia pharmakos | Greek civic | Archaic–Classical (~7th–4th c. BCE) | None documented |
| Romulus/Remus | Roman founding mythology | ~3rd c. BCE literary crystallization | None |
| Dionysus sparagmos | Orphic Greek | ~6th c. BCE | None to Leviticus |
| Babylon kuppuru rites | Mesopotamian | ~9th c. BCE | Possible indirect influence on Leviticus (debated) |

Girard's claim: the mechanism is **not** a transmitted meme but an independently operative social-psychological algorithm discoverable by any human community in crisis. This makes it a **cross-tradition convergence finding**, not a diffusionist-influence finding. The parallel does not imply borrowing; it implies the mechanism is a structural property of human social organization.

The Babylonian *kuppuru* purification rites (9th–7th c. BCE) involve transferring sin onto animals or human beings expelled or killed; some scholars (Wright, *The Disposal of Impurity*, Scholars Press, 1987) argue for direct influence on the Levitical scapegoat ritual given the Israelite–Babylonian contact during the exile (6th c. BCE). This would make the Leviticus 16 ↔ Babylon connection a transmission finding, not a convergence finding. The Greek and Roman cases remain independent.

## Limitations and critique

Girard's framework has attracted substantial scholarly criticism:

1. **Over-totalizing.** Not all religion is sacrifice-based. Jain, early Buddhist, and Quaker traditions are explicitly anti-sacrifice from the outset — their existence is awkward for a theory that grounds all religion in sacrificial founding violence.
2. **Christocentric teleology.** The theory's trajectory — Hebrew Bible begins to expose mechanism → Gospels complete the exposure → Christianity is uniquely demythologizing — can be read as apologetics dressed as anthropology.
3. **Empirical unfalsifiability.** The "founding murder" is a hypothetical reconstruction of pre-historical events; there is no archaeological evidence for the specific scenario Girard describes.
4. **Strenski's critique.** Ivan Strenski (*Theology and the First Theory of Sacrifice*, 2003) argues Girard misreads Israelite sacrifice — the Yom Kippur ritual is not about guilt-transference in the way Girard claims, and Hebrew *korban* (offering) is not semantically equivalent to "scapegoating."
5. **J.Z. Smith's alternative.** Jonathan Z. Smith (*Imagining Religion*, 1982) argues sacrifice is better understood as a controlled site of killing within a culture's taxonomic system — not about social crisis resolution.

Use Girard's framework as a **cross-tradition investigative lens**, not a total theory: it is maximally productive when identifying *structural parallels* across independent instances of surrogate-victim logic, least productive when applied as a universal origin-of-religion claim.
