# Ancestor Veneration

The practice of maintaining ongoing relationships with deceased family members through ritual — offerings, prayers, propitiation, commemoration — treating the dead as active participants in the community of the living. One of the most widespread religious practices in human history, attested across every inhabited continent and most major civilizational traditions.

## The core logic
Dead ancestors retain social personhood — they are not gone but present in a different register. They can bless or harm the living depending on whether proper obligations are fulfilled. The living owe the dead ongoing care; in return, the dead provide protection, fertility, and continuity.

## Cross-tradition parallels
- **[[tradition-chinese-folk-religion]]**: annual Qingming tomb-sweeping; Ghost Festival; ancestral altars in every home; paper goods burned at funerals
- **[[tradition-confucianism]]**: *xiao* (filial piety) extends naturally to the dead; ancestor rites are Confucian civic practice
- **[[tradition-shinto]]**: *sorei* (ancestral spirits) receive periodic propitiation at household shrines (*butsudan*)
- **African traditions**: ancestors (*egungun* in Yoruba; *mizimu* in Bantu) mediate between living and divine
- **Ancient Rome**: Lares (household ancestors) and Manes (the collective dead) received cult; *Lemuria* festival to propitiate restless dead
- **[[tradition-yoruba-ifa]]**: *Egungun* masquerade society channels ancestral wisdom; ancestors are actively present in ritual

## The Chinese Rites Controversy
The most consequential debate over ancestor veneration in world religious history: when Jesuits began evangelizing China (1580s onward), Matteo Ricci argued that Confucian ancestor rites were civil rather than religious — and therefore compatible with Christianity. The Dominicans and Franciscans argued they were idolatry. Pope Clement XI ruled against Ricci (1715); Benedict XIV banned the rites (1742). The Chinese emperor Kangxi expelled Catholic missionaries in response. The controversy effectively ended Catholic missions in China for a century and is one of the clearest cases of theological rigidity destroying a potential cross-cultural synthesis.
