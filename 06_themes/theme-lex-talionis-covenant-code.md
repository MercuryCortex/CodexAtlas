# Lex Talionis and the Covenant Code — Hammurabi ↔ Mosaic Law

## Definition

The legal principle of *lex talionis* ("law of retaliation," Latin for "eye for eye, tooth for tooth") and the broader cluster of casuistic law provisions in Exodus 21–23 (the "Covenant Code") exhibit structural and content parallels with the Laws of Hammurabi (CH; ~1755–1750 BCE) that are close enough to have sustained over a century of scholarly debate about whether the relationship is one of direct literary borrowing or parallel development from a shared ancient Near Eastern legal tradition.

The theme covers: (1) the *lex talionis* formula specifically; (2) the "goring ox" provisions; (3) the kidnapping / runaway-slave cluster; (4) the broader structural parallels between CH and Exodus 21–23; and (5) the scholarly debate between direct-dependence and common-tradition models.

## Earliest attestations

- **Sumerian (Laws of Ur-Nammu, ~2100 BCE):** The earliest known law collection, with casuistic provisions on bodily injury — though Ur-Nammu typically imposes monetary compensation rather than strict physical retaliation (Roth 1997).
- **Sumerian (Laws of Lipit-Ishtar, ~1900 BCE):** Another precursor law collection; provisions on debt, property, family law. Shows the genre was standard well before Hammurabi.
- **Akkadian (Laws of Eshnunna, ~1770 BCE):** Immediately pre-Hammurabi collection with provisions on bodily injury, debt, and animals. Contains a goring-ox provision (§§53–55) structurally similar to both CH and Exodus.
- **Akkadian (Code of Hammurabi, ~1755–1750 BCE):** The paradigmatic monumental expression: §§195–214 (bodily injury: eye for eye, bone for bone, tooth for tooth at CH 196–200, stated symmetrically with class distinctions); §§229–232 (builder liable if house collapses and kills occupant); §§14–16 (kidnapping, harboring runaway slaves).
- **Hebrew (Covenant Code, Exodus 21–23):** Scholarly date disputed — composition likely in the 8th–7th century BCE in Judah (Wright 2009; deuteronomistic and J/E debate ongoing). Key parallels: Exod 21:24 (*lex talionis*); Exod 21:28–32 (goring ox and owner liability); Exod 21:16 (kidnapping); Exod 21:18–27 (bodily-injury law).

## The specific parallels

**1. Lex talionis** — CH 196–197: "If a man destroys the eye of a free man, they shall destroy his eye. If he breaks the bone of a free man, they shall break his bone." Exod 21:24: "eye for eye, tooth for tooth, hand for hand, foot for foot." The formula also appears in Lev 24:20 and Deut 19:21, suggesting it was a stable legal formula across multiple compositional layers of the Hebrew Bible.

**2. Goring ox** — CH 250–252 stipulates liability if an ox known to gore kills a person: if the ox-owner was warned and did not restrain the animal, he is liable for the victim's life. Exod 21:28–32 is structurally and verbally close: the ox must be "known" to gore, and the owner warned; liability differs by the victim's status. The Eshnunna Laws §§53–55 provide an even earlier version of the same provision, confirming the genre's stability.

**3. Kidnapping / harboring runaway slaves** — CH 14 penalizes kidnapping a free person's child with death; CH 16 penalizes harboring a fugitive slave. Exod 21:16 penalizes kidnapping with death; Deut 23:15–16 (Deuteronomic, later) notably *inverts* CH 16 — it requires harboring a runaway slave rather than punishing it. Wright (2009) argues this inversion is deliberate theological revision.

## The scholarly dispute

**Wright's direct-dependence thesis (Oxford, 2009):** Wright argues that a Hebrew scribe in the Neo-Assyrian period (740–640 BCE, when Mesopotamian cuneiform culture penetrated Judah through Assyrian imperial administration) had access to a cuneiform copy of the Laws of Hammurabi and composed Exodus 21–23 as a creative adaptation of it. The argument rests on: (a) the structural sequence of topics in Covenant Code mirroring CH's sequence; (b) specific verbal echoes that require literary rather than oral transmission; (c) the deliberate *revision* of CH provisions — especially Deut 23:15–16's inversion of CH 16 on runaway slaves — as a theological counter-statement. Wright's reading frames Mosaic law not as independent divine revelation but as a learned, politically charged adaptation of Babylonian imperial legal prestige.

**Westbrook's common-tradition model (Maarav, 2008):** Raymond Westbrook, the field's leading ANE legal historian (died 2009), argued that Wright's parallels are better explained by a shared pan-ANE customary legal tradition with deep roots predating Hammurabi. Westbrook emphasized that actual Mesopotamian court records rarely cite specific CH provisions by number — suggesting CH was more exemplary/scholastic than a live code — and that legal concepts like goring-ox liability were simply standard across the region. On this model, Israelite law drew on oral and institutional traditions common to the entire Fertile Crescent, not on a specific cuneiform tablet.

**The earlier-law problem:** Both positions must reckon with the Eshnunna Laws (~1770 BCE), which contain goring-ox provisions *predating* Hammurabi. This complicates Wright's specific claim that the Covenant Code derives from CH rather than from the broader ANE legal tradition that both CH and Exodus inherit.

## What this means for the vault

This theme is a **MASSIVE-win cross-tradition bridge**: it traces the legal-ethical framework of Mosaic law — the heart of Judaism and (through rabbinic tradition) of Christianity and Islam — back into Mesopotamian royal-legal theology. Whether the mechanism is direct borrowing (Wright) or shared milieu (Westbrook), the Israelite lawgiver tradition did not emerge in a vacuum but in dialogue with the same legal-theological currents that produced Hammurabi's Shamash-commissioned stele.

## Connections claimed

- **Direct borrowing**: Wright 2009 argues CH → Covenant Code through Neo-Assyrian intermediary.
- **Common ANE tradition**: Westbrook 2003/2008 argues independent common source in pan-ANE customary law.
- **Both agree**: The parallels are real and demand explanation; Mosaic law is not formally distinctive from its ANE context.
