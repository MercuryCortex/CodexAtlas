# Tawḥīd — Divine Unity

## Overview

**Tawḥīd** (Arabic: تَوْحِيد, "making one / asserting oneness") is the foundational theological doctrine of Islam: the absolute, unqualified, indivisible oneness of God (Allāh). It is the positive content of the shahāda — "There is no god but God" — and the theological axis around which all of Islamic doctrine, law, mysticism, and philosophy revolves. Ibn Taymiyya called it "the purpose of creation." The *shirk* (associating partners with God) condemned throughout the Quran is the precise negation of tawḥīd; the Quran's shortest chapter, Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ (112), is the concentrated declaration: "Say: He is God, the One. God, the Self-Sufficient. He does not beget, nor was He begotten. And there is nothing like Him."

## The Three Classical Divisions

Classical Islamic theology (particularly in the Ibn Taymiyya / Salafi formulation) distinguishes three dimensions of tawḥīd:

1. **Tawḥīd ar-Rubūbiyya** — Oneness of Lordship (*rabb*): the recognition that God alone is the creator, sustainer, and sovereign of the universe. The Quran attributes this acknowledgment even to polytheists (29:61); it is necessary but not sufficient.

2. **Tawḥīd al-Ulūhiyya** — Oneness of Worship (*ilāh*): directing all acts of worship (prayer, sacrifice, hope, fear, reliance) exclusively to God. This is the core of prophetic mission — what Abraham, Moses, Jesus, and Muhammad all called humanity toward. Shirk occurs precisely here.

3. **Tawḥīd al-Asmāʾ waṣ-Ṣifāt** — Oneness of Names and Attributes: affirming God's self-descriptions in the Quran and hadith without likening them to creaturely attributes (tashbīh) or stripping them of meaning (taʿṭīl). The 99 Beautiful Names (al-asmāʾ al-ḥusnā) are authentic but cannot be projected analogically onto God's nature.

## Kalām Debates: Muʿtazilites vs. Ashʿarites

The *Muʿtazila* (8th–10th c.) pressed tawḥīd to radical conclusions: God's attributes (knowledge, power, will, life) cannot be real distinct properties without compromising divine unity — they must be identical with the divine essence, or else God is multiple. They denied the Quranic text's "eternity" (qidam) so as not to posit an eternal entity alongside God. The Quran was therefore *created* (khalq al-Qurʾān) — a politically explosive position.

The *Ashʿarites* (al-Ashʿarī, d. 935 CE) countered: attributes are neither identical with nor distinct from the essence — they subsist *in* the essence in a mode beyond creaturely analogy (bilā kayf). The Quran is eternal in its "meaning" but created in its "verbal expression." This became Sunni mainstream.

The *Māturīdiyya* (contemporary with Ashʿarism; Central Asian) occupied a mediating position, granting more room for human reason in theological inference.

## Ibn Taymiyya's Formulation

Ibn Taymiyya (1263–1328 CE) criticized what he saw as the *Ashʿarite* concession to Aristotelian philosophy and *Sufi* interpretation of tawḥīd as metaphysical monism (*waḥdat al-wujūd*). His three-division schema (above) was designed to insist that tawḥīd is fundamentally about *worship* and *obedience*, not metaphysical speculation. This formulation became the theological foundation of Wahhābī/Salafī movements (Ibn ʿAbd al-Wahhāb, 18th c. Arabia; Sayyid Quṭb, 20th c. Egypt).

## Sufi Tawḥīd: Waḥdat al-Wujūd

Ibn ʿArabī (1165–1240 CE) developed tawḥīd toward an ontological thesis: *waḥdat al-wujūd* (unity of being) — there is only one genuine Being, and all multiplicity is the divine self-disclosure (*tajallī*) in its own mirrors. This was not pantheism but an extreme asymmetric monism: the world has no independent being, it is God's self-manifestation; but God is not reducible to the world. Al-Ghazālī's more sober Sufi path (*Iḥyāʾ ʿUlūm al-Dīn*) maintained the distinction between creature and Creator while affirming experiential proximity to God.

In Attar's *Manṭiq al-Ṭayr*, the fifth valley is explicitly called the Valley of Unity — where the birds discover that the Simorgh they sought and themselves are not two. Rūmī's *Masnavi* embeds tawḥīd as an ontological claim: "all multiplicity is the One appearing as many."

## MASSIVE Wins — Cross-Tradition Edges

**Tawḥīd ↔ Jewish *Echad* (Shema Yisrael)**: "Hear O Israel, YHWH our God, YHWH is *one*" (Deuteronomy 6:4). Both traditions assert absolute divine unity against polytheism; Maimonides' formulation in the *Mishneh Torah* (13 Principles of Faith, first two: God exists; God is one and unique) directly parallels Islamic tawḥīd and was composed in a milieu of Jewish-Islamic philosophical exchange (Guide for the Perplexed is saturated with Avicennan and Averroist influence). The Arabic-speaking Jewish philosophy of the 9th–12th c. (Saʿadia Gaon, al-Bāhya ibn Paqūda, Maimonides) conducted its monotheism debates in direct dialogue with Muʿtazilite and Ashʿarite kalām.

**Tawḥīd ↔ Zoroastrian Ahura Mazda as sole uncreated good**: Zoroastrian monotheism (or proto-monotheism) asserts Ahura Mazda alone is uncreated (*azdā*) and wholly good; Angra Mainyu is a secondary counter-principle. The Zoroastrian *aša* (truth/order) and Islamic tawḥīd share the function of grounding ethical monotheism in the nature of the One. Modern scholars (Boyce, Gnoli) debate whether Zarathustra's system was already strict monotheism or monolatry with dualistic residue.

**Tawḥīd ↔ Advaita Vedanta *Nirguṇa Brahman***: Śaṅkara's *nirguṇa Brahman* (qualityless Absolute) and Ibn ʿArabī's tawḥīd converge: both posit a single, unqualified Being behind apparent multiplicity; both generate accusations of "pantheism" from orthodox interpreters; both resolve individual selfhood into the Absolute. The 20th-c. Traditionalist school (Guénon, Schuon) made this convergence their central thesis; Brahman-impersonal and tawḥīd are the apex of what they called the *sophia perennis*.

**Tawḥīd ↔ Plotinus' The One**: The Neoplatonic One (*to hen*) is the source of all being, absolutely simple, beyond predication — it does not "know" or "be" in any creaturely sense. The Islamic Aristotle-transmission spine (al-Kindī, al-Fārābī, Avicenna) received Plotinus' Enneads as the *Theology of Aristotle*, reframing the One as the Islamic God who necessarily exists and from whom all being emanates. Avicenna's distinction between *wujūd* (existence) and *māhiyya* (essence) — where God alone is the Necessary Existent whose essence IS existence — is a direct theological translation of Plotinian henology into Islamic tawḥīd.

**Tawḥīd ↔ Christian Negative Theology (Pseudo-Dionysius, Meister Eckhart)**: The Dionysian tradition (5th–6th c.) asserts that God transcends all positive predication — the divine darkness beyond being and non-being parallels the Islamic *bilā kayf* (without asking how). Meister Eckhart's *Gottheit* (Godhead, beyond the personal God) — "God and I are one in this birth" — is the Christian mystical extreme that most closely mirrors Ibn ʿArabī's *waḥdat al-wujūd*. Medieval Christian theologians (Albertus Magnus, Aquinas) engaged directly with Avicennan causality, importing tawḥīd-inflected metaphysics into scholasticism.
