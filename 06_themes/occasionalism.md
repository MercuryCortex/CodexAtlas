# Occasionalism

## Definition
The doctrine that God is the *only* true cause; that what we call natural causation between created things is illusory or merely a divinely-coordinated *occasion* for God's direct causal action. The cotton does not burn because the fire touches it; God burns the cotton, having *occasioned* the burning by the contact. Secondary causation in finite substances is denied; God acts at every moment in every event. Diagnostic marker: the *univocal* denial of *any* genuine causal efficacy in finite substances, distinguished from *concurrentism* (God concurs with genuine secondary causes) and from *mere conservation* (God conserves substances that exercise their own causal powers).

## Earliest attestations
- **Early Ashʿarite kalām:** **al-Ashʿarī** (~874–936), founder of the Ashʿarite school of Islamic theology, in *Kitāb al-Lumaʿ* and *Maqālāt al-Islāmiyyīn*; **al-Bāqillānī** (~950–1013) and **al-Juwaynī** (1028–1085) systematize the doctrine. The Ashʿarite theology of *atomism + occasionalism* (the world is composed of indivisible *jawāhir* / atoms, sustained moment-to-moment by God's direct action) is the classical Islamic articulation.
- **Al-Ghazālī's *Tahāfut al-Falāsifa*** (Incoherence of the Philosophers, 1095): the most *famous* occasionalist argument — Discussion 17 — against the Avicennan-Aristotelian doctrine of natural causation. Al-Ghazālī argues there is no *necessary connection* between what we call cause and effect; the burning of cotton on contact with fire is custom-of-God (*ʿādat Allāh*), not natural necessity. Hume's later *Treatise* (1739) repeats much of this argument structurally, though without acknowledged dependence.
- **Note on attribution:** al-Ghazālī is the **systematizer and most famous expositor**, not the *originator*. The Ashʿarite tradition is the genuine origin; al-Ghazālī inherits and brilliantly weaponizes the doctrine against the *falāsifa*.
- **Christian Western occasionalism:** **Géraud de Cordemoy** (*Le Discernement du corps et de l'âme*, 1666) — first explicit Cartesian occasionalist. **Arnold Geulincx** (*Ethica*, 1675) develops the doctrine. **Nicolas Malebranche** (*De la recherche de la vérité*, 1674–1675; *Entretiens sur la métaphysique*, 1688) — the most systematic Western occasionalist; the standard 17th-c. articulation.
- **Whether the medieval Islamic occasionalism *influenced* the 17th-c. Cartesians** is contested. Direct textual borrowing has not been established; the parallel may be an independent re-derivation from similar metaphysical commitments (radical divine sovereignty + mechanistic constraints on finite causation). Perler & Rudolph 2000 treat this carefully.

## Variants
- **Strict Ashʿarite atomic occasionalism:** atoms + occasions; everything depends on God's direct action moment-to-moment.
- **Al-Ghazālī's modified version:** acknowledges he could allow some role for created powers if it preserved divine omnipotence and freedom of action, but the *Tahāfut* presents the strict view.
- **Cartesian occasionalism (Malebranche):** modes of substance cannot causally act on other modes or substances; God is the only true cause; the only "real" causation is *general volitions* by which God maintains lawlike regularities in the modes of created substances.
- **Spinoza's rejection:** *Ethics* (1677) — only one substance, which is God-or-Nature; modes do not cause each other but follow from the divine substance with necessity. This is *not* occasionalism (Spinoza denies the multiplicity of substances that occasionalism takes for granted) but is closely related.
- **Leibniz's pre-established harmony:** an alternative solution to the mind-body problem and to causal interaction — monads do not really interact, but God has pre-arranged their states to correspond. An alternative to occasionalism.

## Connections claimed
- **Atomism and occasionalism.** Ashʿarite occasionalism is intimately tied to its atomism — discrete *jawāhir* require God's recreation moment-to-moment. The combination yields a metaphysics radically different from Aristotelian substance-causation. Wolfson 1976, Pines 1936 are the classical Western studies; Dhanani 1994 is the modern reassessment.
- **Hume's parallel.** Hume's argument in *Treatise* I.III.14 and *Enquiry* §VII that we have no *impression* of a necessary connection in causation is structurally parallel to al-Ghazālī's argument in *Tahāfut* 17. Whether Hume knew al-Ghazālī directly (via Pococke's 1672 Latin?) or indirectly is disputed; the parallel is real.
- **Theological motivation.** Occasionalism is generally motivated by a maximalist account of divine sovereignty — to preserve God's omnipotence and the radical contingency of nature on God's free will. Both the Islamic and the Cartesian articulations share this motivation.

## Disputes
- **Did al-Ghazālī actually hold occasionalism?** Frank 1992 famously argued al-Ghazālī's apparent occasionalism is *dialectical* (against the *falāsifa*) and does not represent his settled view; al-Ghazālī's positive metaphysics, on Frank's reading, allows secondary causation. The Frank thesis has been influential and contested (Marmura, Griffel 2009 are more skeptical of Frank).
- **Coherence of occasionalism.** The principal philosophical objections: (a) it makes natural science impossible (if there are no natural causes, there are no natural laws to discover, only custom-of-God); (b) it conflates God's action with what is normally explained by created agents; (c) it makes free human action problematic — if God is the sole cause, human moral responsibility is hard to ground. Malebranche addresses (a) with general-volition theology; (c) remains a serious philosophical problem.
- **Whether contemporary divine-action theories presuppose occasionalism.** Some divine-action theologians (Plantinga in places, classical theists generally) have been read as committed to occasionalist or near-occasionalist views; others (Stoeger, Russell) reject this.
