# Paradise Etymology — *pairidaeza* to Paradise

The word "paradise" carries within its letters the most direct, unambiguous, and etymologically traceable cross-tradition transmission from Zoroastrian Iran to the Abrahamic tradition. Unlike the doctrinal parallels of Satan, resurrection, or angelology — which require scholarly inference about probable borrowing — **the word "paradise" is not a parallel: it is the same word**, passed through six languages over twenty-five centuries, its Old Iranian form still visible in its modern English body.

## The Etymological Chain

The word passes through these stages without break:

| Language | Form | Meaning | Date / Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Avestan (Old Iranian) | *pairidaeza* | "walled enclosure" (*pairi* 'around' + *daeza* 'wall') | ~1000–500 BCE |
| Old Persian | *paradaida* / *paridaida* | "walled royal garden/estate" | Achaemenid inscriptions |
| Greek | *paradeisos* (παράδεισος) | "enclosed park, royal garden" | Xenophon, *Anabasis* 1.2.7, ~400 BCE |
| Hebrew / Aramaic | *pardes* (פרדס) / *pardaysa* | "orchard, garden, royal park" | 3 post-exilic HB texts; ~450–300 BCE |
| Latin | *paradisus* | "pleasure garden; heaven; Eden" | LXX via Vulgate |
| English | *paradise* | "heaven, place of perfect happiness" | via Old French *paradis* |

The Avestan compound is exact: *pairi-* (cognate with Greek *peri-*, "around") + *daeza* (from *daez-*, "to pile up, build"; cognate with Greek *teichos*, "wall"). The literal meaning is "that which is built up around" — a walled enclosure. In the Achaemenid imperial context, this became the technical term for the royal-garden estates that were simultaneously hunting parks, pleasure gardens, and ideological statements of royal power over nature.

## The First Greek Attestation: Xenophon at Celaenae (~401 BCE)

Xenophon, the Athenian historian and mercenary soldier who served in the army of Cyrus the Younger (not the Cyrus who conquered Babylon, but his namesake heir), records in *Anabasis* 1.2.7 that Cyrus the Younger had a *paradeisos* at Celaenae (Phrygia, modern Turkey) — a walled park of great beauty containing wild animals for royal hunting. Xenophon uses *paradeisos* throughout the *Anabasis* and *Oeconomicus* to describe Persian royal parks.

This is the **oldest surviving Greek text** to use the word. Xenophon did not invent it — he borrowed it directly from the Old Persian *paradaida*, calquing the Persian institutional term into Greek. The dating (~400 BCE) confirms that the word had already crossed from Old Persian into Greek usage during the late Achaemenid period, precisely the period of Zoroastrian-Jewish maximum contact.

## The Three Hebrew Bible Occurrences: All Post-Exilic

The word *pardes* appears exactly three times in the Hebrew Bible, and all three occurrences are in post-exilic texts:

1. **Nehemiah 2:8** (~445 BCE): Nehemiah asks the Persian king Artaxerxes for timber from *the king's pardes* (הַמֶּלֶךְ פַּרְדֵּס — *pardes ha-melek*) to build the gates of Jerusalem. The term refers directly to a Persian royal estate/garden. This is the **most transparent usage**: a Jewish official asking a Persian king for timber from the king's Iranian-institution garden, using the Iranian institutional term in Hebrew.

2. **Ecclesiastes 2:5** (~5th–3rd c. BCE): Qohelet describes making *pardēsîm* (gardens/parks) for himself — the plural form, used in the context of accumulated wealth. The Persian-period provenance of the book is supported by this and other Persian loanwords.

3. **Song of Solomon 4:13** (~5th–3rd c. BCE): the beloved's body compared to a *pardes* of pomegranates. The erotic-garden imagery draws on Persian royal-garden aesthetics.

**The pattern is unambiguous**: *pardes* enters Hebrew in the Persian period, is used in Persian-period texts, and its first Hebrew Bible appearance (Nehemiah 2:8) directly invokes the Persian royal institution from which the word derives. This is a **datable, context-specific, Persian-period loanword** — the gold standard of linguistic transmission.

## The Septuagint Transformation: *Pardes* and *Gan* → *Paradeisos*

When Jewish scholars translated the Hebrew Bible into Greek (Septuagint, ~3rd–1st c. BCE), they used *paradeisos* to translate **both** the Hebrew *pardes* (the obvious choice) and the Hebrew *gan* ("garden") — including specifically *Gan Eden* (the Garden of Eden). This translation decision transformed the word's theological weight: the royal Iranian walled garden became the Greek name of the primordial garden, and through that identification, the name of the afterlife.

Specifically: Genesis 2:8 in the LXX reads: "God planted a *paradeisos* in Eden in the east." The word that began as an Achaemenid royal park is now the primordial human garden — and through the Greek tradition, the afterlife destination.

## The New Testament: *Paradeisos* as Eschatological Afterlife

The New Testament uses *paradeisos* three times, each with distinct eschatological reference:

1. **Luke 23:43**: Jesus to the dying thief on the cross — "Today you will be with me in *paradeisos*." This is the locus classicus of the word as "heaven" / "afterlife destination."

2. **2 Corinthians 12:4**: Paul describes being "caught up into *paradeisos*" in his visionary experience — *paradeisos* as the third heaven.

3. **Revelation 2:7**: "To the one who conquers I will grant to eat of the tree of life, which is in the *paradeisos* of God." The Edenic garden-with-tree-of-life is now the eschatological destination.

The word has traveled from Achaemenid royal park to afterlife garden in roughly four centuries — a semantic journey made possible by the LXX's decision to use *paradeisos* for *Gan Eden*, which made the Persian royal-garden metaphor the default Greek name for the primordial-and-eschatological garden simultaneously.

## Why This Matters for the Vault

The paradise etymology is **the single clearest single piece of evidence** for Zoroastrian-Jewish cross-tradition transmission because:

1. It is **not a parallel** — it is an **identity**. The same word, with identifiable Old Iranian roots, passes through six languages.
2. The **timing is exact**: the word appears in Hebrew precisely in the Persian period (post-539 BCE) and is absent from pre-exilic Hebrew.
3. The **context is unmistakable**: the first Hebrew use is Nehemiah asking a Persian king for timber from his *pardes* — the Persian royal institution is present in plain sight.
4. The **semantic expansion** (from Persian royal park → Hebrew orchard → LXX Garden of Eden → NT heaven) is fully traceable.

No single piece of evidence shows more clearly that the Zoroastrian-Persian world entered Jewish theology not just as vague influence but as living vocabulary.
