    note: Pythagoras is the first identifiable individual in Western history to whom the doctrine of personal soul-transmigration is securely attributed. He claimed to remember his own past lives (including the Trojan hero Euphorbus), and his metempsychosis doctrine was foundational for all subsequent Greek engagement with transmigration. Herodotus attributes the Greek reincarnation doctrine to Egyptian sources; the Greek → Indian transmission question remains debated.
  - target: pherecydes-of-syros
    type: possible-origin
    note: Ancient tradition (Diogenes Laertius, Cicero) identifies Pherecydes of Syros as Pythagoras's teacher who taught soul-immortality and possibly transmigration. His cosmogonic text (*Heptamychos*) established the concept of the soul's divine origin — the intellectual context from which Pythagorean metempsychosis emerged.
  - target: tradition-orphic
    type: parallel
    note: Orphic and Pythagorean transmigration doctrines are parallel developments in the same Greek cultural zone (Magna Graecia, 6th–5th century BCE). Both require vegetarianism, ritual purity, and post-mortem soul-judgment. The Derveni Papyrus and Orphic Gold Tablets document the Orphic version; which influenced which is unresolved.
  - target: tradition-jainism
    type: parallel
    note: Jain transmigration doctrine (jīva undergoing repeated saṃsāra until liberation via moksha) and Pythagorean metempsychosis are structural parallels formulated in the same Axial Age epoch (6th–5th century BCE). Both require strict vegetarianism as a practical consequence. Mahavira and Pythagoras were contemporaries. No documented contact; parallel convergence.
  - target: tradition-catharism
    type: heir-of
    note: Cathar perfecti (12th–14th century France and northern Italy) held that souls transmigrate through repeated earthly lives until liberation through the consolamentum. This is the clearest documented case of full transmigration doctrine in Western Christianity. The Cathar doctrine's source — Bogomil dualism, Manichaean residue, or independent development — is debated; its structural parallel to Pythagorean-Platonic metempsychosis is clear.
  - target: soul-immortality
    type: sibling
    note: Soul-immortality (the soul survives bodily death) and reincarnation (the soul re-enters a new body) are logically distinct but practically linked claims. All reincarnation doctrines require soul-immortality; the Pythagorean-Platonic tradition holds both. Early Buddhism holds reincarnation but denies a permanent soul (anatta) — transmigration without a transmigrating self.

# Reincarnation

The doctrine that the soul *transmigrates* — passes from one body at death to another body at a subsequent birth — until some final liberation, exhaustion, or eschatological state is reached. The doctrine arises independently and is then variously articulated in: Indian (Upanishadic, Buddhist, Jain — the cycle of rebirth driven by karma), Greek (Orphic-Pythagorean-Platonic — *metempsychōsis*), and a residual presence in some Hellenistic-Jewish, Manichaean, and Cathar traditions.

- [[phase-2-001-rig-veda-family-books]] — earliest Indian traces; the doctrine is not yet fully developed.
- [[phase-2-012-brihadaranyaka-upanishad]] — BĀU 4.4 is among the earliest fully-articulated Indian reincarnation passages: the dying person passes on according to deeds.
- [[phase-2-016-early-buddhist-suttas]] — Buddhist reformulation: no permanent self transmigrates; what continues is a stream of conditioned dharmas (*paṭiccasamuppāda*).
- [[phase-3-002-plato-dialogues]] — Greek tradition: the *Phaedo* (the soul's pre-existence and survival), *Republic X* (the Myth of Er), *Phaedrus* (the chariot of the soul); Plato attributes the doctrine to the Pythagoreans-Orphics.

Western reception is mostly suppressed by Christian orthodoxy (Origen condemned at Constantinople II 553 partly for adjacent doctrines); the doctrine returns through Renaissance Hermetism (Ficino, Bruno), Theosophy (Blavatsky), Anthroposophy (Steiner), and modern New Age. Christian-Cathar transmigration is attested in *Liber de duobus principiis*.
