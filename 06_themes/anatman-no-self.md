# Anātman (No-Self)

## Definition
*Anātman* (Sanskrit) / *anattā* (Pali) — "no-self" or "not-self" — is the diagnostic Buddhist doctrine that there is no permanent, unchanging, independent self underlying the phenomenal aggregates of personality. The doctrine is articulated as the second of the **Three Marks of Existence** (*ti-lakkhaṇa* / *tri-lakṣaṇa*): all conditioned phenomena are *anicca* (impermanent), *dukkha* (suffering / unsatisfactory), and *anatta* (without enduring self). The Buddha's central analysis: when you look for a self in the five aggregates (*pañca-khandhā*) — body, feelings, perceptions, mental formations, consciousness — you find only the aggregates themselves, none of which individually or together constitutes a permanent self. The "self" is a designation arising from the assembling of impermanent factors, like "chariot" is a designation arising from the assembling of pole, axle, wheels, frame (the [[nagasena|Nāgasena]] chariot-simile of the [[phase-3-029-milindapanha|Milindapañha]] Mil 2.1.1).

The doctrine is **diagnostic of Buddhism's separation from surrounding traditions**: it differentiates Buddhism from Upaniṣadic Brahmanism (*ātman = brahman*), Jainism (the eternal *jīva*), and Sāṃkhya-Yoga (*puruṣa*); the Buddha is unique among Greater Magadha śramaṇa teachers in this systematic denial. Anātman is the doctrinal joint at which Buddhism and Vedānta most sharply disagree.

A passage "has" the anātman theme if it explicitly denies a permanent self, articulates the five-aggregates analysis, employs the chariot-simile or analogous reductionist analyses, or operates within the Mādhyamika "dharma-nairātmya" (no-self of phenomena) extension.

## Earliest attestations
- **Buddhist (Pali)**: *Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta* (SN 22.59; Vinaya Mahāvagga 1.6) — the Buddha's **second sermon** at Isipatana (Sarnath), traditionally delivered five days after the first sermon ([[four-noble-truths|Four Noble Truths]]). The text systematically goes through each of the five aggregates: "Form is non-self; if form were self, this form would not lead to affliction... Form is impermanent; what is impermanent is suffering; what is suffering and impermanent is not fit to be regarded as 'This is mine, this is I am, this is myself.'" Dated to the Buddha's teaching career (~-450 to -400). Multiple parallel suttas across the Saṃyutta and Majjhima Nikāyas elaborate the analysis.
- **Buddhist (Sanskrit and Chinese)**: parallel anātman expositions in the Sarvāstivāda Āgamas and Chinese parallels.
- **Buddhist (Milindapañha)**: [[phase-3-029-milindapanha|Mil 2.1.1]] — Nāgasena's chariot-simile to King Menander I is the canonical reference-statement of anātman analysis from ~-100 BCE forward.
- **Buddhist (Mādhyamika)**: [[nagarjuna|Nāgārjuna's]] [[phase-4-075-mulamadhyamakakarika|MMK]] extends anātman from persons (*pudgala-nairātmya*) to all dharmas (*dharma-nairātmya*) — not only is the self empty, but all phenomena are empty of inherent self-existence (*svabhāva*).
- **Buddhist (Mahāyāna sūtra)**: the [[phase-5-002b-diamond-sutra|Diamond Sūtra]] famously articulates the **fourfold negation of personal identity**: "no self, no being, no soul, no person" (*ātman, sattva, jīva, pudgala*).
- **Pre-Buddhist**: the Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad's "*neti neti*" (not this, not this) is a related but distinct apophatic methodology that some scholars have read as the Upaniṣadic substrate to which the Buddha is responding (Gombrich 1996); the Upaniṣadic conclusion is *ātman = brahman*, which the Buddha rejects.

## Variants
- **Early-Buddhist / Theravāda canonical**: anātman as denial of permanent self in five aggregates; the chariot-simile analysis. Reductionist personal-identity view: persons are aggregates plus designation.
- **Pudgalavāda heresy**: the early Buddhist school of the *Vātsīputrīyas* / Pudgalavādins held that there is a "person" (*pudgala*) that is neither identical with nor different from the five aggregates. Rejected by Theravāda and by Mahāyāna; the [[event-third-buddhist-council-c-250-bce|Third Buddhist Council]] under Aśoka is associated with refutation of this position via the *Kathāvatthu*.
- **Mādhyamika extension to all phenomena** (*dharma-nairātmya*): not only no self in persons, no inherent existence in any dharma. [[nagarjuna|Nāgārjuna's]] MMK is the systematic articulation; the [[phase-3-031-asthasahasrika-prajnaparamita|Aṣṭasāhasrikā Prajñāpāramitā]] is the doctrinal scripture.
- **Yogācāra reframing**: [[asanga|Asaṅga]] and [[vasubandhu|Vasubandhu's]] Yogācāra retains anātman but adds the *ālayavijñāna* ("storehouse consciousness") as the karmic-substrate explanation for personal continuity-without-self. Some critics charged this with smuggling a self back in; Yogācāra defended *ālayavijñāna* as itself empty.
- **Tathāgatagarbha tradition**: some Mahāyāna sūtras (Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra, Śrīmālā Sūtra) speak of *tathāgatagarbha* / Buddha-nature in terms that sound like a "true self." Modern scholarship (King 1991; Williams 2008) reads this as upāyic (skillful means) language — Buddha-nature is not a substantial self but a potential for awakening, articulated provisionally to comfort beings attached to self-language. The tathāgatagarbha-as-ātman position is a minority view (most fully developed in some Tibetan *gzhan stong* / "other-emptiness" lineages).

## Connections claimed
- **Polemic-against Upaniṣadic *ātman***: the most consequential disagreement. The Buddha addresses brahmins repeatedly (e.g., in the Pārāyanavagga of [[phase-2-016-early-buddhist-suttas]]) and denies their *ātman* doctrine. Gombrich 1996 reads anātman as specifically responsive to a developed Upaniṣadic *ātman* doctrine; Bronkhorst 2007 reads it as part of a broader non-Vedic Greater Magadha culture.
- **Differentiator from Jainism**: Jainism's eternal individual *jīva* (soul) is rejected by Buddhism; both traditions share the rebirth-karma framework but differ sharply on what is reborn.
- **Modern analytic-philosophy reception** (Parfit 1984; Siderits 2007; Westerhoff 2018): Buddhist anātman is the canonical pre-modern analogue of reductionist analyses of personal identity. Parfit explicitly acknowledged the parallel in *Reasons and Persons* (1984). The connection has become a standard reference-point in contemporary philosophy of mind. **The chariot-simile of Mil 2.1.1 is the canonical Buddhist reference passage in analytic-philosophical discussions.**
- **Christian-Buddhist comparative theology**: the apparent radical difference between Christian soul-doctrine and Buddhist anātman has been a central comparative-theological problem since the 19th-c. Western Buddhist reception (Olcott; Müller). Modern dialogue (Buddhist-Christian Studies journal; Masao Abe + John Cobb) treats it as the deepest soteriological-anthropological asymmetry between the two traditions.
