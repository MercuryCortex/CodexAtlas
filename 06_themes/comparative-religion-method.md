# Comparative Religion as Methodological Theme

The discipline of treating religious traditions as objects of cross-cultural comparison and history, and the methodological reflexive question of how (or whether) such comparison is legitimate. The Atlas itself operates within this methodological frame; the entry exists partly to surface the frame to view rather than to leave it hidden. The discipline runs from Max Müller's 19th-c. *Science of Religion* through phenomenology (van der Leeuw, Otto), Eliade's history-of-religions, the late-20th-c. critique (J.Z. Smith, Talal Asad, Russell McCutcheon, Tomoko Masuzawa), to cognitive science of religion (Boyer, Atran). Methodologically reflexive: every comparative claim is also a meta-claim about *the act of comparing*.

## Manifestations

- **19th-c. comparative philology.** Max Müller, *Lectures on the Science of Religion* (1873). Founded on the Indo-European philological model; "religion" treated as an analyzable family of phenomena across cultures. Genealogically connected with [[totemism]] and Tylor-Frazer-Robertson-Smith evolutionary anthropology.
- **Early 20th-c. phenomenology.** Rudolf Otto, *Das Heilige* (1917) — the *numinous* as cross-cultural religious *a priori* (cf. [[numinous]], [[hierophany]]). Gerardus van der Leeuw, *Religion in Essence and Manifestation* (1933) — systematic phenomenology, classifying religious phenomena by structure rather than tradition.
- **Mid-20th-c. history of religions.** Mircea Eliade, *The Myth of the Eternal Return* (1949), *Patterns in Comparative Religion* (1958), *The Sacred and the Profane* (1957). The peak of *universalist* comparative religion: cross-cultural patterns of [[sacred-and-profane]], [[hierophany]], [[axis-mundi]] (cf. [[world-axis]]). Joachim Wach's *Comparative Study of Religions* (1958). The Chicago history-of-religions school.
- **Late-20th-c. critique.** Jonathan Z. Smith, *Map Is Not Territory* (1978), *Imagining Religion* (1982), *To Take Place* (1987). Talal Asad, *Genealogies of Religion* (1993). Tomoko Masuzawa, *The Invention of World Religions* (2005). The "religion-as-category" critique: arguing that "religion" itself is a modern-Western concept retrojected onto cross-cultural data; that Eliade's *axis mundi*, [[sacred-and-profane]], [[hierophany]] universalisms over-generalize; that cross-cultural comparison without local-historical specificity produces distortions.
- **Cognitive science of religion.** Pascal Boyer, *Religion Explained* (2001); Scott Atran, *In Gods We Trust* (2002). A different kind of comparison: not phenomenological but cognitive-evolutionary. Identifies cross-cultural religious patterns as outputs of common cognitive mechanisms (agent-detection, predator-detection, social-monitoring).
- **The "world religions" paradigm critique.** Masuzawa 2005 demonstrates that the modern category-set "Hinduism / Buddhism / Christianity / Islam / Judaism / Confucianism / Daoism / Shinto" is a 19th-c. construction by European scholars; the very list is a methodological artifact, not a pre-given object.

## Disputes

- **The Eliade-Smith debate.** Eliade's universalism (every religion has *axis mundi*, sacred / profane) vs. Smith's deflation (these are contingent local arrangements, not universals). The mainstream of the discipline has moved toward Smith but Eliade's categories remain widely used as descriptive shorthand.
- **The "religion as colonial-Western category" critique (Asad).** Asad argues "religion" emerges as a category through Christian-Protestant-secularist developments and is then exported to colonized regions; "Hinduism," "Buddhism" as unified-religions are partly Western constructions. The critique is methodologically corrosive — if "religion" is not a stable trans-cultural category, what is comparative religion comparing? Defenders (Schilbrack, *Philosophy and the Study of Religions* 2014) argue a chastened comparative method survives the critique.
- **The cognitive-science-of-religion vs. cultural-history methods.** Boyer's universalism (religion explained by cognitive architecture common to humans) competes with Asad's particularism (religion as historically-formed Western category). The two approaches typically don't engage each other directly.

## Significance for the Atlas

- The Atlas is a comparative-religion project. Every cross-tradition theme node (every entry in `06_themes/`) is a comparative-religion claim. The methodological-theme entries (`oral-tradition-transmission`, `commentary-as-genre`, `comparative-religion-method`) make the act of comparing visible to itself.
- The Atlas's "investigation, not advocacy" principle (per [[methodology]]) is itself a methodological stance within the comparative-religion tradition — closer to Smith's chastened comparativism than to Eliade's universalist phenomenology, while still using Eliade's categorical vocabulary (sacred / profane, hierophany) descriptively.
