# Pharmakos (Greek Ritual Scapegoat)

## The ritual

The *pharmakos* (φαρμακός, plural φαρμακοί) was a human being ritually designated as a bearer of communal pollution in ancient Greek cities. The institution is best attested for Athens (at the **Thargelia** festival, late spring, honoring Apollo and Artemis) and Abdera, with references also to Massilia (modern Marseille) and Leucas. The evidence is fragmentary — no continuous description survives; the ancient sources are late (Helladius preserved in Photius, 9th c. CE; Harpocration's *Lexicon*; Istros fr. 334 FGrHist) and themselves dependent on lost earlier sources — but the institutional pattern is clear:

1. **Selection**: Two individuals — in Athenian sources, typically one male and one female, or two males representing two social categories — were chosen from among criminals, the deformed, or social marginals. Hipponax (fl. ~540–520 BCE, the earliest witness) describes them as ugly and socially base.
2. **Maintained at public expense**: The pharmakoi were fed and maintained by the city for a period before the festival — they were, paradoxically, honored guests of the community they were about to be expelled from. This is a typical feature of scapegoat rituals cross-culturally: the victim is elevated before being destroyed.
3. **Procession through the city**: The pharmakoi were led through Athens, beating and being beaten with branches of fig and squills (plants associated with purification), to absorb the city's accumulated pollution (*miasma*) — guilt, plague, bad omens — as they moved through every district.
4. **Expulsion (or execution)**: At the city boundary, the pharmakoi were driven out. Helladius (via Photius) says they were thrown into the sea at some cities; Hipponax's poems seem to imply execution by stoning at Ephesus. Jan Bremmer (1983) reviews the evidence and concludes that expulsion was the norm in Athens, with execution possible in some other cities, but that the ancient sources' reliability is uneven.

The word *pharmakon* (φάρμακον) means simultaneously **poison**, **medicine**, and **remedy** — the pharmakos *embodies the paradox*: the bearer of disease who is also its cure, the figure most harmful to and most needed by the community. This semantic instability — which is not a modern reading but an ancient Greek observation — is the conceptual heart of the ritual.

Source: Bremmer (1983) — Tier 1; Burkert (1983), ch. 3 — Tier 1; Parker (1983), ch. 7 — Tier 1.

## Cross-tradition structural parallels

The pharmakos is not unique to Greece; it is the Greek instantiation of a structural pattern attested independently across traditions.

**Levitical scapegoat / Yom Kippur (Leviticus 16):** On the Day of Atonement, the High Priest [[azazel|laid Israel's sins on a live goat]], which was then driven into the wilderness "for Azazel." The structural elements are identical: communal pollution transferred onto a designated victim; victim expelled from the community; peace (atonement, *kippurim*) restored. The English word "scapegoat" was invented by William Tyndale in 1530 for this Levitical ritual — *escape-goat*, the goat that escapes/departs. The Athenian and Levitical institutions are independent (no documented transmission), making this a cross-tradition convergence, not a diffusion finding. See [[scapegoat-mechanism]] for the comparative analysis.

**Babylonian kuppuru rites (~9th–7th c. BCE):** Mesopotamian purification rituals involving transferring sin and pollution onto animals or human victims expelled or sacrificed. David Wright (*The Disposal of Impurity*, 1987) argues for direct Babylonian influence on Leviticus 16 given Israelite-Babylonian contact during the 6th c. BCE exile — if so, this is a transmission finding (Babylon → Leviticus), not a pure convergence.

**Structural pattern summary:**

| Tradition | Ritual | Victim type | Mechanism | Resolution |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Greek (Athens) | Thargelia pharmakos | Human (criminal/marginal) | Procession → expulsion | Pollution removed; city cleansed |
| Israelite | Yom Kippur | Goat (for Azazel) | Hands on head → wilderness | Sins confessed, transferred, expelled |
| Babylonian | kuppuru | Animal / human | Ritual substitution | Impurity transferred |

## The pharmakon paradox and its appropriations

Derrida's essay "Plato's Pharmacy" (*La Dissémination*, 1972) reads Socrates' critique of *writing* in Plato's *Phaedrus* through the lens of the pharmakos/pharmakon — the structural instability of a word that means both poison and cure. Writing, like the pharmakos, is presented as *external* to the living community (a substitute for real memory) and simultaneously *essential* to it. Derrida notes: the pharmakos is inside the city as its guest, outside it as its expelled pollution; he is the city's remedy and the city's disease simultaneously. The pharmakos cannot be fixed in a binary — he is neither simply inside nor outside, neither simply guilty nor innocent.

This double-bind is precisely what Girard finds at the heart of the scapegoat mechanism: the victim is both the source of the crisis and the source of peace; both hated and venerated; both murdered and sacralized. The Derridean reading (from deconstruction) and the Girardian reading (from anthropology of religion) reach the same structural observation from different theoretical starting points.

**Pauline appropriation**: The language of pharmakos resonates directly with two Pauline texts:
- **Galatians 3:13**: "Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by *becoming a curse for us*" (*genómenos hyper hēmōn katára*) — Christ absorbs communal pollution (the curse) as the pharmakos absorbed communal miasma
- **2 Corinthians 5:21**: "God made him who had no sin *to be sin for us*" (*hamartían epoíēsen*) — the innocent one made to embody guilt, pharmacological logic of substitution

Whether Paul was consciously drawing on the Greek pharmakos ritual is contested (Hengel argues Hellenistic milieu made the connection available; Thiselton reads the Galatians text in purely Jewish-legal terms). The structural resonance is clear; the intentionality is debated.

## Pharmakos and tragedy

Walter Burkert (*Homo Necans*, 1983) argues that Greek tragedy is the institutionalized aesthetic replay of the founding scapegoat event: the tragic hero (*pharmakos*-in-disguise) is brought to the height of power, then cast down, expelled, or destroyed by the unanimous judgment of the chorus and audience. Sophocles' **Oedipus** is Burkert and Girard's paradigmatic case: Oedipus is the king who becomes the outcast, the solver of the city's riddle who *is* the city's pollution, the healer who is the disease — a figure whose trajectory structurally replicates the pharmakos.

René Girard (*Violence and the Sacred*, Part 2) develops this reading in detail: Oedipus is innocent, but he bears all the marks of the scapegoat victim — he is both inside (king of Thebes) and outside (exposed child, wanderer), both all-powerful and powerless. His expulsion resolves the plague; his return causes it. The pharmakos logic is complete.
