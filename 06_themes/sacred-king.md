# Sacred King

The ancient-Near-Eastern (and broadly ancient-Mediterranean) institution in which the king holds a *sacred* office mediating between the divine and human orders — performing key rituals (New Year, sacrifice, temple-building), embodying the cosmic order, and (in some reconstructions) ritually dying and being renewed. Distinguished from but related to [[divine-kingship]] (which emphasizes the king as a god or god-incarnate). The Cambridge Ritualists (Frazer, Jane Harrison, Hooke's *Myth and Ritual* school) argued for a near-universal pattern: the dying-and-rising sacred king as the ritual root of [[dying-rising-god]] mythology and of [[chaoskampf]]-style annual ritual combats. Modern scholarship has substantially deflated the more grandiose claims while retaining a more modest descriptive thesis.

- [[phase-1-007-code-of-hammurabi]] — Hammurabi as Marduk's appointed steward; the prologue's sacred-king theology.
- [[phase-1-013-baal-cycle]] — the Baal-Yamm and Baal-Mot combats often read as templates for royal ritual (Baal as the divine prototype of the Ugaritic king).
- [[phase-2-010-hebrew-bible-early-prophets]] — Davidic-Solomonic sacred kingship in Samuel-Kings; the king's cultic role at the temple's dedication; the messianic-royal psalms (Pss 2, 110, 132).
- [[phase-7-007-golden-bough]] — Frazer's encyclopedic accumulation of sacred-king material; the Nemi priest-king as anchor.

Modern critique: Henri Frankfort's *Kingship and the Gods* (1948) carefully distinguished Egyptian (king-as-god) from Mesopotamian (king-as-divine-steward) models; later scholarship (Postgate, van der Toorn) has further differentiated regional patterns.
