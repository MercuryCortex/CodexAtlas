# Trinitarian formula (una substantia, tres personae)

## Definition
The doctrine that God is *one in substance* and *three in person* (Latin: *una substantia, tres personae*; Greek: *mia ousia, treis hypostaseis*). The technical vocabulary is a 2nd–4th century coinage; the doctrine takes its mature form at Nicaea (325), Constantinople I (381), and in the Cappadocian synthesis. Diagnostic marker: the explicit *one-substance / three-distinction* schema, distinguishing the doctrine from modalism (one God appearing in three modes), subordinationism (Father > Son > Spirit ontologically), tritheism (three Gods), and Arianism (Son created, not co-eternal).

## Earliest attestations
- **New Testament:** triadic baptismal formula (Matthew 28:19); triadic benedictions (2 Cor 13:14); functional triadic ascriptions to Father, Son, and Spirit. The texts do not yet articulate the metaphysical formula.
- **Tertullian:** *Adversus Praxean* (~213, against the modalist Praxeas) — the first sustained Latin theological articulation. Tertullian coins *trinitas* (the Latin word "Trinity"), introduces *una substantia / tres personae*, and works out distinctions of *persona*, *substantia*, *status*. The terminology is permanent.
- **Origen:** [[phase-4-026-origen-on-first-principles]] — three eternal *hypostaseis* (Father, Son, Spirit) with subordinationist tendencies (Son and Spirit derived from Father).
- **Nicene Council (325):** *homoousios* (consubstantial) inserted into the creed against Arius; the Son is "of one substance with the Father."
- **Cappadocian synthesis** (Basil of Caesarea, Gregory of Nazianzus, Gregory of Nyssa, 360s–380s): *mia ousia, treis hypostaseis*; the *ousia* / *hypostasis* distinction stabilizes Greek terminology.
- **Constantinople I (381):** revised creed; full divinity of the Spirit affirmed against the Pneumatomachoi.
- **Augustine:** *De Trinitate* (~399–419) — the magisterial Latin synthesis; the psychological analogy (memory-intellect-will as image of trinitarian relations).

## Variants
- **Eastern (Cappadocian):** the *Monarchia* of the Father (Father as source of the Son and the Spirit, who share his substance); *hypostasis* foregrounded.
- **Western (Augustinian / Latin):** relational ontology; the divine essence as primary, the persons as subsisting relations (Father-Son: paternity-filiation; Father/Son-Spirit: spiration).
- **Economic trinity:** the Father, Son, and Spirit as God *toward us* in the economy of salvation.
- **Immanent (or ontological) trinity:** the Father, Son, and Spirit as God *in himself* eternally.
- **Modalist:** Sabellius and the patripassianists — the persons are *modes* of one divine subject, not distinct hypostases. Rejected as heresy by both Tertullian (against Praxeas) and the Nicene tradition.

## Connections claimed
- The doctrine emerges through a complex interaction of Jewish monotheist commitment, Christological reflection on Jesus's relation to God, ritual practice (baptism, the Eucharist), and Hellenistic philosophical resources (Middle Platonism, Stoicism).
- Hanson 1988 dismantles the older Harnackian narrative that "Hellenization" *imposed* metaphysical categories on a purely Hebrew Christianity; the metaphysical articulation arises from internal Christian theological pressure to make sense of how Jesus and the Spirit relate to the God of Israel.

## Disputes
- **Economic vs. immanent.** Rahner's principle ("the economic Trinity is the immanent Trinity, and vice versa") and the modern controversy over whether immanent-trinity speculation overreaches its evidential base (Moltmann, Jenson, LaCugna).
- **The *Filioque* controversy.** The Latin addition to the Nicene-Constantinopolitan Creed (the Spirit proceeds "from the Father *and the Son*") inserted in Spain ~6th c., adopted by the Carolingian church 9th c., resisted by Constantinople (Photius), became a principal *theological* cause of the Great Schism 1054. Modern ecumenical scholarship (Vatican 1995, Munich Statement 1982) has sought rapprochement.
- **Social vs. Latin trinitarianism.** Modern revival of "social trinity" framings (Moltmann, Boff, Plantinga) has been contested by recent scholars (Holmes 2012, Ayres 2004) as a misreading of the patristic tradition, which is closer to Augustinian relational unity than to a fellowship-of-three.
- **The vocabulary itself.** *Hypostasis* and *prosōpon*, *persona*, *substantia*, *ousia*, *physis* — none of these terms had a fixed technical meaning prior to the 4th-c. controversies; the doctrine and the vocabulary co-emerge.
