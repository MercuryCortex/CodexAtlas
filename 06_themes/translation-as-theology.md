# Translation as Theology

The recognition that scriptural translation is *not* a neutral linguistic operation but a theological-political act: which texts are translated, by whom, into what target language, with which lexical choices, distributed to whom — these decisions encode and produce theological-political commitments. The Letter of Aristeas already mythologizes the Septuagint translation as divinely-authorized (the seventy-two translators producing identical Greek versions independently). Luther's German Bible (1522 NT, 1534 full) and the King James (1611) decisively shaped vernacular Protestant Christianity. Ficino's Latin Hermetica (1463, see [[event-ficino-corpus-hermeticum-translation-1463]]) opened the Renaissance Hermetic reception.

- [[phase-3-006-septuagint]] — the foundational case: the Hellenistic translation that became *the* Bible of Diaspora Judaism and earliest Christianity; certain lexical choices (LXX *parthenos* at Isa 7:14, *kyrios* for *YHWH*) had massive theological downstream effect.
- [[phase-6-001-ficino-pimander]] — Ficino's 1463 Latin translation that launched Renaissance Hermetism; his ordering and selection of texts shaped a century of reception.
- [[phase-6-005-luther-95-theses]] — Luther's polemical-vernacular translation of the Bible into German is a foundational case of [[sola-scriptura]] enacted in translation; the Catholic counter-translation tradition (Douay-Rheims) is the parallel response.

Modern critical-translation theory (Nida, Venuti, Skopos theory) has formalized what was always implicit: translation is interpretation; interpretation is theology.
