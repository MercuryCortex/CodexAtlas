# Satisfaction Atonement (Anselm)

## Definition
The theory that Christ's death is required because human sin has infringed on God's *honor* (later: *justice*), and only an act of infinite worth — God himself as man, freely offering himself — can render the *satisfaction* (*satisfactio*) the divine honor/justice demands. Christ's death is owed not to the devil (ransom theory) but to God. Diagnostic marker: the categorial language of *debt*, *honor*, *satisfaction*, owed to God himself rather than to a third party. The Reformation penal-substitution variant downstream replaces *honor* with *punishment*: Christ bears the penalty owed by sinners.

## Earliest attestations
- **Anselm of Canterbury, *Cur Deus Homo*** (composed 1094–1098, while exiled at Schiavi during the Investiture Controversy): the first sustained articulation. Anselm rejects the older patristic ransom-to-the-devil schema and argues from feudal-legal categories of *debitum* (debt) and *honor*. The book is structured as a dialogue with Boso; the argument is "remoto Christo" — without presupposing Christ — for apologetic purposes.
- **Preparation:** Anselm draws on Augustine's *De Trinitate* XIII for the necessity that the redeemer be both God and man, and on the Pauline language of *redemption*; the *honor*/*debitum* schema is his own innovation, conditioned by the feudal-legal world.
- **Aquinas:** [[phase-5-024-aquinas-summa-theologiae]] *IIIa* qq. 46–49 — accepts the Anselmian framework but softens *necessity* to *fittingness* (*conveniens*) — God could have saved humanity by other means, but the Incarnation-and-Cross was supremely fitting.
- **Penal-substitution variant:** Calvin's *Institutes* II.16, Reformed orthodoxy, the Westminster Confession (1646). Christ does not satisfy honor but *bears penalty* (poena) as the sinner's legal substitute.

## Variants
- **Anselmian satisfaction (classical):** infringement of God's honor → debt → infinite satisfaction by God-man.
- **Aquinas's modification:** satisfaction as *fitting* not *necessary*; the infinite worth of Christ's self-offering as fulfillment of divine justice and demonstration of love.
- **Reformation penal substitution:** Christ legally substitutes for the elect, bearing the penalty their sin deserves. Calvin, the Reformed and Lutheran scholastics, Hodge, and most modern conservative evangelicalism.
- **Governmental theory:** Grotius (1617) — Christ's death satisfies not infringed honor but the *moral order* of the universe, which God as moral governor must uphold.
- **Modern critiques:** moral-influence retrieval (Abelard 12th c., revived by Bushnell 1866, Aulén 1931), Christus Victor retrieval (Aulén), participation theories (J.B. Torrance, Hans Boersma), feminist and womanist critiques of "cosmic child abuse" framings (Brown & Parker 1989, Rita Brock).

## Connections claimed
- Southern 1990: Anselm's argument arises in a specific feudal-legal world — the categories of *honor* and *debitum* belong to a particular cultural moment. The doctrine has historical contingency built in.
- Aulén 1931 argues the *classic* (ransom / Christus Victor) view dominated patristic Christianity and was *displaced* by Anselm's satisfaction model. The Christus-Victor typology has been hugely influential but is historically contested.
- The Reformed penal-substitution model is genuinely distinct from Anselm: Anselm operates from *honor* (a relational-feudal category), the Reformers from *poena* (a legal-criminal category).

## Disputes
- **Aulén's typology.** Aulén argued there are three discrete types — Christus Victor (classic), satisfaction (Anselm/Latin), and moral-influence (Abelard/liberal). The typology has been enormously influential pedagogically but is contested historically: most patristic and medieval authors hold versions of *several* atonement images simultaneously; the typology imposes a clarity the sources resist (Hart 2017, McIntyre 1992).
- **Anselm's feudal frame.** Whether the *honor* language is dispensable cultural packaging around an enduring theological insight, or whether it is constitutive of the theory in a way that makes the theory inseparable from feudal Christendom, is the central interpretive question (McGill 1968, Cur Deus Homo controversies in modern liberal Protestant theology).
- **Penal substitution and divine character.** Whether penal substitution renders God morally monstrous (the "divine child abuse" critique of feminist theology) or is biblically required (conservative evangelical defense) is the principal contemporary atonement controversy.
