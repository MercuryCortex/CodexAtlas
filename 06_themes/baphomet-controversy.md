# Baphomet controversy

## Definition
The cluster of historical and historiographical problems surrounding the charge — extracted under torture during the 1307–1311 phases of the [[event-trial-of-templars-1307-1314|Templar trial]] — that the [[tradition-knights-templar|Knights Templar]] worshipped or venerated an idolatrous object variously called **"Baphomet"**, a *head* (sometimes bearded, sometimes with three faces, sometimes a skull, sometimes covered in human skin), a *cat*, or an *idol* in their secret initiation rites. The charge has three distinct layers, each requiring its own evidentiary treatment.

### 1. The medieval charge (1307–1311)

In the prepared 1307 articles of accusation, drawn up by Guillaume de Nogaret's office for [[philip-iv-of-france|Philip IV]]:
- The Templars venerated, in their secret chapter meetings, an object referred to in the French confessions as "*Baphomet*" (or in some confessions as *Bafomet*, *Baffometi*, *Baphometz* — orthographic variants in the trial transcripts).
- The object was variously described as a *head* (with a beard, with three faces, with one face on front and one on back; sometimes a skull covered in human skin), or a *cat* (sometimes black, sometimes silver), or simply an *idol*.
- The object was reportedly venerated, kissed, worshipped as god-of-the-Order, source of wealth, source of fertility for the land.

**Modern scholarly consensus on the medieval charge (Barber 2006 ch. 7):**
- The confessions are not credible historical evidence of Templar practice. They are torture-extracted, contradictory across geographies (English, Aragonese, German, Cypriot tribunals largely *did not* obtain matching confessions to the French formulary), and structurally suspicious (the prepared articles antedate the confessions).
- The etymology of "Baphomet" itself is most plausibly a French/Provençal phonetic corruption of *Mahomet* / Mohammed — the medieval Latin Christian default-trope for "the false god of the Saracens." Multiple medieval Provençal sources (esp. crusade-era troubadour poetry) refer to "Bafometz" / "Baphomet" as Muhammad. The 14th-c. application to a Templar idol is consistent with the standard "Templars-have-gone-native-with-Muslim-idolatry" trope of the trial articles, not evidence of an actual head-idol.
- **No physical object answering to the description has ever been recovered, in any Templar archaeological context, across the entire 1307–present search history.** A wooden / silver / metallic ritual head used by an organization with ~5,000 European members would be expected to leave material traces. None exist.
- One *possible* non-zero-evidence connection: the Order possessed several authenticated reliquaries containing the *skulls* of saints (standard medieval Latin practice). Templar reliquary-heads of (esp.) various female saints, plus a head-reliquary associated with the cult of St. Euphemia at Limassol, may have generated the "head" element of the confessions through inquisitorial misinterpretation. Barber 2006 ch. 7, Frale 2007.

### 2. The 19th-c. occult construction (Hammer-Purgstall 1818; Lévi 1854)

Austrian Orientalist Joseph von Hammer-Purgstall's *Mysterium Baphometis revelatum* (1818) argued — philologically densely but evidentially weakly — that:
- "Baphomet" derives from a Greek phrase *βαφή Μήτεος* ("baptism of Metis" / "baptism of wisdom") or a similar Greek/Coptic Gnostic phrase.
- The Templar idol was a Gnostic / Ophite / Manichaean ritual object.
- Templar rites continued Gnostic / Manichaean / Mandaean mystery-practice.

Hammer-Purgstall's argument was rejected by serious scholarship from the start (his contemporary critic Friedrich Münter demolished the etymology) but generated enormous downstream influence — especially through:

**Eliphas Lévi (Alphonse Louis Constant), *Dogme et rituel de la haute magie* (1854–1856),** which produced the now-iconic image of "Baphomet, the Sabbatic Goat":
- Goat head with pentagram on forehead
- Female breasts
- Torch between horns
- Crossed arms with *solve* and *coagula* tattooed on them
- Hermaphroditic body

This image is **Lévi's mid-19th-c. construction**. It has no medieval Templar basis. It has subsequently become the dominant Western iconographic representation of "Baphomet" — used by Aleister Crowley's Thelema (Crowley adopted "Baphomet" as his magical-O.T.O. name), by Anton LaVey's Satanic Bible (1969, using a Lévi-derived sigil-image), and by 20th-21st-c. countercultural / Satanic / merely-edgy iconography.

### 3. The modern reception phenomenon

The Lévi image circulates as effectively the dominant Western occult-iconographic image of *something* — Templar idol, Gnostic Sophia, Satanic god, magical Hermetic hermaphrodite — but *what* it represents depends entirely on the appropriating tradition. The Satanic Temple's 2014–present *Baphomet statue project* (a Lévi-derived bronze used in First Amendment monument-religion litigation) is the most recent major instance: the iconography is Lévi, the historical referent is *officially* "religious liberty" rather than any specific medieval Templar claim.

## Variants
- **As trial-charge:** Mostly literary-rhetorical inquisitorial construction; etymology = Mahomet corruption.
- **As Gnostic idol:** A 19th-c. occult-revivalist construction (Hammer-Purgstall, Lévi).
- **As Lévi's Sabbatic Goat:** A 19th-c. occult-iconographic creation.
- **As Crowley's Thelemic O.T.O. name:** A 20th-c. esoteric appropriation.
- **As Satanic / countercultural icon:** A 20th-21st-c. mass-cultural appropriation.

## Connections claimed
- **Tier-1 medieval historical claim:** The Templars worshipped Baphomet. **Rejected by current critical scholarship.**
- **Tier-2 etymological claim:** "Baphomet" = phonetic corruption of "Mahomet." **Widely accepted etymologically.**
- **Tier-2 trial-misinterpretation claim:** Inquisitors may have misread Templar reliquary-heads. **Plausible.**
- **Tier-3 reception claim:** The Lévi Sabbatic Goat is a 19th-c. construction. **Uncontested fact.**
- **Tier-4 modern-occult claim:** Baphomet is a real esoteric reality / Gnostic Sophia / Satanic deity. *Cite for reception-history, not for primary claims.*
