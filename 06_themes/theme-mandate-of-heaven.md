# The Mandate of Heaven (Tianming)

## What it is

**Tianming** (天命, "Heaven's decree/mandate") is the Chinese doctrine that **Heaven (Tian) confers the right to rule on a virtuous dynasty and withdraws it when the ruler becomes corrupt.** First articulated in the early Western Zhou (c. 1046 BCE) to legitimate the Zhou overthrow of the older and more powerful Shang, its classic statement is the *Shao Gao* ("Announcement of the Duke of Shao") in the [[phase-2-033-shujing-book-of-documents]]: "God (Di/Shangdi), dwelling in the great heavens, has **changed his decree**" — ending the mandate of the Yin (Shang) because their last king "kept the wise in obscurity and the vicious in office," and granting it to the Zhou.

## The cross-tradition finding — conditional vs. unconditional legitimacy

The Mandate of Heaven is the sharpest ancient articulation of **conditional, revocable divine legitimacy**, and its value in the vault is comparative:

- **Contrast — Egyptian divine kingship (irrevocable).** In Egypt the living king *is* [[horus]] and the dead king *is* [[osiris]]: kingship is an ontological, cosmic office (see [[document-contendings-of-horus-and-seth]]). Frankfort (1948) makes the contrast explicit — there is no Egyptian mechanism by which Heaven un-makes a king for vice.
- **Contrast — Mesopotamian divine kingship (elective, not morally revocable).** Hammurabi is "called by name" by Anu and Enlil to make justice prevail ([[phase-1-007-code-of-hammurabi]]); the king is the gods' chosen agent, but the grant is an election, not a conditional trust a corrupt successor forfeits.
- **Parallel — the Hebrew conditional covenant.** The nearest structural cousin: legitimacy grounded in a divine grant that can be *lost by covenant infidelity*. The Deuteronomistic History ([[phase-2-019-deuteronomistic-history]]) has YHWH reject Saul and condition the throne on obedience — the Hebrew analogue to Tianming's withdrawal (Levenson 1979; Noth).
- **Contrast — European "divine right of kings."** Made monarchy divinely instituted and the king answerable to God alone (Figgis 1896), but built in *no* doctrine of a mandate that Heaven itself revokes for misrule — which is why later deposition theory had to argue *against* divine right rather than from within it.

Tianming thus sits inside the broad [[divine-kingship]] / [[sacred-king]] family as its **revocable pole**, structurally akin to the [[covenant]] and opposed to the ontological/elective divine kingships of Egypt and Mesopotamia. Its downstream consequence is large: because Heaven "sees as the people see," a failed dynasty forfeits the mandate — the classical Chinese justification of rebellion, later systematized by Mengzi.

## Relations to neighbours
- → [[divine-kingship]] — *comparative-parallel*: Tianming is the revocable form of divine legitimation, against which the irrevocable Egyptian/Mesopotamian forms are measured.
- → [[covenant]] — *structural-parallel*: conditional legitimacy that can be lost by infidelity (the Hebrew analogue).
- → [[sacred-king]] — *comparative-parallel*: the king as Heaven's "great son" (Tianzi, Son of Heaven), a filial trust rather than an identity with the god.
- → [[phase-2-033-shujing-book-of-documents]] — *primary-source*: the Shao Gao and Kang Gao chapters are the classical loci of the doctrine (see the reader entry `shujing-mandate-of-heaven`).
- → [[phase-2-019-deuteronomistic-history]] — *cross-tradition-parallel*: the conditional Deuteronomistic kingship.
