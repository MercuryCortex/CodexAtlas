# Flood Motif

A cataclysmic flood sent by the gods (or God) to destroy humanity, survived by a single forewarned righteous figure who builds a vessel to preserve human and animal life. Across the Near-Eastern and Mediterranean traditions the variants share a tight sequence of elements — divine decision, secret warning to one chosen man, construction of an ark or boat, loading of animal pairs, the flood itself, landing on a mountain, the release of birds to find dry land, a sacrifice whose smell draws the gods, and a divine covenant or vow of restraint. It is the most-studied case of cross-tradition transmission in comparative religion — and a useful test of its limits, because not every "flood myth" is the same motif.

## The cross-tradition roster
- **Sumerian — [[ziusudra]]** (Eridu Genesis; Sumerian King List): the earliest named flood-hero, warned by [[enki-ea]], granted immortality in Dilmun.
- **Old Babylonian — [[atrahasis-flood-hero]]** (the Atrahasis epic): the gods send the flood because humanity has grown too noisy; Enki warns Atrahasis through the reed wall.
- **Standard Babylonian — [[utnapishtim]]** ([[phase-1-004-gilgamesh-old-babylonian|Gilgamesh]] XI): the same hero, who recounts the flood to Gilgamesh; the fullest Mesopotamian version.
- **Hebrew — [[noah]]** (Genesis 6–9): the Mesopotamian flood-hero in Israelite form, with the Noahide covenant and the rainbow sign.
- **Greek — [[deucalion]]** and **[[pyrrha]]** (Apollodorus; Ovid): warned by Prometheus, they land on Parnassus and repopulate the earth by casting stones.
- **Vedic / Hindu — [[manu]]** and the fish **[[matsya-avatar|Matsya]]** (Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa; later Purāṇas): a fish warns Manu and tows his boat to a mountain.
- **Norse — [[bergelmir]]** (Prose Edda): the lone giant who survives the deluge of [[ymir|Ymir's]] blood — the Germanic reflex.
- **East Asian — [[tangun]]** and related traditions carry further reflexes of the deluge.

## The bird-scout — the signature parallel
The single most-cited proof of literary dependence is the **release of birds** to test whether the waters have receded. In Gilgamesh XI, Utnapishtim sends out a **dove** (it returns, finding no resting place), then a **swallow** (it returns), then a **raven** (it does not return — it has found food and dry ground). In Genesis 8, Noah sends a **raven**, then a **dove** three times (the second returns with an olive leaf; the third does not return). The episode is structurally identical and verbally close; the difference in the order and species of the birds is exactly the variation expected under transmission, not independent invention. The released [[dove]] becomes, downstream, one of the most loaded symbols in the Western tradition.

## Transmission and its limits
The **Mesopotamian → Hebrew** line is the strongest case of literary dependence in comparative religion (Heidel 1949; George 2003): the shared ark-dimensions, the bird-scout, the gods/God "smelling" the sacrifice, and the mountain landing leave little room for coincidence. The **Greek** Deucalion story is best explained by eastern-Mediterranean diffusion; the **Hindu** Manu–Matsya story is older than once assumed (Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa) and its relation to the Near-Eastern tradition is debated between diffusion and independent growth.

Two cases mark the **boundary** of the motif. The **Chinese** flood of Yu the Great ([[yu-the-great]]) is a *different* structure — a story of flood **control**, in which Yu drains and channels the waters by engineering and virtuous labour, with no chosen survivor, no ark, and no divine warning; "flood myth" is therefore not a single motif. And the **Mesoamerican** floods (the wood-people destroyed in the Popol Vuh; the Aztec Nota–Tata story) arose with no possible Old-World contact — genuine convergence, the same evidential role the [[primordial-sea-popol-vuh|Maya primordial sea]] plays for the cosmogony cluster. The flood motif is thus a mixture of one exceptionally well-documented transmission core and a periphery of diffusion and independent invention.
