# Avatar Doctrine (Avatāra)

The Vaishnava doctrine that Vishnu (and in later forms, the supreme deity generally) descends into the world in finite forms — *avatāras*, "those who cross down" — to restore *dharma* when it falters. The canonical list of ten *daśāvatāra* (Matsya the fish, Kurma the tortoise, Varaha the boar, Narasimha the man-lion, Vamana the dwarf, Parashurama, Rama, Krishna, Buddha, and the future Kalki) is consolidated by the late Puranic period.

The *Bhagavad Gita* (4.7–8) is the classical statement: *"Whenever dharma declines and adharma rises, then I send myself forth"*. The doctrine establishes a Vaishnava theology of recurrent divine intervention compatible with the cyclical Hindu cosmology — repeated descents through repeated *yugas*.

Comparative resonances (with caveats):
- Christology (Incarnation) — structural similarity often noted; *crucial* difference: avatar is recurrent, Christ's incarnation in classical theology is unique. See Parrinder 1970 for the careful comparison.
- Mahayana Buddha as appearance-body (*nirmāṇakāya*) — similar logic, doctrinally distinct.
- Ramakrishna and 19th-century Hindu universalism rebranded the avatar doctrine: Krishna, Christ, Buddha, Muhammad all as expressions of one divine reality.

- [[krishna]] — eighth avatar; the most theologically and devotionally elaborated
- [[rama]] — seventh avatar; the Ramayana hero, foundation of North Indian bhakti
- [[phase-5-001-devi-mahatmya]] — the female counterpart: Devi takes parallel descending forms in Shakta theology
