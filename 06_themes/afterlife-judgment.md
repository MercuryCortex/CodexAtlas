# Afterlife and post-mortem judgment

The dead are evaluated for moral or ritual worthiness, with consequences (paradise, annihilation, transformation, return). The Egyptian tradition develops the most elaborated judgment scene — the Weighing of the Heart against Ma'at's feather, presided over by Osiris with Anubis weighing and Thoth recording.

## Earliest articulation
- **Egyptian:** Already present in the [[phase-1-002-pyramid-texts]] (royal afterlife), democratized in [[phase-1-009-coffin-texts]] (non-royal afterlife), elaborated in [[phase-1-010-book-of-the-dead]] (especially Spell 125 — the "negative confession" before 42 judges).
- **Mesopotamian:** Bleak — netherworld (Kur, Irkalla) is a dusty grim place for all dead regardless of merit. No moral judgment in pre-Hellenistic Mesopotamian afterlife.
- **Zoroastrian:** Chinvat Bridge judgment (Phase 2) — widely seen as a key source of later Jewish/Christian/Islamic eschatology.
- **Hebrew Bible (early):** Sheol — like Mesopotamian, no moral differentiation. Differentiated afterlife emerges only late (Daniel 12; Phase 3 work).
- **Greek:** Tartarus / Elysium develops over Homeric → Orphic → Platonic period.

## Significance
Egyptian moralized afterlife stands out for being early, elaborated, and individualized (every person, not just kings). The contrast with the Mesopotamian undifferentiated netherworld is one of the sharpest religious-anthropology contrasts in the ancient Near East.
