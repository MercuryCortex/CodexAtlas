# Stoic Virtue (aretē)

The Stoic ethical thesis: *virtue alone is good, vice alone is evil, all else is indifferent*. The four cardinal virtues — *phronēsis* (practical wisdom), *dikaiosynē* (justice), *andreia* (courage), *sōphrosynē* (temperance) — are not separate qualities but aspects of a single perfected character; the wise person has all four or none. External goods (health, wealth, reputation, even life itself) are *preferred indifferents* — to be selected appropriately when available, but not necessary to happiness (*eudaimonia*). The Stoic sage is *apatheia*-possessed, unbothered by passions because passions are false judgments about what is genuinely good.

- [[phase-3-005-stoic-foundational-texts]] — the locus: Cicero *De Finibus* III, *Tusculan Disputations*; Seneca's *Epistulae Morales*; Epictetus's *Discourses*; Marcus Aurelius *Meditations*.
- [[phase-3-003-aristotle-metaphysics]] — Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics* provides the philosophical background (the doctrine of *aretē* and *eudaimonia*) which the Stoics critique and radicalize.
- [[phase-3-019-4-maccabees]] — Hellenistic-Jewish appropriation: 4 Macc 1:1–6 expounds the Stoic doctrine of reason's mastery of the passions, applied to Torah-observance under torture.

Pauline ethics borrows Stoic vice/virtue lists (Gal 5:19–23, "fruits of the Spirit" / "works of the flesh") even while displacing the Stoic anthropology. Patristic ethics (Clement of Alexandria, Origen, Ambrose, Augustine) integrates Stoic-cardinal-virtue language with Christian theology.
