# Four Noble Truths

## Definition
The Four Noble Truths (*cattāri ariya-saccāni* / *catvāri ārya-satyāni*) are the foundational diagnostic-soteriological frame of Buddhism, articulated by [[siddhartha-gautama-buddha|the Buddha]] in his first sermon at the Deer Park, Isipatana (modern Sarnath) — the *Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta* ("Setting in Motion the Wheel of Dhamma," Saṃyutta Nikāya 56.11). The structure is medical-diagnostic: identify the disease, identify its cause, identify the cure, prescribe the treatment.

1. ***Dukkha-sacca*** — the truth of *dukkha* (suffering, unsatisfactoriness, dis-ease): birth, aging, sickness, death, separation from the pleasant, contact with the unpleasant, not getting what one wants — all involve *dukkha*; the five aggregates of clinging are themselves *dukkha*.
2. ***Samudaya-sacca*** — the truth of *dukkha*'s origin: craving (*taṇhā* / *tṛṣṇā*) — the craving for sense-pleasure, the craving for existence, the craving for non-existence — is the cause.
3. ***Nirodha-sacca*** — the truth of *dukkha*'s cessation: the cessation of craving is the cessation of *dukkha*; this cessation is nirvana (*nibbāna* / *nirvāṇa*).
4. ***Magga-sacca*** — the truth of the path: the [[noble-eightfold-path|Noble Eightfold Path]] is the way to the cessation of *dukkha*.

A passage "has" the Four Noble Truths theme if it makes the diagnostic-causal-cessational-prescriptive structure explicit, or if it cites the formula directly, or if it operates within the explicit Four-Truths frame.

## Earliest attestations
- **Buddhist (Pali)**: *Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta* (SN 56.11; Pali Vinaya Mahāvagga 1.6) — the canonical first sermon. Saṃyutta Nikāya book 56 (*Sacca-saṃyutta*) is entirely devoted to the Four Truths. Dated to the Buddha's teaching career (~-450 to -400) in canonical attribution; in present extant Pali form, the standard recension is post-Aluvihāra writing-down (~-29 BCE) but the doctrinal content is recognized as foundational throughout the early Pali stratum (Aṭṭhakavagga, Pārāyanavagga, the Nikāyas).
- **Buddhist (Sanskrit and Chinese)**: parallel formulations in the Sarvāstivāda Sanskrit Āgamas (preserved in Chinese T 99 *Saṃyukta Āgama*) — the formula is consistent across schools.
- **Buddhist (Mahāyāna)**: the Four Truths are preserved in Mahāyāna sūtras but reframed: in the [[phase-5-002-heart-sutra|Heart Sūtra]]'s negation chain, "no suffering, no origination, no cessation, no path" famously negates them — not as wrong but as empty in the Mādhyamika sense.
- **Pre-Buddhist parallels** — Jain *catvāri* ("four") doctrinal formulations exist but the precise diagnostic-causal-cessational-prescriptive structure is distinctively Buddhist.

## Variants
- **Theravāda systematic exposition**: [[buddhaghosa|Buddhaghosa's]] [[phase-4-076-visuddhimagga|Visuddhimagga]] ch. 16 ("The Faculties and Truths") is the standard Theravāda articulation, with the four truths organized in three trainings (*sīla* / *samādhi* / *paññā*) frame and the *vipassanā-ñāṇa* progressive stages culminating in their direct realization.
- **Mādhyamika reframing**: [[nagarjuna|Nāgārjuna's]] [[phase-4-075-mulamadhyamakakarika|MMK]] ch. 24 famously addresses an opponent's objection that emptiness destroys the Four Truths, responding (24.20): "If all this were non-empty, as in your view, then there would be no arising and ceasing. The four noble truths would become non-existent." Nāgārjuna inverts the charge: emptiness *enables* the Four Truths because they require dependent origination, which is what emptiness names.
- **Mahāyāna soteriological frame**: the bodhisattva-ideal in Mahāyāna recontextualizes the Fourth Truth (the path): not only the eightfold path to personal nirvana, but the bodhisattva-path (*bodhisattva-mārga*) — six pāramitās — for the liberation of all beings. The Four Truths frame is preserved; the path-component is expanded.
- **Tibetan and East Asian pedagogical use**: the Four Truths are the standard introductory frame in modern Tibetan and East Asian Buddhist teaching contexts.

## Connections claimed
- **Direct borrowing**: no significant pre-Buddhist source claims direct articulation of the four-fold structure. The structural parallel to *medical diagnostic structure* of ancient Indian Āyurveda (diagnosis-cause-cure-treatment) has been noted by some scholars (Wezler 1984) — possibly a shared cultural-cognitive frame of medical-pedagogical reasoning in the Greater Magadha milieu.
- **Independent-development claims**: the standard view is that the Four Truths are a distinctively Buddhist formulation, possibly drawing on the broader śramaṇa diagnostic-soteriological style but in this specific structural form unique to the Buddha and his school.
- **Modern reception parallels** (not transmission): the Four Truths' diagnostic structure has been compared to Stoic philosophical therapy (Hadot 1995, *Philosophy as a Way of Life*) and to existential-psychotherapeutic frameworks (Kornfield 1993). The parallel is structural / functional; no claim of historical transmission.
