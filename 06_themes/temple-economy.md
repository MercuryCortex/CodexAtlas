# Temple Economy

The ancient Near Eastern (and broader pre-modern) institution in which the temple is simultaneously religious sanctuary, granary, employer, tax-collector, lender, and central node of the redistributive economy. The temple-deity is the *theological owner* of the land and herds; human officials administer in the god's name; surplus flows in as offerings and tithes and out as priestly rations, dependent-worker maintenance, and public works. Diakonoff's and Gelb's mid-20th-c. work (refined by Postgate) established that the Sumerian city-state was simultaneously a *temple-state* and a *palace-state*, with priestly and royal economic spheres overlapping. The category remains diagnostic for ANE religion: the temple is *not* a "place of worship" in the modern Protestant-private sense but an economic-administrative-cultic complex.

## Manifestations

- **Sumerian *é-temple* economy.** [[phase-1-001-kesh-temple-hymn]] — the temple as cosmic-economic-political center. The Ur III *Drehem* archive shows massive flows of sheep, grain, oil through the temple-and-palace complex.
- **Akkadian / Babylonian temple-corporations.** The *é-anna* of Inanna at Uruk, the *é-sagila* of Marduk at Babylon — institutional persistence across millennia. The Neo-Babylonian Eanna archive (~8000 tablets) documents the temple as creditor, employer of dependent workers, agricultural manager, and tax-farmer.
- **Pharaonic Egypt.** [[phase-1-002-pyramid-texts]] requires permanent priestly establishments (*ḥm-nṯr*) funded by royal endowment. By the late New Kingdom Amun's temple at Karnak controlled an estimated one-third of Egyptian agricultural land.
- **Israelite Jerusalem temple.** [[phase-2-019-deuteronomistic-history]] — Solomon's temple as royal-cultic complex; priesthood inheriting tithes (Num 18); the post-exilic Second Temple administering Yehud as a recognized temple-state under Persian and Hellenistic rule.
- **Greco-Roman.** Temple economies persist (Delphi's Apollo as banker; Egyptian temples under Rome — Bagnall) but are typically smaller and increasingly state-controlled.
- **Aztec.** The *teocalli* tribute system: provincial tribute flows to Tenochtitlan; human-sacrificial tribute one component within a broader tribute economy. See [[phase-8-004-florentine-codex-sahagun]].

## Significance

- The "secularization" of ancient religion in modern scholarship has often projected modern church-state separation backward. Temple-economy resists that projection.
- The Hebrew prophetic critique of the temple cult (Amos, Isa 1, Jer 7) makes sharper sense once the temple's economic functions are foregrounded.
- The temple was always economic — this is not a corruption of an originally-pure religion.
