# Royal Hymn

A poetic-liturgical text praising a reigning (or recently-dead) king as cosmically significant: chosen by the gods, conqueror of enemies, builder of temples, dispenser of justice. Distinguished from neutral panegyric by liturgical use and theological claims. Royal hymns are the literary back-formation of [[divine-kingship]] and [[sacred-king]] ideology: they make the king's god-given role audible in the cult.

## Manifestations

- **Sumerian *Shulgi hymns*.** [[phase-1-023-shulgi-hymns]] — Shulgi of Ur (r. ~2094–2047 BCE) commissioned a corpus of 30+ self-praise hymns, in which he is divine son, swift runner, scribe, warrior, builder. Sets the template for ANE royal self-praise (Klein 1981).
- **Egyptian royal hymns.** Embedded in [[phase-1-002-pyramid-texts]] and later New Kingdom inscriptions: the king as Horus-in-life, Ra's son, victorious over chaos; Akhenaten's *Great Hymn to the Aten* contains royal-hymnic elements blended with monotheizing theology.
- **Hebrew royal psalms.** [[phase-2-005-davidic-psalms]] — Pss 2, 18, 20, 21, 45, 72, 89, 101, 110, 132, 144 are conventionally classified as royal psalms (Gunkel; Eaton). Psalm 2's "you are my son, today I have begotten you" applies the ANE adoptive-divine-sonship formula to the Davidic king. Psalm 110's "you are a priest forever in the order of Melchizedek" is read messianically in later Jewish and Christian reception.
- **Hittite royal prayers.** The *Plague Prayers of Muršili II* — a royal hymn-prayer hybrid, the king confessing dynastic guilt and asking the gods to lift the plague.
- **Ugaritic royal hymn-fragments.** The *Keret* and *Aqhat* epics contain royal-blessing material; the *RS 24.252* hymn celebrates a deified deceased king.

## Disputes

- **Whether Hebrew royal psalms were composed for actual coronation / liturgical use or are literary fictions.** Mowinckel (1922) and Eaton (1976) argued for real cultic use in a pre-exilic Jerusalem enthronement festival; minimalists (Auld) doubt the festival hypothesis. The Sumerian and Hittite parallels strengthen the cultic case.
- **The transition from royal hymn to messianic prophecy.** The royal psalms become messianic-prophetic texts in Second Temple and early Christian reading (Ps 2 in Acts 4:25–26; Ps 110 in Matt 22:41–46). The "messianic" use is reception-historical; the original royal-hymn function is reconstructive.
