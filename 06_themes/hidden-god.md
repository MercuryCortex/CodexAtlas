# Hidden god (Deus Absconditus)

## Definition

The supreme divine reality is **unknowable in principle** — beyond predicates, beyond being itself, beyond every concept that could pretend to grasp it. Affirmative (*cataphatic*) statements about the highest god are at best provisional concessions to limited human cognition; the only adequate theology is *apophatic* — negation: God is *not* this, *not* that, *not* even (in the deepest versions) "God" in any sense humans can mean by the word.

The pattern is distinct from mere divine transcendence (a high god who is far away or only loosely involved with creation). The hidden god is hidden **in principle**: knowable only by what it is *not*, or only through emanated lower hypostases that "appear" while the source remains concealed. The apophatic move is the active production of unknowability through systematic linguistic negation — not a failure of theology but its supreme accomplishment.

This is one of the most consistently attested cross-tradition theological structures in world religion. The convergence across Kabbalistic Judaism, Christian apophaticism, Akbarī Sufism, Daoism, Advaita Vedānta, and Mahāyāna Buddhism is so close that it constitutes one of the most striking cross-tradition findings in the comparative study of religion (Sells 1994).

## MASSIVE-WIN — the great apophatic convergence

The cross-tradition agreement of Kabbalistic [[ein-sof]] / Plotinian [[the-one-plotinus]] / Akbarī [[al-haqq]] / Eckhartian [[the-godhead-eckhart]] / Daoist *Dao* / Advaitic *nirguṇa Brahman* / Madhyamaka *śūnyatā* / Zuni [[awonawilona]] is so structurally close that **the same negative-theological language appears nearly verbatim across all of them**:

- "Beyond being, beyond non-being"
- "Not this, not this"
- "No predicate can be applied"
- "Even the name we use is provisional"
- "Knowable only by un-knowing"
- "The source from which both being and non-being arise"

Sells 1994 (*Mystical Languages of Unsaying*) argues that this is **not coincidence and not transmission** — it is the *performative structure of apophasis itself* recurring. Whenever a tradition follows the logic of "the supreme reality cannot be limited by predicates" rigorously, the same language emerges, because the language is dictated by the logic.

The central claim is therefore **convergent emergence** of a theological structure: independent traditions arriving at near-identical apophatic vocabularies because each is reasoning through the same problem with the same constraints. Documented historical transmission contributes to some specific cases (Plotinus → Pseudo-Dionysius → Eckhart is a clean transmission chain; Kabbalah and Sufism almost certainly engaged with Neoplatonist sources via Arabic philosophy), but the **Awonawilona ↔ Ein Sof** convergence cannot be explained by transmission (zero contact between 13th-c. Castilian Kabbalists and pre-Conquest Zuni; documented in Tedlock 1983) and is frequently cited (Sells 1994; Tedlock 1983) as a striking case of cross-cultural theological convergence.

## The apophatic operation

The apophatic move has a consistent technical structure across traditions:

1. **The cataphatic affirmation** — God is good, just, powerful, one, being itself.
2. **The first negation** — God is not good in the way creatures are good; not just in the way humans are just; not powerful in the way kings are powerful.
3. **The negation of the negation** — God is not "not-good" either; the negation is not privation but supereminence. God is *hyper-good* (beyond good and not-good).
4. **The silence** — at the apex, even *hyper-* concepts are dropped; the apophatic theologian holds silence. Pseudo-Dionysius: "leaving behind everything... ascend by unknowing toward the union, as far as possible, with him who transcends all being and all knowledge."

This is **not** the same as agnosticism. Agnosticism says: "we cannot know whether there is a God." Apophaticism says: "God IS — but God's mode of being is such that to know God is to un-know God." The hidden god is fully real; the hiddenness is metaphysical, not epistemic.

## Tension with cataphatic / kataphatic theology

Most religious traditions are **dual**: they have BOTH affirmative and negative theologies, in productive tension.

- Christian tradition has both the *via positiva* (Aquinas's analogical predication, the creeds' positive affirmations) AND the *via negativa* (Pseudo-Dionysian apophaticism). Both are needed: pure cataphaticism becomes idolatry (mistaking concepts for God); pure apophaticism becomes silence (the tradition stops speaking).
- Kabbalistic Judaism distinguishes *Ein Sof* (utterly hidden) from the *Sefirot* (the structured emanation that mediates Ein Sof to creation). Theurgical Kabbalah operates entirely in the Sefirot; apophatic discourse names the unreachable beyond.
- Sufism has both *al-Ḥaqq* (the Real, hidden) and the 99 Beautiful Names (cataphatic divine attributes). The Akbarī tradition holds these as the same Real seen from two sides.
- Advaita Vedānta distinguishes *nirguṇa* (qualityless) Brahman from *saguṇa* (qualified) Brahman / Ishvara. Practice may begin with the saguṇa form; realization terminates in the nirguṇa.

The tension is **productive**: cataphasis gives the tradition something to say; apophasis prevents the tradition from confusing what it says with what God is. The traditions that lose one pole become brittle.

## Historical disputes

**(1) Biblical roots vs. Platonic intrusion.** Some scholars (von Rad, Westermann) see the *deus absconditus* of Second Isaiah as a strictly Israelite-prophetic motif — God hides *in judgment* during exile, not metaphysically by nature. Others (Bousset, Quispel) see the philosophical apophatic-absolute of Gnostic and Hermetic texts as a Platonic graft onto biblical theism. Most current scholarship (Quispel, Layton, Pearson) sees both streams **converging** in Hellenistic Judaism (especially [[philo-of-alexandria]]) and early Christianity — the biblical hiding-God and the Platonic transcendent-One fused via Middle Platonism into the apophatic theology Christianity inherited from late antiquity.

**(2) Plotinus's priority vs. independent Gnostic-Hermetic apophaticism.** Plotinus's mature articulation in the *Enneads* (~250–270 CE) is the most rigorous philosophical account. But the cascading-negations style of the *Apocryphon of John* (~150 CE) predates Plotinus by a century. The scholarly consensus is that both draw from a shared Middle Platonist common stock — Numenius, Eudorus, the second-century Platonist environment — rather than one borrowing from the other.

**(3) The Eckhart trials.** Meister Eckhart's apophatic formulations ("If God could die, the Godhead would not die"; "God's Godhead alone is hidden") provoked the 1329 papal bull *In agro dominico* condemning 28 propositions from his sermons. The trials raised the question Christian theology has never quite settled: does apophaticism dissolve the distinction between God and creature (the heresy charge) or save it (the Eckhartian defense)? The same tension shows up in Akbarī Sufism (heresy charges against Ibn ʿArabī from Ibn Taymiyya through modern Salafism) and in Zen (the "mountains are mountains / mountains are not mountains / mountains are mountains again" formulation).

**(4) The Awonawilona-Ein Sof problem.** Tedlock 1983's explicit comparison of Zuni Awonawilona to Kabbalistic Ein Sof was initially controversial — comparative-religion scholars (post-Smith) are wary of pan-cultural structural claims. But the parallel is so textually specific (both are the "unmanifest mist before manifestation," both are the unmanifest that "thinks itself" into the differentiated cosmos, both are addressed in nearly identical apophatic negations) that subsequent scholarship has accepted it as a clean convergent-emergence case — strengthening rather than weakening Sells 1994's argument about apophatic discourse's structural universality.
