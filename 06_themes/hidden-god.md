# Hidden God (Deus Absconditus)

The supreme reality is *unknowable*, *ineffable*, beyond predicates, beyond being itself. Cataphatic statements about the highest god are at best provisional; the only adequate theology is *apophatic* — negation. Distinguished from mere divine transcendence (a high god who is far away) — the hidden god is hidden *in principle*: knowable only by what it is not, or only through emanated lower hypostases that "appear" while it remains concealed.

## Manifestations
- **Second Isaiah:** "Truly you are a God who hides yourself" (Isa 45:15) — surely you are a *deus absconditus*. See [[phase-3-001-second-third-isaiah]]. Here hiddenness is contextual (exilic theodicy) rather than metaphysical.
- **Apocryphon of John:** [[phase-4-002-apocryphon-of-john]] — the supreme Father (the Invisible Spirit / [[the-monad-gnostic]]) is described in cascading negations: not corporeal, not incorporeal, not great, not small, etc.
- **Valentinian Pleroma:** [[phase-4-003-gospel-of-truth]] — the Father is unknown to all the aeons; salvation begins when the Father chooses to be known through the Son.
- **Hermetism:** [[phase-4-011-corpus-hermeticum-i]] — Poimandres reveals a Father who is the source of Mind, beyond apprehension by the senses, accessible only through nous.
- **Neoplatonism:** Plotinus's One ([[the-one-plotinus]]) — beyond being, beyond intellect, beyond predication. See [[phase-4-019-plotinus-enneads]] V.1, VI.9. The most rigorous philosophical articulation of the hidden god in antiquity.
- **Manichaean Father of Greatness:** [[father-of-greatness]] dwells in the unreachable Land of Light.
- **Negative theology in Christian tradition:** Influenced by Plotinus, carried forward by Pseudo-Dionysius and the Cappadocians.

## Disputes
- **Source.** Negative-theological vocabulary in 2nd–3rd c. Gnostic and Hermetic texts predates Plotinus's mature articulation — suggesting these draw on a shared Middle Platonist common stock rather than one borrowing from another.
- **Biblical roots vs. Platonic intrusion.** Some scholars (von Rad, Westermann) see the *deus absconditus* of Second Isaiah as a strictly Israelite-prophetic motif (God hides in judgment); others (Bousset, Quispel) see the philosophical absolute behind Gnostic and Hermetic versions as a Platonic graft. Most current scholarship sees both streams converging in Hellenistic Judaism and early Christianity.
