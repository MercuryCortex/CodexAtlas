# Sola Scriptura

The Reformation doctrine that Scripture alone — without Roman magisterium, papal pronouncement, conciliar decree, or unwritten apostolic tradition — is the sufficient, supreme, and norming authority for Christian doctrine and practice. One of the so-called "Five Solas" of the Reformation. Distinct from *solo scriptura* (Scripture in radical isolation from any ecclesial mediation); the Reformers retained a high view of creeds and councils — but only as derivative, normed-by-Scripture authorities. The doctrine is a Protestant-distinctive negation of the Roman-medieval two-source (Scripture + Tradition) theory of revelation; it produces a permanent Protestant problem of *how to adjudicate* among competing biblical interpretations once Magisterial arbitration is rejected.

## Manifestations
- **Luther's stand:** [[phase-6-005-luther-95-theses]] — the 1517 *95 Theses* are technically a call for debate, not an *sola scriptura* manifesto; but by the 1519 Leipzig Debate Luther denies the infallibility of councils and pope, anchoring authority in Scripture; at the 1521 Diet of Worms ("Here I stand: I cannot do otherwise, God help me, amen") he refuses to recant except on scriptural-rational grounds.
- **Luther's *Bondage of the Will*:** [[phase-6-007-luther-bondage-of-will]] — Luther's 1525 reply to Erasmus's *De Libero Arbitrio*: Scripture is *perspicuous* in essentials, requiring no magisterial interpretation; humility before the divine Word is the Christian posture.
- **Calvin's systematization:** [[phase-6-010-calvin-institutes]] — Calvin's 1559 *Institutes* I.6–10 develops the doctrine of Scripture's authority as *self-authenticating* via the inner witness of the Holy Spirit (*testimonium internum Spiritus Sancti*); the Spirit confirms scriptural authority in the believer; Catholic-style appeals to magisterium are not just unnecessary but reverse the proper authority structure.
- **Reformed and Anglican variants.** The Westminster Confession (1647) gives the locus classicus systematic statement. The Anglican Articles (Article VI, "Of the Sufficiency of the Holy Scriptures for Salvation") are softer — Scripture alone is sufficient for *salvation*, but tradition retains a real if subordinate authority. Anabaptist and Restorationist movements push toward *solo* scriptura; classical Reformers don't.

## Disputes
- **Catholic and Orthodox response.** Trent's 1546 Decree on Sacred Scripture insists revelation is contained both in Scripture *and* in unwritten apostolic Traditions, both received with equal piety. The Eastern Orthodox version: Scripture is *within* Tradition, not over it; the tradition is the matrix that *produced* and *preserves* Scripture. *Sola scriptura* is Catholically and Orthodoxly heretical.
- **Internal-Protestant problem.** Once Magisterial arbitration is gone, who decides among competing readings? The history of Protestantism is partly the history of denominational fragmentation as different communities make different exegetical-systematic moves. The hermeneutical "right of private judgement" was both an emancipatory and a fragmenting principle.
- **Patristic continuity claim.** Mathison and other Reformed apologists argue *sola scriptura* simply formalizes the patristic and medieval principle that no doctrine binding on the Church can lack scriptural support; opponents argue this either reads the principle anachronistically or makes the doctrine essentially Catholic.
