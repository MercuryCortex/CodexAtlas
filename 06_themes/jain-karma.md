# Jain Karma — Karma as Subtle Matter

Jainism's karma doctrine is unique in the history of religion and philosophy: karma is not a moral accounting mechanism or a metaphysical law of cause and effect, but a *literal subtle physical substance* (*karma-pudgala*) — fine-grained particles of matter that accrete to the soul (*jīva*) through action, thought, and speech. The soul itself is inherently pure, luminous, and all-knowing; its entanglement in *saṃsāra* (the cycle of rebirth) is caused by its being literally weighted down and colored by accumulated karmic matter.

Eight types of karma (*aṣṭa-karma*) with distinct effects:
1. *Jñānāvaraṇīya* — knowledge-obscuring
2. *Darśanāvaraṇīya* — perception-obscuring
3. *Vedanīya* — feeling-producing
4. *Mohanīya* — deluding (the most dangerous — causes attachment and aversion)
5. *Āyus* — lifespan-determining
6. *Nāma* — body-type determining
7. *Gotra* — social status-determining
8. *Antarāya* — obstructing (blocks the soul's natural powers of energy, charity, gain)

Liberation (*mokṣa*) requires two simultaneous operations: preventing *new* karmic inflow (*saṃvara* — "stopping") and burning off accumulated karma through austerity (*nirjarā* — "shedding"). Complete liberation is the state of a *siddha*: a soul with zero karmic matter, which then ascends to the apex of the universe (the *siddha-śilā*) and dwells there forever in perfect omniscience.

## Cross-tradition parallels

**The unique feature: karma as literal matter**
Every other tradition that uses the concept of karma (Buddhist, Hindu, Sikh) treats it as a *functional-causal* process — the traces or seeds (*vāsanā*, *saṃskāra*) that condition future experience. Jainism alone makes karma ontologically *physical matter*. This means:
- Karma is accumulated not only by intentional acts but by *any* physical action that disturbs living beings — including breathing (which kills microorganisms)
- The strictest Digambara monks must not swat insects, can eat only what they can hold in their cupped hands, and must not even use a broom with bristles that could harm microbes
- Liberation is, literally, a physical process: the purification and lightening of the soul

**Gnostic pneuma trapped in hylē**
The Gnostic pneumatic (divine spark) trapped in material darkness (*hylē*) by the archons maps structurally onto the Jain *jīva* (soul) weighed down by karma-matter. In both:
- The true self is luminous / pure / divine in nature
- Its bondage is the result of attachment to / entanglement in matter
- Liberation requires extracting the self from the material prison
The Jain doctrine differs in treating the karmic matter as *self-generated* (the soul attracts karma through its own passions); the Gnostic pneuma is imprisoned by external cosmic powers. But the underlying logic of luminous soul trapped in heavy matter is identical.

**Trika *āṇava-mala* (contraction of consciousness)**
In Kashmir Shaivism, the root impurity (*āṇava-mala*) is the soul's sense of being a limited, contracted individual rather than infinite Śiva-consciousness. This is an *epistemological* bondage — wrong self-identification — rather than a physical accretion. Jain karma is the reverse: purely *physical*. The Trika diagnosis is "you think you're less than you are (infinite consciousness)"; the Jain diagnosis is "you're carrying more than you should be (subtle matter)." Both converge on the liberation as a return to primordial purity — but the mechanisms are polar opposites.

**Buddhist *cetanā*-karma — the foil**
Buddhist Abhidharma defines karma as *cetanā* (intentional action / volition) — the mental act of willing, not the physical act itself. Unintentional killing (an unavoidable treading on an insect) generates no Buddhist karma. Jain karma-theory makes this distinction impossible: any physical disturbance of any life-form accretes karma, regardless of intention. This difference tracks to the metaphysical split: Buddhism denies any permanent self (*anātman*); Jainism posits a distinct, real *jīva* that literally accumulates matter. The Buddhist-Jain debate on this point (preserved in the *Sūtrakṛtāṅga*) is one of the earliest documented philosophical debates in history.
