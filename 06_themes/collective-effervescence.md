# Collective Effervescence

Durkheim's term in *The Elementary Forms of the Religious Life* (1912) for the heightened collective emotional state arising in densely gathered ritual contexts — the corroboree, the festival, the revival, the political rally — in which individuals feel themselves possessed by a force greater than themselves. Durkheim identified this state as the *sociogenic* origin of the sense of the sacred: the energy of the collective is experienced as a transcendent power and named, symbolized, totemized. Religion is the way society makes itself an object of reverence to itself.

- [[phase-7-010-elementary-forms-religious-life]] — Durkheim's locus classicus; Australian Aboriginal corroboree as the ethnographic anchor.

The concept has had a long subsequent career: in Victor Turner's *communitas*, in crowd psychology (Le Bon, Tarde), in studies of revival movements (William James), in the sociology of religious experience (Stark, Bainbridge), and in recent neuro-anthropological work on synchronized ritual. Critics (Steven Lukes 1973 *Émile Durkheim*) note the difficulty of operationalizing the concept and the suspicion that Durkheim derives the existence of effervescence from his theoretical need for a sociogenic origin of the sacred.
