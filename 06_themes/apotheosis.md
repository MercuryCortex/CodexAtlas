# Apotheosis (Deification of Mortals)

The making-divine of a mortal — by ritual decree (Roman imperial cult), by gradual ascent through philosophical discipline (Platonic, Christian *theosis*), by post-mortem reception into the company of the gods (Greek hero-cult, Daoist *xian*), or by canonization (Christian sainthood as a softer form). The motif occupies an unstable theological space: monotheistic traditions resist saying *deification* in the strong sense, but each develops variants — Christian *theosis*, Sufi *fanāʾ*, Jewish *zaddik*-veneration — that approach it.

## Manifestations

- **Greek hero-cult.** Hercules' apotheosis on Mt. Oeta, Asclepius' raising from death to the stars; Hellenistic city-cults of heroes. The hero is not equal to the Olympians but receives sacrifice and is divine in a lower register.
- **Hellenistic ruler-cult.** Alexander's deification (formally proposed by the Greek cities; he himself wavered); the Ptolemaic theos-soter cults (Ptolemy I and Berenice as *Theoi Sōtēres*); Seleucid theos-epiphanic cult.
- **Roman imperial cult.** [[phase-3-024-suetonius-twelve-caesars]] — the *consecratio* by senatorial decree of deceased emperors (Augustus, Claudius — Seneca's *Apocolocyntosis* mocks Claudius's deification — Vespasian's deathbed "I think I am becoming a god"). The *divus* (= deified-by-decree) is technically distinct from *deus* (god). Fishwick 1987 is the systematic study.
- **Christian *theosis*.** [[phase-4-030-pseudo-dionysius]], [[phase-5-003-maximus-confessor-ambigua]] — "God became man so that man might become God" (Athanasius, *De Inc.* 54). The Greek Fathers' deification tradition develops via Irenaeus, Athanasius, the Cappadocians, Maximus, Palamas, into the Eastern Orthodox dogma of participation-in-the-divine-energies. Russell 2004 traces the development. (Cf. [[in-christ]], [[the-godhead-eckhart]].)
- **Plotinian "becoming god."** [[phase-4-019-plotinus-enneads]] — Plotinus speaks of the soul *becoming the One* in mystical union. Cf. [[henosis]].
- **Daoist *xian*.** The immortal — adept-practitioner of inner alchemy who attains immortality (*chang-sheng pu-ssu*); the Eight Immortals are the iconic case. Lower-tier divinization through cultivation.
- **Christian sainthood.** The post-Constantinian canonization tradition softens apotheosis: saints are not gods but mediators-with-special-divine-favor. The line is theologically maintained but devotionally fuzzy in popular Catholicism, Orthodoxy, and Anglicanism.

## Disputes

- **The status of the Roman imperial cult.** Older scholarship (Nock) treated it as a "political loyalty test" with little religious content; current scholarship (Fishwick, Price *Rituals and Power*) treats it as genuinely religious, with provincial enthusiasm and theological seriousness. The Christian refusal to sacrifice to the emperor's *genius* makes sense only if the act was genuinely religious.
- **Whether *theosis* preserves Creator-creature distinction.** Eastern Orthodox Palamite theology says yes (participation in energies, not essence). Western Catholic theology has often been suspicious. Protestant theology has often been hostile, treating *theosis*-language as semi-Pelagian or pantheistic.
- **Whether the Christian doctrine of the resurrection-body's *glorification* (Phil 3:21, 1 Cor 15:42–49) is structurally a form of apotheosis or theologically distinct.** The Greek Fathers said yes; modern Reformed theology says no.
