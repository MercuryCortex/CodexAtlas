# Falsafa (the Greek-Arabic philosophical tradition)

## Definition
**Falsafa** (Arabic *falsafa*, فلسفة; from Greek *philosophia*) is the **Greek-Arabic philosophical tradition** — the tradition of philosophical-scientific inquiry developed in the Arabic-language Islamic world from the 9th c. CE onward, founded on the systematic translation of Greek philosophical, scientific, medical, and astronomical works into Arabic and the subsequent original development of that material in dialogue with Quranic monotheism, Islamic kalām theology, and Persian-Islamic intellectual culture. The four canonical falāsifa are **[[al-kindi|al-Kindi]] (~801-873) → [[al-farabi|al-Farabi]] (~872-950) → [[ibn-sina|Ibn Sīnā / Avicenna]] (980-1037) → [[ibn-rushd|Ibn Rushd / Averroes]] (1126-1198)**, with [[ibn-tufayl|Ibn Ṭufayl]] (~1105-1185) and the *Brethren of Purity* (10th c., [[phase-5-011-rasail-ikhwan-al-safa|*Rasāʾil Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ*]]) and [[suhrawardi|Suhrawardī]] (1154-1191) and [[mulla-sadra|Mullā Ṣadrā]] (1571-1640) extending the tradition in major variations.

Distinctive features of falsafa, by contrast with kalām (Islamic dogmatic theology) on one side and Sufism (Islamic mysticism) on the other:
1. **Demonstrative method.** Falsafa proceeds by logical demonstration (*burhān*) from first principles, in deliberate continuity with the Aristotelian *Posterior Analytics* model of science. Kalām's dialectical method (*jadal*) is regarded as inferior; mystical insight is regarded as supplementary, not foundational.
2. **Greek-Arabic source-base.** The falsafa curriculum is the Greek philosophical corpus as translated into Arabic by the 9th-c. Bayt al-Ḥikma translation programs and revised through the next two centuries: Aristotle's *Organon* + *Physics* + *De Anima* + *Metaphysics* + *Nicomachean Ethics*; Plato's *Republic* + *Laws* + *Timaeus*; substantial portions of the Neoplatonic tradition (Plotinus via the [[phase-5-048-theology-of-aristotle-arabic|*Theology of Aristotle*]], Proclus via the *Liber de Causis*); Galenic medicine; Ptolemaic astronomy; Euclid; Diophantus; Hellenistic-medical and Hellenistic-mathematical sources.
3. **Aristotle-Plato harmonization, mediated by Neoplatonism.** Following late-antique Alexandrian Neoplatonism (Ammonius, Simplicius, Philoponus), the falsafa tradition reads Plato and Aristotle as substantially harmonized — Aristotle providing the demonstrative-scientific framework, Plato providing the metaphysical-political vision; both saying-essentially-the-same-thing in different idioms. The harmonization is partly an artifact of the misattribution of the [[phase-5-048-theology-of-aristotle-arabic|*Theology of Aristotle*]] (actually Plotinus) to Aristotle, but al-Farabi's *Harmony of the Opinions of the Two Sages* makes the harmonization-thesis explicit.
4. **Necessary-Being metaphysics.** Avicenna's mature falsafa elaborates the Necessary-Being / contingent-being structure (essence vs. existence; modality of being; the emanational cosmology) as a philosophical natural-theology compatible with Islamic monotheism. This is the most distinctive philosophical contribution of the falsafa tradition.
5. **Political philosophy of the imam-philosopher-prophet.** Following al-Farabi's reframing of Plato's *Republic*, the falsafa political tradition centers the imam-philosopher-prophet as the apex figure (the prophet's intellect is, like the philosopher's, in conjunction with the Active Intellect — the prophetic imagination then communicates the resulting intelligibles to the multitude in revealed-religious form). This produces a *philosophy of revelation* in which philosophy is the inner content and revealed religion is the outer political-pedagogical form.

## Earliest attestations
- **By tradition, where do we first see falsafa**:
  - **Arabic, 9th c.** [[al-kindi|al-Kindi]]'s *Fī al-falsafa al-ūlā* (~840) is the foundational text. The Bayt al-Ḥikma translation movement (Gutas 1998) is the institutional context.
  - **Persianate Islamic East, 10th-11th c.** [[al-farabi|al-Farabi]]'s political philosophy and [[ibn-sina|Avicenna]]'s metaphysics elevate falsafa to its mature classical form.
  - **Andalusi-Maghribi falsafa, 11th-12th c.** Ibn Bājja, [[ibn-tufayl|Ibn Ṭufayl]], [[ibn-rushd|Averroes]] develop the Western branch culminating in Averroes's Aristotelian commentaries.
  - **Post-Avicennian Eastern Persian falsafa, 12th-17th c.** [[suhrawardi|Suhrawardī]]'s *ḥikmat al-ishrāq* (Illuminationism) and [[mulla-sadra|Mullā Ṣadrā]]'s *ḥikmat al-mutaʿāliya* (transcendent wisdom) extend the falsafa tradition in directions absorbing Sufi metaphysics and Shīʿī esoteric theology.

## Variants
- **Classical falsafa.** The four canonical falāsifa (al-Kindi → al-Farabi → Avicenna → Averroes).
- **Post-Avicennian Persian falsafa.** Suhrawardi → Mulla Sadra; absorbs Sufi metaphysics and Shīʿī esoteric theology; continues vigorously in the Persianate world through the 19th c.
- **Andalusi peripatetic falsafa.** Ibn Bājja → Ibn Ṭufayl → Averroes; emphasizes the *mutawaḥḥid* ("solitary philosopher") and the political-philosophical separation of demonstrative from rhetorical / political modes of teaching.
- **Brethren of Purity (Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ).** 10th-c. Ismāʿīlī-affiliated philosophical-religious encyclopedia ([[phase-5-011-rasail-ikhwan-al-safa|*Rasāʾil*]]) that integrates falsafa with Pythagorean number-mysticism, Hermetic astral theology, and Shīʿī esoteric political eschatology. Sometimes treated as a separate stream rather than mainstream falsafa.
- **Latin Averroism / Latin Avicennism.** The 12th-13th-c. Latin reception in Christian Europe — falsafa is recognized as such in Aquinas's distinction of *philosophus* (= Aristotle), *Avicenna*, *Algazel* (= Ghazālī), *Commentator* (= Averroes), *Rabbi Moyses* (= Maimonides). Latin Averroism at Paris (Siger of Brabant, Boethius of Dacia, ~1265-1280) develops a strong reading of Averroes condemned in the [[event-trial-of-templars-1307-1314|13th-c. theological climate]] (specifically Bishop Tempier's 219 Propositions of 1277).
- **Maimonidean Jewish falsafa.** The Andalusi-Jewish reception ([[phase-5-019-maimonides-guide-for-the-perplexed|Maimonides's *Guide*]] is substantially al-Farabi- and Avicenna-formed; Gersonides, Levi ben Gerson, Isaac Abravanel) develops a parallel Jewish falsafa.

## Connections claimed
- **Aristotle (Greek) → falsafa → Latin scholasticism.** The single most consequential intellectual-transmission edge of the medieval Mediterranean. Gutas 1998 makes the case that the Greek-into-Arabic translation movement at the Bayt al-Ḥikma (8th-10th c.) is the necessary precondition for the 12th-c. Toledo-school Arabic-into-Latin translation movement (Gerard of Cremona, Avendauth + Gundissalinus, Michael Scot) that gives Latin Europe its Aristotle. **Without falsafa there is no Aquinas in his historical form.**
- **Plotinus (Greek Neoplatonism) → [[phase-5-048-theology-of-aristotle-arabic|*Theology of Aristotle*]] → falsafa.** The Plotinian core of falsafa via the misattributed pseudo-Aristotelian *Theology* is foundational; the misattribution was corrected only in the 15th-16th c.
- **Falsafa → Latin Avicennism / Latin Averroism.** Aquinas engages Avicenna more than any other non-Christian philosopher (~400 citations); Averroes is *the Commentator*; the medieval Latin Aristotle is in substantial part the Avicennian-Averroist Aristotle.
- **Maimonides reads al-Farabi and Avicenna.** [[phase-5-019-maimonides-guide-for-the-perplexed|The *Guide for the Perplexed*]] is substantially al-Farabi- and Avicenna-formed; Jewish philosophical theology is structured by falsafa.
- **Ghazālī ↔ falsafa.** [[al-ghazali]]'s [[phase-5-045-al-ghazali-tahafut-al-falasifa|*Tahāfut al-Falāsifa*]] is the keystone anti-falsafa polemic; [[phase-5-046-ibn-rushd-tahafut-al-tahafut|Averroes's *Tahāfut al-Tahāfut*]] is the canonical defense. The Tahafut controversy ([[tahafut-controversy]]) is the principal kalām-vs-falsafa dispute in classical Islam.
- **Falsafa → modern Islamic philosophical revivalism.** Mourad Wahba 1996 *Averroes and the Enlightenment*; Mohammed Arkoun on the falsafa-revival; modern Iranian philosophical curriculum continues the Mulla Sadra branch of post-Avicennian falsafa.

## Disputes
- **Did Ghazālī kill falsafa?** Traditional Western Islamicist narrative: yes; falsafa shrinks institutionally in Sunni learning after Ghazālī. Contemporary revisionist view (Griffel 2009; Eichner 2020): no; falsafa continues vigorously in the Persianate East and is substantially absorbed into post-Razian kalām. Both readings agree that the institutional location of falsafa shifted after the 12th c.
- **Falsafa vs. kalām.** The relationship between the two — falsafa as autonomous demonstrative science vs. kalām as revealed-theology-using-philosophical-tools — is the principal late-medieval Islamic-intellectual demarcation question. Post-Avicennian kalām (Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī, Naṣīr al-Dīn al-Ṭūsī) is substantially Avicennianized; the distinction blurs.
- **Falsafa and Sufism.** Falsafa's mystical-union vocabulary (Avicennian Active-Intellect conjunction; Suhrawardi's *ishrāq*) overlaps substantially with Sufi *fanāʾ* discourse; whether falsafa absorbs Sufism or Sufism absorbs falsafa in the post-Avicennian Persianate tradition is partly definitional.
- **Falsafa and Shīʿīsm.** Post-Avicennian Persian falsafa (Suhrawardi, Mulla Sadra) is substantially Shīʿī in institutional context; whether falsafa has *intrinsic* Shīʿī tendencies (as some Ismāʿīlī-influenced readings of the Brethren of Purity would suggest) or whether the Shīʿī institutional location is historical accident is contested.
