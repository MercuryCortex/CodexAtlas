# Ideal-state myth (Plato's political device)

## Definition
Plato's literary-political technique of constructing an *imagined ideal society* as a critical mirror of the actual — a thought-experiment whose purpose is *not* to serve as a blueprint for action, *nor* to record a historical society, but to make visible (by contrast) the structural pathologies of existing political orders and the principles that would order a city well. The *Republic*'s Kallipolis (the "Beautiful City" of philosopher-rulers and the three-class structure) and the *Timaeus-Critias*'s Atlantis (the corrupted maritime empire that the ancient Athens of 9,000 years ago defeated) function as paired political myths. Diagnostic markers: (a) explicit *constructed* status of the city; (b) used as critical mirror, not blueprint; (c) the philosophical-mythic register (Plato repeatedly calls these accounts *mythoi*, *logoi*, *plasma*); (d) downstream legacy in the utopian / dystopian literary tradition.

## Earliest attestations
- **Plato, *Republic* II–V, VIII–IX** (~-380 to -370): Kallipolis is constructed in speech (*en logois*) to make the structure of justice visible at city-scale; explicitly framed as inquiry not prescription.
- **Plato, *Timaeus* + *Critias*** (late, ~-360s): the Atlantis story — preserved in fragmentary form, the *Critias* breaks off in mid-sentence. Atlantis is presented as a corrupt, expansionist maritime empire from 9,000 years prior; ancient Athens (Plato's idealized version) defeats it; both are then destroyed by cataclysm.
- **Plato, *Laws*** (final dialogue): a more practicable "second-best" city, less utopian but still a constructed ideal.

## Variants
- **Strict-allegory reading (Vidal-Naquet 1964 / 2007, Brisson 1992, Gill 1980):** Atlantis is a *political allegory* of Athens itself — the hubristic, sea-empire-building, ultimately doomed maritime power that Athens *became* in the Periclean and post-Periclean period. Atlantis is the inverted image of Plato's idealized "ancient Athens"; the myth is a critique of contemporary Athenian imperialism. This is the **scholarly consensus**.
- **Historicist reading:** Atlantis is a memory or partial-memory of a real prehistoric civilization. Held in antiquity by Crantor (Plato's first commentator), defended in modernity by Ignatius Donnelly (1882, [[phase-7-033-donnelly-atlantis-antediluvian-world]]) and the modern pseudoarchaeological tradition. Not supported by current archaeology or Plato-scholarship.
- **Synthetic / partial-historical:** Atlantis as memory of the Thera eruption ~1600 BCE ([[event-thera-eruption-c-1600-bce]]) or of late-Mycenaean trauma. Speculative.
- **Republic-as-blueprint reading:** Karl Popper *The Open Society and Its Enemies* (1945) treats the *Republic* as a serious totalitarian political proposal, founding the "Plato as totalitarian" tradition. Most Plato scholarship (Vlastos, Annas) treats Popper's reading as a misreading that ignores Plato's own framing of Kallipolis as inquiry-in-speech.

## Connections claimed
- **Augustine's *City of God***: [[phase-4-029-augustine-city-of-god]] (see [[city-of-god-vs-earthly-city]]) is the major Christian transposition — but Augustine inverts the device: the two cities are *real* eschatological communities, not imagined ones; the *Civitas Dei* is the pilgrim Church and the *Civitas Terrena* is the actual political order under sin.
- **More's *Utopia*** (1516): the founding text of modern utopian literature; explicitly engages the Platonic genre. Subsequent utopian writers (Campanella, Bacon, Andreae's *Christianopolis*, Harrington's *Oceana*) develop the form.
- **Modern dystopian literature** (Wells, Zamyatin, Huxley, Orwell, Atwood): the inverted-utopia is structurally a Platonic *anti-Kallipolis* — the imagined city held up as critical mirror of present pathology.
- **Influence on theology of two-cities, two-kingdoms, two-realms.** Luther's two-kingdoms doctrine, Calvin's church-state structure — all stand at some distance in the Platonic-Augustinian lineage of ideal-state-as-critique.

## Disputes
- **Did Plato intend Atlantis as historical?** Vidal-Naquet, Gill, Brisson decisively no — the textual markers (Plato's own framing language, the schematic numerical-allegorical structure, the philosophical purpose) make this clear in modern scholarship. The historicizing reading is a popular but not scholarly position.
- **Republic-as-totalitarian.** Popper's reading vs. mainstream Plato scholarship. Most modern interpreters (Vlastos, Annas, Schofield, Lear) read the *Republic* as primarily an ethical-psychological argument (justice in the soul) using the city as analogy, not a serious political program.
- **Plato's late political development.** Whether the *Laws*' "second-best" city represents a moderation from the *Republic*'s utopianism or a continuous philosophical position is debated (Klosko, Bobonich).
