# Planetary Correspondences

## Identity

**Planetary correspondences** is the master comparative scheme of the pre-Copernican cosmos: the seven classical planets — in their canonical descending order **Saturn, Jupiter, Mars, Sun, Venus, Mercury, Moon** — taken as the seven "wandering stars" (Greek *planḗtai astéres*, the seven *planḗtai*) and used as the organizing grid onto which an entire encyclopedia of seven-fold correspondences is hung: seven planets ↔ seven gods (across pantheons) ↔ seven metals ↔ the seven days of the week ↔ seven colours ↔ seven organs and the bodily humours ↔ seven musical notes/modes. "Planet" here is the ancient seven — the five true planets visible to the naked eye plus the Sun and Moon — not the modern astronomer's planets; Uranus, Neptune, and (formerly) Pluto belong to a different, post-1781 system, and the Earth is not counted because it is the centre from which the seven are seen to wander.

The scheme is the single most productive correspondence-frame in the Western and South Asian esoteric traditions, and it sits behind several institutions so familiar that their planetary origin is invisible: the **seven-day week** (whose day-names *are* the planet-gods), the **seven metals** of alchemy (whose chemical signs *are* the planet-glyphs), and the **harmony of the spheres** (whose notes *are* the planets in order). In the Atlas, planetary-correspondences is the spine that ties [[hermetic-correspondences]], [[microcosm-macrocosm]], and [[as-above-so-below]] to the alchemical metals ([[alchemy-sulfur-mercury-theory]]), to the planetary modes of music ([[music-planetary-modes-medieval]]), and to the planet-deities of half a dozen pantheons.

A crucial honesty governs this node (see **Cross-tradition significance**): the **seven-planet scheme and the seven-day week are a documented historical transmission** — a single Hellenistic invention that spread west into Latin and Germanic Europe and east into India — whereas the **identification of each planet with a particular god** is *interpretatio*, the matching of one culture's deities to another's by perceived function, and is therefore secure as a transmission of the *frame* but interpretive in its *content*.

## The planetary week — the key transmission

The most striking single product of the scheme is the **seven-day planetary week**, and it is a genuine, traceable historical transmission rather than a typological coincidence. The week did not exist in classical Greece or republican Rome; it was assembled in the **Hellenistic world** (Egypt/Alexandria, c. 1st c. BCE – 1st c. CE) by combining Babylonian seven-planet astrology with the Greek device of **planetary hours**. In that device each hour of the day is ruled by a planet, cycling through the seven in Ptolemaic order; the planet ruling the *first hour* of a day gives that day its name. Running the arithmetic (24 hours stepping through 7 planets) generates precisely the familiar order **Saturn → Sun → Moon → Mars → Mercury → Jupiter → Venus**, which is exactly the sequence of the weekdays. This is the decisive evidence that the week-order is *derived from* the planetary scheme and not an independent custom (Zerubavel 1985; the mechanism is described already by Dio Cassius, *Roman History* 37.18–19, in the early 3rd c. CE).

From this Hellenistic core the week spread in three directions, and in each the **day-names are simply the planet-gods**:

- **Latin / Romance.** The Roman week names the days for the planet-gods directly: *dies Saturni, dies Solis, dies Lunae, dies Martis, dies Mercurii, dies Iovis, dies Veneris* — surviving in Romance languages (French *mardi, mercredi, jeudi, vendredi* = Mars, Mercury, Jupiter, Venus days).
- **Germanic.** When the planetary week reached the Germanic-speaking world (by the early centuries CE), the four "inner" planet-gods were rendered by *interpretatio germanica* — the Roman god replaced by the closest Germanic equivalent: **Mars→Tiw/Týr** (*Tuesday*), **Mercury→Woden/Odin** (*Wednesday*), **Jupiter→Thor** (*Thursday*), **Venus→Frigg** (*Friday*) — while Saturn, Sun, and Moon were kept (*Saturday, Sunday, Monday*). The English weekday names are thus a fossil of the planetary scheme passed through a Germanic god-for-god translation.
- **Sanskrit.** The same seven-day week appears in India as the *vāra* cycle, named for the *Navagraha* planet-deities in the identical planetary order: *Ravivāra* (Sun/Sūrya), *Somavāra* (Moon/Soma), *Maṅgalavāra* (Mars/Maṅgala), *Budhavāra* (Mercury/Budha), *Guruvāra/Bṛhaspativāra* (Jupiter/Bṛhaspati), *Śukravāra* (Venus/Śukra), *Śanivāra* (Saturn/Śani). The Indian week is attested from about the 5th century CE (e.g. in the astronomical literature surrounding Āryabhaṭa and in inscriptions) and is widely held to reflect the same Hellenistic transmission, carried east with Greco-Roman astrology (*yavana-jātaka*, "Greek astrology").

The week is therefore the rare case where a comparative correspondence can be tracked as an actual cultural object moving across the map — *the same seven planets, the same order, the same god-naming logic*, surfacing in Latin, in English, and in Sanskrit.

## Cross-tradition significance (transmission vs. interpretatio)

The honest framing of this node turns on a sharp distinction between two very different kinds of cross-tradition claim that the scheme bundles together.

**(1) The seven-planet frame and the week are transmission — and securely so.** Planetary astronomy itself is a Mesopotamian achievement: the systematic observation, ordering, and prediction of the seven moving lights was developed in Babylonia and passed, largely intact, into the Greek world in the Hellenistic period (Neugebauer 1975). The seven-planet scheme, the Ptolemaic ordering of the spheres, the planetary-hours mechanism, and the resulting seven-day week are a *single connected invention* with a documented line of descent: Babylon → Hellenistic Egypt/Greece → (west) Rome and the Germanic world, (east) India. This is not typological resemblance; it is the migration of a specific technical-cultural package, and it is about as well evidenced as any transmission in the history of religion (Cumont 1912; Zerubavel 1985).

**(2) The planet↔god identifications are *interpretatio* — interpretive matching, not cognate inheritance.** That Mars "is" Nergal "is" Týr "is" Maṅgala is *not* a statement that these gods share an origin. They are the product of *interpretatio* — the ancient and medieval practice of mapping one pantheon's deities onto another's by perceived function (the red, war-associated planet gets matched to each culture's war-or-blood god). The match is *motivated* (Mars is reddish; war-gods are a natural fit) but it is a cross-cultural *equation imposed by the scheme*, not a linguistic or genealogical relationship between the gods. Nergal, Ares, Týr, and Maṅgala have entirely independent origins; what they share is a *slot* in a transmitted grid. This is the same interpretive logic that produced *interpretatio graeca* and *romana* generally, and the planet-week is its most durable monument.

So the correct two-level statement is: **the scheme (the seven, the order, the week) travelled; the deity-content of each slot was filled locally by interpretatio.** Collapsing these — treating "Mars = Týr" as evidence of a shared Indo-European Mars-god, say — is exactly the error the Atlas's membership-vs-wire discipline guards against: Týr's home is Norse, Maṅgala's is the Sanskrit *Navagraha*, Nergal's is Mesopotamian, and their alignment under "the planet Mars" is a *wire carried by the transmitted scheme*, not a shared membership.

The further correspondences — planet↔metal, planet↔mode, planet↔organ — sit on the transmission side for the *frame* (the seven-fold structure is inherited together) but become increasingly *constructed* in their detail: the seven-metals equation is ancient and stable (Saturn–lead, Sun–gold, etc., attested from Greco-Egyptian alchemy; Principe 2013), whereas the planet↔mode and planet↔colour assignments vary between authors and are partly the work of medieval and Renaissance systematizers (Boethius, Ficino, Agrippa, Fludd, and ultimately Kepler, who tried to *vindicate* the sphere-harmony with real orbital measurements; see [[music-kepler-orbital-harmonics]]).

## Disputes

- **Origin of the week: planetary-astrological vs. Jewish Sabbath.** Two seven-day cycles converge in late antiquity — the *astrological* planetary week and the *Jewish* Sabbath week — and their relationship is debated. The dominant view (Zerubavel 1985) is that they were originally independent (the Jewish week being Sabbath-counted, not planet-named) and only later fused, with Christianity inheriting the seven-day count from Judaism while the *day-names* came from the planetary scheme; the planet-named days are not Jewish in origin. A minority has argued for earlier mutual influence. The Atlas follows the mainstream: the *naming* of days for planet-gods is the astrological tradition's contribution.
- **How "Babylonian" is the scheme?** While planetary astronomy is securely Mesopotamian, some of the *correspondence symbolism* (the metals, the developed melothesia, the modes) is demonstrably Greco-Egyptian and later, not Babylonian. Care is needed not to back-project the full Renaissance correspondence-encyclopedia onto Babylon; the Babylonian contribution is the astronomy and the planet-deity associations within Mesopotamian religion, not the seven-metals-and-modes table.
- **Direction and dating of the Indian week.** That the Sanskrit *vāra* week reflects Hellenistic transmission (via *yavana* astrology) is the mainstream position, supported by the late date of attestation (c. 5th c. CE) and the shared planetary order; a few scholars have explored indigenous development, but the order-identity with the Greco-Roman week is generally taken as decisive for transmission.
- **Interpretatio is not cognation.** The strongest methodological caution, restated: the planet↔god equations are interpretive matches and must not be read as evidence of shared divine origin across the pantheons. Where genuine cognate relationships between gods exist, they are argued on independent linguistic grounds, not on co-occupancy of a planetary slot.
