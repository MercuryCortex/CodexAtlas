# Nostos (the hero's homecoming)

## Identity

Nostos (Greek **νόστος**, "return, homecoming") is the Greek epic theme of the hero's homeward journey — above all the return by sea from Troy — and the name of the song-type that narrates it. It is the organizing theme of the *Odyssey*, whose proem announces a hero "striving to win his own life and the *nostos* of his companions" (Od. 1.5), and it named an entire epic of the Trojan Cycle: the lost ***Nostoi*** ("Returns"), attributed to Agias of Troezen, which told the homecomings of the other Achaean leaders. The word is inherited: Greek *néomai* ("I return") and *nóstos* continue the Proto-Indo-European root ***nes-**, whose Germanic cognates (Gothic *ganisan* "be saved," German *genesen* "recover") preserve the sense Douglas Frame reconstructed for the root — not mere arrival but "**return to light and life**," a survival-return with overtones of rescue from death. On Frame's reading the name of Nestor, the epic's great home-bringer, comes from the same root. In the Atlas, nostos sits beside [[katabasis-and-anabasis]] as one of the great Greek narrative armatures: where katabasis is the descent-and-return through death, nostos is the long way home — and in the *Odyssey* the two are one road, since the way to Ithaca leads through the house of the dead.

## In Greek epic

The *Odyssey* is the nostos-poem of the tradition, and it is self-conscious about the genre: in Book 1 the bard Phemius sings for the suitors "the baneful *nostos* of the Achaeans" (Od. 1.326-327) — often taken as the earliest surviving epic reference to nostos-song as a recognized type. The poem measures its hero's return against a foil fixed in its opening scene: Zeus's speech on Aegisthus (Od. 1.32-43) invokes **Agamemnon's murdered homecoming**, the anti-nostos retold by Nestor and Menelaus (Od. 3-4), by Agamemnon's own shade (Od. 11.405-434), and again in the second Nekyia (Od. 24) — the returning king slaughtered at his own table, the faithless wife set against the faithful Penelope. The Telemachy doubles as a catalogue of returns: Nestor's easy sailing, Menelaus storm-driven to Egypt and home only in the eighth year (Od. 3.299-312; 4.81-91), Ajax the Lesser drowned on the way. Crucially, the poem insists that **arrival is not return**. Odysseus reaches Ithaca at the poem's midpoint; the nostos is completed only by disguise, testing, and recognition — the scar (Od. 19), the bow (Od. 21), the immovable olive-tree bed (Od. 23.177-230), the orchard-proof to Laertes (Od. 24) — the reintegration of the returned man into household and kingship. Even then closure is deferred: Teiresias's prophecy sends Odysseus on a final inland journey, carrying an oar until it is mistaken for a winnowing-fan (Od. 11.119-137), a nostos beyond the nostos.

The cyclic ***Nostoi*** (five books, known chiefly through Proclus's Chrestomathy summary; West 2013) carried the theme across the rest of the heroic company: the quarrel of the Atreidae over the departure, Menelaus's wanderings, the storm at the Kapherean rocks, Neoptolemus's overland return through Thrace, and Agamemnon's murder. Beyond the poems, the *nostoi* of the heroes did political work: as **Irad Malkin** (1998) showed, tales of returning Trojan-War heroes wandering the western Mediterranean — Odysseus in the Adriatic and Italy, Diomedes in Apulia — served archaic Greek colonization as charter myths, making the theme genuinely **pan-Hellenic** in reach: the return-song became a map by which new settlements attached themselves to the heroic past.

## Cross-tradition significance

The comparative reach of nostos runs along four distinct rails, and the honest framing keeps them apart.

**Secure literary transmission: the *Aeneid*.** Virgil built the *Aeneid*'s first half on the *Odyssey* — Knauer (1964) demonstrated the scene-by-scene reworking — and in doing so produced the great **inverted nostos**: [[aeneas]] is a Trojan whose home has burned, a hero of the defeated side for whom there is no Ithaca. Virgil transposes the return-form into a **foundation-journey**: the oracle's command *antiquam exquirite matrem*, "seek out your ancient mother" (Aen. 3.96), relocates "home" ahead of the wanderer, in Italy as the ancestral land of Dardanus — so that within the fiction the emigration is itself a deeper homecoming. The return-form survives; its direction is reversed.

**Argued Bronze-Age transmission: Gilgamesh.** The Standard Babylonian epic is framed as a return — the prologue's traveller who "came a far road" and the walls-of-Uruk lines that close the ring at XI.323-328 (George 2003) — a homecoming with wisdom in place of immortality. West (1997) makes the Odyssey-Gilgamesh relation the strongest case for Near-Eastern-to-Greek epic transmission; it remains argued, not proven.

**Lexical descent without thematic transmission: nostalgia and saudade.** The word "nostalgia" really is a child of nostos — **Johannes Hofer** coined it in 1688 from *nóstos* + *álgos* for the medical homesickness of Swiss mercenaries — but the coinage transmits a word, not a myth: the ache it names is universal. [[saudade]], the Portuguese "presence of an absence" whose canonical image is the watcher on the shore waiting for the unreturned ship, is the fullest affective elaboration of the **unachieved nostos** (Boym 2001).

**Typological convergence: the returning king.** The [[sebastianism-quinto-imperio|Sebastianist]] *Encoberto* — the hidden king, not dead, who will return to restore the kingdom — shares the deep grammar of nostos (absence, presumed death, return, restoration of the disordered house), and stands in a family with Arthur, Barbarossa, and the Twelver Hidden Imam. But its lineage is messianic-eschatological (Bandarra's *Trovas*, Jewish messianism, the Daniel 2 four-kingdoms schema), not epic; no scholarship derives Sebastianism from the Greek return-tradition, and the Atlas wires the edge as **parallel-form, not influence**. The structural difference is the point: epic nostos is a return *accomplished within the story*; the returning-king is a return *perpetually deferred* — a nostos held open as expectation, which is why it breeds prophecy and pretenders rather than recognition scenes.

## Disputes

- **The Frame etymology.** That *nostos* and *néomai* descend from PIE **nes-* "return (safely) home" is standard. Frame's stronger claims — that *nóos* ("mind") derives from the same root, and that the Vedic Nāsatyas (the Aśvins as rescuers/home-bringers) are cognate — are influential but contested; standard etymological reference works treat the derivation of *nóos* as uncertain. The Atlas cites the strong form as Frame's argument, not as settled.
- **Reconstructing the *Nostoi*.** The cyclic poem survives only in Proclus's late summary and a handful of fragments; how much of the transmitted content is archaic and how the poem related to the *Odyssey* (rival? complement? source?) is debated (West 2013).
- **Is Aeneas's journey a nostos at all?** The "inverted nostos" reading coexists with the observation that Virgil frames Italy as the *ancestral* home, making the voyage a literal (if genealogically distant) return; both readings are live in Virgilian scholarship, and the inversion is best stated as Virgil's transformation *of* the form rather than an abandonment of it.
- **Nostos and the returning king.** Wiring Sebastianism here is a typological claim only. The two structures differ in genre (narrative theme vs. eschatological expectation), in lineage (epic vs. messianism), and in closure (achieved vs. deferred); the edge is kept at parallel-form deliberately, and any future claim of transmission would need a documented conduit that no current scholarship supplies.
