# Liberation Theology

**Liberation Theology** is the theological pattern in which religious doctrine is interpreted as a primary mandate for the emancipation of the marginalized — the poor, the enslaved, the colonized, the oppressed. In its most explicit 20th-century form, it was articulated by the Latin American Catholic movement (Gustavo Gutiérrez, *A Theology of Liberation*, 1971), but the pattern recurs across traditions and centuries.

## Cross-tradition instances

| Tradition | Figure / Text | The Liberation Move |
|---|---|---|
| Latin American Catholicism | Gutiérrez (1971), Boff, Romero | Jesus as preferential option for the poor; Exodus = liberation template |
| Navayana Buddhism | [[ambedkar]] (1956), [[phase-7-045-ambedkar-buddha-and-dhamma]] | Karma excised as casteist ideology; nirvana = social liberation |
| Korean Donghak | [[phase-7-042-donggyeong-daejeon]] (1861) | *Innaecheon* (each person is Heaven) = anti-colonial theological mandate |
| Vietnamese Cao Dai | [[phase-7-044-cao-dai-canon]] (1926) | Syncretic religion as anti-French resistance |
| African-American Christianity | Spirituals, Nat Turner, MLK Jr. | Exodus and Resurrection as templates for Black liberation |
| Jewish Exodus theology | Hebrew Bible, Passover Haggadah | The Exodus event is a liberation narrative that every generation re-enacts |

## The structural move

All instances share the same theological operation:
1. **Identify** which doctrines or interpretations within the tradition have been co-opted to legitimate oppression
2. **Excise or reinterpret** those elements (karma, quietism, "render unto Caesar," hierarchical cosmology)
3. **Re-read** the tradition's core narrative as a mandate for the liberation of the marginalized

This is why Liberation Theology is always controversial within its own tradition: it claims the tradition's authentic center for an oppositional reading, dispossessing those who used the same tradition to legitimate the status quo.
