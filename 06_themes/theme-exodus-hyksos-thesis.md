# Exodus-as-Hyksos-Expulsion Thesis

## Definition

The claim that the biblical Exodus narrative (Exodus 1–15) encodes — at whatever historical distance — a transformation of the memory of the Hyksos expulsion from Egypt (c. 1550 BCE): specifically, the expulsion of a large Semitic-speaking population from the Nile Delta following the Theban reconquest. On a strong form: the Exodus *is* a fictionalized Hyksos-expulsion narrative. On a weaker form: the Hyksos expulsion is *one strand* woven into a multi-layered tradition that the 7th century BCE Exodus author drew on. Both forms are associated with the critical-secular wing of biblical scholarship.

## Earliest attestations of the connection

Josephus (*Contra Apionem* I.82–106, 1st c. CE) was the first to explicitly link the Hyksos to the Israelites, arguing that the Hyksos *were* the Israelites and that Manetho's account of the expulsion described the Exodus. Josephus's argument goes in the opposite direction from Redford's — Josephus used Manetho apologetically to argue for Jewish antiquity and Egyptian presence. The scholarly inversion (Redford: Exodus is a Canaanite re-telling of Hyksos memory) came in the late 20th century.

## Variants

**Strong form (Redford 1993).** The Exodus narrative preserves Canaanite collective memory of the Hyksos' settlement in Egypt and their eventual expulsion. Almost all geographic details in Exodus reflect 7th century BCE or later Egyptian conditions (26th Dynasty Saite period) — particularly the store cities Pithom and Pi-Ramesses, whose archaeology points to Late Period founding. The text's author had no access to authentic 15th century BCE Egyptian data.

**Memory model (Finkelstein and Silberman 2001).** A more diffuse "collective memory" account: the 7th-century Exodus author drew on multiple strands of Semitic-Egyptian contact memories, of which the Hyksos expulsion is one. The account is not historically literal but is not purely fictional either.

**Authenticity counter-position (Hoffmeier 1997).** The Egyptian background in the Joseph and Exodus narratives includes details authentic to the Hyksos and New Kingdom periods — slave prices, titles, court protocol — that a 7th century author could not have fabricated. This is evidence that the traditions, however transmitted, contain genuine 2nd millennium BCE Egyptian material.

## The sociology problem

The Hyksos–Exodus equation runs against the narrative's own sociology: the Hyksos were *expelled rulers*, politically dominant, exiting Egypt in organized military groups after a military defeat. The Exodus narrative depicts *slaves* — the bottom of the social order — escaping Egyptian coercion and being *pursued* by the Egyptian army. These are opposing social positions. At most, the Hyksos expulsion is a *memory strand* inverted in the retelling — a tradition of Semites leaving Egypt transformed from expulsion-of-rulers into liberation-of-slaves. This is Redford's "collective memory" argument at its most specific.

## Archaeological state (2020s)

- The Tell el-Dab'a excavations (Bietak 1966–present) confirm a large Semitic Delta community in the 12th–15th Dynasty period — providing the archaeological substrate the thesis requires.
- No Semitic-language document survives from the Hyksos period naming the Hyksos' own account of their time in Egypt or their departure.
- The toponymic evidence in Exodus (Pithom, Ramesses, Goshen) fits Late Period geography better than New Kingdom geography, supporting a 7th century composition date.
- No archaeological evidence of a mass departure of Semites from Egypt corresponding to any period has been found.
