# Predestination (Protestant Reformation)

The Reformed doctrine that God, from eternity and unconditionally, has elected some humans to salvation and (in the strong "double predestination" of Calvin and Beza) reprobated others to damnation. The doctrine is meant to safeguard divine sovereignty: salvation is *entirely* God's act, not in any way grounded in foreseen human merit or even foreseen human faith. Augustine's anti-Pelagian writings are the patristic source; the Reformation's contribution is to make the doctrine pastorally and systematically central.

- Luther's *De Servo Arbitrio* (1525, against Erasmus): the will is *bound*, predestination is taught in scripture, do not speculate about the *deus absconditus*.
- Calvin's *Institutes* III.21–24: developed double-predestination; "decretum quidem horribile, fateor" — "the decree is horrible, I confess" — but biblical.
- Synod of Dort (1618–1619): codified the Five Points (TULIP) against Arminianism. Unconditional Election is the second point.
- Lutheran orthodoxy modified: predestination is *to* salvation only, not symmetrically to reprobation.
- Jansenism (17th-century Catholic Augustinian revival, e.g. Pascal) carried predestinarian theology inside the Roman Catholic Church until its condemnation.

The doctrine functioned sociologically (Weber's *Protestant Ethic* thesis, contested) as a generator of inner-worldly asceticism: anxiety about election → vocational diligence → capitalist habitus.
