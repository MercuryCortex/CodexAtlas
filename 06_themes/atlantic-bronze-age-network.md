# The Atlantic Bronze Age Network (c. 1300–700 BCE)

## The System
The **Atlantic Bronze Age** is the name given to a remarkable cultural and commercial system that operated along the Atlantic façade of Europe from approximately 1300 to 700 BCE — connecting the Atlantic coastal populations from Morocco and the Iberian Peninsula to Scotland and Scandinavia through a dense network of maritime trade, shared material culture, and (probably) shared ideological frameworks.

The system is defined by:
1. **Strikingly similar artifact types** found from Portugal to Denmark: certain sword types (*Carp's-tongue swords*, *Huelva-type swords*), shield types (*Yetholm shields*), cauldrons, horse-gear, and decorative metalwork appear across the entire Atlantic zone in closely related forms — not identical, but clearly made by craftspeople sharing design traditions
2. **Long-distance raw material movement**: the copper of the Iberian Peninsula (Rio Tinto; Huelva; Alentejo) and the tin of Cornwall, Brittany, and possibly northwest Iberia combine to make bronze — the defining technology of the age. The tin must be transported to the copper, or vice versa, over thousands of kilometers
3. **Shared settlement patterns**: the hilltop enclosed settlements (*castros* in Iberia, *hill-forts* in Britain and Ireland) that characterize the late Bronze Age and early Iron Age Atlantic zone appear simultaneously across the region
4. **Maritime connectivity**: sea travel along the Atlantic coast was routine, not exceptional — the currents and winds of the Atlantic actually favor coastal travel from Iberia to Ireland, and from Ireland to Scandinavia, more than inland travel across mountain ranges

## The Tin Trade: The System's Engine
The critical commodity was **tin** — without which there is no bronze (bronze = ~90% copper + ~10% tin). Tin deposits are rare: in the ancient Western European context, the primary sources were:
- **Cornwall** (southwest England): the *Cassiterides* ("Tin Islands") of ancient geography, famous for 2,500 years of tin mining
- **Brittany** (northwest France): significant tin deposits
- **Northwest Iberia** (Galicia and northern Portugal): smaller but documented deposits
- **Bohemia and central Europe**: inland sources that feed the eastern Mediterranean trade

For the Atlantic Bronze Age, Cornish and Breton tin + Iberian copper = the western Mediterranean/Atlantic bronze supply. The trade route was coastal: ships loaded with copper ingots (*bun-shaped copper oxhide ingots*, found in Atlantic shipwrecks) or tin traveled the Atlantic coast for thousands of kilometers.

The economic and political implications: whoever controlled the western tin supply controlled bronze technology for the western Mediterranean. **Tartessos** (see [[tradition-tartessos]]) was positioned at the southern anchor of this system — receiving Atlantic tin and copper from its own Rio Tinto mines, and channeling both through the Phoenician commercial network at **Gades** into the Mediterranean. The wealth of Tartessos was the wealth of the Bronze Age Atlantic trade.

## MASSIVE WIN — The Bronze Age Laid Down the Sacred Geography

**The Atlantic Bronze Age is the material foundation of everything that came after in Atlantic European religion and mythology.**

1. **The movement routes became the sacred routes**: The Bronze Age coastal trade paths from Iberia to Britain to Ireland became the routes along which Celtic cultures later dispersed. The **Camino de Santiago** runs across northern Iberia precisely where Bronze Age coastal trade moved. The Atlantic coast pilgrimage geography (ending at Cape Finisterre) follows paths that were first worn into the landscape by Bronze Age traders 3,000 years before Saint James.

2. **The metalwork created the sacred geography**: the famous Bronze Age sun-disc symbols (e.g., the **Trundholm Sun Chariot**, Denmark, c. 1400 BCE — a bronze horse pulling a gilded sun-disc), the gold lunula collars found across Ireland and Atlantic Iberia, the cauldrons with solar-symbolic decoration — this Bronze Age solar iconography is the material expression of the solar religion that underlies everything from Melqart's *egersis* to the Camino's solar-route logic to the megalithic winter-solstice alignments. The Bronze Age metalworkers were making ideology as well as tools.

3. **Tin from Cornwall and gold from Ireland + Copper from Iberia = the Atlantic sacred triangle**: The regions of Britain, Ireland, and Portugal/Iberia that are most richly served by megalithic monuments, Celtic mythology, and surviving pre-Christian sacred geography are exactly the regions that were most intensively connected in the Atlantic Bronze Age trade. The sacred geography is the cultural residue of the material connectivity. See [[tradition-megalithic-atlantic]].

4. **The Bronze Age collapse (~1200 BCE) created the Celtic migration**: The catastrophic Late Bronze Age collapse that destroyed or disrupted the palace economies of the eastern Mediterranean (~1200 BCE — the same event that ended Mycenaean Greece, Hittite Empire, and several Levantine cities) also disrupted the eastern end of the tin trade. This probably accelerated the Atlantic Bronze Age system's intensification as the eastern routes closed — and the subsequent Iron Age saw the first clearly Celtic-speaking cultures emerging in the Alpine zone (Hallstatt) and then spreading west, partly along the Bronze Age trade routes. The Celts didn't create the Atlantic sacred geography — they inherited it from the Bronze Age peoples who preceded them.

## The Genetic and Archaeological Picture
Modern archaeogenetics (ancient DNA analysis) is clarifying the Bronze Age Atlantic story:
- The **Yamnaya steppe migration** (~4500–2500 BCE) brought Indo-European languages and genotype into Europe, largely replacing the earlier Anatolian Neolithic farmers' genetic signature
- The **Bell Beaker phenomenon** (~2800–1800 BCE) — associated with characteristic pottery, archery equipment, and copper/gold metalwork — represents a cultural and genetic movement across the Atlantic zone that may have been an early vector of proto-Celtic languages
- By the Bronze Age (~1300 BCE), the Atlantic populations share both the steppe-derived genetic ancestry and the Atlantic-Bronze-Age-derived material culture
- The **Iron Age Celtic expansion** is in most areas a cultural overlay on populations that were already Atlantic Bronze Age communities — the language changed (to identifiably Celtic) but the people often didn't

For Portugal: the population of Atlantic Iberia in 1300 BCE is already a genetic mixture of Neolithic Anatolian farmers (who built the megaliths) + Yamnaya steppe migrants (who brought Indo-European languages) — and it's this population that runs the Bronze Age trade with Britain and Ireland. The Celtic-speaking Lusitanians and Gallaecians of the Roman period are largely the descendants of these Bronze Age Atlantic traders.

## From Bronze Age to Celtic Othwerworld
The Celtic mythology of the western ocean paradise — [[celtic-otherworld]] — does not emerge from nothing. It emerges from a cultural community that had been sending boats across the Atlantic for 500 years before the first identifiably Celtic texts appear. The sailors who traded tin from Cornwall to Iberia in the Bronze Age knew the Atlantic as a real maritime environment — dangerous, powerful, unpredictable, and full of islands. When Celtic mythology transformed this real ocean into the mythological Otherworld, it was working with a shared geographical imaginary built up over centuries of Bronze Age trade.

**The Hesperides parallel**: The Greek myth of the **Garden of the Hesperides** — located at the world's western edge, with golden apples, tended by the daughters of Evening — may encode Bronze Age memory of the Atlantic trade. The "golden apples" of the Hesperides may be the gold of Atlantic Ireland and Iberia (the Bronze Age Atlantic zone produced extraordinary quantities of gold jewelry and ornament). The myth preserves the Bronze Age geography: go west, past all known land, and you will find gold beyond imagining. See [[finisterre-end-of-world]], [[melqart]].
