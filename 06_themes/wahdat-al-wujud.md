# Waḥdat al-Wujūd (unity of being)

## Definition
The Akbarī Sufi doctrine that there is, ultimately, only *one being* (*wujūd*) — God (*al-Ḥaqq*, the Real) — and that everything else exists only insofar as God's being is *self-disclosed* (*tajallī*) in finite, partial, evanescent forms. Creation is not a separate ontological order *added* to God's being; it is the self-manifestation of the One Being in a hierarchy of *names* and *fixed entities* (*aʿyān thābita*). Diagnostic marker: the metaphysical claim that *wujūd* — being, existence — has only *one* real referent, distinguished from (a) standard Ashʿarite theology (creator distinct from creation); (b) pantheism in the Western sense (God *is* the totality, which Ibn ʿArabī rejects — God is not the cosmos, but the cosmos has no being apart from God); (c) the rival Sufi formulation *waḥdat al-shuhūd* (unity of *witnessing*, Sirhindī).

## Earliest attestations and terminological history

**Critical distinction (per the brief):**
- The **doctrine** originates with **Muḥyī al-Dīn Ibn ʿArabī** (1165–1240) in *Fuṣūṣ al-Ḥikam* (~1230) and the vast *al-Futūḥāt al-Makkiyya* (~1202–1231).
- The **technical term** "waḥdat al-wujūd" is **largely a coinage of his student Ṣadr al-Dīn al-Qūnawī** (1207–1274) and the later **Akbarī tradition** — Saʿīd al-Dīn al-Farghānī (d. ~1300), Muʾayyid al-Dīn al-Jandī (d. ~1300), ʿAbd al-Razzāq al-Kāshānī (d. ~1330), Dāʾūd al-Qayṣarī (d. ~1350).
- Ibn ʿArabī himself uses *wujūd*, *waḥda*, and related vocabulary extensively, but does not use the precise nominal compound "waḥdat al-wujūd" as a school-doctrine label. Chittick 1989 emphasizes this carefully.

- **Ibn ʿArabī, *Fuṣūṣ al-Ḥikam*** (~1230 in Damascus): the most concise and read-in-school articulation. 27 chapters, each on a prophet, each working out a "bezel" (*faṣṣ*) of divine wisdom.
- **Ibn ʿArabī, *al-Futūḥāt al-Makkiyya*** (~1202–1231): 560 chapters, ~37 volumes in printed editions; the encyclopedic systematic exposition.
- **Al-Qūnawī, *Iʿjāz al-Bayān*** and **Miftāḥ al-Ghayb***: the principal expositions that fix the technical vocabulary.
- **Reception in Persian and Indian Sufism:** Farghānī's commentary on Ibn al-Fāriḍ's *Tāʾiyya*, Kāshānī's commentary on the *Fuṣūṣ*, Jāmī (15th c.) — diffuse the doctrine across the Persianate world.

## Variants
- **Strict Akbarī:** all "other than God" is, ontologically considered, *nothing* (only God truly *is*); the cosmos is God's self-manifestation in the modes of his names and the fixed entities.
- **Mullā Ṣadrā's Safavid synthesis** (*al-Asfār al-Arbaʿa*, ~1640): *tashkīk al-wujūd* — the *systematic ambiguity* / *gradation of being* — being is one in essence but graded in intensity. A modulation of Ibn ʿArabī that preserves Akbarī commitments while integrating Avicennan-Suhrawardian philosophy.
- **Aḥmad Sirhindī (1564–1624), *waḥdat al-shuhūd***: the *unity of witnessing* — what the mystic *experiences* as unity, but ontologically, creator and creation remain distinct. An explicit Naqshbandi-Mujaddidi reform of what Sirhindī took to be Ibn ʿArabī's heterodox monism.
- **Modern Sufi articulations:** ʿAbd al-Karīm al-Jīlī (15th c.), Aḥmad ibn ʿAjība (18th c.); 20th-c. reception in Henry Corbin, Toshihiko Izutsu.

## Connections claimed
- **Neoplatonic background.** Knysh 1999 and Corbin discuss the Plotinian-Procline emanation schema as a structural ancestor; Ibn ʿArabī's *self-disclosure* (*tajallī*) inherits the Plotinian *prohodos*.
- **Avicennan distinction of essence and existence.** Avicenna's metaphysics of *wujūd* and contingency is the immediate philosophical background; Ibn ʿArabī radicalizes it.
- **Ismāʿīlī and Brethren of Purity cosmology** in the eastern Mediterranean Islamic intellectual milieu shape the cosmological imagination.
- **Jewish mystical parallel:** *Ein Sof* / Sefirotic Kabbalah's emanationist monism is structurally analogous; the parallels were noticed already in medieval cross-tradition mysticism and are intensively discussed in modern comparative scholarship (Schimmel 1975, Wolfson 2005).

## Disputes
- **The terminological-coinage point.** Ibn ʿArabī never used "waḥdat al-wujūd" as a slogan; the term is largely a school-coinage by al-Qūnawī and the Akbarīs. Chittick (1989: 3) is unambiguous; this is now scholarly consensus. The brief is therefore right to flag this; the doctrine is Ibn ʿArabī's, the term-as-school-label is his successors'.
- **Heresy controversies.** Ibn Taymiyya (~1300) and his disciple Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya wrote extensive refutations of *waḥdat al-wujūd*, accusing Ibn ʿArabī of pantheism (*ittiḥād*) and incarnationism (*ḥulūl*). Knysh 1999 is the definitive study of the polemical reception. Modern Salafism inherits this critique.
- **Pantheism question.** Whether *waḥdat al-wujūd* is pantheism in the strict Western philosophical sense (God = cosmos) is the most discussed interpretive question. Chittick, Corbin, Izutsu, and most Western scholarship: *no* — the doctrine is *more radical than pantheism*: the cosmos has no being to be identified with God; only God is. The critics (Ibn Taymiyya, modern Wahhabism): yes, in effect. The doctrine is sometimes more precisely classed as *acosmic monism* or *theomonism*.
- **Sirhindī's *waḥdat al-shuhūd***. Whether Sirhindī genuinely improved on Ibn ʿArabī or misread him (Friedmann 1971) is a perennial debate in Indian-subcontinent Sufi historiography.
