# Henosis: The Mystical Union Across Traditions

## Overview

**Henosis** (Greek: *ἕνωσις*, "union") is Plotinus's term for the soul's ultimate union with the One — the experience in which individual consciousness dissolves into undifferentiated unity with the divine ground. This theme documents the cross-tradition chain of the **mystical union** concept from its probable Egyptian roots through Neoplatonism into Christian, Islamic, Jewish, and Hindu mysticism — and demonstrates that the core structure of mystical experience as a dissolution of subject-object duality appears independently across multiple traditions while also showing documented cross-tradition transmission.

## The Chain

### Egyptian Priestly Union with the God (c. -2686 onward)

Egyptian priestly theology describes the culmination of ritual not as mere offering but as a **union with the divine**: the priest who successfully performs the *Opening of the Mouth* ritual on the statue of the god becomes, for the duration of the ritual, the god himself. The dead pharaoh is explicitly identified with Osiris: not merely as his representative but as *becoming* Osiris through the mortuary ritual.

**[[iamblichus]]**'s *De Mysteriis Aegyptiorum* (On the Mysteries of the Egyptians, c. 310 CE) explicitly describes this Egyptian priestly union-with-the-divine as the highest form of philosophical-mystical life — superior to Greek philosophical contemplation because it involves the full person (body + soul) in theurgic union, not merely intellectual reflection.

This Egyptian priestly union is the probable experiential precedent that **[[ammonius-saccas]]** and **[[plotinus]]** encounter in Alexandria and translate into philosophical vocabulary.

### Plotinus's Henosis (c. 253–270 CE)

**[[plotinus]]**'s *Enneads* VI.9 (*"On the Good, or the One"*) is the founding text of Western mystical union theology. Plotinus describes the soul's ascent:

1. The soul turns away from the sensible world and withdraws into itself
2. It ascends through the levels of the intelligible world (Soul → Intellect)
3. At the apex of Intellect, it reaches the threshold of the One
4. In henosis, the soul "touches" the One — all multiplicity disappears, subject and object vanish, and there remains only simple unity

Plotinus's own description: *"Often I have woken from the body to myself, having become external to all other things and self-centred; beholding a marvellous beauty... then I assure myself that I belong to the better part; energetically established in it, I arrive at identity with the divine... living the life of the gods."* (*Enneads* IV.8.1)

This is the template for all subsequent Western descriptions of mystical union.

### Pseudo-Dionysius: Darkness and Divine Union (c. 500 CE)

**[[pseudo-dionysius]]** translates Plotinus's henosis into Christian biblical language via Moses's ascent into the "Divine Darkness" on Sinai (*Mystical Theology*). The apophatic path:

1. **Cataphatic theology** — affirm all divine names (God is Good, Beautiful, One)
2. **Apophatic negation** — negate all divine names (God is beyond-Good, beyond-Beautiful, beyond-One)
3. **Mystical silence** — beyond both affirmation and negation, the soul enters the "Divine Darkness" where union occurs

This becomes the theological framework for all subsequent Christian mysticism:
- **Meister Eckhart**: the *Godhead* (*Gottheit*) beyond God; *Abgeschiedenheit* (detachment) as the path; union through *Gelassenheit* (letting-go)
- **The Cloud of Unknowing** (14th c.): the cloud between soul and God that blocks ordinary knowledge but admits mystical love
- **John of the Cross**: the Dark Night of the Soul and Spirit as the purgative path to union (*unio mystica*)

### Islamic Fana (10th–13th c. CE)

**Fana** (Arabic: *فناء*, "annihilation") is the Sufi term for the mystical union — the annihilation of the ego-self in the divine. Key Sufi figures:
- **Al-Hallaj** (858–922 CE): *"Ana'l-Haqq"* ("I am the Truth/God") — the most famous utterance of mystical union; he was executed for it
- **[[rumi]]** (1207–1273 CE): the *Masnavi*'s opening reed-flute lament is a metaphor for the soul separated from its divine origin and longing to return — henosis as the cure for ontological separation
- **[[ibn-arabi]]** (1165–1240 CE): *Wahdat al-Wujud* ("Unity of Being") — the universe is the self-disclosure (*tajalli*) of the One; mystical experience is recognizing this ontological unity

Ibn Arabi explicitly knew Neoplatonic sources (via Arabic translations of Plotinus circulating as the *Theology of Aristotle*) — the Plotinian henosis is the philosophical background of his *wahdat al-wujud*.

### Kabbalistic Devekut (12th–16th c. CE)

**Devekut** (Hebrew: *דְּבֵקוּת*, "cleaving/adhesion to God") is the Kabbalistic term for mystical union — not the full dissolution of Plotinus/fana (which Jewish theology generally resists as too threatening to divine transcendence and individual personhood), but an intimate "cleaving" to God in ongoing prayer and intention.

The highest expression: the *Baal Shem Tov* (18th c. Hasidism) makes devekut the central practice — every moment of everyday life as an opportunity for intimate union with the divine, accessible to non-scholars through sincerity of heart. This is the democratization of the Plotinian henosis.

### Hindu Samadhi and Advaita Vedanta (parallel form)

Hindu **samadhi** (particularly *nirvikalpa samadhi*, absorption without conceptual distinction) is the experiential parallel to Plotinian henosis: the complete dissolution of subject-object duality in pure consciousness. The philosophical framework is **Advaita Vedanta** (non-dualism) — **Adi Shankaracharya** (8th c. CE): *Brahman* (the Absolute) alone is real; *Atman* (the individual self) is identical with Brahman; the appearance of multiplicity is *maya* (illusion).

The parallel with Plotinus is structural (the phenomenology of the experience is virtually identical across accounts) rather than documented transmission — though Greek philosophy and Indian philosophy were in documented contact from the Hellenistic period onward.

## The Common Structure

Across all traditions, mystical union has consistent features (Stace 1960):
1. **Dissolution of subject-object distinction** — the "I" that experiences disappears
2. **Experience of unity** — all multiplicity collapses into One
3. **Ineffability** — the experience exceeds conceptual description
4. **Noetic quality** — felt as the highest form of knowledge, not mere emotion
5. **Paradoxicality** — described through contradictions ("full emptiness," "dark light")

These features appear in Plotinus, pseudo-Dionysius, Eckhart, Ibn Arabi, the Baal Shem Tov, and the Upanishads — suggesting either a common experiential reality or a common psychological structure that different traditions learn to cultivate.
