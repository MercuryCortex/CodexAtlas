# Shamanism

## What it is
Shamanism designates a cross-cultural religious complex centered on the shaman — a specialist who enters altered states of consciousness (trance, ecstasy) to travel between the human world and the spirit world, healing the sick, communicating with the dead, interceding with nature spirits, and maintaining the community's relationship with the invisible order. The term derives from the Tungus (Evenki) word *šaman*, originally denoting a specific type of spirit-medium in Siberian indigenous tradition.

[[mircea-eliade]]'s *Shamanism: Archaic Techniques of Ecstasy* (1951/1964) proposed that shamanism is not merely a Siberian phenomenon but a universal religious complex appearing in its "classic" form across Siberia and Central Asia, and in structurally parallel forms across the Americas, Africa, Southeast Asia, and prehistoric Europe. Eliade's core claim: shamanism represents the most archaic stratum of human religiosity — the original form of human spiritual practice, predating all codified religion.

## Core features (Eliade's model)
- **Trance and ecstasy:** The shaman's defining technique. Through drumming, chanting, fasting, or plant substances, the shaman exits ordinary consciousness and travels in the spirit world.
- **Spirit travel:** The soul of the shaman leaves the body to ascend to upper worlds or descend to lower worlds — the "three-level cosmos" (underworld, earth, sky) traversed via the World Tree or Axis Mundi.
- **Healing and soul retrieval:** Illness is frequently diagnosed as soul loss; the shaman travels to recover the lost soul.
- **Intercession:** The shaman negotiates with spirits, ancestors, and nature beings on behalf of the community.
- **Initiation:** Becoming a shaman typically involves a crisis experience — "initiatory death and resurrection," often involving symbolic dismemberment and reassembly.

## The critique (Kehoe and others)
Alice Beck Kehoe (*Debunking Shamanism*, 2000) and Jonathan Z. Smith argue that Eliade's "shamanism" is a scholarly construction that projects a Siberian-specific concept onto radically different practices worldwide, erasing cultural specificity. The critique is well-taken: Siberian shamanism and, say, the vision-quest tradition of Plains Native American communities involve very different social roles, cosmologies, and techniques. The word "shamanism" can obscure more than it reveals. The vault follows the nuanced position: the term is used as a family-resemblance category while noting that specific traditions are better analyzed on their own terms.

## MASSIVE WIN — Prehistoric cave art

The most consequential application of the shamanism framework is J. David Lewis-Williams's argument (2002) that the imagery in the Upper Paleolithic caves — [[event-chauvet-cave-paintings-c-32000-bce]] (~32,000 BCE) and [[event-lascaux-cave-paintings-c-17000-bce]] (~17,000 BCE) — represents the visual vocabulary of shamanic altered-state experiences. Lewis-Williams identifies *entoptic phenomena* (the geometric patterns — dots, grids, spirals — that the human nervous system generates in trance states) in the cave walls, alongside the therianthropic figures (human-animal hybrids) and the characteristic "wall permeability" of figures appearing to emerge from or enter the rock face.

If Lewis-Williams is correct, the earliest attested human symbolic/artistic practice (~32,000 BCE) is already a shamanic religious practice — making shamanism the oldest religious tradition in the vault, antedating all others by at least 25,000 years. This is the vault's deepest temporal root.
