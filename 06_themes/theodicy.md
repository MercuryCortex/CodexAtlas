# Theodicy (the problem of evil)

## Identity

**Theodicy** (from Greek *theós* "god" + *díkē* "justice" — literally "the justification of God") is the problem of reconciling the existence of **evil and innocent suffering** with belief in a deity that is at once **all-good, all-powerful, and all-knowing**. The word was coined by **Leibniz** in his *Essais de Théodicée* (1710), but the underlying question is far older than the term and is posed independently across traditions. In its sharpest classical form — the so-called **Epicurean trilemma** — the problem is a logical dilemma:

> Is God willing to prevent evil, but not able? Then he is impotent.
> Is he able, but not willing? Then he is malevolent.
> Is he both able and willing? Whence then is evil?

(The formulation is transmitted by **Lactantius**, *De ira Dei* 13, who attributes it to [[epicurus|Epicurus]]; whether Epicurus framed it exactly this way is disputed.) In the Atlas, theodicy is treated as a **comparative theme-hub** — like [[chaoskampf]] — that gathers the *independent answers* different traditions have given to one shared human problem: not a doctrine transmitted along a single line of descent, but a recurrent question that thought after thought has been forced to confront.

Theodicy must be distinguished from its near-neighbour [[origin-of-evil]]. The origin-of-evil question is **metaphysical / cosmogonic** — *where does evil come from?* (an evil principle, a deficient demiurge, a fallen angel, the misuse of freedom). Theodicy is **forensic** — *given that evil exists, how is a good and omnipotent God justified?* The two overlap heavily (a dualist answer to one is also an answer to the other) and the present node is wired to its sibling, but their organizing axes differ: origin-of-evil maps *sources*, theodicy maps *justifications and protests*. (See Disputes.)

### The cross-tradition grid of responses

| **Mesopotamian (wisdom)** | The pious sufferer: a righteous man is afflicted; the gods' justice is inscrutable | **Agnostic submission** — the gods' minds are remote; right conduct is still owed | [[phase-1-032-babylonian-theodicy]] (acrostic dialogue, Kassite era); *Ludlul bēl nēmeqi* ("I will praise the Lord of wisdom") |
| **Hebrew Bible** | Job, blameless, is destroyed; the friends insist he must have sinned | **Protest + refusal of the friends' calculus** — God answers from the whirlwind not with a reason but with the scale of creation (Job 38–41); the retribution theology of the friends is rejected | [[phase-2-035-job]] |
| **Second-Temple Jewish apocalyptic** | Why does the covenant people suffer catastrophic defeat (post-70 CE)? | **Eschatological deferral** — the reckoning is moved to the age to come; raw protest precedes resolution | [[phase-3-032-4-ezra-ethiopic-recension]] (4 Ezra 3–7, the most sustained theodicy-protest in canonical Jewish-Christian literature) |
| **Greek (Epicurean)** | The logical trilemma: power, goodness, and evil cannot all stand | **Dissolution by denying providence** — the gods exist but are unconcerned with the world; evil is no objection because no provident god is claimed | [[epicurus|Epicurus]] (via Lactantius, *De ira Dei* 13) |
| **Greek (Stoic / Platonist)** | Evil in a rationally-ordered cosmos | **Privative / pedagogical** — apparent evils serve the order of the whole; evil has no positive substance | Plotinus, *Enneads* I.8 (on the nature of evil); Stoic providence (*pronoia*) |
| **Latin Christian (Augustinian)** | If God made all things and all things are good, whence evil? | **Free-will defence + *privatio boni*** — evil is not a thing God made but the *privation* of good, arising from the misuse of created free will by angels and humans | [[augustine-of-hippo|Augustine]], *Confessions* VII, *Enchiridion* 11, [[phase-4-029-augustine-city-of-god]] |
| **Christian (Irenaean / "soul-making")** | Why a world with suffering at all? | **Soul-making** — the world is a "vale of soul-making"; moral and spiritual growth requires real adversity | Irenaeus, *Adversus Haereses* IV; modern formulation Hick 1966 |
| **Islamic (Ashʿarite)** | Could God have made a better world? | **Optimism + voluntarism** — "there is not in possibility anything more wonderful than what is" (al-Ghazālī); God's justice is whatever God wills | al-Ghazālī (Ormsby 1984); the dispute Leibniz later echoes |
| **Early-modern rationalist** | The full systematic justification | **"Best of all possible worlds"** — a perfect God necessarily actualizes the optimal world; the evils in it are the price of the greatest overall good | [[leibniz|Leibniz]], *Théodicée* (1710) — the work that *named* the field |
| **Zoroastrian** | Whence evil, if the good God is good? | **Cosmic dualism** — evil is the work of an *independent* hostile principle, **Angra Mainyu / Ahriman**, coeternal (in Zurvanite forms) or co-original with Ahura Mazda; evil is removed not by argument but by the eventual victory of the good at the *Frashokereti* | [[phase-2-002-gathas-of-zarathustra]]; [[angra-mainyu-ahriman]]; [[saoshyant]] |
| **Indian (karma / saṃsāra)** | Why do beings suffer unequally? | **Dissolution by moral causation** — suffering is the just fruit of one's own prior deeds across lives; no external judge is impugned because the cosmos is morally self-regulating | [[jain-karma]]; the doctrine of *karman* across Hindu, Buddhist, and Jain thought |
| **Modern post-Holocaust** | Can the question even be asked after Auschwitz? | **Anti-theodicy** — the attempt to justify God in the face of atrocity is itself rejected as obscene; protest replaces explanation | Levinas, "Useless Suffering" (1982); some post-Shoah Jewish theology |

### The recurring answer-families

Across the grid, the independent responses fall into a small number of recurring **types** — a convergence that is itself the comparative finding:

1. **Free-will defence** — evil is the cost of genuine creaturely freedom (Augustinian; Plantinga's modern logical version). See [[free-will-vs-determinism]].
2. ***Privatio boni*** — evil has no positive being; it is the absence or corruption of good (Augustine, drawing on Plotinus). This is why, in this family, God is "not the author of evil."
3. **Greater-good / soul-making** — evils are instrumentally necessary to a higher end (Irenaean; Leibniz's optimism; the Stoic order-of-the-whole).
4. **Eschatological deferral** — the imbalance is real now but will be set right in the age to come (Job's vindication-hope; 4 Ezra; apocalyptic). See [[righteous-suffering-vindication]].
5. **Dualist externalization** — evil is the work of a rival power, sparing the good God authorship (Zoroastrian Ahriman; Gnostic demiurge; Manichaean Darkness). This is where theodicy passes into [[origin-of-evil]] and [[ethical-dualism]].
6. **Moral causation (karma)** — suffering is self-caused across lives; the problem is *dissolved* rather than answered, since no omnipotent benevolent judge is posited to be defended.
7. **Refusal / protest / mystery** — the demand for a reason is itself rebuffed: by God in Job, by the inscrutability of the Babylonian Theodicy's gods, and by modern anti-theodicy.

## Cross-tradition significance (convergence, not transmission)

Theodicy is, in the Atlas's terms, a **MASSIVE-WIN of convergence rather than diffusion**. Unlike [[chaoskampf]] — where Watkins and West can reconstruct a genuine inherited Indo-European *formula* (a genealogical link) — there is **no claim of a single transmitted doctrine of theodicy**. The Babylonian Theodicy (c. -1100), the book of [[phase-2-035-job|Job]] (c. -540), the Epicurean trilemma (c. -300), Augustine's *privatio boni* (c. 400 CE), al-Ghazālī's optimism (c. 1100 CE), and Leibniz's "best of all possible worlds" (1710) are **independent confrontations with the same structural pressure**: the joint assertion of divine goodness, divine power, and real evil cannot be held without strain, and every tradition that makes all three assertions is forced toward one of the recurring answer-families above.

The honest qualifications:

- **Some links *are* real transmission.** The chain from Greek philosophical optimism → Islamic *kalām* (al-Ghazālī) → Leibniz is a genuine line of textual influence (Ormsby 1984 documents the Ashʿarite "best possible world" debate that anticipates Leibniz by six centuries); Augustine's *privatio boni* is directly inherited from Plotinus (*Enneads* I.8). These are **within** the broadly Mediterranean–monotheist stream.
- **The deep cross-cultural parallels are structural, not genealogical.** The resemblance between the Babylonian sufferer-dialogue and Job is a **typological** parallel of the wisdom-genre (debated: some, e.g. building on W. G. Lambert 1960, see the Mesopotamian *Ludlul* and the Babylonian Theodicy as the upstream *form* that the Joban poet knew of through the shared scribal culture of the ancient Near East; others read the resemblance as independent response to a universal experience). The parallel between Indian *karma* and any monotheist theodicy is purely **functional** — karma *answers the same question* (why unequal suffering?) by **dissolving** rather than defending the premise, since classical karma posits no single omnipotent benevolent creator to be justified.
- **The problem only takes its sharpest form under strict monotheism.** Polytheism can distribute responsibility across gods, and dualism can externalize evil to a rival power; the *trilemma* bites hardest precisely where a single God is asserted to be omnipotent **and** wholly good (the Abrahamic and Leibnizian setting). This is why the most acute statements (Epicurus against the philosophers' provident god; Job; 4 Ezra; the Augustinian and Leibnizian systems; post-Holocaust anti-theodicy) cluster in the monotheist and philosophical-theist traditions, while the Zoroastrian and Indian "solutions" work largely by **declining one of the three premises**.

So the comparative claim the Atlas makes here is precise: theodicy names a **convergent problem-and-response space**, in which a few transmission-lines (Plotinus → Augustine; Greek/Islamic optimism → Leibniz) run inside a much larger field of **independent re-discovery** of the same dilemma and the same handful of escapes from it.

## Disputes

- **Theodicy vs. origin-of-evil — are they one node or two?** The existing [[origin-of-evil]] node lists "theodicy" and "the problem of evil" in its *aka*, and its body already enumerates several answer-types (dualist, Gnostic/demiurgic, Watcher, free-will, soul-making, mystery, anti-theodicy). A reasonable editor could treat them as a single theme. They are kept distinct here because scholarship distinguishes the **forensic** question (justifying God given evil — the Epicurean/Leibnizian/Joban axis) from the **aetiological** question (the metaphysical *source* of evil — the cosmogonic/dualist/Gnostic axis); the two nodes are reciprocally wired so the distinction is navigable rather than duplicative. *If the project prefers a single hub, this node's grid should be merged into origin-of-evil and this slug redirected.* (Flagged for the review gate.)
- **Did Epicurus author the "trilemma"?** The argument survives only in **Lactantius** (*De ira Dei* 13, 4th c. CE), a hostile Christian source, and may be Lactantius's own polemical reconstruction or a Skeptical-Academic argument retrojected onto Epicurus. Its attribution is genuinely uncertain; the Atlas presents it as the *classic forensic form* of the problem, not as a secure Epicurean fragment.
- **Is the Job–Babylonian-Theodicy link transmission or convergence?** Long debated. The shared ancient-Near-Eastern wisdom milieu makes some awareness plausible (Lambert 1960), but there is no textual borrowing; most commentators treat Job as an independent, far more radical treatment that *rejects* the retributive consolation the Mesopotamian dialogues largely retain.
