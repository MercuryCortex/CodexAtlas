# Iconography and Aniconism

The recurring theological-political problem of whether divinity may be visually represented, and whether such representations may be venerated, painted, sculpted, or destroyed. The aniconic pole (Hebrew, Islamic) holds that representing God risks idolatry by mistaking the image for the divine; the iconic pole (Hindu *mūrti*-pūjā, Catholic Christian, Eastern Orthodox after 843) holds that images either depict legitimate divine manifestations or function as windows-to-the-prototype. Most traditions have *internal* iconoclastic moments (Hebrew prophetic critique, the Byzantine iconoclasm controversy, the Protestant Reformation, modern Hindu reform movements).

## Manifestations

- **Hebrew aniconism.** [[phase-2-018-hebrew-bible-p-source]] — the second commandment (Exod 20:4–5; Deut 5:8–9) and the rest of the Deuteronomic anti-image polemic. Archaeological work (Mettinger, *No Graven Image?* 1995) shows the aniconic tradition gradually emerged from a more permissive earlier Israelite cult that did use cultic images; the Hebrew Bible's anti-iconism is partly retrospective polemic. Israel becomes the most consistently aniconic ancient Mediterranean tradition.
- **Islamic aniconism.** [[phase-4-034-quran]] — the Qur'an does not contain a clear image-prohibition but condemns idolatry (*shirk*) of pre-Islamic Arabian Kaaba-deities. The image-prohibition develops in hadith (the angels-do-not-enter-houses-with-images traditions) and becomes definitive in classical Sunni and Shia jurisprudence — though Persian and later Mughal Islamic art produced rich figural traditions (with God Himself and Muhammad's face never depicted).
- **Byzantine iconoclasm controversy (726–843).** [[event-second-council-of-nicaea-787]] — Leo III's image-prohibition of 726; Constantine V's *Definition of Hieria* 754; the Empress Irene's restoration of icons at II Nicaea 787; the second wave of iconoclasm under Leo V (815); final restoration on the "Triumph of Orthodoxy" in 843. John of Damascus (~676–749) wrote the classical theological defense of icons (*Three Treatises on the Divine Images*): the Incarnation justifies image-making, because God himself took visible form. Theodore the Studite (759–826) deepened the case. Brubaker & Haldon 2011 reassess the older view of iconoclasm as a "movement"; they argue it was more a controversy than a popular uprising.
- **Reformation iconoclasm.** Karlstadt's Wittenberg iconoclasm of 1522; the Zurich iconoclasm under Zwingli (1524); the Edward VI / Elizabeth I Anglican removal of images; the Dutch *Beeldenstorm* of 1566; Cromwell's later English Puritan campaign. Luther was more moderate (allowed images stripped of cultic veneration); Calvin systematically theologized iconoclasm in *Institutes* I.11–12.
- **Hindu *mūrti*-pūjā.** The temple-image is the deity's *real presence* after *prāṇa-pratiṣṭhā* ("breath-installation") ritual. The Brahmo Samaj (Roy, 1828) and Arya Samaj (Saraswati, 1875) launched modern Hindu iconoclasm against *mūrti*-pūjā; orthodox response defended the practice. Contemporary Hinduism is mostly iconic but with persistent reform-iconoclastic strands.
- **Buddhist transition.** Early Buddhism (Theravada Pali canon) was aniconic — the Buddha was represented by symbols (Bodhi tree, wheel, footprints, empty throne) rather than figural form. The Gandhara-Mathura figural Buddha emerges ~1st–2nd c. CE under Hellenistic-Bactrian influence (debated). From then onward Mahayana and Vajrayana develop massive iconic traditions.

## Disputes

- **The dating and origin of the Hebrew image-prohibition.** Maximalist (the prohibition is original to Israelite religion): Wellhausen, much 19th-c. scholarship. Minimalist (the prohibition is a late Deuteronomistic / P-source innovation, retrojected): Mettinger 1995, Smith's *Origins of Biblical Monotheism*. Current consensus tilts minimalist.
- **What caused Byzantine iconoclasm.** Older explanations cite Islamic influence (Leo III as crypto-Muslim — implausible) or Paulician / Manichaean dualist influence. Brubaker & Haldon and other current scholars de-emphasize external causation, emphasize the imperial-court politics around military setbacks attributed to wrong worship.
- **Whether Reformation iconoclasm was a popular movement or top-down magisterial policy.** Eire's *War Against the Idols* (1986) traces the popular dimensions but acknowledges substantial top-down magisterial coordination.
