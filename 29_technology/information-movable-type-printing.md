# Movable-Type Printing (Bi Sheng → Jikji → Gutenberg)

## Origin and invention

**Movable-type printing** — the technique of composing pages from individually-cast or carved character-blocks that can be re-arranged for new pages — was independently invented or re-invented in **at least three distinct contexts**:

1. **Song Dynasty China — Bi Sheng's ceramic movable type, ~1040 CE**. The polymath Shen Kuo describes the invention in his *Mengxi Bitan* (Dream Pool Essays, ~1088 CE): the artisan Bi Sheng (~990–1051) fashioned individual character-types from hardened clay, arranged them in an iron frame onto a layer of pine-resin-and-wax which was heated and then cooled to fix the types in place, printed the page, then re-melted the resin to release the types for re-use. Wang Zhen perfected wooden movable type (~1300, *Nong Shu*), and Chinese movable type was sporadically used through the Yuan, Ming, and Qing — but **woodblock printing remained dominant** in China because the Chinese script has 5000+ commonly-used characters, making movable type's economic advantage marginal.

2. **Goryeo / Joseon Korea — metal movable type, ~1230s perfected by 1377**. Korean printers under the Goryeo dynasty produced the **first known metal-cast movable type** (Sangjeong Yemun, mentioned ~1234, no extant copies). The earliest **surviving** book printed with metal movable type is the **Jikji** (백운화상초록불조직지심체요절, *Anthology of Great Buddhist Priests' Zen Teachings*), printed in **1377** at Heungdeok Temple in Cheongju, Korea — **78 years before Gutenberg's Bible**. The Jikji is preserved in the Bibliothèque nationale de France and is the **earliest extant book printed with movable metal type anywhere in the world**. Joseon-dynasty Korea perfected metal type into a state-organized printing apparatus.

3. **Europe — Gutenberg's metal movable type, ~1450 Mainz**. Johannes Gutenberg (~1400–1468), goldsmith of Mainz, integrated several pre-existing technologies — wine-press, parchment + paper substrate, oil-based printing ink, lead-tin-antimony type-metal alloy, and the **type mould** (his crucial innovation, allowing rapid and consistent casting of individual letter-types) — into the first **economically viable mass-printing system** in Europe. The *42-line Bible* (Gutenberg Bible, ~1455) was printed in ~180 copies, of which 49 survive — the **single most famous physical artifact** in book history.

The question of whether Gutenberg's invention was **independent** of the Korean precedent (which preceded it by 78 years and existed in a culture connected via the Silk Road) is **contested but not resolved**: there is no documentary evidence of direct transmission, but the Mongol Yuan dynasty connected Korea to Europe via Silk Road diplomacy, and small Korean-style printed books were carried west in this period. The conventional view (Eisenstein, McLuhan, most Western historians) treats Gutenberg as an independent invention; revisionist historians (some Korean scholars, Bloom 2001) argue for likely diffusion influence. The clearest summary: **the technical idea was definitely available in Eurasia before Gutenberg; the specific Mainz implementation has no documented foreign source; the question of inspiration is genuinely open.**

## Mechanism — how it works

A working movable-type system requires **five distinct technologies** working together:

1. **Type-pieces**: individual character / letter blocks, each with the character in mirror-image relief on its top face. Must be (a) dimensionally identical so they line up, (b) mass-produced cheaply so a full set is economic, (c) durable enough to survive thousands of impressions. Bi Sheng used ceramic; Wang Zhen wooden; Koreans + Gutenberg metal.
2. **The type mould** (Gutenberg's crucial innovation): a hand-held adjustable mould that allows fast casting of consistent-dimension letter-types from a master matrix. Without the type mould, casting metal type is too slow to be economic.
3. **Composing stick + chase**: framework for arranging the types into a justified, locked page.
4. **Press**: mechanical apparatus for forcing inked type evenly onto paper. Gutenberg adapted the **screw press** of the Rhine wine + olive-oil industry. Korean and Chinese movable-type printing used direct burnishing with a brush, not a press.
5. **Oil-based ink**: water-based Chinese ink works for woodblock + brush burnishing but bleeds on metal type and does not transfer well under pressure. Gutenberg developed an **oil-based ink** (boiled linseed oil + lampblack + copper / lead salts) borrowed from contemporary Netherlandish oil painting. Oil-based ink transfers cleanly from metal type to paper under press pressure.

## Transmission chain

**Bi Sheng** (Song China, ~1040) — ceramic movable type, sporadically used through Yuan + Ming → **Korea, Goryeo dynasty** (~1230s, metal movable type) → **Jikji, 1377** (Cheongju, Korea — earliest extant metal-type book) → **Joseon Korea** (state-organized metal-type printing, 15th c.) → **Gutenberg** (Mainz, ~1450; *42-line Bible* ~1455 — whether by transmission or re-invention, the printing revolution begins) → **rapid European diffusion**: by 1500 there are **printers in 250 European cities** and an estimated **20 million books in circulation** (Eisenstein 1979, the canonical scholarly figure) → **Aldus Manutius** (Venice, ~1500 — the Aldine press perfects the small portable *octavo* book and the italic typeface; books become genuinely affordable to literate non-elites) → **Luther's 95 Theses** (1517 — printed on the new presses, distributed across Germany in weeks; the Reformation is the **first mass-print political-religious event** in human history) → **Calvin's *Institutes*** (1536) → **Tyndale's English New Testament** (1526) → **King James Bible** (1611) → ubiquitous print culture by 1700 → **rotary press** (1843 Richard Hoe) → **modern industrial newspapers + paperbacks** → **digital displacement** in the late 20th–21st century.

## Cultural and religious impact

- **The Reformation as mass-print phenomenon**: Andrew Pettegree's *Brand Luther* (2015) makes the case definitively — the Reformation **is not just enabled by printing; it is constituted by printing**. Luther was the first mass-print celebrity; his pamphlets (cheap, vernacular, illustrated by Cranach) sold in tens of thousands across the Holy Roman Empire within months. The Catholic Counter-Reformation responded by **also adopting mass print** (Jesuit catechisms, Tridentine devotional manuals, Index of Forbidden Books) but lost the early initiative. The doctrinal claim of **sola scriptura** is technologically conditioned by the existence of a printed vernacular Bible in lay hands.

- **The printing-press → Scientific Revolution chain**: the Scientific Revolution (~1543 Copernicus *De Revolutionibus* through ~1687 Newton *Principia*) is a print phenomenon. The standardization of figures, tables, mathematical notation, and reproducible diagrams (Eisenstein argues) is what permits the **cumulative-knowledge structure** of modern science. Pre-print science was condemned to recursive copying error; print science could correct, refine, and build.

- **Vernacular literacy as religious-political force**: print drives mass literacy in vernaculars. The Bible in English / German / French / Italian / Dutch is read by lay people; theology becomes a popular discourse; the medieval clergy's monopoly on biblical interpretation collapses; **the priesthood-of-all-believers doctrine becomes practically possible**.

- **Korea's printing as state-Confucian project**: the Joseon Korean printing apparatus was a state-sponsored Confucian-scholarly enterprise — its purpose was the production of the Confucian classics for the meritocratic examination system. **Different religious purpose, same technology**: the technology serves whichever religious-cultural priority commissions it.

- **Print and the modern subject**: McLuhan, *The Gutenberg Galaxy* (1962), and Walter J. Ong, *Orality and Literacy* (1982), argue that the print substrate produces a new kind of human cognition — silent, linear, private, interiorized — that becomes the cognitive substrate of modernity. The thesis is contested but the link between print and modernity is unbroken in the historical record.

## MASSIVE-WIN clusters

- **Three independent re-inventions**: Bi Sheng (Song, 1040, ceramic), Korean metal-type (1230s–1377), Gutenberg (Mainz, ~1450, metal-with-screw-press). One technology, three independent or quasi-independent inventions, each unlocking a **different cultural rupture**: Chinese Song printing fuels the Confucian classical revival; Korean metal type serves the Joseon Confucian state; Gutenberg's press fuels the Protestant Reformation. The same technology produces three completely different religious-political consequences depending on what the press was commissioned to print. **Technological determinism is bounded by what the printer chooses to print.**

- **The Gutenberg → Luther chain**: ~1450 Gutenberg Bible. ~1500 Aldine octavo + italic. 1517 Luther's 95 Theses. 1522 Luther's German New Testament. 1526 Tyndale's English New Testament. 1534 Luther's complete German Bible. 1611 King James Bible. **The Protestant Reformation's central religious claim is materially conditioned by 167 years of compounding print technology.** Without the press, Luther is a local Augustinian's grievance; with the press, Luther is a continental religious revolution.

- **Jikji 1377**: the existence of the Korean Jikji as the earliest extant metal-type book — 78 years before Gutenberg's Bible — is one of the most important corrections to **Eurocentric histories of technology** in the modern scholarly record. UNESCO Memory of the World registered Jikji in 2001 precisely to encode this correction publicly.

- **Print + paper + Reformation = the modern world**: this is the canonical example of **co-emergent technologies producing a civilizational rupture**. Movable type alone is uneconomic without cheap paper. Paper alone produces hand-copied scriptoria. Print + paper together produce the modern information era. The three preconditions — paper (Han / Talas / Játiva / Fabriano), movable type (Bi Sheng / Korea / Gutenberg), oil-based ink (Netherlandish painting / Mainz) — converge in a single workshop in Mainz around 1450, and the world is different forever within a single century.
