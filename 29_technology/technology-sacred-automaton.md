# Sacred and Wonder Automata

## Identity

A **sacred or wonder automaton** (Greek αὐτόματον, "self-moving") is a mechanical figure — a bird, a beast, a servant, a god, a monk — engineered to move, sound, or act *on its own*, in service of religious awe, court spectacle, or the marking of sacred time. This node treats the automaton as a *tradition of the artificial-living figure* whose home is the **Hellenistic-Greek pneumatic and hydraulic mechanics of Ptolemaic Alexandria** (Ktesibios, Philo of Byzantium, and above all Hero of Alexandria), and whose long afterlife runs through Byzantine and Islamic courts, Chinese and European clock-towers, the medieval church, and the philosophical androids of the Enlightenment.

It is deliberately distinguished from the clock as such: many automata are *clock-mounted* (the jack that strikes the hour, the Strasbourg cock, Su Song's time-announcing figures), and the node wires to that mechanical substrate — but its own subject is the **figure** and the theological question the figure raises: does a made thing that moves *live*? That is the golem/anima question, and the automaton is the place where mechanism confronts it directly (Truitt 2015; Riskin 2016).

## In the record — temple, court, and clock-tower

**Hellenistic Alexandria (3rd c. BCE – 1st c. CE).** Ktesibios (fl. c. 270 BCE) and Philo of Byzantium pioneered pneumatic and hydraulic devices; **Hero of Alexandria**'s *Pneumatica* (1st c. CE) preserves the classic *sacred* automata: **temple doors that open by themselves** when a fire is lit on the altar (heated air expands, displaces water, and works a counterweight), **figures that pour libations** as the altar burns, a **coin-operated holy-water dispenser** (the first vending machine), and hydraulic **singing birds** silenced by an owl. Hero's companion *Automata* describes self-moving figure-theatres. Whether these were genuine cult apparatus, priest-craft, or engineering demonstrations is debated (see Disputes).

**Byzantium.** The imperial **Throne of Solomon** in the Magnaura palace — a gilded plane tree full of singing mechanical birds, **roaring golden lions**, and a throne that rose toward the ceiling — was described with astonishment by the Latin envoy **Liutprand of Cremona** (*Antapodosis*, 949 CE). Automata here served sacral kingship: the emperor's throne performed the wonder of his authority.

**Islamic world.** The **Banū Mūsā** brothers' *Kitāb al-Ḥiyal* (Book of Ingenious Devices, 9th c. Baghdad) and, at the apex, **al-Jazarī**'s *Kitāb fī maʿrifat al-ḥiyal al-handasiyya* (1206) — the **elephant clock**, the **castle clock**, a musical automaton boat, and a **hand-washing automaton** with an automatic attendant. Al-Jazarī built directly on translated Greek mechanics and on Banū Mūsā; many devices are timekeeping / prayer-time instruments.

**China.** **Su Song**'s astronomical **clock-tower** at Kaifeng (1088–1092), documented in his *Xin Yi Xiang Fa Yao*, carried tiers of **jack-work figures** that emerged to ring bells, beat drums, and display time-plaques — following an older Chinese lineage (Zhang Heng's armillary; the water-clock of Yi Xing and Liang Lingzan, 725 CE, with striking figures). Needham argues this is an **independent** clockwork tradition, not a Western import.

**Medieval Latin church.** Automated **"jacks" (jacquemarts)** struck the hours on bells from the 13th–14th centuries; the **Strasbourg astronomical clock cock** (1354) — a mechanical rooster that flapped and crowed — is the **oldest surviving automaton in the world**, and its Petrine crowing bound machine-wonder to sacred narrative. In the 16th century the **mechanical praying monk** (c. 1560, attributed to Juanelo Turriano, reportedly an ex-voto for the recovery of Don Carlos) walked, beat its breast, kissed a rosary, and mouthed prayers — a *devotional* automaton (King 2002).

**Enlightenment.** **Jacques de Vaucanson**'s Flute Player (1738) and the **Digesting Duck** (1739), and the **Jaquet-Droz** androids — The Writer, The Draughtsman, The Musician (c. 1768–1774) — shift the automaton's meaning from temple-miracle to *philosophical demonstration*: living-seeming motion as evidence for (or spectacle of) the body-as-machine.

## Cross-tradition significance

Two different kinds of connection run through this material, and they must be kept apart.

**The mechanical spine is largely a documented transmission.** Greek mechanical texts (Hero, Philo, and the Ktesibian tradition) were translated into Arabic and stand behind the Banū Mūsā and **al-Jazarī**; surviving Greek and Arabic mechanics in turn fed the clockwork of Byzantium and Latin Europe (Truitt 2015; Hill 1974). This Alexandria → Byzantium → Islam → Latin-Europe arc is a **genetic** chain, not mere coincidence. **China is the debated exception**: Needham (1960) argues that Su Song's clock-jacks descend from an autochthonous Chinese tradition (Zhang Heng → Yi Xing 725 → Su Song 1088), a **convergent** parallel rather than a borrowing from the West — the mainstream view, against older and largely-rejected diffusionist speculation.

**The theological through-line — the artificial-living question — is convergent, not transmitted.** The recurring anxiety of the made-that-moves takes independent forms: the Egyptian **[[ritual-opening-of-the-mouth|Opening of the Mouth]]** animates a manufactured cult statue by *rite*; the Hermetic **[[phase-4-012-asclepius|Asclepius]]** has Egyptian priests draw spirits into statues to make "gods" (attacked by Augustine, *City of God* 8.23–24); the Jewish **[[rabbi-judah-loew|golem]]** is quickened by the divine *Name*; and the mechanical automaton poses the same question in a **mechanist** key — Descartes' animals-as-machines (**[[philosophy-cartesian-dualism|bête-machine]]**) and La Mettrie's *L'Homme Machine* (1747). These are the same boundary-problem worked out by different means (ritual, word, gearwork), not a single lineage — the human echo of the **[[demiurge]]**'s making of a living cosmos, and the standing provocation of the **[[critique-of-idolatry]]** that a made image is being taken for a living god. Truitt and Riskin both frame the automaton as the object on which "is motion life?" is repeatedly re-fought.

## Disputes

- **Were Hero's temple devices religious?** Scholars divide on whether the *Pneumatica*'s automata were functioning cult apparatus producing genuine temple "miracles," clerical showmanship, or engineering set-pieces and toys (Truitt 2015 surveys the range; the corpus itself is a technical manual, not a liturgical one).
- **China: independent or diffused?** Needham's independent-invention thesis for Chinese clockwork-with-jacks is the mainstream position; the alternative (transmission from Hellenistic/Islamic mechanics) is generally rejected for the escapement-clock lineage but remains a background debate.
- **Is the golem an "automaton" at all?** Grouping the ritually/verbally animated golem and cult-statue with the geared automaton under one modern rubric of "artificial life" (or Truitt's own coinage "medieval robots") is partly an **anachronism**: pre-modern sources sharply distinguish animation-by-spirit/word (theurgy, magic) from animation-by-mechanism. Idel (1990) cautions against reading the golem as proto-robotics. The parallels here are typological — a shared *question* — not a shared category the traditions themselves recognised.
