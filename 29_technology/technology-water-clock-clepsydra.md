# Water-Clock (Clepsydra)

## Identity

The **water-clock** or **clepsydra** (Greek *klepsydra*, κλεψύδρα, "water-thief") is the principal pre-mechanical instrument for **measuring the hour** — the flow of a regulated stream of water used to mark the passage of time within the day and night. It stands apart from the [[calendars-comparative|calendar]], which *reckons* the day, month, and year from astronomical cycles: the water-clock does not count days but **subdivides the day into hours**, the unit no celestial cycle supplies directly. Two basic forms recur across every tradition that built one: the **outflow** clepsydra, a vessel that empties through a small orifice while an interior scale reads the falling water-line (the Egyptian and early Greek type), and the **inflow** clepsydra, a receiving vessel that fills at a regulated rate while a **float** rises to drive an indicator (the Hellenistic-Chinese-Islamic precision type). The engineering problem in every case is the same — water drains faster when the head is high and slower as it falls — and the history of the device is largely the history of solutions to it: sloped vessel walls, constant-head overflow tanks, and float-valve regulators that hold the flow-rate steady.

Because it measures the hour rather than the day, the water-clock is repeatedly bound to **ritual time**: the Egyptian temple's hourly night-liturgy, the equal speaking-time of the Athenian law-court, the watches of the Chinese imperial night, and the five daily prayer-times of Islam. In the Atlas it is filed as a technology (a timekeeping instrument), wired to its comparative hub [[theme-three-independent-reinventions]] and to the Egyptian night-hour system [[astronomy-egyptian-decans]] with which it shares a function; it is the ancestor of the weight-driven **mechanical clock**, to which it hands off the problem of the regulated escapement.

## In Egypt — the temple night-clock

The **oldest surviving water-clock in the world** is the alabaster **Karnak clepsydra**, an outflow vessel from the Temple of Amun-Re at Karnak bearing the cartouches of **Amenhotep III** (c. -1391 to -1353), recovered in 1904 and now in the Egyptian Museum, Cairo (JE 37525). It is a truncated-cone bucket with a small drain-hole near the base; the inner wall carries **twelve columns of scale-marks, one per month**, because the Egyptian night was divided into **twelve "hours" of varying length** (seasonal hours, longer in winter, shorter in summer), and the water-line had to be read against a different scale in each month. Egyptian temple service required the priest on the night-watch to know the hour in order to perform the **hourly offices**; the water-clock supplied that knowledge on cloudy nights and indoors, complementing the stellar method of the **decans** — the 36 ten-day stars whose risings and transits marked the night-hours on coffin-lid star tables (see [[astronomy-egyptian-decans]]). A tomb-biography of the official **Amenemhet**, under Amenhotep I (c. -1500), already boasts of having built a water-clock, so the Egyptian instrument is at least early-Eighteenth-Dynasty. The Egyptian priority is not merely chronological: the very idea of the **twelve-hour night**, carried on the water-clock's monthly scales, is the ancestor of the 12 + 12 = 24-hour day the world still keeps.

## In Greece and Rome — the court-timer and Ctesibius's clock

The Greek *klepsydra* had two lives. The humble one was the **law-court speaking-timer**: a plain outflow pot that allotted each litigant a fixed measure of water, and so a fixed measure of speech — an instrument of *isonomia*, of equal time before the law. A surviving Athenian example from the Agora (c. -400) holds about two *choes* (~6.4 litres) and runs for roughly six minutes; Aristotle's *Constitution of the Athenians* (67) describes the court officer stopping the flow while documents were read. The grand life of the Greek water-clock belongs to **Ctesibius of Alexandria** (fl. c. -270), founder of the Alexandrian school of pneumatics, who built the first genuinely **precision** water-clock. His decisive innovation was the **constant-head regulator** — an overflow chamber that keeps the water level (and hence the pressure and flow-rate) constant — feeding a receiving vessel in which a **float** rose to turn a pointer or lift a figure that marked the hours, in some versions against a rotating drum that adjusted for the changing length of the seasonal hour through the year. **Vitruvius** (*De architectura* IX.8) gives the fullest ancient description. Ctesibius's clock is the engineering summit of Greco-Roman horology, and the point at which the water-clock becomes an automaton as much as a timer.

## In China — the astronomical water-clock and the escapement

China developed the **inflow astronomical water-clock** as a largely independent tradition, and carried it further mechanically than anyone else. Chinese horologists solved the falling-head problem not with a float-valve but with a **cascade of compensating tanks** (the "steelyard clepsydra," which weighed the inflow and adjusted it), stabilising the flow to a degree that made the water-clock an instrument of state astronomy. **Zhang Heng** (78–139 CE) coupled a water-clock to a rotating **armillary sphere**; **Yi Xing** and **Liang Lingzan** built a water-driven celestial globe with an escapement-like link-work in 725 CE. The tradition culminates in the great **clock-tower of Su Song** at Kaifeng, completed in **1092** and documented in his treatise *Xin Yi Xiang Fa Yao* ("New Design for an Armillary Clock," c. 1094). A constant-level clepsydra poured onto the scoops of a great water-wheel, which a **"celestial balance" (*tianti*)** released **one step at a time** — a true **escapement**, the mechanism that converts continuous flow into the discrete, counted beat of a clock. Su Song's ~12-metre tower drove an armillary sphere, a celestial globe, and a five-storey pagoda of time-announcing jacks. Joseph Needham's *Heavenly Clockwork* (1960) argued that this Chinese escapement is the direct **ancestor of the mechanical clock's escapement**, the single most consequential horological invention before the mainspring.

## In the Islamic world — the monumental water-clock

Islamic engineers inherited the **Hellenistic** water-clock — the float-regulated pneumatics of Ctesibius, Philo of Byzantium, and Hero of Alexandria — through the Baghdad translation movement, and built it into the most elaborate **monumental clocks** of the medieval world. The **Banū Mūsā** brothers' *Book of Ingenious Devices* (9th c.) and al-Murādī's *Kitāb al-asrār* (11th-c. al-Andalus, with geared trains) begin the corpus; the **Jayrūn gate-clock** of the Umayyad Mosque at Damascus (described by the father of Ibn al-Sāʿātī, "the son of the clockmaker") was a public civic clock dropping balls and moving figures to mark the hours. The tradition peaks with **Badīʿ al-Zamān al-Jazarī** (1136–1206), whose *Book of Knowledge of Ingenious Mechanical Devices* (1206) describes float-regulated water-clocks of astonishing complexity — the **candle-clock**, the **castle clock**, and the celebrated **elephant clock**, which self-consciously assembles a Greek water-mechanism, an Indian timing-bowl, a Chinese dragon, and an Egyptian phoenix into one machine, an emblem of the device's own cross-cultural genealogy. That the Islamic monumental water-clock was a marvel of *diplomacy* as well as engineering is fixed by the brass water-clock **Hārūn al-Rashīd sent to Charlemagne in 807**, which dropped a ball for each hour and astonished the Frankish court (Royal Frankish Annals; Einhard).

## Cross-tradition significance

The water-clock is a rare object that is at once a case of **independent reinvention** and a case of **documented transmission**, and the honest account keeps the two apart tradition by tradition.

- **Egypt → Greece: probable diffusion, Greek refinement.** The outflow clepsydra of pharaonic Egypt and the earliest Greek klepsydra are close enough in form, and Alexandria (where Ctesibius worked, under Ptolemaic rule, with Egyptian temple water-clocks in daily use) is so obvious a meeting-point, that diffusion from Egypt to the Greek world is the mainstream view. What is *Greek* is the **float-regulator** — the constant-head refinement is a Hellenistic invention, not an Egyptian inheritance.
- **Greece → Islam: secure transmission.** This is the strong link. Al-Jazarī and the Banū Mūsā build **explicitly** on the named Hellenistic pneumaticians through their Arabic translations; the float-regulated flow of the Islamic clocks is directly Ctesibian. Here the chain is textual and undisputed, not typological.
- **China: independent.** The Chinese inflow-and-overflow astronomical water-clock, with its own flow-compensation and its own escapement, develops without contact with the Mediterranean line. It is the clearest of the three "independent reinventions," and the reason the device sits under [[theme-three-independent-reinventions]].

Across all four zones the instrument is bound to **religious time** ([[theme-technology-as-religious-transmission-vehicle]]): the Egyptian hourly night-office, the Athenian equal-time-before-the-law, the watches of the Chinese imperial and monastic night, and the Islamic office of the ***muwaqqit*** (mosque timekeeper), whose determination of the five daily prayer-times made horology a religious science. The water-clock even crosses into medicine: **Herophilus of Alexandria** (c. -300) timed the pulse against a portable clepsydra, the first quantitative instrument of clinical diagnosis (see [[medicine-pulse-diagnosis-cross-tradition]]).

## Disputes

The live controversy is the **succession to the mechanical clock**. The weight-driven European clock with a **verge-and-foliot escapement** appears, fully formed and undescribed, around 1275–1300 in Latin Christendom. Because a genuine escapement had existed in Chinese water-clocks since Yi Xing (725) and Su Song (1092) — centuries earlier — **Joseph Needham** (*Heavenly Clockwork*, 1960) raised the possibility that knowledge of the escapement reached Europe along the Mongol corridors, making the European clock a transmitted, not an independent, invention. **David Landes** (*Revolution in Time*, 1983) and most Western horologists dissent: there is **no documentary chain** of transmission, and the two mechanisms are mechanically unlike — the Chinese "celestial balance" gates a continuously turning **water-wheel** hydraulically, while the European verge is a **weight-driven oscillator** with no water at all. The mainstream verdict is therefore **independent invention** of the mechanical escapement in Europe, with the Chinese hydraulic escapement a magnificent but separate achievement. As with the deep's descent from Tiamat, the parallel is real and the priority is Chinese, but the *transmission* is unproven — a structural convergence more securely than a borrowing.

A second, smaller dispute concerns **priority within the ancient Near East**: whether the Egyptian temple clepsydra or the Babylonian **weight-of-water** clock (which measured time by the *mass* of water discharged, in the mul.apin astronomical tradition) is the earlier form. The two are best read as broadly contemporary, independent Bronze-Age solutions rather than one derived from the other.
