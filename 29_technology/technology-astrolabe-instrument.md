# Astrolabe (Planispheric Astrolabe)

## Identity

The **astrolabe** (Greek *astrolabon*, "star-taker"; Arabic *al-asṭurlāb*) is a hand-held brass instrument that carries a **two-dimensional stereographic projection of the celestial sphere** on a flat plate, over which a pierced star-map rotates. It is the great **analog computer of the pre-modern world**: with it a user can tell the time by day (from the Sun's altitude) or by night (from a star's), find the altitude and position of any bright star or the Sun, determine the times of sunrise and sunset, solve problems of spherical astronomy that would otherwise require trigonometric tables, and — in its Islamic religious application — fix the **times of the five daily prayers** and orient toward the **qibla**, the direction of Mecca. For roughly eighteen centuries, from Late Antiquity to the eighteenth century, it was the single most important astronomical instrument in the Greek, Islamic, and Latin-Christian worlds.

In the Atlas it is filed as a **technology** — an *instrument* — deliberately kept distinct from the astronomy-lens nodes it serves ([[astronomy-almagest]], [[astronomy-antikythera-mechanism]], [[hipparchus-astronomer]], [[ptolemy-astronomer]]): those are the *theory* the astrolabe embodies, and they are wired here as parallels rather than duplicated. Its home tradition is taken to be **Islamic**, because although the projection-mathematics is Hellenistic, the astrolabe as a fully realized, mass-produced brass instrument — with its surviving corpus of dated examples and its systematic construction-treatises — is an achievement of the medieval Islamic world.

## Mechanism — how it works

The instrument is a stack of engraved brass discs held in a rim and pinned at the centre:

- **Mater** ("mother") — the base disc with a raised rim (the *limb*), graduated in hours or degrees around its edge, hollowed to hold the plates.
- **Plates / tympans** (*ṣafīḥa*) — interchangeable discs, each engraved with the **stereographic projection** of the sky for one specific **latitude**: the horizon, the almucantars (circles of equal altitude), the azimuth lines, and — crucially — the **lines of the unequal ("seasonal") hours** used for prayer-timing. A latitude change means swapping the plate.
- **Rete** (*ʿankabūt*, "spider") — an openwork disc, a cut-out **map of the fixed stars**: the ecliptic circle bearing the zodiac, with pointers reaching to the positions of the brightest named stars. The rete **rotates** over the plate, reproducing the daily rotation of the heavens.
- **Rule and alidade** — a straight rule on the front for reading coordinates; on the back, a sighting bar (**alidade**) turning over an altitude scale, used to *take* the altitude of a star or the Sun. The back also typically carries a shadow-square, calendar, and zodiac scales.

The governing mathematics is the **stereographic projection**: the celestial sphere is projected from the south celestial pole onto the plane of the equator. This projection has the vital property that **it maps every circle on the sphere to a circle (or straight line) on the plate** — so the horizon, the altitude circles, and the star-paths can all be drawn with a compass. To use the instrument, one measures an altitude with the alidade, then rotates the rete until the corresponding star (or the Sun's ecliptic position) sits on the right almucantar of the plate; the whole configuration of the sky for that moment is then laid out mechanically, and time, rising-times, or house-cusps are read straight off.

## Origin and transmission (Greek → Islamic → Latin)

The astrolabe's history is one of the best-documented **long-distance transmission chains** in the history of science, and — unlike many cross-tradition parallels in this vault — it is a case of genuine **transmission, not independent convergence**.

- **Hellenistic theory (c. -150 to +150).** The stereographic projection is traditionally credited to **Hipparchus** (c. -150), though on inference rather than surviving text (see Disputes). Its earliest *extant* mathematical statement is **Ptolemy's *Planisphaerium*** (2nd c. CE), which survives only through its Arabic and Latin translations — a fact that itself epitomises the transmission story.
- **Late Antiquity (4th-7th c.).** The instrument proper first appears by name in this period: **Theon of Alexandria** (4th c.) reportedly wrote a treatise on it (now lost, known from later citation); the earliest *surviving* Greek account is by **John Philoponus** (6th c., *On the Use of the Astrolabe*), and the earliest in **Syriac** is by the bishop **Severus Sebokht** (7th c.).
- **Islamic perfection (8th-11th c.).** Under the Abbasids the astrolabe became the flagship instrument of a mature mathematical astronomy. **al-Fazārī** (8th c.) is named in the tradition as the first Muslim to construct one; **al-Khwārizmī** (9th c., Baghdad) wrote an early systematic treatise on its construction and use; **al-Bīrūnī** (11th c.) produced the exhaustive theoretical survey. In al-Andalus, **al-Zarqālī (Azarquiel)** of Toledo (11th c.) invented the **universal astrolabe** (the *saphaea* / *al-shakkāziyya*), a single latitude-independent plate that dispenses with the stack of latitude-plates.
- **Latin Europe (11th-14th c.).** The instrument passed north through the Andalusian frontier and the **Toledo translations**; the earliest Latin astrolabe texts appear around the turn of the millennium, and the tradition culminates in **Geoffrey Chaucer's *A Treatise on the Astrolabe*** (1391) — written in English for "little Lewis," the **earliest technical manual in the English language**.

## Religious use — prayer-times and the qibla

What makes the astrolabe a **religious** technology, and not merely a scientific one, is its integration into the ritual timetable of Islam. Islamic prayer (*ṣalāt*) is due at five moments defined **astronomically** — by the Sun's altitude and by the length of shadows — and prayer must be performed facing the **qibla**, the great-circle direction to the Kaaba in Mecca, itself a problem of mathematical geography. From roughly the thirteenth century, mosques employed a salaried **muwaqqit** (timekeeper), an astronomer whose instrument was the astrolabe: many surviving Islamic plates are engraved with **prayer-lines** for the *ẓuhr* and *ʿaṣr* prayers directly among the hour-lines. The astrolabe thus stands with crescent-visibility computation ([[astronomy-islamic-lunar-crescent-visibility]]) and the qibla-compass as one of the central meeting-points of exact science and ritual obligation in Islam — the same convergence of instrument and devotion catalogued at [[navigation-compass]] for the qibla-compass. In Latin Christendom the same instrument governed the astronomically-defined **canonical hours** and, above all, served **astrology** — the casting of horoscopes ([[divination-astrology-comparative]]), for which the ascendant and house-cusps are read straight off the plate.

## Cross-tradition significance (transmission, not convergence)

The astrolabe is the Atlas's cleanest example of a **single instrument physically carrying a body of knowledge across three religious civilisations without losing its identity**. Where most cross-tradition parallels in this vault are *typological* (independent cultures filling the same slot), the astrolabe's chain is *genealogical*: the very projection Ptolemy described was translated into Arabic, engineered into brass by al-Khwārizmī and al-Bīrūnī, universalised by al-Zarqālī, and translated onward into Latin — the same mathematical object migrating through Greek, Syriac, Arabic, Hebrew, and Latin hands. Its one true *typological* parallel is the [[astronomy-antikythera-mechanism|Antikythera Mechanism]]: the other great analog astronomical computer of antiquity, which solved the same problem — embodying mathematical astronomy in a working device — by an entirely different technology (bronze gearing rather than flat projection). The Antikythera design died; the astrolabe design lived, precisely because it was cheap to make, easy to copy, and endlessly re-usable. The monumental instruments of the Indian [[astronomy-jantar-mantar-observatory|Jantar Mantar]] are a later, downstream cousin: Mughal-era Indian astrolabe workshops were themselves a branch of the Islamic tradition.

## Disputes

- **Who invented the projection.** The attribution of the stereographic projection to **Hipparchus** is the standard textbook claim but is **inferential**: no work of his describing it survives, and the earliest secure witness is Ptolemy's *Planisphaerium* three centuries later. Some historians therefore credit the projection's instrumental application to the Late-Antique Alexandrian milieu (Theon, Philoponus) rather than to Hipparchus personally (Neugebauer 1949 lays out the evidentiary limits).
- **Did the Greeks build a working astrolabe, or only theorise the projection?** The textual references (Theon, Philoponus) show the *instrument* was known in Late Antiquity, but **no Greek or Roman astrolabe survives**; the oldest extant instruments are Islamic (9th-10th c.). Whether a fully-developed planispheric astrolabe existed before the Islamic period, or whether the Islamic astronomers were the ones who turned a described device into a standardised, mass-produced instrument, is a genuine question — and it is the reason this node's home tradition is set as **Islamic** rather than Greek: the surviving material and textual record of the *instrument as such* is overwhelmingly Islamic, even though the *mathematics* is Hellenistic.
