# Mongolian — The Vertical Script: Aramaic Letters Turned Ninety Degrees

## What It Is

Classical Mongolian script (*Hudum Mongol bichig*) is the only major script in the world written **vertically top-to-bottom in columns that run left-to-right**. Chinese is traditionally vertical with columns right-to-left; Mongolian alone combines downward lines with a leftward-to-rightward page. The reason is a single mechanical event in script history: somewhere in the Uyghur-to-Mongol transmission, a horizontal right-to-left Aramaic-family script was **rotated ninety degrees counterclockwise** — likely under the gravitational pull of Chinese column-writing (Kara 1996). Rotate a right-to-left page and the lines become top-to-bottom columns ordered left-to-right. The strangest page-geometry in active use is a fossilized change of reading posture.

The adoption is precisely datable and personal. In **1204**, having defeated the Naiman confederation, **Genghis Khan** captured the Naimans' Uyghur seal-keeper **Tata-tonga** — and rather than executing the bureaucracy, hired it: Tata-tonga was ordered to teach the Uyghur script for writing Mongolian to the imperial princes (Atwood 2004; Kara 2005). The earliest surviving monument, the **Stele of Yisüngge** (the "Genghis Stone," c. 1224–25, now in the Hermitage), commemorates — fittingly for the culture — a record-setting bowshot by the khan's nephew.

## The Deep Line: From the Achaemenid Chancellery to the Steppe

Mongolian is the easternmost living daughter of the **Aramaic** scribal tradition. The chain is documented at every link (Kara 1996; Skjærvø 1996):

```
Aramaic (Achaemenid chancellery, c. -500)
    └─→ Sogdian (by 312 CE) — the Silk-Road merchant cursive
          └─→ Old Uyghur (8th–9th c.) — adopted with the Manichaean conversion of 762
                └─→ MONGOLIAN (1204, Tata-tonga) — rotated vertical
                      ├─→ Manchu (1599/1632) — the script of the Qing empire
                      └─→ Oirat Clear Script (1648, Zaya Pandita)
```

The same Iron-Age letter-skeleton that became the Hebrew square script and (via Nabataean) the Arabic of the Quran also became, by the eastern road, the script in which the decrees of the largest contiguous land empire in history were issued. When the Manchus adapted it in 1599 (refined with diacritics in 1632), the Aramaic line acquired the chancellery of the Qing — meaning that into the 20th century, the empires at BOTH ends of Asia (Ottoman Arabic-script paperwork in the west, Qing Manchu-script paperwork in the east) were administered in scripts descending from the same Aramaic hand. See [[alphabet-sogdian]] for the carrier stage and [[alphabet-aramaic]] for the pivot.

## The Script of a Buddhist Canon

The script's religious payload came late but at full scale. After the Mongol re-conversion to Tibetan Buddhism (16th c.), translation bureaus undertook one of the great canon-transfer projects in religious history: the **Mongolian Kanjur** — the entire Tibetan Buddhist scriptural canon — completed under Ligdan Khan (1628–29) and block-printed at Beijing (1718–20), followed by the 225-volume **Tanjur** commentary canon (1742–49) (Kara 2005; Atwood 2004). Mongolian monastic civilization ran bilingually — Tibetan for liturgy, Mongolian script for the vernacular canon, chronicles, and devotional literature — which is why the vertical script's largest corpus is Buddhist. The liturgical partner script is [[alphabet-tibetan-tantric]].

Before the Buddhist era, the script's most resonant religious formula is the chancellery invocation that opens imperial documents: *Möngke Tengri-yin küchün-dür* — "By the power of Eternal Heaven" — the Tengrist sky-mandate in which the khans framed their rule.

## The Phags-pa Detour

The vertical script survived a designed replacement. In 1269 **Kublai Khan** commissioned the Tibetan lama **Phags-pa** to create a universal square script (derived from Tibetan, also written vertically) intended to write every language of the Yuan empire — Mongolian, Chinese, Tibetan, Turkic. It was used on seals, paper money, and edicts, and died with the Yuan dynasty (1368). The organic Uyghur-Mongolian script outlived the engineered one — a recurring pattern in script history: imposed rational designs lose to entrenched scribal habit unless a scripture locks them in (contrast Hangul, which had a royal scripture-and-printing apparatus behind it; [[alphabet-hangul]]).

## A Living Script, Politically Alive

The script remains in daily use in **Inner Mongolia** (China), where it is co-official. The independent state of **Mongolia** was moved to Cyrillic in the Soviet era (1940s); a 2015/2020 language law mandates restoring the vertical script to official use alongside Cyrillic — an unusual case of a state legislating its way back up its own script genealogy. The vertical script functions as a sovereignty symbol on both sides of the border, for opposite reasons.
