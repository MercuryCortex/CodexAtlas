# Latin Alphabet — Western Greek via Etruscan

## What it is
The Latin alphabet — today the most widely used writing system on earth — is the direct descendant of the Western Greek (Euboean/Chalcidian) branch of the Greek alphabet, transmitted via the Etruscan intermediary. The Etruscans adopted the Greek alphabet c. 700 BCE from Greek colonies in southern Italy (Cumae, Pithecusae); the early Romans adopted the Etruscan alphabet c. 600 BCE, adapting it for Latin phonology.

The Latin script — with its characteristic C, G, Q forms that differ from Eastern Greek — reflects the Western Greek variant that was in circulation before the Athenian standardization of 403 BCE. All Western European scripts (French, Spanish, German, English, Portuguese, Italian, etc.) are Latin descendants. The Latin alphabet is the most widely used writing system in the world today.

## MASSIVE WIN — cross-tradition edges
- **The script that conquered the world — and its letters are a fossil record of three earlier civilizations.** Every time the Latin alphabet is used — for over 70 languages and the majority of humanity's writing today — it carries a layered inheritance: the *shapes* come from Western Greek via the Etruscans (whence the oddities of C, G, and Q), the Greek letters themselves came from **Phoenician**, and the Phoenician letters from **Proto-Sinaitic**, whose signs were acrophonic pictures borrowed from **Egyptian hieroglyphs**. The English letter "A" is a Phoenician ox-head (*ʾalp*) turned on its horns; "B" is a Semitic house (*bayt*). Through the Roman Empire and then the Church (which wrote scripture and liturgy in Latin script for a millennium), this one branch of the alphabet tree became the default writing system of the modern world — a single unbroken line of transmission running from a Bronze Age turquoise mine in Sinai to this sentence.
