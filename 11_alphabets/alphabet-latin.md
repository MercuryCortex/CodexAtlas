# Latin Alphabet — Western Greek via Etruscan

## What it is

The **Latin alphabet** — today the most widely used writing system on earth, the script of more than 70 languages and the majority of the world's written text — is a *true alphabet* (it writes both consonants and vowels with full letters) descended from the **Western Greek** (Euboean / Chalcidian) branch of the Greek alphabet, transmitted through an **Etruscan** intermediary.

The chain is short and well-attested: Greek colonists at Cumae and Pithecusae brought the Western Greek alphabet to Italy; the Etruscans adopted it c. 700 BCE; the early Romans adopted the Etruscan alphabet c. 600 BCE and reworked it for Latin phonology. The earliest Latin inscriptions — the Lapis Niger in the Roman Forum and the Praeneste fibula — are conventionally dated to around 600 BCE.

## A fossil record in the letterforms

Because Latin froze a *Western* Greek variant before the Athenian standardization of 403 BCE, its letters preserve archaic features the classical Greek alphabet later abandoned:

- **C, K, Q** — three letters for a single /k/ sound — are a direct inheritance of the **Etruscan** three-way k-grapheme system (Etruscan used C before E/I, K before A, Q before U). Latin kept all three even though it never needed them; English spelling still lives with the residue.
- **C and G are one letter split in two.** Early Latin C wrote both /k/ and /g/. Around the 3rd century BCE the censor Spurius Carvilius Ruga (or his school) added a stroke to C to make **G**, a new letter for the voiced sound, and slotted it into the alphabet at the old position of Greek zeta (which Latin had dropped).
- **X = /ks/**, the Western-Greek value, not the Eastern Greek Ξ.
- **Y and Z** were re-borrowed late, directly from Greek, by educated Romans transcribing Greek loanwords — which is why they sit at the very end of the alphabet, after the native letters.

Three more letters are medieval-to-early-modern additions: **J** split from I, **U** and **W** split from V. The "complete" 26-letter Latin alphabet is therefore younger than printing.

## How a single branch became the world's default

Two engines carried Latin script far beyond Rome:

1. **Empire.** The administrative and legal reach of Rome planted the alphabet across the western Mediterranean and into northern Europe.
2. **The Church.** For roughly a millennium, Western Christianity wrote scripture, liturgy, law, and learning in Latin script. When the Latin Church evangelized northern and central Europe, it carried the alphabet with it; the Germanic, Celtic, and later Baltic and Finno-Ugric languages of the Latin-Christian world were written down in Latin letters, displacing the native [[alphabet-elder-futhark]] runes and [[alphabet-ogham]] alongside conversion.

Where Eastern (Byzantine) Christianity reached instead, the parallel Greek-derived [[alphabet-glagolitic-cyrillic]] took root — so the religious fault line between Latin and Greek Christianity is still legible today as the Latin-vs-Cyrillic boundary across the Balkans and Eastern Europe.

## Cross-tradition findings

- **The script that conquered the world is a layered fossil of three earlier civilizations.** Every use of the Latin alphabet carries a stacked inheritance: the *shapes* come from Western Greek via the Etruscans (whence the oddities of C, G, and Q); the Greek letters came from [[alphabet-phoenician]]; the Phoenician letters from [[proto-sinaitic]], whose signs were acrophonic pictures adapted from Egyptian [[alphabet-medu-netjer]]. The letter "A" is a Phoenician ox-head (*ʾalp*) turned on its horns; "B" is a Semitic house (*bayt*). A single unbroken line of transmission runs from a Bronze-Age turquoise mine in Sinai to this sentence.
- **The Latin / Cyrillic divide is a religious boundary made visible.** Latin and [[alphabet-glagolitic-cyrillic]] are sibling children of the Greek alphabet; which one a people writes in tracks, with remarkable fidelity, whether they were evangelized from Rome or from Constantinople. The alphabet is a thousand-year-old map of a church schism.
- **The Latin alphabet displaced its northern rivals through conversion, not conquest.** [[alphabet-elder-futhark]] (Germanic runes) and [[alphabet-ogham]] (Irish) were both functioning native scripts; both receded as Latin-Christian literacy spread. The runic þ (thorn) survived inside Latin-script English for centuries before "th" replaced it — a last trace of the older system absorbed into the newer.
