# Sogdian — The Aramaic Line's Silk-Road Carrier

## What It Is

Sogdian is what the Aramaic alphabet became when it went east and took up trade. The Sogdians — an Eastern Iranian people centered on **Samarkand and Bukhara** — were the master merchants of the Silk Road from roughly the 4th to the 9th century CE, with permanent colonies strung from their Zarafshan-valley homeland to Dunhuang and the Chinese capitals (de la Vaissière 2005). Their script, like their Iranian siblings Parthian and Pahlavi ([[language-middle-persian-pahlavi]]), is a cursive descendant of the **Imperial Aramaic** of the Achaemenid chancellery ([[alphabet-aramaic]]) — kept in use for a thousand years after the empire that standardized it had fallen (Skjærvø 1996).

The inheritance was so direct that Sogdian scribes retained **heterograms**: common words *written* in Aramaic but *read* in Sogdian — the scribe wrote the Aramaic MLK' ("king") and said the Sogdian word aloud. Fossilized inside every Sogdian document is a layer of the Iron-Age Levant.

The earliest securely dated corpus is already a merchant archive: the **Ancient Letters** (312–313 CE), found by Aurel Stein in a collapsed watchtower west of Dunhuang — undelivered mail from Sogdian traders in China to partners in Samarkand, datable because Letter II reports the Xiongnu sack of Luoyang as news (Henning 1948).

## One Script, Three World Religions

Sogdian's place in the Atlas is not what was written first in it, but what was carried through it. Sogdiana sat at the funnel-point where every eastbound religion entered Central Asia and China, and the Sogdian language — written in the native script and its sisters — became the relay medium for three of them simultaneously:

- **Buddhism.** Sogdian translations of Buddhist sutras (largely from Chinese) survive in quantity from Dunhuang and Turfan; Sogdian merchants and monks were among the carriers of Buddhism along the road their families traded on.
- **Manichaeism** ([[tradition-manichaeism]]). The Manichaean church's eastern mission ran on the Sogdian network; a substantial Manichaean Sogdian literature survives (in both the native script and Mani's own Estrangelo-derived script). The payoff event is below.
- **Christianity (Church of the East)** ([[tradition-church-of-the-east]]). The "Nestorian" mission east produced a Christian Sogdian literature — psalters, gospel lectionaries, hagiography — written mostly in **Syriac script** ([[alphabet-syriac]]), Sogdian's own sister-script, with some texts in native Sogdian script (Sims-Williams 2014). The famous Xi'an stele (781 CE) memorializing the Church of the East in China stands one relay further down the same road.

No other script in the Atlas carries three living missionary religions at once while belonging to none of them. Sogdian is the limit case of the **carrier script**: a neutral commercial medium whose network effects did the transmitting. The network itself is mapped at [[sogdian-trading-network]].

## The Payoff: How a Merchant Cursive Became the Script of Empires

In 762 CE the **Uyghur Khaganate** — the steppe power of the hour — adopted **Manichaeism as its state religion**, the only state Manichaeism ever captured. The missionaries were Sogdians; the prestige language of the khaganate's chancellery and faith was Sogdian; and the Uyghurs adapted the **Sogdian cursive** to write their own Turkic language as the **Old Uyghur script** (Kara 1996; de la Vaissière 2005).

That adaptation made the script dynastic. In 1204 Genghis Khan took it from a captured Uyghur chancellor for writing Mongolian ([[alphabet-mongolian]]); in 1599 the Manchus adapted the Mongolian form for the script of the Qing empire. A cursive developed by Aramaic-trained clerks for Persian provincial paperwork ended up writing the decrees of the largest land empire in history — having been passed along not by conquest but by **a religion riding a trade network**.

```
Aramaic (Achaemenid chancellery, c. -500)
    └─→ SOGDIAN (by 312 CE, the Ancient Letters)
          — carries Buddhism, Manichaeism, Church-of-the-East Christianity east —
          └─→ Old Uyghur (8th–9th c., via the Manichaean state conversion of 762)
                └─→ Mongolian (1204) → Manchu (1599)
```

## The End of the Line at Home

The script died at home before it died abroad. The Arab conquest of Sogdiana (early 8th c.) began the slow replacement of Sogdian with New Persian written in **Arabic script** — which is itself the Aramaic line by the other branch (Aramaic → Nabataean → Arabic; see [[alphabet-nabataean]]). The last Sogdian documents thin out by the 10th–11th century. In the hills of the upper Zarafshan a descendant language, Yaghnobi, is still spoken — but it is written, when written at all, in scripts of other lineages. Both alphabets in that replacement — the one retired and the one imposed — are daughters of the same Aramaic chancellery hand: the Aramaic line meeting itself coming the other way around Asia.
