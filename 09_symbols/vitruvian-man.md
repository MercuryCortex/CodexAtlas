# Vitruvian Man

## Identity

The **Vitruvian Man** is Leonardo da Vinci's pen-and-ink drawing of c. 1490 (Gallerie dell'Accademia, Venice) showing a nude male figure, arms and legs drawn in two superimposed positions, inscribed simultaneously in a **circle** and a **square**. It is an iconographic image — a measured proportional diagram annotated in Leonardo's mirror-script — not a deity, a cult object, or a worshipped figure. Its title in the Venetian catalogue is *Le proporzioni del corpo umano secondo Vitruvio* ("The proportions of the human body according to Vitruvius"). In the Atlas it sits among the cosmological symbols as the single most famous **visual crystallisation of human-cosmos correspondence**: the body whose harmonic proportions are taken to mirror the harmonic order of the cosmos. It is placed beside [[microcosm-macrocosm]], [[as-above-so-below]], and the geometric emblems [[pentagram]] and the squaring of the circle because it gives pictorial form to the doctrine those nodes carry in words — while being, importantly, a **Renaissance crystallisation of a far older idea** rather than its source.

## In the Vitruvian tradition

The drawing illustrates a specific text: **Vitruvius, *De Architectura* III.1** (Augustan, c. 30–15 BCE). In setting out the proportions of a temple — which, he argues, must follow the proportions of a well-shaped human body — Vitruvius states (III.1.2–3) that if a man lies on his back with hands and feet extended, and a compass is centred on his **navel**, his fingers and toes touch the circumference of a **circle**; and that the span of the outstretched arms equals his height, so that the body also fits a **square** (*homo ad quadratum*). Vitruvius gives the canon of ratios (the face is one-tenth of the height; the hand, foot, cubit each a fixed fraction; and so on).

Leonardo was not the first to draw this. Illustrated Vitruvian figures circulate in the 1480s in the notebooks of **Francesco di Giorgio Martini** and in a drawing by **Giacomo Andrea da Ferrara**, Leonardo's friend, with whom he is thought to have discussed the problem. Leonardo's distinctive contribution is **geometric**: where the bare Vitruvian text suggests a single concentric figure (and earlier illustrators forced the body awkwardly into a coincident circle and square), Leonardo recognised the two cannot share a centre for a single posture. He **offset** them — the circle is centred on the navel (arms raised, legs spread), the square lower, centred at the groin (arms horizontal, legs together) — so the same body generates both figures from two superimposed positions. His mirror-writing transcribes Vitruvius's ratios and silently corrects them against his own measurements. The result is at once an exercise in applied geometry, an anatomical proportion study, and an emblem.

## Cross-tradition significance (the icon of microcosm-macrocosm)

The Vitruvian Man is the **Renaissance visual emblem of the microcosm-macrocosm correspondence** — the doctrine that the human being reflects, in little, the order of the whole cosmos. Its two figures are routinely read symbolically: the **circle** as the celestial, perfect, heavenly order; the **square** as the terrestrial, rational, measurable earth; and **man** as the measure that commensurates the two. This is the visual form of "as above, so below," and the reason the [[microcosm-macrocosm]] hub names it explicitly.

The honest scholarly point — and the firewall this node must respect — is that **Leonardo did not invent the concept he illustrates**. The human-as-microcosm idea is ancient and cross-traditional: Plato's *Timaeus* (30b–d) makes the cosmos an ensouled living being on whose model the human is fashioned; the Hippocratic *De Hebdomadibus* and the Stoics develop the body-cosmos analogy; the proportional canon itself is **Vitruvian** (1st c. BCE), inherited in turn from Greek sculptural theory (the *Canon* of Polykleitos). Conger (1922) and Allers (1944) trace the doctrine across Greek, Hermetic, Kabbalistic, Indian, and Chinese systems. What c. 1490 Italy supplied was not the idea but its **definitive image**: the body literally inscribed in the two perfect figures, drawn with the new Renaissance command of measured geometry and anatomy. The relationship is therefore one of **crystallisation, not origination** — a typological/structural exemplification of a pre-existing theme, secured by direct textual descent from Vitruvius, not a claim that Leonardo originated human-cosmos correspondence.

This distinguishes the wire cleanly: the edge from Vitruvian Man to [[microcosm-macrocosm]] is *exemplifies* (this image instantiates that doctrine), and the lineage to its ancient source (Vitruvius, [[person-vitruvius]]) is a transmission **note**, not a second membership. The figure's origin tradition is singular — **Italian Renaissance** — and its dependence on ancient Vitruvian and Platonic material is expressed as wires and prose, not crammed into the tradition field.

## Disputes

- **Dating and attribution of the centred figure.** The c. 1490 date for Leonardo's sheet is conventional (stylistic and contextual, not documentary). The discovery (published by Claudio Sgarbi, 1993) of **Giacomo Andrea da Ferrara's** Vitruvian drawing has fuelled debate over how much Leonardo's solution was independent versus shared with his circle; Kemp (2006) and others weigh the priority question. There is no dispute that the *underlying* text is Vitruvius.
- **Whether Vitruvius intended concentric figures.** Vitruvius's Latin is terse and does not say the circle and square share a centre; Renaissance illustrators disagreed, and Leonardo's offset solution is one reading among several. The "incompatibility" the drawing resolves is partly an artefact of how the text was construed.
- **Symbolic vs. technical reading.** Modern popular reception treats the image almost entirely as a mystical microcosm emblem. Art historians (Wittkower 1949; Kemp 2006) stress that for Leonardo it was first an **applied-geometry and proportion study** within an architectural-anatomical programme; the cosmic-correspondence reading is genuine to the period's culture but is a frame placed *around* a working diagram, not its sole purpose. Both registers are real; conflating them inflates the drawing's intended esoteric content.
