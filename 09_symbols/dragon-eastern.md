# Eastern Dragon (Lóng / Ryū / Yong)

## Identity

The Chinese **lóng** (龍 / 龙) is a benevolent, serpentine supernatural creature — a composite of nine animals (camel's head, stag's horns, rabbit's eyes, cow's ears, serpent's neck, frog's belly, carp's scales, hawk's claws, tiger's paws) — associated with rain, rivers, the sea, the sky, and imperial power. Unlike the Western dragon, the lóng is fundamentally **auspicious**: it brings rain to crops, governs the seas, embodies cosmic vitality (*qi*, 氣), and personifies imperial legitimacy. It is one of the four divine animals (*sìshéng*, 四聖) alongside the phoenix, qilin, and tortoise. The dragon is the first and foremost of the 12 animals of the Chinese zodiac, and the dragon year is the most sought-after birth year.

## Earliest attestation

- **Iconographic earliest**: Dragon motifs on Shang dynasty bronze ritual vessels (*zūn* and *gǔ* types), c. 1600–1045 BCE — the *tāotiè* mask often incorporates serpentine dragon-body elements. The **Puyang tomb dragon** (c. 4500 BCE, Yangshao culture, Henan province) — a shell mosaic of a dragon-like figure — may push earliest iconographic attestation to the Neolithic.
- **Textual earliest**: *Yìjīng* (易經, Book of Changes), hexagram 1 (*Qián*, 乾 — Heaven): "A dragon flies in the sky; it is profitable to see a great man." This cosmological identification of the dragon with Heaven, the sky, and the ruler is the foundational textual statement.
- **Zhuangzi** (庄子, c. 300 BCE) includes the dragon among the divine creatures.

## Meaning(s) attested

- **Rain and water**: The Dragon Kings (*Lóng Wáng*, 龍王) govern the four seas. Farmers performed rain-prayer rituals (*qíyǔ*) at dragon-king temples. Dragons rise from rivers and seas to generate clouds and rain — control of the water-cycle is their primary function.
- **Imperial embodiment**: The Son of Heaven (*Tiānzǐ*, 天子) was the "True Dragon" (*zhēn lóng*, 真龍). The five-clawed dragon was the emperor's exclusive heraldic symbol; imperial robes, thrones, and palace architecture were saturated with five-clawed dragon imagery. The Qing emperor's throne-name was "Dragon Throne."
- **Cosmic power / yang**: In Daoist and Chinese cosmological thought, the dragon embodies *yang* energy — celestial, solar, active, ascending. It pairs with the phoenix (yin, terrestrial) to form the cosmic complementary pair.
- **Zodiac**: Dragon is the fifth of the twelve zodiac animals, the only mythological creature in the cycle. Dragon-year births (every 12 years) are considered the most auspicious; birth rates in China measurably spike in dragon years.

## Variants

### Lóng Wáng (Dragon Kings)
The four sea-governing Dragon Kings — bureaucratic deities in Chinese folk-religion cosmology who report to the Jade Emperor (*Yù Huáng*). Each maintains an underwater crystal palace staffed by sea-creatures. The Dragon King of the East Sea (Ao Guang) appears in *Journey to the West* (*Xīyóu Jì*, 16th c.) as a significant character. Temple networks dedicated to the Dragon Kings extended across coastal and river-valley China.

### Imperial Five-Clawed Dragon
The heraldic claw-count hierarchy (5 = emperor, 4 = nobility, 3 = commons) was formalized in the Ming dynasty (1368–1644 CE) and strictly enforced in Qing (1644–1912). The iconic yellow five-clawed dragon on the Qing imperial flag (1862–1912) was the first Chinese national flag. This heraldic system encoded political theology: the emperor's legitimacy derived from dragon-embodiment.

### Yamata no Orochi — the Japanese exception
The *Yamata no Orochi* (八俣遠呂智 — eight-forked great serpent) of *Kojiki* and *Nihon Shoki* is a notably monstrous serpentine creature slain by the storm-god Susanoo — showing that even within East Asia, the serpent-creature mythology is not uniformly benevolent. The Orochi is the structural equivalent of the Western dragon-as-monster, appearing in the same tradition (Japan) that also venerates the benevolent *ryū*. This internal variance demonstrates that the East/West dragon polarity is a tendency, not an absolute rule.

## Cross-family appearance

- **Japanese Ryū**: Transmitted from China to Japan via Korean intermediary and direct Tang-period contact (7th–8th c. CE). Japanese temple architecture and garden design (dragon-gate waterfall, *ryūmon-baku*) incorporate the dragon; Zen painting produced major dragon iconographic works (Soga Shōhaku, 18th c.).
- **Korean Yong**: Four-clawed variant; integral to Korean Buddhist temple decoration, shamanic (*musok*) traditions, and royal legitimacy.
- **Vietnamese Rồng**: The Vietnamese dragon (*rồng* or *long*) is the founding symbol of the Vietnamese people — according to the *Lĩnh Nam Chích Quái* (14th c.), the Vietnamese are descendants of the Dragon Lord (*Lạc Long Quân*) and the Fairy (Âu Cơ). The dragon is the primary emblem of Vietnamese national identity.
- **Buddhist assimilation**: When Buddhism spread into East Asia, the Indian *nāga* (serpent water-deity) was assimilated to the local lóng/ryū/yong without friction — both were serpentine water-guardians. Buddhist dragon iconography merged the two traditions; nāga-kings in Chinese Buddhist texts are translated as "dragon kings."

## Contested interpretations

- **Origin**: Whether the Chinese dragon developed indigenously from Neolithic serpent-worship, from crocodile or monitor lizard observation, or via Central Asian contact is debated. The Puyang shell-dragon (c. 4500 BCE) predates Central Asian contact, supporting an indigenous origin.
- **East-West polarity**: Some scholars argue the Western dragon's malevolence is uniquely Semitic/Christian and the "polarity" is overstated — the Norse *Níðhöggr* and Greek Python are not Christian by origin. The counterpoint is that the Chinese dragon's consistent *auspiciousness* is the truly distinctive feature across all attestations.

## Cross-symbol edges (provisional)

- → **dragon-western** (`polemic-inversion`): Same morphology, opposite valence — benevolent vs. adversarial. One of the clearest cross-tradition symbol-polarity cases in comparative religion.
- → **naga-serpent** (`parallel-form`): Serpent-as-cosmic-water-power complex shared by Chinese lóng and Indian nāga; Buddhist transmission fused them iconographically.
- → **feathered-serpent** (`parallel-form`): The feathered/winged dragon (*Yīng Lóng*) = structural parallel to Quetzalcoatl; serpent + avian = celestial-chthonic synthesis. No transmission.
- → **serpent-cosmic-enemy** (`polemic-inversion`): Structural opposite — where the Western serpent-creature = cosmic enemy, the Eastern = cosmic benefactor.
