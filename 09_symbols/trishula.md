# Trishula

## Identity
The **Trishula** (Sanskrit: त्रिशूल, *triśūla* = "three spear" / "three-pointed") is Shiva's trident — his primary divine weapon and the most iconographically versatile of the Hindu divine arms. Its three prongs encode the **Trimurti** (Hindu trinity: Brahma-Vishnu-Shiva = creation-maintenance-destruction), the three divisions of cosmic time (past-present-future), the three realms of the cosmos (heaven-earth-underworld), the three gunas (sattva-rajas-tamas), and the three principal nadis of the subtle body (ida-pingala-sushumna). The Trishula is simultaneously a cosmic weapon, a cosmological diagram, and a subtle-body map — a geometric encoding of Hindu theological structure in three prongs.

It is also the center of the most structurally precise cross-tradition sky-weapon parallel in the Indo-European world: Greek Poseidon's trident and Hindu Shiva's Trishula share not only their three-pronged form but the same Proto-Indo-European root *\*trei-* (three) in their names, making them either shared IE inheritances or extraordinarily close convergent parallels. This question — transmission vs. parallel development — is at the heart of West (2007)'s analysis of the trident tradition.

## Earliest attestation
- **Mahabharata**: The earliest explicit description of Shiva's Trishula is in the [[phase-2-017-mahabharata-ramayana-oral-layers]] (c. -400 to +400 CE), particularly in the *Karna Parva* and the *Drona Parva* sections describing the Tripura Vadha (destruction of the three demon cities). The Mahabharata describes the trishula as a gift from Brahma to Shiva for this specific cosmic task.
- **Shaiva Puranas**: The *Shiva Purana*, *Linga Purana*, and *Matsya Purana* (c. 3rd–8th c. CE) systematize the Trishula's theological significance, encoding the Trimurti meaning, the time/gunas/nadi readings.
- **Greek Poseidon trident**: Attested in Homer (*Iliad*, *Odyssey*, c. -750 to -700 BCE) and Hesiod (*Theogony*) — earlier attestation than the Sanskrit Trishula in textual sources.
- **Iconographic**: Shiva's Trishula iconography is established in **Gupta-period sculpture** (c. 4th–6th c. CE) and appears on **Kushana coins** (c. 1st–3rd c. CE). Pre-Gupta iconographic evidence is more diffuse.

## Meaning(s) attested

### Shiva's weapon in cosmic combat: the Tripura Vadha
The primary mythological context for the Trishula is the **Tripura Vadha** — the destruction of the three demon cities (*Tripura*) built by the Tripurasura, three demon brothers (Maya, Vidyunmalin, and Tarakaksha). The three cities are respectively made of gold (heaven), silver (atmosphere), and iron (earth) — and they orbit the three cosmic levels. The demons use the cities as fortresses, and their cosmic circling makes them invulnerable except at the single moment when all three cities align.

Shiva's response is cosmic engineering:
- He uses **Mount Meru** as his bow
- The **Sun and Moon** as his chariot wheels
- **Brahma** as his charioteer
- **Vishnu** as his arrow
- Fires a **single trishula-shot** at the moment of alignment, destroying all three cities simultaneously

This narrative is the Hindu instance of the IE sky-weapon vs. cosmic-enemy pattern: three prongs for three demonic cities = the Trishula literally designed for this one cosmic act. The weapon's geometry encodes the cosmic task.

### Semantic encoding in three prongs
The Trishula's theological meaning proliferates across multiple interpretive traditions:
- **Trimurti**: Brahma (right) + Vishnu (center) + Shiva (left) = the three cosmic functions encoded in the weapon of the third function
- **Cosmic time**: past + present + future = the Trishula masters all three temporal states
- **Three realms**: heaven + earth + underworld = cosmic sovereignty across all three worlds
- **Three gunas**: sattva (clarity) + rajas (activity) + tamas (inertia) = the Trishula transcends all three modes of material nature
- **Three nadis**: ida (lunar, left) + pingala (solar, right) + sushumna (central) = the subtle body's three principal energy channels, meeting at the *ajna chakra* (third eye) in a trishula configuration

### Modern Hindu devotional use
The Trishula remains one of the most widely used divine emblems in contemporary Hindu devotion. Shiva temples across India feature trishula-topped flagpoles; clay trishulas are offered at festivals; the trishula is tattooed as a Shaivite devotional mark. It is among the most recognizable symbols in living Hindu practice.

## Cross-family appearance
The trident as a divine weapon appears across multiple traditions with varying degrees of IE cognate relationship vs. independent parallel:

| Tradition | Deity | Weapon | Relationship to Trishula |
|-----------|-------|--------|------------------------|
| Hindu Shaivite | [[shiva]] | Trishula | Primary — the origin form |
| Greek | [[poseidon]] | Trident (*tridaina*) | IE cognate or close parallel — same PIE root *\*trei-* |
| Roman | Neptune ([[neptune-roman]]) | Trident | Direct Greek → Roman transmission |
| Etruscan | Nethuns | Trident | Greek → Etruscan reception |
| Buddhist (Tantric) | Multiple deities | Khatvanga (a trident-topped staff) | South Asian transmission from Shaivism into Vajrayana |
| Modern Christian | Devil / Satan | Pitchfork | Polemic inversion of Poseidon/Neptune trident |

The **Poseidon-Shiva trident parallel** is the most debated. West (2007) notes the shared PIE *tri-* root and structural similarities (major deity + trident = elemental cosmic power), but is cautious about positing a specific IE sky-weapon transmission for the trident as opposed to the more clearly attested vajra-Mjolnir-keraunos axe/hammer/thunderbolt cluster.

**The Devil's pitchfork** is among the most documented polemic inversions in Western religious iconography: Neptune's trident → late antique sea-god = ruler of the deep = ruler of hell (in popular imagination) → medieval Christian Devil's pitchfork. The same mechanism operates on the pentagram and the horned god imagery.

## Contested interpretations
- **IE inheritance vs. convergent parallel**: Is the Poseidon trident a shared inheritance from a PIE sky-weapon tradition, or are tridents simply a universally intuitive weapon form (and the three-prong count a universal number symbolism) such that Poseidon and Shiva developed tridents independently? West (2007) is cautious but inclines toward IE cognate relationship.
- **Trishula vs. Khatvanga in Tantric Buddhism**: Vajrayana Buddhism uses the *khatvanga* (a staff topped with three prongs/skulls) which is iconographically similar to the trishula and may represent a Buddhist adaptation of the Shaivite trishula tradition. This would be a South Asian intra-traditional transmission rather than an IE inheritance.
- **The fleur-de-lis as trishula**: The heraldic proposal that the French royal fleur-de-lis encodes a Poseidon/Neptune trident or Hindu trishula is an interpretive hypothesis in heraldic scholarship, not a documented transmission. Most heraldic historians prefer the straightforwardly botanical interpretation (lily = *fleur de lys*).
