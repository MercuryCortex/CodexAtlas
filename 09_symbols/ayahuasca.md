# Ayahuasca

## Identity
**Ayahuasca** (Quechua: *ayahuasca* — "vine of the soul" or "vine of the dead"; the second meaning refers to the drink's access to the ancestor realm) is a psychoactive brew prepared by Amazonian peoples by boiling the **ayahuasca vine** (*Banisteriopsis caapi*) with leaves of the **chacruna plant** (*Psychotria viridis*) or other DMT-containing plants. The vine contains **β-carboline alkaloids** (harmine, harmaline, tetrahydroharmine) that function as **monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs)**; the chacruna contains **DMT** (N,N-Dimethyltryptamine), which is orally inactive without the MAOI. Together, the brew activates DMT orally, producing a 4–8 hour visionary experience.

Ayahuasca is the **Amazonian node** in the cross-continental entheogen category. Its use is attested archaeologically from at least 900 CE (ceramic vessels with residues in Ecuador; Shanon 2002), though Amazonian oral tradition universally claims much greater antiquity. It is used by approximately **70+ distinct indigenous Amazonian traditions** across Peru, Ecuador, Colombia, Brazil, and Bolivia, each with distinct ceremonial forms. In the 20th century it became the sacrament of two major Brazilian syncretic Christian churches (Santo Daime and União do Vegetal), and since c. 2000 has spread globally as an element of neo-shamanic spiritual practice.

## The great botanical mystery
The most significant question about ayahuasca is not spiritual but ethnobotanical: **how did indigenous Amazonian peoples discover this formula?**

The Amazon contains approximately 80,000 plant species. The combination of *Banisteriopsis caapi* and *Psychotria viridis* requires:
1. Knowing that the vine contains MAO-inhibiting alkaloids
2. Knowing that the chacruna (or similar plant) contains DMT
3. Knowing that DMT is orally inactive alone
4. Knowing that combining the MAOI with the DMT-plant makes the DMT orally active

This is a **pharmacologically precise biochemical interaction** that Western science did not fully characterize until the 1960s (McKenna et al.). Amazonian healers explain that **the plants told them** — the spirits of the plants communicated their properties during ceremonial relationships with earlier plant-teachers. Whether this is the literal explanation or a culturally encoded description of a very long empirical-trial process is a question the vault does not resolve; it documents both the scientific puzzle and the indigenous explanation.

Schultes & Hofmann 1979 (Tier 1): "The extraordinary pharmacological discovery of the native peoples of Amazonia... remains perhaps the most remarkable example of indigenous pharmaceutical knowledge anywhere in the world."

## Earliest attestation
- **Archaeological** (c. 900 CE): ceramic vessels from Ecuador containing *Banisteriopsis caapi* alkaloid residues (documented in Shanon 2002); the context suggests ceremonial use.
- **First Western documentation**: Francisco Pizarro's expeditionary chronicles (1533) mention indigenous "sorcerers'" trances; the plant is not identified.
- **First botanical identification**: Richard Spruce (1851) identified *Banisteriopsis caapi* (which he called *Banisteria caapi*) in the Rio Vaupés region and documented its ceremonial use; his notes were published posthumously (1908).
- **First chemical analysis**: The active compounds identified progressively: harmine (1920s, called "telepathine" by some early researchers); DMT identified as active in chacruna component (1950s-60s); full MAOI mechanism characterized by McKenna et al. (1984).

## What happens in ceremony
The traditional ayahuasca ceremony is led by a **curandero** (healer) or **vegetalista** (plant-specialist) — in indigenous contexts, a shaman who has undergone years of apprenticeship to the plant spirits through a strict diet (*dieta*) and isolation. The ceremony occurs at night; the healer sings **icaros** (spirit songs received from the plant teachers) to guide the experience, call healing spirits, and protect participants from malevolent entities.

The phenomenological structure documented across hundreds of accounts (Shanon 2002 — Tier 1, 178 in-depth interviews):
1. **Onset**: nausea, purging (the "purge" is considered a healing — physical and spiritual toxins leaving the body)
2. **Descent**: vision of a world-boundary or threshold; often experienced as death or dying
3. **Encounter**: visual realm of extreme complexity and beauty; encounters with entities (serpents, jaguars, geometric forms, ancestor spirits, plant beings, divine figures)
4. **Teaching**: the entities communicate knowledge — healing information, life guidance, relational truths
5. **Return**: gradual re-entry to ordinary consciousness; often experienced as re-birth

The **death-and-rebirth structure** is the most consistently reported experiential pattern across cultures and individuals. This is the same structure as: Eleusinian initiation (descent into the Telesterion → vision → re-birth as initiate), Christian baptism (death to the old self → new life in Christ), Sufi fanāʾ (annihilation of the nafs / ego → baqa, subsistence in God), Buddhist anattā practice (dissolution of the self-illusion), and shamanic initiatory death (Eliade 1964). The ayahuasca ceremony is **the most pharmacologically explicit** enactment of this cross-tradition initiatory pattern.

## The serpent vision: a neurological universal
Shanon 2002 (Tier 1) documents that **giant serpents** — anacondas, boa constrictors, world-serpents — are the **single most commonly reported visionary entity** in ayahuasca experience, appearing across dozens of indigenous Amazonian traditions and in modern research subjects from Western cultural backgrounds with no prior knowledge of the serpent motif. Shanon proposes this reflects a **neurological universal** — that 5-HT2A receptor agonism consistently activates serpent-imagery in the human visual cortex.

If this is correct, it may provide a neurobiological substrate for **the global prevalence of the divine serpent** as a religious symbol: the Naga (Hindu-Buddhist serpent deity), the Rainbow Serpent (Australian Aboriginal), Quetzalcoatl (Mesoamerican feathered serpent), the Leviathan (Hebrew Bible), the Ophite Gnostic world-serpent, the Agathodaimon (Greek good serpent), Jörmungandr (Norse world-serpent). The vault does not claim that all these traditions derive from ayahuasca use; it documents the structural resonance and the Shanon hypothesis as a modern comparative-religion datum.

## Santo Daime and União do Vegetal: syncretic Christian churches
The 20th-century Brazilian syncretic churches represent the most institutionally successful contemporary integration of a plant sacrament into a religious organization:

**Santo Daime** (founded 1930s, Acre, Brazil, by Raimundo Irineu Serra — "Mestre Irineu"): ayahuasca (*Daime*) is the sacrament of a Christian-spiritist church combining Catholic symbols, Afro-Brazilian elements, and indigenous Amazonian plant-medicine practice. Mestre Irineu claimed a visionary encounter with the Virgin Mary (*Clara da Floresta* — "Clara of the Forest") who commissioned him to create the religion. The Daime is drunk while participants sing Christian-influenced hymns (*hinários*) and perform ritual dances (*bailados*). Legally recognized in Brazil; has spread to 40+ countries.

**União do Vegetal** (UDV, founded 1961, Porto Velho, Brazil, by José Gabriel da Costa — "Mestre Gabriel"): ayahuasca (*Vegetal* — "The Plant") is the sacrament of a more formally structured Christian-Spiritist church. The UDV was legally recognized in Brazil, and in 2006 won **Gonzales v. O Centro Espirita Beneficente União do Vegetal** before the US Supreme Court — the first case in which the Supreme Court ruled that the government must accommodate a religious group's use of an otherwise illegal substance. This is one of the most significant legal precedents for religious freedom in the United States.

Both churches represent the same historical phenomenon as the Native American Church in North America: indigenous plant-sacrament tradition + Christian framework = a new syncretic religion capable of legal survival and institutional persistence.

## Cross-tradition "divine teacher plant" pattern
Ayahuasca is the Amazonian node in the cross-continental convergence:
- Ayahuasca (*B. caapi* + DMT-plants) — Amazonia (→ this node)
- Peyote (*Lophophora williamsii* / mescaline) — North America (→ peyote node)
- [[haoma-zoroastrian]] (Vedic Soma / Avestan Haoma) — Indo-Iranian
- Eleusinian kykeon (barley + ergot alkaloids, hypothetically) — Greek (→ eleusinian-kykeon node)

No common origin is documented. The convergence is independent, continent-by-continent, reflecting what Shanon 2002 calls "the common human response to certain pharmacological invitations."
