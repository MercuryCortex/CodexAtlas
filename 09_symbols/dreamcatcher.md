# Dreamcatcher

## Identity

CRITICAL: The dreamcatcher originates specifically among the **Ojibwe (Anishinaabe) peoples** of the Great Lakes region. It is NOT pan-Indigenous — it was not used by Plains Indians, Southwest peoples, or other groups before the late 20th century. This specificity is not pedantry; it is the scholarly and ethical foundation for understanding the object's meaning, history, and subsequent transformation.

The original Ojibwe object is the **asabikeshiinh** (spider web charm): a willow hoop strung with sinew in a web pattern, threaded with beads, hung with feathers, and suspended above an infant's cradleboard. The web catches harmful dreams in its mesh; good dreams, knowing their way, pass through the central hole and descend gently on the feathers to the sleeping child. Bad dreams are caught in the web and destroyed by morning light.

The object's mythological source is **Asibikaashi (Spider Woman)**, the Ojibwe cosmic protector of children. Asibikaashi cannot be everywhere to protect all children simultaneously; she taught women to replicate her protective web in miniature using the materials of the earth — willow for the frame, sinew for the web, beads and feathers as anchors for the good. The dreamcatcher is Asibikaashi's web in portable, reproductive form: any grandmother can make the spider's protection manifest for any child.

## Origin and earliest attestation

Earliest documentary evidence appears in Jesuit Relations reports (17th century CE) describing Anishinaabe protective charms for infants, though these do not always specify the web form precisely. Frances Densmore's *Chippewa Customs* (1929, Bureau of American Ethnology) provides the first systematic ethnographic description of the asabikeshiinh in its cradleboard context. M. Inez Hilger's fieldwork (1939-1940, published 1951) independently confirms the tradition among multiple Ojibwe communities around the Great Lakes.

The period-earliest of 1600 is conservative — the practice is likely older, but documentary evidence does not extend earlier with specificity.

## Meaning(s) attested

- **Asibikaashi's protective web**: Spider Woman cannot be present everywhere simultaneously, so she teaches women to make her web in portable form. The dreamcatcher is not a charm in the generic sense — it is a specific relational contract between the maker, Spider Woman, and the protected child. The sinew web replicates Asibikaashi's cosmic web; the beads hold the threads; the feathers guide good dreams. This is documented in Johnston (1995) as an insider Ojibwe account.

- **Dream as cosmological access point**: In Anishinaabe cosmology, dreaming is a primary mode of contact with the spirit world — with the *pawaganak* (dream visitors) who are real agents in the relational universe (Hallowell 1992). The dreamcatcher's filtering function presupposes that the spirit world delivers both beneficial and harmful content through the portal of sleep. The web is a cosmic filter at the boundary between ordinary and spirit reality.

- **The circle as wholeness**: The willow hoop is deliberately bent into a circle — the circle of life, the form of completeness that has no beginning and no end. The web fills the circle. The circle-plus-web form encodes both the shape of the cosmos (circular) and the relational structure of existence (web/network).

- **Grandmother as weaver of protection**: The dreamcatcher is made by women, specifically grandmothers and female relatives. The act of making is itself a ritual act — calling on Asibikaashi, weaving protection into physical form. This positions women's craft work as cosmological maintenance, not domestic labor.

## The transformation: sacred object → pan-Indian symbol → commercial product

The dreamcatcher's 20th-century history is a case study in the transformation of sacred objects through cultural appropriation:

1. **1960s-1970s**: The American Indian Movement (AIM) deliberately created pan-Indian unity symbols to build solidarity across tribal nations under shared political pressure. The dreamcatcher — visually striking, easily reproducible, with a positive protective meaning — became one such symbol, adopted far beyond Ojibwe communities.

2. **1970s-1980s**: The New Age movement adopted the dreamcatcher as a generic "Native American spiritual object," further divorcing it from Ojibwe specificity.

3. **1980s-present**: Industrial mass production (often in Asia) flooded tourist markets with cheap synthetic dreamcatchers — plastic beads, dyed feathers, synthetic cord. This produced the object most non-Indigenous people encounter as "a dreamcatcher."

4. **Ojibwe scholarly response**: Most Ojibwe scholars and community members view the mass commercial dreamcatcher as a form of cultural appropriation that erases specific Ojibwe origin. Adrienne Keene (Cherokee Nation), in her ongoing *Native Appropriations* scholarly blog, documents the harms: erasure of tribal specificity, commodification of sacred objects, economic extraction from Indigenous communities, and the displacement of Ojibwe people's ability to control their own cultural heritage.

The dreamcatcher is thus simultaneously (1) a sacred Ojibwe protective object with specific cosmological meaning, (2) a pan-Indian political solidarity symbol, and (3) a mass commercial object stripped of meaning. These three objects share a form but are not the same thing.

## Cross-tradition analysis

**Spider Woman as cosmic weaver** — Ojibwe Asibikaashi and Hopi Kokyangwuti (Spider Grandmother) are neighboring but **distinct** traditions with **independent** spider-woman figures. Both are:
- Female spider beings who create or maintain the cosmic web
- Mediators between spirit world and human world
- Associated with protective/generative weaving

These are **structural convergences**, not the same figure. The Ojibwe and Hopi are linguistically and culturally distinct peoples separated by over 2,000 km. The spider-woman protective figure is a genuinely independent convergence within North American indigenous cosmology.

**Cosmic web as universal metaphor** — The dreamcatcher web participates in a global cluster of cosmic-web imagery:
- **Indra's Net** (Buddhist: the cosmic jewel-net in which each node reflects all others — the universe as interdependent whole)
- **Moirai/Norns** (Greek/Norse: fate as spinning and weaving thread — life = thread in the cosmic fabric)
- **Tibetan thread-cross** (*nam-mkha'*): ritual fiber object trapping harmful spirits in its geometric threads
- **Spider's web in Sufi poetry**: the universe as divine web (al-Ghazali)

All encode the universe as woven structure rather than heap of objects. The dreamcatcher adds the specifically Ojibwe contribution: the **web as filter** — not all that passes through the spirit world is beneficial; a cosmic sorting mechanism exists.
