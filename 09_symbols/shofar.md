# Shofar

## Identity

The **shofar** (Hebrew: שׁוֹפָר — *shofar*; plural *shofarot*) is a **ram's horn**, specifically the curved horn of a male domestic sheep (*Ovis aries*), used as a sacred acoustic instrument in Jewish religious practice. It is not manufactured from cow horn (disqualified due to association with the Golden Calf; Talmud *Rosh Hashanah* 26a). The shofar is blown at the New Year (*Rosh Hashanah*), the Day of Atonement (*Yom Kippur*), the proclamation of the Jubilee year (*yovel*), and — in Temple times — at the inauguration of the Sabbath, new moons, and public fasts.

The shofar is not merely a musical instrument but a **theological object**: the instrument that announces divine presence (Mount Sinai), proclaims liberation (the Jubilee), sounds the alarm at the walls of Jericho, and calls the nation to repentance. Its sound (*tekiah, shevarim, teruah*) is one of the oldest sounds in continuous human liturgical use. The shofar completes a cross-traditional pattern that the vault has documented: the **natural horn instrument at the cosmic threshold** — the boundary between ordinary and sacred time, between the human and the divine.

## Earliest attestation

- **Torah (written strata c. -800 to -400 BCE)**: 
  - **Exodus 19:16–19** (P source): Mount Sinai theophany — God descends onto Sinai amid thunder, lightning, thick cloud, and *the sound of the shofar growing exceedingly loud*. The shofar is the *sound of divine presence*.
  - **Leviticus 25:9** (P source): "You shall sound the shofar blast (*teruah*) on the tenth day of the seventh month — on Yom Kippur you shall have the shofar sounded throughout your land" — the Jubilee year proclamation.
  - **Joshua 6:4–20** (D source): The walls of Jericho — seven priests carrying seven shofars march around the city for seven days, on the seventh day marching seven times, and at the great shofar blast the walls collapse.
- **Prophetic literature (c. -800 BCE)**: The shofar appears throughout the Hebrew prophets as the instrument of divine warning and divine assembly — Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, Joel, Amos, Zephaniah all use the shofar blast as a prophetic image.
- **Archaeological**: Horn instruments are attested across the ancient Near East from the 3rd millennium BCE. The specific Hebrew shofar tradition is difficult to date independently of the written sources.

## Meaning(s) attested

### The sound of divine presence — Mount Sinai

The defining theophany of the Hebrew Bible (Exodus 19:16–19) includes the shofar as the auditory dimension of divine arrival: *"And it came to pass on the third day, when it was morning, that there were thunders and lightnings, and a thick cloud upon the mount, and the voice of a shofar exceeding loud; and all the people who were in the camp trembled."* As God's presence intensifies, the shofar sound grows: *"And the sound of the shofar waxed louder and louder."*

The shofar is thus the **acoustic signature of divine presence descending into the world** — it is not played by human hands at this moment; the shofar-blast accompanies the divine arrival as thunder accompanies lightning. The theological claim is radical: God's arrival has a sound, and it is the shofar.

### The ram of the Akedah — mercy over judgment

The shofar is specifically a RAM's horn — and the theological meaning of this specificity is the *Akedah* (the Binding of Isaac, Genesis 22). Abraham, commanded to sacrifice his son Isaac, binds him on the altar; at the last moment God stays Abraham's hand and provides a *ram caught in a thicket by its horns* as the substitute sacrifice. The ram's horn = the horn of the substitute sacrifice = the sound of divine mercy at the moment of potential destruction.

Rabbinic tradition (Talmud *Rosh Hashanah* 16a; Midrash Tanchuma) draws this connection explicitly: the shofar is blown on Rosh Hashanah to invoke the Akedah and to ask that God's attribute of mercy (*rachamim*) override the attribute of strict judgment (*din*). **Every Rosh Hashanah shofar blast re-enacts the Akedah** — the ram is slaughtered again; its horn is blown again; God's mercy is invoked again.

### The Jubilee — liberation and restoration

Leviticus 25 establishes the **Jubilee year** (*yovel*) — the 50th year (after seven cycles of seven sabbatical years), on Yom Kippur of which the shofar is blown throughout the land to announce: all Hebrew slaves are freed; all ancestral land sold under financial duress reverts to its original family; all debts are cancelled. The shofar IS the sound of liberation.

The etymology: *yovel* (Jubilee) = "ram's horn" in Hebrew → Latinized to *iubilaeum* → Medieval Latin *jubilaeus* → English *Jubilee*. The English word "jubilee" — meaning a celebration of liberation and return — is derived directly from the Hebrew word for the shofar blast. This etymology makes the shofar's Jubilee function the linguistic origin of one of English's most common words for communal celebration.

**American connection**: The Liberty Bell in Philadelphia bears the inscription "Proclaim liberty throughout all the land unto all the inhabitants thereof" (Leviticus 25:10 — the Jubilee verse) — a direct connection between the shofar's Jubilee proclamation of freedom and the founding iconography of the United States.

### The walls of Jericho — sound as divine weapon

Joshua 6: the Israelites march around the walls of Jericho for seven days; seven priests carry seven shofars before the Ark of the Covenant; on the seventh day they circuit the city seven times; at the great *teruah* blast of all seven shofars simultaneously, the people shout, and *the wall fell down flat*. **Sound — specifically the shofar blast — is the weapon of divine power against the walls of the world.**

This tradition is theologically consistent with the Mount Sinai theophany: at Sinai, the shofar accompanies divine presence descending onto the mountain; at Jericho, the shofar blast channels divine power against the enemy. Sound is the medium through which divine power moves in the world.

### Rosh Hashanah — the awakening call

Maimonides (*Mishneh Torah*, Laws of Repentance 3:4) gives the rationalized meaning of the Rosh Hashanah shofar: *"Awake, awake, O sleepers from your sleep! O slumberers, arouse yourselves from your slumbers! Examine your deeds, return in repentance, and remember your Creator."* The shofar is the alarm that wakes the human soul from the sleep of habit, complacency, and moral unconsciousness. It calls the nation back to its covenant obligations.

## Cross-family appearance

### The divine horn at the cosmic threshold — the complete pattern

The shofar completes a cross-traditional pattern documented across the vault:

| Tradition | Horn instrument | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Jewish | Shofar (ram's horn) | Divine presence (Sinai); liberation (Jubilee); battle (Jericho); repentance (Rosh Hashanah) |
| Hindu | [[shankha-conch]] (sacred conch) | Vishnu's divine call; battle-signal; sacred invocation |
| Greek | Triton's conch | Calming or raising the seas; the god's authority over water |
| Aztec | Ehecatl's wind-jewel conch | The wind-god's divine breath; Quetzalcoatl's attribute |
| Norse | Gjallarhorn (Heimdall's horn) | Blown at Ragnarök — the end of the world |
| Greek/Roman | *Lituus*, *buccina*, *cornu* | Military signals and sacred boundary-marking |

**Five independent traditions using a natural horn instrument to mark the boundary between ordinary and sacred time, between human and divine.** This is the vault's most clearly documented cross-traditional convergence in the instrument category. The natural horn at the cosmic threshold is a **pan-cultural pattern** — not because of transmission but because the sound of a horn is one of the oldest human technologies for marking distance, alerting community, and invoking shared attention.

### The shofar in early Christianity

Eric Werner (1959) documents the transmission of Temple Jewish liturgical patterns — including shofar-like horn instruments — into early Christian liturgical practice. The Christian liturgical tradition of trumpet-calls (the "last trumpet" of 1 Corinthians 15:52, the trumpets of Revelation 8–11) is theologically continuous with the shofar tradition: the trumpet/horn that sounds at the cosmic threshold, at resurrection, at the end of the age.

### The shofar and Islamic practice

The *sūr* (Arabic: صُور — the cosmic trumpet) blown by the angel Israfil to announce the Day of Resurrection in Islamic eschatology is conceptually parallel to the shofar blown at the cosmic threshold. The *sūr* is not derived from the shofar but shares the same theological category: the horn/trumpet as the instrument of divine announcement at the eschatological boundary.

## Contested interpretations

- **The exact shape requirement (Talmudic debate)**: The Mishnah and Talmud contain extensive debate about which horn-types qualify as shofar, how it must be held (curve facing up or down), whether the inner lining may be removed, and what constitutes a valid blast. This is not scholarly but legal-religious contested interpretation.
- **Yovel vs. shofar terminology**: Some scholars distinguish *yovel* (the ram's horn of the Jubilee) from *shofar* (the more general term). Milgrom (1990) and Levine (1993) treat these as overlapping categories rather than distinct instruments.
- **The Jericho account as historical**: The archaeological evidence for the destruction of Jericho is contested. Kathleen Kenyon's excavations showed that Jericho was not inhabited during the period traditionally assigned to the Conquest (c. -1200 BCE). The Jericho shofar narrative is theologically significant regardless of its historical status.
