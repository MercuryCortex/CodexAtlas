# Pontos Riscados

## Identity
**Pontos riscados** (Portuguese: "scratched/drawn points" — also called *pontos cantados* ["sung points"] when the spirit-signature is expressed as a chant, and *pontos de força* ["points of power"]) are the ritual graphic diagrams of the **Afro-Brazilian religions of Candomblé and Umbanda** — geometric signatures of the Orixas (Candomblé) and Exus/Pomba Giras (Umbanda) that are drawn on the ground, on objects, on paper, or traced in the air to invoke divine presence and communicate with the spirits. Each Orixa and each spirit-category has one or more distinctive *ponto riscado* that functions as their visual signature and calling-card.

The *ponto riscado* (the **drawn** point, marked in chalk or other media on the ground or ritual surfaces) and the *ponto cantado* (the **sung** point — a chant-formula specific to each spirit) are understood as two modalities of the same thing: the spirit's signature expressed in two channels simultaneously, graphic and sonic. Together they constitute the complete invocation.

The structural function — a **geometric diagram that IS the spirit's identity, drawn to invoke their presence** — is identical to the Haitian Vodou [[veve]], making these the two principal African diaspora ritual diagram systems in the Americas. Both descend from West African symbolic traditions, but through different colonial pathways and from partially different African source traditions (Candomblé's Ketu nation derives primarily from Yoruba and Fon sources transported to Bahia; Umbanda is a 20th-c. syncretic synthesis integrating Yoruba-Candomblé, Spiritism, Indigenous Brazilian, and Catholic elements).

## Earliest attestation
- The **Candomblé** tradition crystallized in 19th-century Bahia, Brazil, brought by enslaved Yoruba, Fon, and Bantu Africans. Written documentation of the *ponto riscado* system begins in the late 19th century, with ethnographic documentation intensifying from the 1930s onward.
- **Umbanda** emerged as a distinct syncretic religion in the 1920s–1930s in Rio de Janeiro, formalizing the *ponto riscado* system as an integrated part of its ritual structure.
- Pierre Verger's photographic documentation (1940s–1990s) and Roger Bastide's sociological studies (1950s–1960s) are the foundational scholarly records.

## Meaning(s) attested
- **Invocation and identification**: The *ponto riscado* is drawn before invoking a specific spirit; the act of drawing it opens the channel for that spirit's presence. It is the spirit's graphic *senha* (password/signature).
- **Geometric embodiment of divine nature**: Each spirit's *ponto* encodes their domain — Ogum's iron-and-sword geometry, Iemanjá's wave-and-fish forms, Exu's crossroads-and-pitchfork structure, Oxalá's white-cross purity. The geometric form is not arbitrary; it is held to embody the spirit's essential nature.
- **Protection and binding**: *Pontos* drawn on objects or spaces (thresholds, altars, ritual tools) invoke protection or bind the spirit's energy to that location.
- **Catholic syncretism**: Candomblé developed under Catholic colonial surveillance, and the Orixas were mapped onto Catholic saints — Oxalá/Jesus, Ogum/Saint George, Iemanjá/Our Lady. The pontos often encode iconographic elements from both the Yoruba and Catholic traditions simultaneously.

## Cross-family appearance
The *ponto riscado* system participates in a global family of **ritual diagram systems for spirit invocation**:

- **Haitian Vodou veve** (see [[veve]]) — the most direct parallel: both are African diaspora geometric deity-signatures drawn in ritual space to invoke specific spirits. Different West African source streams, different colonial pathways, structurally convergent.
- **Kongo *firmas*** (Cuban Palo Monte, Haitian Petwo nation) — ritual graphic signs from the **Bantu Kongo** tradition, distinct from the Yoruba-derived systems; drawn in chalk or charcoal to invoke the *mpungos* (Palo spirits) and the *nkisi* (power objects). The Kongo *firma* (signature) tradition is a third major African diaspora diagram system.
- **European grimoire sigils** — the Lesser Key of Solomon (*Goetia*, 17th c.) specifies individual geometric sigils for each of the 72 demons of the hierarchy; these function as calling-cards and binding instruments identical in structural purpose to the *ponto riscado*. No direct contact between these traditions is documented; the convergence is **parallel-form**: the problem "how do you visually encode a spirit's specific identity to invoke and bind them?" generates similar solutions across independent traditions.

## Contested interpretations
- **Kongo vs. Yoruba sources for Exu's ponto**: In Umbanda, Exu figures are understood to derive from both the Yoruba Eshu tradition and the Kongo Nkisi-Exu tradition. The *ponto* iconography (crossroads + trident) reflects this ambiguous ancestry.
- **The Exu-devil equation**: Brazilian Pentecostalism and conservative Catholic tradition have long equated Exu with the Christian devil (reinforced by the trident/pitchfork iconographic convergence). Afro-Brazilian religious scholars and practitioners firmly reject this equation — Exu is a complex trickster-messenger-threshold figure, morally ambiguous but not evil in the Christian sense. The devil-equation is documented as a **colonial theological polemic**, not a genuine identification.
- **Umbanda's "whitening" of African tradition**: The emergence of Umbanda in the 1920s–30s involved significant reconfiguration of Candomblé elements through Spiritist (Kardecist) and European esoteric frameworks — some scholars argue this represents a "whitening" or domestication of African religious forms; others see it as a genuinely syncretic Brazilian religious synthesis.
