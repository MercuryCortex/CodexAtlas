# Double-Headed Eagle

## Identity

The double-headed eagle is a theriomorphic symbol depicting a single eagle body with two heads facing outward in opposite directions, typically with wings spread. It is one of the most pan-civilizational heraldic-religious symbols in world history, attested continuously from Hittite Anatolia (c. 1400 BCE) through Byzantine Constantinople, the Holy Roman Empire, the Russian Romanov dynasty, the Balkans (Serbia, Montenegro, Albania, Georgia, Armenia), and — in its most recent major institutional role — as the supreme emblem of the 33rd degree of the Scottish Rite of Freemasonry. The two heads conventionally signify: dual authority (East and West; spiritual and temporal), dual vision (past and future; beginning and end), or the union of opposites in a single royal-divine power.

## Earliest attestation

- **c. 1400–1200 BCE** — Hittite relief at Alacahöyük, central Anatolia (modern Turkey); the earliest documented double-headed eagle in the Old World. The Hittite figure is flanked by ceremonial attendants, indicating a sacred or royal-ceremonial context.
- **c. 2500–2000 BCE context** — The Sumerian *Anzû* (or Imdugud) bird — a lion-headed eagle attested at Lagash (Girsu Stele, *British Museum BM 114207*, c. 2500 BCE) — is a related but distinct eagle-power motif; it is NOT double-headed. Albert Pike's Masonic identification of the double-headed eagle with the "Eagle of Lagash" conflates the Anzû/Imdugud motif with the double-headed form. The conflation is a Masonic mytho-history construct (see Contested Interpretations).
- **12th–13th c. CE** — Seljuk Anatolian architectural ornament, independent of Byzantine use.
- **1259–1453 CE** — Byzantine Palaiologos dynasty, Constantinople.

## Meaning(s) attested

- **Hittite:** The two-headed eagle in Hittite reliefs appears in a ceremonial-royal context, possibly indicating divine protection of the throne from multiple directions or the dual nature of royal power.
- **Seljuk Muslim:** The double-headed eagle as architectural ornament in a Muslim-ruled Anatolia; the meaning is debated — decorative, protective, imperial-prestige, or residual Anatolian substrate iconography.
- **Byzantine:** Dual authority over East and West; the Emperor as the unique bridge between the two halves of the Roman world; later, dual authority over the spiritual (Church) and temporal (Empire).
- **Russian Third Rome:** After Constantinople's fall (1453), the double-headed eagle embeds a theological claim: Moscow is the *Third Rome*, the legitimate successor to both Rome and Byzantium. The Romanov double-headed eagle wears a crown and holds an orb and scepter; the breast-shield bears St. George and the Dragon.
- **Scottish Rite 33°:** Albert Pike (*Morals and Dogma*, 1871) interpreted the Eagle of Lagash as the symbol of the supreme degree because it "looks to the East and to the West simultaneously, to the beginning and to the end" — the 33rd degree adept has achieved the synthesis of all knowledge. Pike's interpretation frames Freemasonry as the guardian of primordial sacred wisdom reaching back to ancient Sumer. This is Masonic mytho-history, not documented historical transmission (see Contested Interpretations).

## Contested interpretations

1. **The "Eagle of Lagash" equation:** Pike's identification of the 33rd-degree double-headed eagle with the Lagash Anzû/Imdugud is anachronistic. The Lagash stele (c. 2500 BCE) shows a lion-headed eagle — *not* a double-headed eagle. The earliest double-headed eagle is Hittite (c. 1400 BCE). Pike's reading is a 19th-c. Masonic mytho-history construct, not a documented genealogy. The claim has NO Tier-1 support.
2. **Hittite → Seljuk transmission:** There is no documented transmission from the Hittite double-headed eagle to the Seljuk form (c. 2,500 years later). Either a continuous Anatolian substrate tradition was preserved in local art and then reactivated under the Seljuks, or the Seljuks arrived at the form independently (parallel invention of a natural heraldic idea). The honest answer is: no documented transmission.
3. **Byzantine imperial origin of the symbol:** Some scholars argue the Palaiologos adoption of the double-headed eagle drew on earlier Roman or Sasanian precedents rather than directly from Hittite sources. The Byzantine form is the documented origination point for all *subsequent* Western and Russian uses.
4. **Albanian national continuity:** Whether the Albanian *shqiponja* derives from the Kastrioti clan's Byzantine-influenced heraldry, from a pre-Ottoman Illyrian tradition, or from a combination, remains debated in Albanian historiography.
