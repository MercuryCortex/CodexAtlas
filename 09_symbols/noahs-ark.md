# Noah's Ark

## Identity
**Noah's Ark** is the rectangular three-decked wooden vessel that Noah builds, on Yahweh's instruction, in Genesis 6:14–16 to preserve himself, his family, and animal-pairs through the divine-judgment Flood. The Hebrew word is *tēvāh* — used in the Hebrew Bible only here and for the basket in which the infant Moses is set afloat on the Nile (Exodus 2:3, 5), creating a deliberate intratextual *vessel-of-salvation-through-water* linkage. The LXX renders *kibōtos*, the Vulgate *arca*, the Quran *safīnat Nūḥ*.

The Ark is the **single most iconic symbol of the Mediterranean / Near-Eastern flood-tradition**: it appears in cuneiform Mesopotamian flood-narratives (Atrahasis Epic Tablet III; Gilgamesh XI — there as a *cube* with seven decks rather than the Hebrew *rectangle* with three), in the Hebrew Genesis 6–9, in the Quran 11:36–48, and in continuous Christian-Islamic-Jewish iconographic-and-theological reception for 4000+ years.

## Earliest attestation
- **Mesopotamian:** [[phase-1-006-atrahasis]] Tablet III + Gilgamesh XI describe the antediluvian boat-building. The Mesopotamian vessel is *not* rectangular like the Hebrew Ark — Gilgamesh XI describes a *cube* or *coracle-round* vessel with 7 decks; the Atrahasis Epic is fragmentary on this point. The Hebrew rectangular three-deck pattern of Genesis 6:14–16 is a Hebrew-redactional specification.
- **Hebrew:** Genesis 6:14–16 (P-source ~-550 BCE) — the *tēvāh* specification (300 × 50 × 30 cubits; cypress wood with pitch coating; three decks; single window; single door on the side). Genesis 7–8 (J + P sources) narrate the loading, the flood, and the grounding on the mountains of Ararat (Gen 8:4).
- **Greek (LXX):** *kibōtos* — the same word LXX uses for the Ark of the Covenant (the *aron* / *kibōtos diathēkēs*), creating a Hebrew-Greek typological link between the *salvation-vessel* of Noah and the *covenant-vessel* of Sinai.
- **Quran 11:36–48; 23:23–30; 7:64; 26:118–122; 54:13–15** — the *safīnat* (boat) of Nūḥ.

## Meaning(s) attested

- **Mesopotamian (Atrahasis/Gilgamesh XI):** the vessel of survival through the divine-decreed flood. No theological-typological meaning beyond the immediate narrative.
- **Hebrew (Genesis):** vessel of YHWH's preservation of righteous Noah and the chosen species through the Flood-judgment.
- **Christian (patristic):** *typological reading* — the Ark = the Church; the Flood = baptism; the eight souls saved in the Ark = the soteriological community in Christ (1 Peter 3:20–21 makes the typology explicit; subsequent patristic elaboration: Justin Martyr *Dialogue* 138, Tertullian *De Baptismo* 8, Cyprian *De Unitate* 6, Augustine *City of God* 15.26).
- **Islamic:** the obedient-believers separated from the disobedient — Noah's son Kan'an refuses the Ark in the Quran 11:42–43 and drowns, becoming the canonical Islamic example of the *unbelieving relative*.

## Cross-family appearance

- **Mesopotamian → Hebrew transmission:** documented via the broader Mesopotamian → Hebrew flood-tradition (West 1997; Lambert + Millard 1969). The *vessel-of-salvation* motif is structurally cognate; the *specific dimensions and three-decked rectangular form* of the Hebrew Ark are Hebrew-redactional.
- **Greek-Roman parallel:** Deucalion's vessel ([[deucalion]]) is variously called a "chest" (*larnax*) or "boat"; less elaborated iconographically than the Hebrew Ark.
- **Vedic-Hindu parallel:** Manu's ship ([[manu]]) is a *nau* or *navam*, tethered to [[matsya-avatar]]'s horn; less iconographically elaborated.
- **Norse parallel:** Bergelmir's hollowed tree-trunk or *lúðr* ([[bergelmir]]) — distinctively *not* a built ark, reflecting the Norse cosmogonic-violence frame.

## Contested interpretations

- **Historicity of Noah's Ark.** No archaeological evidence has been found for a literal historical Ark; periodic claims of Ark-discoveries on Mount Ararat (Hagopian 1970s; Ron Wyatt 1980s) are not accepted by mainstream archaeology. The Ark is a theological-canonical narrative-object, not a historically-attested artifact.
- **The cube vs. rectangle question.** The Mesopotamian cube-vessel and Hebrew rectangle are theologically-redactionally distinct; the change of shape reflects Hebrew-redactional specification.
