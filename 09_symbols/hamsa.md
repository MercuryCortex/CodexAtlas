# Hamsa

## Identity

The **hamsa** (Arabic خَمْسَة *khamsa* = five; Hebrew חַמְסָה *ḥamsāh*) is an open-palm amulet used for protection against the **evil eye** across Jewish, Muslim, and (in some regions) Christian communities. The name refers to the five fingers of the protective hand. Its most characteristic modern form shows a non-anatomical bilateral palm — thumb and little finger equal — with an eye at the center, though the earliest examples lack the eye. The hamsa is simultaneously the **Hand of Fatima** in Islamic tradition, the **Hand of Miriam** in Jewish tradition, and in some Moroccan Christian communities, the **Hand of Mary**. The same physical object carries three different proper names across the three Abrahamic traditions — a cross-religious sharing that has no equivalent in world religion.

Note: `09_symbols/hamsa-khamsa.md` is the more fully developed companion node covering the Mesopotamian antecedents, the detailed Jewish (Yad Miriam) and Islamic (Khamsa Fatima / Ahl al-Kisāʾ) layers, and the full iconographic analysis. This node focuses on the cross-tradition overview and the Phoenician/Punic material record.

## Earliest Attestation

- **Phoenician/Punic material culture (-1500 to -146 BCE)**: Hand-shaped stelae and protective hand-plaques at Tophet sites (Carthage, Tharros, Motya) — the open hand as divine blessing and protection is documented in the Phoenician-Punic sacred material record before either Jewish or Islamic formal religion crystallizes. The raised hand of the goddess Tanit appears on Tophet stelae in a form functionally equivalent to the later hamsa.
- **Mesopotamian parallels** (from -2000): terra cotta hand-shaped protective plaques in household cult contexts; "hand of Ishtar" protecting the Assyrian king in Neo-Assyrian palace iconography.
- **Medieval North Africa and Iberia** (post-700 CE): the hamsa as a distinctly named object with Islamic and Jewish community associations becomes documentable in Arabic and Hebrew sources.

## Meaning(s) Attested

- **Protection against the evil eye** (*nazar*): the fundamental and universal function. The evil-eye belief — that malevolent envious gaze can cause harm — is pan-Mediterranean and pan-Semitic. The hamsa is the physical counter-agent.
- **Blessing and good luck**: alongside the apotropaic function, the hamsa is a positive talisman for prosperity and health.
- **Five as sacred number**: the khamsah root (five) connects to the Five Books of Moses (Jewish), the Five Pillars of Islam, and the Five People of the Cloak (Shia — linking it directly to Fatima, Ali, Hasan, Husayn, and the Prophet).
- **The divine hand**: "hand of God" (*yad YHWH*) is a standard biblical metaphor for divine agency; the hamsa participates in this register even when its origin is folk-magical rather than theological.

## MASSIVE WIN: The Shared Sacred Object

**The hamsa is the only sacred object in world religion simultaneously used, under different names, by Jewish, Muslim, and Christian communities without ideological conflict.** In Morocco, Tunisia, and across the Maghreb, Jewish and Muslim households hang visually identical objects above their doors. The same craftsperson in the Fez medina makes them for customers of both faiths.

This cross-monotheistic sharing is possible because:
1. The symbol predates both Judaism and Islam — it belongs to the Phoenician-Punic cultural substrate both communities inherited in North Africa.
2. It functions at the level of popular folk religion rather than official theology.
3. Both traditions have reinterpreted it through their own proper names (Fatima / Miriam / Mary) without erasing the shared form.
4. The practical need for protection against envy is more durable than any theology.

**Theological irony**: strict monotheism in both Judaism (*darkhei ha-Emori* halakhic restrictions on magic) and Islam (bid'a innovations, shirk concerns around amulets) officially disapproves of intermediary magical objects. The hamsa persists in both traditions because the popular need outweighs the theological objection.

## Cross-Family Appearance

### Phoenician-Punic (pre-Abrahamic)
The Tophet inscriptions and hand-shaped votive objects from Carthage (c. -800 to -146) document the protective open hand in the Phoenician-Punic cult vocabulary. This is the most likely material ancestor of the medieval hamsa, transmitted through North African cultural continuity rather than deliberate theological inheritance.

### Jewish
The **Hand of Miriam** (*Yad Miriam*) reinterprets the pre-Jewish apotropaic symbol by attaching it to Miriam the Prophetess (Exodus 15:20), who led the women of Israel in song at the Red Sea and was associated with deliverance. The Judaization of a shared symbol is medieval in origin; the biblical Miriam has no hand-amulet association in the Hebrew Bible. See [[miriam-prophetess]].

### Islamic
The **Hand of Fatima** (*Khamsa Fatima*, or *Yad Fatima*) names the amulet for Fatima al-Zahra, daughter of the Prophet and wife of Ali. In Shia Islam, the five fingers specifically encode the **Five People of the Cloak** (Ahl al-Kisāʾ). See [[fatima-bint-muhammad|Fatima al-Zahra]].

### The Central Eye
The integration of the eye motif into the palm produces a second-order symbol: the palm-hand catches the evil gaze while the central eye deflects and returns it. The blue glass nazar bead (the classic anti-evil-eye amulet of the Levant and Anatolia) is frequently embedded in the palm — making the hamsa a fusion of the Semitic hand-power tradition and the Mediterranean evil-eye-bead tradition. See [[eye-of-horus-wedjat]] for the Egyptian layer.

## Contested Interpretations

- **Phoenician transmission vs. independent origin**: scholars debate whether the medieval North African hamsa is a direct descendant of Phoenician hand-plaques or a convergent re-emergence. The spatial and temporal continuity in North Africa favors transmission; the absence of explicit textual linkage means this remains inference rather than documented fact.
- **Which tradition "owns" it**: Israeli and Arab communities have periodically contested cultural ownership of the hamsa; the scholarly consensus is that the symbol predates both and belongs to neither exclusively.
