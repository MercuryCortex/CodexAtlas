# Sankofa

## Overview

Sankofa (pronounced *san-KO-fah*) is an Akan concept and visual symbol originating from the Twi-speaking peoples of Ghana and surrounding regions of West Africa. It is represented in two canonical forms: (1) a stylized bird with feet firmly planted forward while its head is turned backward — often with an egg in its beak — and (2) a heart-shaped spiral glyph. The word *Sankofa* in the Twi language translates as **"Go back and get it"** — derived from:

- *se* — "it is not wrong"
- *wo* — "you"
- *san* — "go back"
- *kofa* — "take/fetch"

The full proverb is: *"Se wo were fi na wosankofa a yenkyi"* — "It is not taboo to go back and retrieve what you forgot." The bird's forward-pointing feet paired with its backward-looking head encodes a teaching of precise elegance: one must understand the past to move forward successfully. The past is not an obstacle to the future — it is the precondition of the future.

Sankofa is one of the **[[adinkra]]** symbols, a system of visual ideograms developed by the Akan peoples of Ghana and Côte d'Ivoire used in textile design, pottery, metalwork, and funeral practices.

## Visual Forms

### The Sankofa Bird

The primary and most recognizable form: a stylized bird, often shown in profile, with its body facing forward (feet pointing forward, indicating the direction of movement) while its head is turned back over its own body. The bird frequently holds an egg in its beak or balances one on its back. The egg represents the future — the next generation, potential, what is yet to come — which the bird carries with it while retrieving wisdom from the past. The bird's posture is thus a visual argument: forward motion does not preclude backward knowing; the egg (future) is carried by the same bird whose head looks back (past).

### The Heart-Spiral Form (Se Wo Were Fi Na Wosankofa A Yenkyi)

The second canonical form is a heart-shaped double spiral without a bird figure. This geometric form is particularly common in Adinkra textile printing (traditionally done by stamping calabash-carved blocks into cloth) and in contemporary graphic and artistic design. The spiraling return of the heart-form mirrors the conceptual content: return to the source, then move outward again.

Both forms carry identical semantic weight; the choice of form is contextual and medium-dependent.

## Akan Philosophical Context

### Adinkra and Funeral Wisdom

Adinkra symbols were traditionally printed on cloth worn at funerals by the Akan (Asante, Fante, and related peoples). The funeral context is not merely morbid — among the Akan, the occasion of death is the primary setting for the transmission of ancestral wisdom. The deceased has rejoined the ancestors; the living must receive what the deceased embodied and carry it forward. Sankofa at a funeral means: *what this person knew, what they lived, must be retrieved and carried by those who remain*. The symbol transforms grief into pedagogy.

### Ancestral Authority in Akan Cosmology

Akan cosmology recognizes multiple categories of being — the living, the ancestors (*asaman*), and those yet to be born. Wisdom (*nyansa*) does not originate in the present generation; it accumulates in the ancestor-body and is transmitted forward through ritual, story, and embodied practice. Sankofa encodes the philosophical recognition that the present generation cannot be wise on its own — it must reach back. This is not nostalgia; it is epistemology. The past is the database of tested solutions.

## The Cross-Tradition Memory-Return Pattern

Sankofa articulates a teaching found independently across multiple traditions — that mastery of the present and navigation of the future requires deliberate retrieval of the past. This is one of the strongest cross-tradition convergences in comparative religion: traditions from West Africa, East Asia, the Middle East, and South Asia independently arrive at the same teaching.

### Akan: Sankofa
Face forward, look back — integrate past wisdom for future motion. The backward gaze is not retreat but epistemic retrieval.

### Hebrew: *Zachor* (Remember)
The Hebrew Bible commands *zachor* — remember — 169 times, more than any other commandment. "Remember what Amalek did to you" (Deuteronomy 25:17). The Passover Haggadah requires ritual re-enactment: "In every generation, each person is obligated to see themselves as if they personally left Egypt" (Pesachim 10:5). This is not historical information transfer — it is ritual re-occupation of the past as a foundation for present freedom and future covenant.

### Confucian *Wen* (文, Culture/Learning)
The Confucian tradition grounds virtue not in innovation but in transmission — the careful study of the ancient sages (*wenxue*, the study of ancient texts) as the prerequisite of present virtuous action. Confucius famously said: *"I transmit; I do not innovate"* (Analects 7.1). The gentleman (*junzi*) who has not studied the ancient masters cannot act rightly in the present. The Confucian backward gaze is the foundation of social order.

### Buddhist *Smṛti* (Mindfulness/Memory)
The Sanskrit *smṛti* (Pali: *sati*), typically translated as "mindfulness," is etymologically a memory word — "that which is remembered," "the presence of the remembered past in the present moment." The Buddha's enlightenment included the recovery of all his previous lives (*jātaka* — birth stories) through *pubbenivāsānussati-ñāṇa* (knowledge of previous existences). The Buddha knows the present and the future because he can fully remember the past — across countless lives. The jātaka tales are the teaching vehicle: stories of the Buddha's past lives that illuminate present ethical choices.

### The Pattern

These four traditions — Akan, Hebrew, Confucian, Buddhist — independently converge on the same epistemic structure: **the past is not behind us; it is within us. To move forward well, you must know where you came from.** The difference is application: Akan Sankofa frames this as bird/egg (individual life-wisdom); *Zachor* frames it as collective memory constituting national identity; Confucian *wen* frames it as scholarly formation of character; Buddhist *smṛti* frames it as meditative presence constituting liberation. Same teaching; four independent transmission lineages.

## Pan-African Diaspora Reception

Sankofa has become the most widely adopted Adinkra symbol in the African diaspora and in Pan-African cultural movements worldwide — a remarkable transmission from Akan funeral cloth to global symbol of cultural identity.

### African-American Communities
Sankofa appears frequently as jewelry (the bird-form in gold or silver), tattoos (very common across African-American communities), and in visual art. The symbol's message — "go back and get it" — resonates powerfully with diaspora communities whose ancestral heritage was deliberately severed by slavery: the recovery of African roots is framed not as cultural tourism but as epistemic necessity.

### Education
Sankofa has been incorporated into African diaspora studies curricula, Afrocentric pedagogical frameworks (following Molefi Asante's Afrocentricity), and community educational programs emphasizing ancestral connection as a foundation for present self-knowledge.

### Film: *Sankofa* (1993)
Ethiopian director Haile Gerima's film *Sankofa* (1993) — produced and distributed independently after rejection by Hollywood studios — dramatizes a Black American model who is spiritually transported back to the experience of slavery on a Caribbean plantation, ultimately understanding her ancestral roots and present identity through that forced retrieval. The film was shown by traveling projectionists in African-American communities across the United States and became a touchstone of diaspora cinema.

### Kwanzaa and Pan-African Contexts
Sankofa iconography appears in Kwanzaa decorations, Pan-African identity-affirmation materials, and Black cultural institutions as an emblem of the teaching that cultural revival requires engagement with — not escape from — ancestral history.

## Material Culture

Sankofa appears in traditional Akan contexts in:
- **Kente cloth** woven with Sankofa-form patterning (Asante tradition)
- **Adinkra-stamped cloth** (funeral wear, ceremonial dress)
- **Goldweights** (*mrammuo*): the Akan used brass goldweights in the shape of Adinkra symbols and other forms to measure gold dust; Sankofa-form goldweights exist
- **Pottery and architectural relief** in Akan regional centers
