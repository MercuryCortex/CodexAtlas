# Sacred Number Seven

Seven is the most universally attested sacred number in world religion and cosmology. Unlike other sacred numbers whose sacredness is culturally specific, the sacredness of seven has a **single, identifiable astronomical root**: the ancient world could observe exactly **seven celestial bodies that moved against the fixed stars** — Sun, Moon, Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn. These seven 'wanderers' (*planetes*) were the movers of cosmic fate.

From this Babylonian astronomical foundation, the sacred seven was transmitted to virtually every subsequent religious tradition that came into contact with Mesopotamian or Hellenistic culture — and from there to global adoption through the seven-day week.

## The Week-Day Transmission

The seven-day week is the most successful Babylonian cultural export in history. The planetary names for each day survive in every European and South Asian language — English Thursday preserves a Babylonian → Roman → Norse transmission: Jupiter (Iuppiter) → Thor's-day. In Hindi, *Guruvara* (Jupiter's day) and *Shukravara* (Venus's day) arrived via Hellenistic astronomy after Alexander. The seven-day week became the global calendar standard, carrying Babylonian astronomical sacredness into every corner of human time-reckoning.

## The Seven-Heaven Ascent Across Traditions

The Babylonian seven-sphere cosmology generated a remarkable cross-traditional template: the **ascent through seven cosmic levels** appears in Jewish hekhalot mysticism, Islamic Mi'raj theology, Mithraic initiation (the seven-rung ladder documented by Celsus), and Gnostic pneumatic ascent. Paul's casual reference to "the third heaven" (2 Corinthians 12:2) implies a numbered seven-heaven hierarchy as common knowledge. The structure is singular; the valuation of the planetary rulers (divine guides vs. demonic prison-guards) varies by tradition.
