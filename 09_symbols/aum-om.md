# Aum / Om (ॐ)

## Identity
**Aum / Om** is the most-foundational **sacred syllable** of the **Indic religious traditions** — Hindu, Buddhist, Jain, and Sikh. The Devanagari glyph **ॐ** and its iconic three-part-plus-bindu form (A + U + M + the resonant silence) is **simultaneously a sound, a glyph, and a cosmological-metaphysical concept**: the **primal sound** (*praṇava*) from which the universe emerges, the **sound-of-Brahman** (the ultimate reality), the **meditational anchor** of Vedantic and Yogic practice, and the **mantra-syllable** that opens and closes virtually every Hindu mantra and ritual sequence.

The Mandukya Upanishad (~ -300 to +200) gives the canonical fourfold-meditation: **A** = waking consciousness; **U** = dream consciousness; **M** = deep-sleep consciousness; **the silence after** = *turiya*, the transcendent fourth state of *pure awareness*. The four states correspond to the four parts of the syllable, and meditation on Aum is meditation on the totality of consciousness.

Aum is **trans-Indic-religious**: it is universal in Hinduism (Vedic, Vedantic, Tantric, Bhakti); central in Tibetan-Vajrayana Buddhism (*Om Mani Padme Hum*); used in Jainism (the five-paramesthi compound); and reframed in Sikhism (*Ik Onkar* — "One Om" — Guru Nanak's monotheist re-articulation).

## Earliest attestation
- **c. -1500 to -1000 BCE** — Vedic *praṇava* in Rig-Veda and Yajur-Veda ritual usage. The Vedic Aum is *opener and closer of mantras*.
- **c. -800 to -300 BCE** — Upanishadic crystallization. The **Mandukya Upanishad** (c. -300 to +200) gives the canonical fourfold-meditation. The **Brihadaranyaka** and **Chandogya** Upanishads ([[phase-2-012-brihadaranyaka-upanishad]], [[phase-2-013-chandogya-upanishad]]) develop the Aum-Brahman identification.
- **c. -500 onward** — Buddhist appropriation. Tibetan Buddhism integrates Om into Mahayana-Vajrayana mantra-practice.
- **c. +1500 (Guru Nanak)** — Sikh reframing as *Ik Onkar* — the monotheist re-articulation.
- **19th-21st c.** — global diffusion through Theosophical, yoga, and meditation traditions.

## Meaning(s) attested
- **Vedic** — *praṇava* = the primal-sacred-utterance; the opener and closer of mantras.
- **Upanishadic (Mandukya)** — the four parts of consciousness (waking + dream + deep-sleep + *turiya*); the sound-of-Brahman.
- **Vedantic / Yogic** — meditation-anchor; the syllable on which one focuses to achieve *samadhi*.
- **Tantric** — bija-mantra (seed-mantra) of cosmic-consciousness; ritual-magical efficacy.
- **Buddhist (Mahayana-Vajrayana)** — opening syllable of mantras; *Om Mani Padme Hum* (the six-syllable mantra of Avalokiteshvara) is one of the most-recited mantras globally.
- **Sikh** — *Ik Onkar* = "One Om" — the foundational Sikh monotheist formulation; opens the [[phase-6-016-guru-granth-sahib|Guru Granth Sahib]].
- **Modern global** — generalized *Indic-spirituality* symbol; iconic in yoga, meditation, and contemporary spiritual culture.

## Cross-family appearance
Aum is **specifically Indic-religious in origin** but has substantial cross-Indic-tradition reception:
- **Universal across Hindu traditions** (Vedic, Vedantic, Yogic, Tantric, Bhakti).
- **Universal across Buddhist traditions** (especially Mahayana and Vajrayana mantra-practice).
- **Universal across Jain traditions** (the five-paramesthi compound).
- **Reframed in Sikhism** as *Ik Onkar* (monotheist).
- **Global modern spiritual culture** — yoga, meditation, popular spirituality.

The cross-tradition Indic reception is **one of the strongest cases of an iconographic-and-soundic symbol genuinely operating across multiple religious traditions** — Aum is meaningful and operative in all four major Indic religious families.

## Contested interpretations
- **The Vedic-vs-Upanishadic priority.** Some scholars (Radhakrishnan 1957) emphasize the Vedic origin; others (Olivelle 1996) emphasize the Upanishadic theological development. Both positions are defensible.
- **The relationship of the four parts to the four states.** The Mandukya's four-state correspondence is canonical but variously interpreted; modern advaita-vedanta and modern academic Indology give different readings.
