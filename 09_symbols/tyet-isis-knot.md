# Tyet (Knot of Isis)

## Identity
The **Tyet** (Egyptian: *ṯꜣyt* — also called the "Knot of Isis," "Blood of Isis," and "Girdle of Isis") is a looped-knot amulet with a loop at the top, a vertical shaft, and arm-elements folded downward — visually similar to the [[ankh]] but with the crossbar bent down rather than horizontal. It is one of the most common Egyptian funerary amulets, attested from the Old Kingdom (-2686 BCE) through the Late Period. In the **Book of the Dead** (Spell 156 — [[phase-1-010-book-of-the-dead]]), the tyet is specifically called "the Blood of Isis" and described as conferring the protection of Isis upon the deceased. Spell 156 specifies the tyet must be made of **carnelian** (deep red stone representing Isis's blood), red jasper, or red glass — the red color encoding the life-force of the divine mother. Placed on the neck of the mummy, the tyet invokes Isis's protective power for the dead person's journey through the Duat. The tyet was standardly paired with the [[djed-pillar]] in funerary use — tyet (Isis) + djed (Osiris) as the complete protective-regenerative dyad.

## Earliest attestation
- **Old Kingdom (c. -2686 to -2181 BCE)**: The tyet form appears on the Narmer Palette and in early dynastic iconography; its association with Isis is Old Kingdom.
- **Middle Kingdom (-2055 to -1650 BCE)**: Carnelian tyet amulets as standard funerary equipment.
- **New Kingdom (-1550 to -1070 BCE)**: Peak use as funerary amulet. Book of the Dead Spell 156 [[phase-1-010-book-of-the-dead]] specifies the tyet's use, composition (carnelian/red jasper/red glass), and placement (neck of mummy). This is the canonical textual anchor.
- **Late Period and Ptolemaic period**: Continued use; the tyet appears in Isis temple iconography and Isiac mystery contexts through the Roman period.

## Meaning(s) attested
- **"Blood of Isis"** (Book of the Dead Spell 156): The tyet IS the blood-of-Isis materialized as a red amulet. Isis's blood represents divine maternal life-force; the mummy wearing it is clothed in Isis's protection. The red carnelian = blood; the knot-form = binding/enclosing protection.
- **Protective binding**: The tyet as a knotted form encodes the protective logic of the closed knot — the knot binds what is inside it, excluding what is outside. The dead person wearing the tyet is "bound" in Isis's protection, enclosed within the divine maternal circle.
- **The girdle/sash of Isis**: Some scholars read the tyet as a representation of Isis's girdle or sash — the knotted garment attribute of the goddess. If correct, wearing the tyet = being clothed in Isis's garment = being enfolded in her body.
- **The tyet-djed dyad**: Paired with the [[djed-pillar]] (the backbone/stability of Osiris), the tyet represents the complementary axis of Isis's active, binding, mobile protective power against Osiris's stable, upright, passive regenerative endurance. Together they form a complete protective theology: motion + stability, female + male, binding + uprightness.

## Cross-family appearance: the protective knot across traditions

### The Knot as Protective Symbol: Independent Attestations
The **closed, self-enclosing knot as a form of protection** is one of the most widely attested cross-tradition symbolic logics. These are independent attestations of the same symbolic claim — that the form of the knot (self-enclosed, with no opening for malign forces to enter or exit) constitutes protection:

**Egyptian tyet**: Red carnelian knot of Isis, placed on the mummy's neck, protecting the dead.

**Buddhist Endless Knot** ([[endless-knot]]): One of the Eight Auspicious Signs (*Ashtamangala*) — an endless interlaced knot with no beginning and no end, representing the interconnection of all phenomena and the protection of the dharma. The endless knot on a practitioner's possessions or meditation objects serves a protective function. No documented transmission from Egyptian tyet; independent development of the protective-knot logic.

**Celtic knotwork**: The interlaced endless knots of Insular Celtic art (Book of Kells, Lindisfarne Gospels, La Tène metalwork) — continuous interlaced forms with no end-points — are widely interpreted in the scholarly literature as protective forms, though the evidence for explicitly magical or protective intention is disputed (vs. purely decorative/aesthetic function).

**Kabbalistic red string**: The Jewish red thread tied around the wrist — associated with Rachel's Tomb (Kever Rachel) near Bethlehem as protection against the Evil Eye — is a red protective binding tied as a knot around the wrist. It is associated with the matriarch Rachel as divine mother-intercessor. The parallel with the tyet is striking: (1) red color = divine life-force; (2) knotted form = protective binding; (3) associated with a divine/holy maternal figure (Isis / Rachel). No documented genetic connection; independent attestation of the divine-mother-red-protective-knot pattern.

### Blood of Isis and the Red Protective Substance

The specific identification of the tyet as "Blood of Isis" connects it to a broader cross-tradition pattern: **red as the color of divine protective life-force**:

- **Egyptian tyet** (Spell 156): "Blood of Isis" in red carnelian protects the dead.
- **Passover blood** (Exodus 12:7, 12:13): Blood of the Passover lamb smeared on doorposts *"shall be a sign for you on the houses where you are. And when I see the blood, I will pass over you"* — red blood as apotropaic (evil-averting) marker. The **protective blood on the threshold** = the tyet's protective blood on the mummy's neck: same logic, independent traditions.
- **Red heifer** (Numbers 19): The ashes of a red heifer purify ritual impurity — the redness is the operative quality. Red as purifying and protective substance.
- **Kabbalistic red string** (above): Red thread as protective binding at Rachel's Tomb.

The **red + binding + divine maternal protection** combination (Egyptian tyet → Kabbalistic red string → Passover blood on the threshold) represents a cross-tradition cluster of independent but structurally convergent practices.

### The Girdle as Divine Attribute Across Traditions
If the tyet is Isis's **girdle** (the knotted sash at the waist), it belongs to the cross-tradition pattern of the **divine belt/cord as attribute of divine power**:

- **Isis's tyet/girdle**: divine maternal protective power
- **Aphrodite's *kestos himas*** (the magical girdle of desire in the *Iliad*): divine erotic power materialized as a garment-attribute
- **Heracles' belt** (divine heroic power)
- **Shiva's snake-girdle** (divine ascetic-tantric power)

The **divine girdle as a portable, wearable form of divine power** is a documented cross-tradition pattern — the amulet form of the tyet is the portable-magical version of this garment-attribute logic.

## Contested interpretations
- **Ankh vs. tyet — variants or independent symbols?** The visual similarity is undeniable; the origin-relationship is unresolved. Wilkinson (1992) presents both possibilities without conclusive resolution. The downward-folded arms may derive from a knotted sash/girdle (tyet) vs. a sandal-strap (ankh) — suggesting independent origins for visually similar forms.
- **What does "Blood of Isis" mean?** Three scholarly interpretations: (1) Isis's menstrual blood (a fertility-protective substance); (2) the blood shed during Osiris's murder and resurrection — the blood of divine regeneration; (3) a purely conventional designation with no literal blood reference. The carnelian's deep red color supports interpretation (1) or (2); Pinch (2002) surveys the debate.
- **Was the tyet a specifically Isiac symbol or a general-purpose knot-amulet adopted by the Isis cult?** The tyet pre-dates its Isis association; it may have been a generic protective knot amulet that was incorporated into the Isis theological system during the New Kingdom's Isis-expansion phase.

## Mystery / unknown variants
The precise origin of the tyet's distinctive form (why the arms fold downward, and what material object — if any — the tyet represents: a knotted sash, a looped strap, a folded papyrus) remains unresolved in Egyptological scholarship. Wilkinson (1992) notes the debate but cannot offer a definitive answer.
