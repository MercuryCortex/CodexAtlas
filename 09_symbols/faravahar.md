# Faravahar

## Identity
The Faravahar (Persian *Faravahar*, *Fravahar*, or *Foruhar*) is **the single most-recognizable visual symbol of Zoroastrianism** — a **winged disc with a bearded male figure emerging from the upper half**, holding a ring in one hand and gesturing with the other. The iconography emerges in **Achaemenid Persia (-550 to -330)** under the kings Cyrus, Darius, Xerxes — pervasive on the rock-reliefs of **Persepolis** and **Naqsh-e Rustam**, on royal seals, on coinage, and on official Achaemenid royal-religious art. The figure persists in **Sasanian iconography** with modifications and in **modern Parsi-Iranian Zoroastrian community identity** as the universally-recognized symbol of the religion. In **post-1979 Iranian secular-nationalist culture** (especially the Iranian diaspora) the Faravahar has been adopted as the **pre-Islamic Iranian national symbol** distinct from Islamic-Republic state-iconography — making it one of the **most politically-loaded religious-iconographic symbols** in the modern Middle East. For the Codex Atlas mission the Faravahar is doubly load-bearing: (1) it is **the visible identity-marker of Zoroastrian religion** in a way no other Zoroastrian symbol matches; (2) it represents **the Iranian-Achaemenid royal-religious iconographic-program** that fused (a) borrowed Near Eastern winged-disc royal iconography with (b) Iranian-Zoroastrian theological content (the *fravaši*-doctrine, royal *xvarənah*-glory, Ahura Mazda or the king-as-divine-representative).

## Earliest attestation
- **Achaemenid royal art (-550 to -330)**: the principal corpus. **Persepolis reliefs** (Darius I and Xerxes audience-scenes, late 6th–5th c. BCE), **Naqsh-e Rustam royal tomb-reliefs** (Darius I, Xerxes I, Artaxerxes I, Darius II — all 5th c. BCE), **Behistun inscription** (Darius I, ~-518; the Faravahar appears above the king-figure), countless royal seals and seal-impressions.
- **Achaemenid coinage**: less common than on rock-reliefs but attested.
- **Sasanian period (+224 to +651)**: the iconography continues with modifications; some Sasanian rock-reliefs show similar winged-disc-with-figure forms.
- **Parsi diaspora and Iranian-Zoroastrian survival (post-651)**: the symbol persists in community-identity contexts.
- **Modern (19th-20th c. onward)**: archaeologically-rediscovered through European exploration of Persepolis; adopted by modern Parsi community + Iranian secular-nationalist movements.

## Meaning(s) attested

The **theological referent of the Faravahar** is contested — multiple readings have been proposed:

1. **Fravaši-doctrine** (Boyce 1982, Encyclopaedia Iranica). The Avestan *fravaši* is the pre-existent personal-spiritual aspect of each human (and divine) being — the soul-aspect that exists before birth, accompanies the living person, and persists after death as ancestral-spirit. The Faravahar visually depicts a *fravaši*. **The currently dominant scholarly reading.**

2. **Ahura Mazda** himself. Older Western scholarship sometimes identified the figure with Ahura Mazda directly. Boyce 1982 rejects this — Zoroastrian theology of the period would not typically depict Ahura Mazda anthropomorphically.

3. **The king's *xvarənah*** (royal divine-glory). The figure depicts the divine glory that legitimates the king's rule. The figure-and-king pairing on royal reliefs supports this reading.

4. **Generic divinity** / the divine-presence as such, without specific theological identification.

The dominant current view treats the Faravahar as visualization of the **fravaši** or the **xvarənah** depending on context — both interpretations are theologically continuous with Zoroastrian doctrine. The "Ahura Mazda directly" reading is older and largely abandoned.

## Cross-family appearance

### Near Eastern winged-disc iconographic lineage

The **winged-disc element** of the Faravahar derives from broader Near Eastern royal-religious iconography:
- **Egyptian winged-sun-disc** (Behdet-form, attested c. -2700 onward) — Horus-of-Behdet, the protective royal-cosmic emblem above pharaohs.
- **Assyrian winged-sun-disc** (attested extensively from c. -1300 onward) — depicting Ashur or Shamash; spread widely through Assyrian imperial conquests.
- **Babylonian / Neo-Babylonian winged-sun-disc** (attested in Neo-Babylonian royal iconography).

The **Persian-Achaemenid Faravahar** combines:
- (a) **Borrowed Near Eastern winged-disc** (Assyrian-derived; the Achaemenids conquered the Assyrian-Babylonian territories and absorbed their royal iconographic vocabulary).
- (b) **Iranian innovation**: a **figure-emerging-from-the-disc**, which is *not* a standard Near Eastern form. The figure-element is Iranian-Achaemenid innovation built on Near Eastern winged-disc background.

This is one of the cleanest documentable cases of **cross-tradition iconographic synthesis** — Iranian religious-iconographic content built on borrowed Near Eastern visual vocabulary.

### Modern reception
- **Parsi community symbol**: pendant + tattoo + display-art ubiquitous in the global Parsi-Zoroastrian community.
- **Iranian secular-nationalist symbol** (post-1979): widely-displayed pre-Islamic-Persian heritage marker; deployed by Iranian diaspora and (in muted form) within Iran as cultural identity-marker distinct from Islamic-Republic state-iconography.
- **Cross-cultural recognition**: the Faravahar is internationally recognizable as "the Zoroastrian symbol" in a way no other Zoroastrian visual element matches.

## Contested interpretations
- **Theological referent**: fravaši vs. xvarənah vs. Ahura Mazda vs. generic-divinity. The dominant current view: fravaši or xvarənah depending on context.
- **Near Eastern borrowing scope**: how much of the iconography is Assyrian-borrowed vs. Iranian-innovative. Most scholars accept the winged-disc as Assyrian-derived; the emerging-figure as Iranian innovation.
- **Date of canonical form**: when the standard Faravahar form crystallizes is debated — most place it in the Darius I period (~-520), with Cyrus-period proto-forms preceding.

## Mystery / unknown variants
None major — the Faravahar is well-attested iconographically; the theological-referent debate is interpretive rather than mystery-status. `mystery-status: partially-deciphered` (because of the theological-referent uncertainty rather than iconographic-mystery).
