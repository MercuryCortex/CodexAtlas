# Dying and Rising God

## Identity

The **Dying and Rising God** is one of the most contested but also most structurally persistent patterns in comparative religion: a divine figure who **dies** (through violence, dismemberment, descent to the underworld, or sacrifice), **passes through** a realm of death or void, and **rises again** — whose return is associated with cosmic, seasonal, or spiritual renewal. The pattern was systematized by James George Frazer in *The Golden Bough* (1890) as a universal feature of ancient religion associated with vegetation cults. The category was subsequently critiqued, refined, and partially rehabilitated by Jonathan Z. Smith, Tryggve Mettinger, and Walter Burkert. The scholarly consensus now is: **the pattern is real but more restricted than Frazer claimed**, and the resemblances between the ancient Near Eastern dying-god figures and the Christian Christ are structural convergences, not direct literary borrowing.

The cross-tradition MASSIVE WIN: **the sequence death → descent into void/underworld → resurrection → cosmic renewal appears in Egypt (Osiris), Mesopotamia (Dumuzi/Tammuz), Greece (Dionysus-Zagreus, Adonis, Persephone), Phrygia (Attis), Phoenicia (Adonis), Norse (Baldr), and Christianity (Christ)** — cultures across 3,000 years and multiple language families. Whether this is the same archetype, independent parallel development, or diffusion from a common ancient Near Eastern source remains debated. The structural fact is that the same narrative shape — the sacred dying-and-returning figure as the model for human death and resurrection — is one of the most widespread theological innovations in the ancient world.

## Earliest attestation

- **c. -2400 BCE** — Osiris mythology attested in the *Pyramid Texts* of Unas; the dying-and-resurrecting god is present at the earliest recoverable layer of Egyptian royal theology.
- **c. -1900 to -1700 BCE** — *Descent of Inanna* (Sumerian) with Dumuzi as divine substitute in the Underworld — attested in Sumerian cuneiform on tablets from Nippur.
- **c. -700 BCE** — Adonis cult at Byblos (Phoenicia) attested; Ezekiel 8:14 (c. -593 BCE) references Tammuz-mourning in Jerusalem.
- **c. -500 to -300 BCE** — Orphic Dionysus-Zagreus theology (dying god as cosmogonic myth) reconstructed from fragments.
- **c. -200 BCE** — Attis cult fully documented in association with Magna Mater/Cybele; Hilaria spring festival (mourning + resurrection celebration) established in Rome by c. 200 CE.
- **c. 30–33 CE** — Crucifixion and Resurrection of Jesus Christ; Pauline theology (*1 Corinthians 15*, c. 54 CE) encodes this as the archetypal death-and-resurrection to which all Christians are joined in baptism.

## Meaning(s) attested

### The Frazerian synthesis (and its critique)

James Frazer's *The Golden Bough* argued that the dying-and-rising god was a universal primitive religion structured around the need for the vegetation deity to die in winter and return in spring. The king-sacrifice, the corn-god, the mourning goddess (Isis/Ishtar/Demeter) — Frazer organized all these into a single religious type. This synthesis was enormously influential: it shaped T.S. Eliot's *The Waste Land*, Jessie Weston's *From Ritual to Romance* (which influenced the Holy Grail tradition), Freud, Jung, and the entire 20th-century comparative mythology project.

Jonathan Z. Smith (1987) critiqued the Frazerian synthesis with philological precision: many of the 'dying and rising gods' don't actually *rise* in their primary sources (Osiris rules the dead but doesn't return to life; Attis's resurrection narrative may be a later accretion). Smith argued the category was constructed by modern scholars, not found in ancient religion. Mettinger (2001) responded: the critique is partially valid but overcorrected. Some dying-rising deities (Baal, Melqart, possibly Adonis) have genuine ancient Near Eastern evidence for resurrection; Osiris is a genuine case of dying-and-resurrection-to-a-different-realm (the underworld). The scholarly middle position: the pattern is real, restricted, varied across instances, and not reducible to Frazer's single vegetation template.

### The five primary instances

**Osiris**: The model case. Osiris dies, is reconstituted by Isis, and rises — but to rule the underworld, not to return to the world of the living. His resurrection is the *model* for human resurrection: the deceased becomes Osiris through funerary ritual. Death is not final; but the 'rising' is to the Duat, not to this world.

**Dumuzi/Tammuz**: The descent-and-return narrative is explicit — Dumuzi spends half the year in the Underworld, half in the world. This is the clearest vegetation-cycle myth: his descent = summer drought; his return = spring fertility. The weeping of the women for Tammuz (Ezekiel 8:14) is the cultural penetration of the Mesopotamian pattern into Israelite religious practice.

**Adonis/Attis**: The Hellenistic mystery-cult forms. Both involve death, mourning, and some form of return — though scholars debate the ancient precedents for the 'resurrection' element in each case. The Hilaria festival of Attis (March 25 — the spring equinox) and the Adonia (summer weeping festivals) established the emotional-mourning-and-restoration cycle as a pan-Mediterranean religious experience.

**Dionysus-Zagreus (Orphic)**: The cosmogonic form. The god's dismemberment by the Titans is not a seasonal cycle but a creation myth — humanity is made from the Titans' ashes containing divine Dionysus-substance. The god's 'resurrection' through Zeus re-engendering him is the theological foundation of Orphic soteriology. This is the most philosophically developed form of the dying-god theology.

**Jesus Christ**: Mettinger (2001) concludes there is no literary borrowing from the Near Eastern dying-god cults into the Christ-narrative — the traditions are independent. But the structural parallel is real: crucifixion as sacrifice, three days in the tomb (death-realm descent), resurrection, ascension. Paul (*Romans 6*) explicitly frames baptism as participation in Christ's death-and-resurrection. Whatever the historical origins, the Christ-pattern functionally instantiates the dying-and-rising theology in its most historically consequential form.

### The Girardian reframe

René Girard (*Violence and the Sacred*, 1972) proposes a materialist interpretation: the dying-and-rising god is the mythological encoding of the **scapegoat mechanism**. Human communities resolve internal violence by redirecting it onto a surrogate victim — the sacred king, the human sacrifice, the corn-god — whose death 'purifies' the community. The myth of the dying god is the community's justification of the murder: the victim *chose* to die, *will rise again*, *wishes the sacrifice*. The distinction between human sacrifice, ritual killing, and the mythological dying-god is, on Girard's reading, not categorical but continuous. The resurrection element adds cosmic endorsement: the community was right to kill the victim because the cosmos endorses the act by returning the victim to life.

## Cross-family appearance

The pattern appears across traditions with varying degrees of contact:
- **Ancient Near East** (Egypt/Mesopotamia/Phoenicia/Canaan): strong documented connections; Egyptian and Mesopotamian dying-god mythologies influenced each other.
- **Greek/Aegean**: the Dionysus-Zagreus and Adonis traditions show Near Eastern influence; the Eleusinian Mysteries (Persephone's descent) may be independent.
- **Norse**: Baldr's death and post-Ragnarök return is disputed for Christian contamination in Snorri's 12th-century recording.
- **Christian**: independent development from Jewish resurrection theology (*Isaiah* 53, *Ezekiel* 37, *Daniel* 12) with possible Hellenistic environmental influence but no documented direct literary borrowing from the pagan dying-god cults.

## Contested interpretations

- **Whether the category 'dying and rising god' is scholarly construction or ancient reality**: Smith (1987) vs. Mettinger (2001) is the central debate.
- **Whether Christ's resurrection is historically related to the Near Eastern dying-god cults**: Mainstream scholarship (Mettinger 2001, Hengel 1981) says no direct relationship; popular comparative religion (Frazer, Joseph Campbell) implies yes. The two positions are based on different methodological standards for 'relationship.'
- **Baldr and Christian influence**: Dumézil argued Baldr is genuine pre-Christian Norse; others argue Snorri's resurrection narrative is a Christian overlay. The original pre-Christian Norse form of the Baldr myth is inaccessible.
- **Osiris as dying-and-rising god**: Smith argues Osiris never 'rises' in the sense of returning to life — he rules the dead but does not return to the living world. Griffiths and Mettinger counter that 'rising to the underworld' is a form of resurrection that the ancient sources clearly intend as such.
