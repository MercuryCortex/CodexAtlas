# Triquetra

## Identity

The **triquetra** (Latin: *three-cornered*) is a geometric symbol consisting of three interlocked arcs or vesica-piscis shapes forming a continuous three-pointed figure — visually a trefoil knot. It belongs first to the **La Tène Celtic decorative vocabulary** (c. 450–50 BCE) as a curvilinear ornamental motif, then acquires its most significant religious career as a **Christian Trinity emblem** in the Insular (Irish-Northumbrian) manuscript tradition from the 7th century CE, most famously in the *Book of Kells* (c. 800 CE) and the *Lindisfarne Gospels* (c. 715 CE). In the 20th century it was adopted by Wicca and neopaganism as the **Triple Goddess emblem** — the same three-fold form serving two completely different theological frameworks across thirteen centuries of reception. The triquetra is also closely related to its structural parallel, the [[valknut]] (three interlocked triangles), both arising in the Norse/Celtic zone as geometric encodings of "three-in-one."

## Earliest attestation

- **Decorative (La Tène):** Trefoil/three-cornered forms appear in La Tène Celtic metalwork and stonework from c. 450–50 BCE — but without documented religious or symbolic significance at this stage. This is the decorative substrate.
- **Religious attestation:** The triquetra as a consciously deployed religious symbol (Christian Trinity emblem) is first securely attested in the **Insular gospel books** of the 7th–8th century CE:
  - *Book of Durrow* (c. 680 CE) — earliest major Insular gospel book with elaborate knotwork.
  - *Lindisfarne Gospels* (c. 715 CE) — Northumbrian; complex interlaced triquetra forms in the carpet pages.
  - *Book of Kells* (c. 800 CE) — Irish; the richest deployment of triquetra-based Trinity knotwork in the Insular tradition.
- **North Germanic:** Funbo Runestone (Uppsala, Sweden, Viking Age) — triquetra on a rune stone, evidence of transmission to Scandinavian visual culture.

## Meaning(s) attested

- **Decorative (La Tène, c. 450–50 BCE):** Ornamental motif. No documented sacred meaning at this stage, though the three-fold form recurs prominently in Celtic visual culture.
- **Christian Trinity emblem (Insular, 7th–8th c. CE):** The triquetra visually encodes Trinitarian theology — three distinct arcs that are formally inseparable and continuous. The shape performs Augustine's claim in *De Trinitate* (c. 400–416 CE): three persons, one substance, none greater or lesser. The Insular monks deploying this form were doing applied visual theology.
- **Neopagan Triple Goddess:** Wicca and neopaganism (20th c. onward) read the triquetra as the **Triple Goddess** — Maiden, Mother, Crone. This is a 20th-century reinterpretation with no documented connection to the pre-Christian La Tène usage or the Christian Insular usage; it is a modern meaning grafted onto an ancient form.

## Cross-family appearance

### Celtic Knotwork Mathematics

Celtic interlaced knotwork is a **sophisticated mathematical art**: it requires systematic planning of over-under passes at every crossing so that the pattern resolves as a set of coherent strands. George Bain's *Celtic Art: The Methods of Construction* (1951, Tier-1) documented the construction rules systematically. The triquetra is the simplest member of a family of Celtic knots that includes forms of arbitrary complexity — the simplest non-trivial case of a class of patterns that are now studied as **knot diagrams in mathematical topology**. The Insular monks were not doing mathematics, but they were working in a tradition of geometric pattern-making whose underlying structure is topological.

### Vesica Piscis: Geometric substrate

The triquetra is constituted by **three overlapping vesica piscis** forms — each arc of the triquetra is the boundary of one vesica. The [[vesica-piscis]] (the lens-shaped overlap of two equal circles) is itself a symbol in sacred geometry. Three vesicae arranged in three-fold rotational symmetry produce the triquetra. This geometric relationship is documented in Bain (1951) and is not a modern reading — the construction method for drawing a triquetra begins with three vesicae.

### Norse parallel: Valknut

The [[valknut]] (three interlocked triangles, c. 700–800 CE) and the triquetra (three interlocked arcs, La Tène origin with religious use from c. 680 CE) are **structurally parallel** symbols from the same Norse/Celtic cultural zone, appearing at roughly the same time. Both encode "three-in-one" in different geometric vocabularies — triangles vs. arcs. Both underwent Christian-Trinity interpretation during the Christianization of northern Europe. They represent two independent geometric solutions to the same formal problem.

### Insular synthesis with Celtic Cross

The triquetra and the [[celtic-cross]] are the two flagship products of the **Insular project** — the 7th–9th century Irish-Northumbrian synthesis of Christian theology with Celtic visual grammar. The celtic-cross fuses Christian cross with Celtic ring; the triquetra encodes Trinity in Celtic knotwork. Both are products of the same creative moment in the same geographic zone.

## Contested interpretations

- **Was the La Tène three-fold form intentionally "the triquetra" or just decorative?** The scholarly consensus (Megaw 2001) treats the La Tène forms as decorative without demonstrated religious intention. The continuity between La Tène decoration and Insular Christian use is a formal continuity, not a documented transmission of meaning.
- **Does the neopagan Triple Goddess interpretation have any historical basis?** No. Ronald Hutton's *The Triumph of the Moon* (1999, Tier-1) documents the Triple Goddess as a 20th-century construction by Gerald Gardner and Robert Graves (*The White Goddess*, 1948). The attribution of the triquetra to the "ancient Celtic Triple Goddess" has no pre-20th-century textual basis.
- **Does the North Germanic rune-stone triquetra carry Norse cosmological or Christian meaning?** The Funbo Runestone context is ambiguous — it could be Christian (Trinity), Norse-cosmological (three Norns, three roots of Yggdrasil), or merely decorative. The Viking Age rune-stone context is a zone where these meanings are in active syncretism and cannot always be cleanly separated.
