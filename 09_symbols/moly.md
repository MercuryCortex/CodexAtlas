# Moly

## Identity

**Moly** (Greek **μῶλυ**, *mōly*) is commonly identified as the **first named magical herb in Greek — and arguably in Western — literature** (Stannard 1962): the plant that Hermes pulls from the ground of Circe's island Aeaea and gives to Odysseus as protection against her drugs (*Odyssey* 10.302–306). Homer describes it in a single couplet that launched two and a half millennia of speculation: "at the root it was black, but its flower was like milk. *Mōly* the gods call it; and it is hard for mortals to dig, but the gods can do all things." The word occurs exactly once in Homer, and the poet gives **only the divine name** — no mortal name exists for it, because the thing itself lies beyond mortal knowledge (Clay 1972). It is the archetype of the *pharmakon*-antidote: the good drug (*φάρμακον ἐσθλόν*, Od. 10.287, 292) that cancels the baleful drugs (*φάρμακα λύγρα*) of the enchantress — a scene that deploys the double-edged word *pharmakon* in both its senses at once (cf. the same doubleness at Od. 4.230, Helen's drugs). In the Atlas, moly stands in 09_symbols beside [[haoma]] and [[amanita-muscaria]] as the Greek epic occupant of the divine-plant slot, and it wires into [[theme-guide-through-underworld]] as the material form of the divine guide's protection: [[hermes-greek]] the psychopomp arming a mortal for passage through hostile supernatural terrain.

## In the Odyssey

The scene (Od. 10.274–306): Odysseus, walking alone from his ship toward Circe's hall — where half his crew have already been turned to swine — is intercepted by Hermes "in the likeness of a young man with the first down on his lip." Hermes tells him plainly that he goes to his ruin, then arms him with both intelligence and object: Circe will mix him a potion and strike him with her wand; the herb will defeat the potion; at the blow he must draw his sword and rush at her as if to kill; and before entering her bed he must exact the great oath of the gods, that she will plot no further harm against him. Then "Argeiphontes gave me the herb, drawing it from the ground, and showed me its nature (*physis*)" — black root, milk-white flower, divine name, hard for mortals to dig (10.302–306).

The herb works by simply being: Circe mixes her drug in a golden cup, Odysseus drinks it down, "but it did not bewitch me" (10.318). Strikingly, **the text never says that Odysseus eats, drinks, or applies the moly** — Hermes gives it, and its possession is apparently enough. The standard commentary (Heubeck & Hoekstra 1989) notes that the mechanics are left wholly unexplained: this is the logic of the fabulous talisman, not of pharmacology. The mastered Circe then becomes, in Odyssey 10.487–540, the very briefing-guide who sends Odysseus to the land of the dead — so the moly gift stands at the head of the chain that leads directly into the Nekyia of Odyssey 11, the foundational Greek underworld-consultation (see [[odysseus]]).

A late tradition preserved in Eustathius's Byzantine commentary on the Odyssey (citing Alexander of Paphos) supplied the herb with an etiology Homer never gives: moly sprang from the blood of the giant Picolous, who fled the war against Zeus, assaulted Circe on Aeaea, and was slain by her father Helios — the flower white like the Sun who killed him, the root black with the giant's blood, the name drawn from the "hard fight" (*mōlos*) over him. It is reception, not Homer, and is documented here as such.

## Botanical identifications, ancient and modern

The identification industry began in antiquity. **Theophrastus** (*Historia Plantarum* 9.15.7) reports a plant called moly growing around Pheneus and on Mount Kyllene in Arcadia — Hermes's own birth-mountain — "like the moly of which Homer speaks," with a round, onion-like root and leaves like squill, used against spells and enchantments; he adds, deflating the epic, that it is *not* in fact hard to dig. **Pliny** (*Natural History* 25.26–27) calls moly the most renowned of plants (*herbarum clarissima*), credits its discovery to Mercury, and repeats the Arcadian reports; **Dioscorides** carries a moly entry in the *De Materia Medica*. The onion-like root seeded a durable garlic-family tradition, fossilized when Linnaeus named the golden garlic ***Allium moly***. Jerry Stannard's survey ("The Plant Called Moly," *Osiris* 14, 1962) traces dozens of proposed identifications across two millennia of herbals and commentaries — the definitive case study in how a poetic plant generates confident botany.

The modern chapter is the **snowdrop/galantamine hypothesis** (Plaitakis & Duvoisin 1983): Circe's potion reads like anticholinergic poisoning of the *Datura stramonium* type (delirium, amnesia, compliance); the snowdrop *Galanthus nivalis* contains galantamine, an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor — today licensed for Alzheimer's disease — which is a genuine physiological antidote to anticholinergic delirium; therefore moly "was" the snowdrop. **The Atlas flags this as speculation, not fact.** The argument is pharmacologically elegant and historically undemonstrable: it requires the Odyssey to be accurate toxicological reportage rather than epic, and classical scholarship (Stannard 1962; Heubeck & Hoekstra 1989) treats moly as a fabulous plant whose black-root/milk-flower antithesis is poetic, not diagnostic. The identification nonetheless circulates in the medical and popular literature as if established — the same pattern of confident over-identification the vault documents for [[amanita-muscaria]] and the Soma question.

One piece of the epic description does have real ancient context: the herb **"hard for mortals to dig."** Greek root-cutters (*rhizotomoi*) surrounded the harvesting of powerful plants with ritual precaution — Theophrastus (HP 9.8) records circle-drawing, prayers, and prescribed procedures for digging dangerous roots, and Josephus (*Jewish War* 7.180–185) describes the lethal baaras root that had to be extracted by a dog because it killed its digger. The dangerous-to-harvest plant is a genuine ancient category; Homer's couplet gives it its oldest literary form — with the twist that for moly the difficulty is not danger but ontology: mortals cannot dig it because it is the gods' plant.

## Cross-tradition significance

**The language of the gods (the secure comparative anchor).** Homer's "*mōly* the gods call it" belongs to a small, well-studied set of Homeric double-namings: Briareos whom men call Aigaion (Il. 1.403–404), the bird the gods call *chalkis* and men *kymindis* (Il. 14.290–291), the river the gods call Xanthos and men Skamandros (Il. 20.74). The device recurs, structurally elaborated, in the Old Norse *Alvíssmál*, where the dwarf recites the names of things in the tongues of gods, men, giants, elves, and dwarves — and in Hittite, Old Irish, and Indic material. Since Güntert's 1921 monograph on the speech of gods, and definitively in Watkins (1970) and West (2007), this has been treated as an **inherited Indo-European poetic institution**, not a borrowing between Greece and Scandinavia: a metalinguistic device by which poets marked the gap between divine and mortal knowledge. Moly is the extreme case within the set — the only instance where the mortal name is not given at all, because (Clay 1972) mortals have no access to the thing named. This is the node's strongest cross-tradition wire, and it is a **linguistic-poetic inheritance claim**, of a different and more secure kind than any botanical parallel.

**The divine-plant slot (typological convergence, honestly framed).** As a plant known to the gods that confers power over supernatural harm, moly stands in the same comparative slot as Indo-Iranian [[haoma]]/Soma and the rejuvenation-plant of *Gilgamesh* XI — the thorny herb at the bottom of the sea, likewise hard to obtain, likewise lost to mortals. The differences matter: haoma is a pressed, hymned, ritually consumed cult-substance; the Gilgamesh plant promises renewed youth; moly is a one-off narrative apotropaic with no cult, no consumption, and no existence outside a single scene. Given that the Odyssey's debt to Gilgamesh in the Circe-Calypso-Siduri and underworld material is a seriously argued transmission case (West 1997; Burkert 1992 — see [[odysseus]]), a Near Eastern background to the Odyssean marvellous-plant motif is at least discussable; but no mainstream scholar rests a specific moly-from-Gilgamesh dependence claim on it, and the Atlas records the parallel as **typological, within an epic-transmission debate that concerns the surrounding episode rather than the herb itself**. The haoma parallel is pure convergence: two traditions independently marking certain plants as belonging to the gods.

**The pharmakon.** The Circe episode is among the earliest Greek texts to stage the ambivalence of *pharmakon* — drug, poison, remedy, charm — that runs through Greek medicine and magic, into Plato's *Phaedrus* and its modern philosophical reception. Moly is the *pharmakon esthlon*, the good drug set against the witch's baleful ones: the founding literary instance of the antidote.

## Disputes

1. **Real plant or poetic invention?** The mainstream position (Heubeck & Hoekstra 1989; Stannard 1962) is that moly is a fabulous plant of story — the black-root/white-flower antithesis is poetic emblem, not field description. The ancient naturalists' Arcadian moly is best read as a real plant *named after* the Homeric one, reversing the direction later identifiers assume.
2. **The snowdrop/galantamine identification** (Plaitakis & Duvoisin 1983): documented above as Tier-2 speculation. Its wide uncritical repetition in pharmacological literature is itself a documented phenomenon of modern myth-making.
3. **Does Odysseus use it?** The text never describes ingestion or application; the mechanism of protection is unstated. Readings that make moly a consumed antidote import more than the text gives.
4. **Etymology.** The word *mōly* has no secure Greek etymology; ancient guesses (the *mōlos* "struggle" of the Picolous story) are folk-etymology, and modern comparanda remain speculative — fitting, for a word Homer assigns to the language of the gods.
