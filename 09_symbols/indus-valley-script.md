# Indus Valley Script

> **Methodology discipline note (`mystery-status: mystery`):** Per `00_meta/methodology.md`'s symbol-research discipline rule, undeciphered scripts get **NO interpretive body claims** — only attestation + the scholarly state-of-debate. This entry holds to that rule: it does NOT propose what the inscriptions mean, what religion or language they encode, or how they relate to subsequent Indic traditions. It DOES document the scholarly state of the principal Parpola-pro-writing vs. Farmer-Sproat-Witzel-against-writing debate.

## Identity

The Indus Valley script (also called Harappan script or Indus script) is the undeciphered corpus of inscriptions from the Indus Valley Civilization (IVC), the Bronze Age urban civilization that flourished in the Indus River basin (present-day Pakistan and northwest India) c. -2600 to -1900. Approximately 4,000 known inscriptions, mostly short (3-7 signs typical; longest known ~26 signs) and predominantly on stamp-seals. Sites: Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, Lothal, Dholavira, Rakhigarhi, and others. The corpus has been intensely studied since John Marshall's 1920s excavations published in 1931; multiple decipherment claims have been advanced (Dravidian, Indo-Aryan, Munda, Sumerian, etc.); none has achieved scholarly consensus. The fundamental scholarly debate is whether the inscriptions are a linguistic writing system at all.

## Earliest attestation

- **c. -2600 BCE** — earliest dated Indus-script inscriptions from the mature Harappan phase.
- **c. -1900 BCE** — terminal phase of the Indus Valley Civilization; the script disappears with the civilization's decline / transformation.
- **1875 CE** — Alexander Cunningham publishes first Indus-script inscription (a seal from Harappa).
- **1920s onward** — large-scale excavation by John Marshall, Madho Sarup Vats, Ernest Mackay yields the bulk of the corpus.
- **1931 CE** — Marshall's *Mohenjo-daro and the Indus Civilization* publishes the first major synthesis.
- **1994 CE** — Asko Parpola's *Deciphering the Indus Script* presents the principal modern attempt + the case for treating the inscriptions as linguistic writing.
- **2004 CE** — Farmer, Sproat, and Witzel's *Electronic Journal of Vedic Studies* article presents the principal counter-case: the inscriptions are NOT a linguistic writing system.

## Variants

The three principal attestation contexts (seals, tablets, pottery graffiti) are documented in YAML. Each provides somewhat different evidentiary material but the corpus is best treated as a single body of inscriptions.

## Cross-family appearance

The Indus-script corpus is **regionally bounded** to the Indus Valley Civilization. No documented attestation outside the IVC region; no documented transmission to subsequent Indic writing systems (Brahmi, Kharosthi, etc., which emerge much later from independent Aramaic-derivation).

The relationship to *subsequent Indic religious traditions* is a major open question:

- Mature-Harappan iconography (the seal-animals, the "Pashupati seal" with a horned-figure-flanked-by-animals that has been variously read as proto-Shiva, proto-yogi, or simply an unidentified iconographic motif) is *iconographically intriguing* but **the IVC-to-later-Hindu continuity claims are interpretive and contested**.
- The Dravidian-Indo-Aryan substrate question is part of the larger Indo-Aryan-migration debate; Parpola's Dravidian-substrate position is one scholarly view among several.

## Contested interpretations

This section is THE substance of the entry, per methodology discipline:

- **Is it writing?** The principal scholarly poles:
  - **Pro-writing (Parpola 1994)**: the sign-frequency and combinatorial behavior of the inscriptions are statistically consistent with a writing system encoding a natural language. Parpola further proposes Proto-Dravidian as the underlying language.
  - **Against-writing (Farmer, Sproat, Witzel 2004)**: the short length of inscriptions, the high sign-frequency variance, the absence of any indication of phonetic-encoding signs (no clear case-markers, no clear grammatical morphemes), and the abrupt non-development of the system (no evolution toward longer texts despite 700 years of attestation) are inconsistent with a fully linguistic writing system. They propose the inscriptions are non-linguistic emblems (clan-markers, guild-marks, religious symbols, ownership-marks, etc.).
- **If writing, what language?** Multiple decipherment proposals: Dravidian (Parpola; B. B. Lal; Iravatham Mahadevan), Indo-Aryan (Rajaram-Jha — discredited), Munda (some Indian scholars), Sumerian (early hypotheses, now rejected). **No proposal has achieved scholarly consensus.**
- **What does the iconography depict?** The seal-animals are identifiable (bull, water-buffalo, elephant, rhinoceros, tiger, the unicorn-like horned-bovine). The famous "Pashupati seal" (Mohenjo-daro DK 5175) depicts a horned-figure-seated-cross-legged-flanked-by-animals; identification with proto-Shiva (Marshall 1931's original proposal) is contested. Mainstream view: the iconographic content is partially decipherable; the theological identification with later Hindu deities is interpretive and uncertain.

## Mystery / unknown variants

**The entire script is mystery.** The corpus is well-attested archaeologically; what the inscriptions *mean* (and indeed whether they encode language at all) is not recovered. This is one of the famous undeciphered ancient scripts (alongside Linear A, the Phaistos Disc, Rongorongo, and others). `mystery-status: mystery`.
