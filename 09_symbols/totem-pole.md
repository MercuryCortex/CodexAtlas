# Totem Pole

## Identity

CRITICAL MISCONCEPTION CORRECTED: Totem poles are **not religious idols**. They are **heraldic monuments** — the Northwest Coast equivalent of a European family coat of arms or genealogical record. Early Christian missionaries who described poles as "idols" that Indigenous people "worshipped" were systematically wrong about function. The poles record history, validate rank, announce identity, and commemorate the dead. They are not objects of worship.

The term "totem pole" is itself a colonial simplification. Indigenous names vary: Haida call a pole *xat*; Tlingit call the pole-raising ceremony *koo.éex'* (potlatch). The figures on poles are **crests** — hereditary rights to depict specific beings (eagle, raven, bear, killer whale, wolf, frog, thunderbird) that encode a family's history of supernatural encounters, marriages, and legally validated rights within the clan system.

A totem pole is thus a compressed archive: each stacked figure represents a story — often a story of an ancestor who encountered a supernatural being, received a gift or right from it, and transmitted that encounter as a hereditary crest to descendants. Reading a pole is reading a family's legal and genealogical record, not a religious text.

## Origin and earliest attestation

Totem poles require large cedar (red cedar, *Thuja plicata*) and iron or copper-bladed tools. The historic pole-carving tradition almost certainly predates European contact, but:

- **Pre-contact**: Small carved figures, house posts, and mortuary containers with crest imagery are archaeologically documented. Large free-standing poles, however, require iron tools for efficient carving of the scale and detail associated with historic poles.
- **Post-contact**: European trade goods (iron tools via maritime fur trade, c. 1790s) dramatically expanded pole scale and carving detail. The 19th century (1820–1900) is the "golden age" of Northwest Coast totem pole production — a paradox: colonial contact both threatened and briefly accelerated the pole tradition by providing better carving technology.
- **Period-earliest**: 1700 is conservative — the tradition of erecting carved commemorative poles is older, but the monumental pole as we know it is largely a post-contact development.

## The crest system and its cosmological meaning

Northwest Coast societies are organized by moieties (Haida: Eagle and Raven) and clans. Every clan has a set of **crests** — hereditary rights to depict specific beings. These crests are not decorative choices; they are legal property, inherited through the matrilineal line (Haida, Tlingit) or patrilineal line (Tsimshian), validated publicly in potlatch ceremonies.

Each crest figure on a pole encodes a specific ancestral story. A Haida eagle on a pole does not mean "we like eagles" — it means "our lineage has the validated right to this eagle crest because our ancestor [specific name, specific event, specific witness] encountered Eagle Being in [specific circumstance] and received from Eagle Being the right to use this image as our clan's property."

This is cosmological in the deepest sense: the pole connects the present family to its supernatural origins. The living family is the current expression of a lineage that includes both human and non-human ancestors. The crest figures mark the points where the human and non-human lineages intersected.

## The potlatch context

A totem pole is not a standalone monument — it is the material culmination of a **potlatch** ceremony. The potlatch (from Chinook Jargon *patshatl*, meaning "to give") is the Northwest Coast institution of:
- Public validation of rank, rights, and crests
- Redistribution of wealth (the host gives, not receives)
- Commemoration of the dead
- Resolution of legal disputes (marriage, inheritance, boundary)

The pole is raised publicly, witnessed by invited guests from allied and rival clans. The witnesses validate the claims embedded in the pole's crest figures. To raise a pole without potlatch would be like filing a legal document without a court — the claim would not be recognized.

**The potlatch ban (1885–1951)**: The Canadian government banned potlatches (and all associated ceremonies and material culture) under the 1885 amendment to the Indian Act and subsequent 1895 revision. The legal rationale was that potlatches were "wasteful" (the wealth redistribution conflicted with Protestant capitalism's ethic of accumulation). The real function was cultural suppression: eliminating potlatch destroyed the legal-ceremonial infrastructure that validated Indigenous land rights, social structure, and identity. Confiscated poles, masks, and regalia were transferred to museums (many remain in Canadian and international museum collections). The ban was lifted in 1951.

The totem pole tradition survived through clandestine practice, and underwent a major revival from the 1960s onward with the development of formal apprenticeship programs and museum repatriation efforts.

## MASSIVE WIN: The totem pole as heraldic-cosmological axis mundi

The totem pole synthesizes two functions that are analytically distinct but practically unified:

**Heraldic function**: The pole is a legal record, a coat of arms in wood. It announces who owns this land, what rights they hold, what their genealogy is.

**Cosmological function**: The pole is a vertical axis connecting earth to sky. The sequence of beings — from earth-level creatures (bear, killer whale) through human figures to sky creatures (eagle, thunderbird, moon/sun) — maps the cosmological hierarchy. The house frontal pole's oval entrance (passing through the lowest figure's body to enter the house) makes the household a cosmological threshold: you enter the house through the body of the ancestral being.

This is the totem pole's distinctive achievement: it makes **heraldic genealogy cosmological** and **cosmological order heraldic**. The family's legal title to land is simultaneously their position in the cosmic order. There is no gap between "our family owns this territory" and "our family is positioned in the cosmos through our Eagle and Raven ancestry." The pole encodes both simultaneously.

Compare to the medieval European Gothic cathedral, which similarly encodes legal, historical, theological, and cosmological claims in a single architectural form — but in a vertical building rather than a vertical carved pole.

## Colonial damage and revival

The suppression of potlatch (1885–1951) meant no new poles could be legally raised in Canada. Many existing poles were removed by missionaries, sold to museums, or left to decay (the Northwest Coast tradition does not preserve poles — they are expected to decay and return to the earth; a 100-year-old pole is an old pole). The potlatch ban is a direct assault on the legal-cosmological infrastructure of Northwest Coast societies.

The revival began in the 1960s–1970s and accelerated with the 1986 *Loo Taas* (Wave Eater) canoe project and Bill Reid's collaborations, the development of apprenticeship carving programs at Ksan (1970) and elsewhere, and ongoing repatriation negotiations with holding museums. Today, poles are carved and raised by active carvers in all major Northwest Coast traditions.
