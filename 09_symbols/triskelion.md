# Triskelion

## Identity

The triskelion (Greek τρισκελής, "three-legged") is a three-fold rotational-symmetry emblem appearing in several distinct iconographic forms: the triple-spiral (three interlocking spirals from a central point), the three-leg pinwheel (three bent human or armored legs radiating from a centre), and various derived three-bent-arm or three-curve forms. The earliest dated attestation is the Newgrange entrance-stone (c. -3200), a Neolithic Atlantic European megalithic carving; the form is inherited by Insular Celtic art (1st millennium BCE onward), independently attested in Sicilian Trinacria (4th c. BCE onward), and adopted as the official emblem of the Isle of Man (13th c. onward).

## Earliest attestation

- **c. -3200 BCE** — triple-spiral on the entrance-stone of Newgrange passage tomb, Brú na Bóinne, Ireland. Neolithic Atlantic European megalithic context. (Eogan 1986.)
- **c. -1500 onward** — Bronze Age and Iron Age Atlantic European continued use; the form recurs on metalwork and stone carving.
- **La Tène period (c. -500 to -100)** — canonical motif in Celtic continental and Insular art. (Aldhouse-Green 1986/1989.)
- **Sicilian coinage (4th c. BCE onward)** — Trinacria emblem on Sicilian Greek city-state coinage (Akragas, Syracuse, Panormos). Central Medusa-head form crystallizes in the Roman period.
- **Insular Christian art (5th–10th c. CE)** — Book of Kells, Book of Durrow, and other Insular manuscripts use the triskelion as canonical Christian-Celtic decorative motif.
- **Isle of Man (13th c. onward)** — three armored legs adopted as Manx emblem; documented on Manx coinage from c. 1290 and on the Manx flag and coat-of-arms continuously thereafter.

## Meaning(s) attested

- **Neolithic Atlantic European** — meaning irrecoverable; the form is canonical megalithic-art vocabulary, possibly cosmological / solar / cyclic but no textual record exists. Recent astronomical-alignment work (the Newgrange winter-solstice illumination) supports a solar / cyclic reading without proving it.
- **Celtic** — three-fold rotational forms read variously as land/sea/sky, past/present/future, or three-fold expressions of deity (Lugus had three faces in some attestations); the iconographic meaning is partly recoverable through context. (Aldhouse-Green 1986.)
- **Sicilian Trinacria** — the three promontories (capes) of triangular Sicily; later read as three legs running in eternal motion.
- **Manx** — official heraldic motto *Quocunque jeceris stabit* ("whichever way you throw it, it will stand") — stability through rotational balance.
- **Insular Christian** — Trinitarian reading is medieval Christian retrofit on pre-Christian Celtic motif; not original to the form.

## Variants

The five attested variants (Newgrange triple-spiral, Celtic La Tène, Sicilian Trinacria, Manx three-legs, and the cognate Buddhist trishula) are documented in YAML. The Buddhist trishula entry is `visual-cognate` only — NOT a documented transmission.

## Cross-family appearance

The triskelion has TWO documented transmission lineages and one independent parallel:

- **Newgrange Neolithic → Celtic La Tène → Insular Christian art** — `ancestor-of` chain at the level of iconographic inheritance: the Atlantic European megalithic vocabulary persists into Iron Age Celtic art and then into Insular Christian decoration. Eogan 1986 + Aldhouse-Green 1986/1989 document this.
- **Sicilian Trinacria → Isle of Man** — possible Norman-Sicilian transmission in the 13th c. (the Hauteville Norman kingdom of Sicily had contacts with the Isle of Man's Norse-Gaelic rulers); also possibly independent. Not fully settled in scholarship.
- **Buddhist trishula** — `visual-cognate`. NOT a documented transmission; the trident's three-prong form is iconographically cognate with three-fold-rotational symbols globally, but no Indic-to-Atlantic-European transmission is documented.

## Contested interpretations

- Whether the Sicilian Trinacria's central Medusa-head is a Greek-archaic apotropaic addition to a pre-Greek (Sicel? Phoenician?) three-leg motif. The Greek-archaic-overlay reading is the more documented; pre-Greek substrate is suggestive but underdetermined.
- Whether the Manx three-legs is Sicilian-transmitted or independently developed. Both views are current; no decisive evidence either way.
- Whether the Newgrange triple-spiral has any documented continuity with the later Celtic La Tène triskelion or is iconographically rediscovered. The "iconographic inheritance" reading is the scholarly mainstream (Eogan 1986).

## Mystery / unknown variants

The Newgrange entrance-stone triple-spiral's specific theological meaning is irrecoverable in the absence of Neolithic textual record. `mystery-status: documented` overall but with caveat on Neolithic interpretation.
