# Peyote

## Identity
Peyote (*Lophophora williamsii*; Náhuatl: *peyōtl*; Wixáritari: *hikuri*) is a small spineless cactus native to the Chihuahuan Desert of northern Mexico and the Rio Grande border region, containing **mescaline** and some 50 other alkaloids. It is the oldest directly dated entheogenic substance in the archaeological record of the Americas: carbon-dated peyote buttons from the Shumla Caves, Texas — excavated in archaeological context — have been radiocarbon-dated to **c. 3700 BCE** (El-Seedi et al. 2005 — Tier 1). Indigenous ceremonial use thus spans at least **5,700 years**, making peyote one of the longest-documented sacramental substances on Earth, comparable in antiquity to Vedic Soma and the Eleusinian kykeon.

For the Codex Atlas mission, peyote is load-bearing in two ways: (1) it is the **primary non-Old-World node** in the cross-continental "divine teacher plant" entheogen category, demonstrating that independent traditions on every inhabited continent have converged on a plant sacrament that mediates contact with divine reality; (2) the **Native American Church** is one of the most significant living examples of Christian-indigenous syncretic fusion in the modern world — a tradition combining peyote ceremony with Christian prayer, serving ~250,000+ members across the United States.

## Earliest attestation
- **Shumla Caves, Texas** (c. 3700 BCE): nine peyote buttons directly carbon-dated; the most ancient confirmed entheogenic use in the Americas (El-Seedi et al. 2005).
- **Tepic area rock art** (c. 2000–200 BCE): stylized Lophophora-form figures in Nayarit and Jalisco rock-art contexts are proposed as peyote representations; archaeological consensus is cautious but supportive.
- **Aztec-Mexica sources** (early colonial period): Sahagún's *Florentine Codex* (1569) documents peyote (*peyōtl*) use among Chichimec people of the north; Aztec-Mexica used it medicinally and ceremonially.
- **Colonial period records** (1591 onward): the Spanish Inquisition formally condemned peyote use in 1620 as an act of "superstition and idolatry," documenting the scale of use by that point.
- **Wixáritari (Huichol) continuous tradition**: the Jalisco Huichol maintain the most continuously documented and least disrupted peyote ceremonial tradition; their practices are the primary ethnographic record (Myerhoff 1974).

## Meaning(s) attested

### The Wixáritari (Huichol) triple identification
The most theologically sophisticated peyote doctrine is Wixáritari: **peyote (hikuri) = the divine deer (Kauyumari) = the primordial sun (Tayau) = the creative fire (Tatewari)**. These are not metaphors but direct identities: when the shaman (*mara'akame*) finds the peyote button in the Wirikuta desert, he is simultaneously hunting the divine deer, greeting the sun, and touching the original creative fire of the cosmos. The three identities collapse into one moment of contact with the divine origin.

### The annual pilgrimage (hikuri neixa)
The Wixáritari pilgrimage to Wirikuta (the sacred peyote desert in San Luis Potosí, ~300 km on foot from their Jalisco homeland) is **a re-enactment of the First Pilgrimage to Creation**. During the journey, the pilgrims *become* the divine ancestors: they take new names, maintain sexual and dietary abstinence, and experience the journey as the gods' original journey to create the world. Arriving at Wirikuta, the first sight of peyote is greeted with tears. The peyote button collected is **the body of the divine deer** — the pilgrims' chief ritual act is to "hunt" and "kill" the deer with an arrow, which simultaneously is collecting and consuming the peyote cactus.

### The NAC sacramental context
In the Native American Church (formally incorporated 1918; developed c. 1880s among southern Plains peoples), peyote occupies the sacramental center of the **all-night prayer meeting**: participants sit in a tipi around a central altar and sacred fire, singing, praying, and consuming peyote buttons throughout the night until dawn. The ceremony combines Christian elements (prayer to Jesus, readings from Christian scripture) with indigenous ones (cedar, sage, sacred songs, the "Peyote Way" ethical code). The NAC is the largest pan-tribal religious organization in North America, crossing ~70+ tribal affiliations.

## The neurochemical substrate
Mescaline's primary mechanism is **agonism at 5-HT2A serotonin receptors** — the same receptor mechanism as psilocybin (from psilocybe mushrooms) and LSD. This neurobiological convergence is significant: the "divine teacher plant" category across traditions (peyote, ayahuasca, kykeon, soma/haoma) — despite being chemically diverse — consistently targets serotonergic pathways implicated in altered states of consciousness, perception, and self-referential processing. The scientific documentation of a shared neurobiological substrate does not reduce the spiritual significance of these traditions; it underscores that human nervous systems respond consistently to certain chemical invitations regardless of cultural context.

## Cross-family appearance

### Indigenous Americas
Peyote ceremonialism spans approximately 40 distinct North American indigenous traditions, including Huichol, Navajo, Lakota, Kiowa, Comanche, Mescalero Apache, Crow, and Ojibwe peoples, with distinct ceremonial forms adapted to each nation's existing religious complex.

### The NAC syncretic fusion
The Native American Church represents one of the most significant living examples of Christian-indigenous syncretic religious creation. It arose in the late 19th century under enormous pressure — the simultaneous destruction of traditional indigenous economies, forced removal, and Christian missionary activity — and synthesized the two religious worlds rather than surrendering to either. The peyote *is* the sacrament in exactly the structural role that wine plays in Christian Eucharist: the ritual-plant-substance consumed in a sacred meal that mediates divine communion.

## Cross-tradition "divine teacher plant" pattern
Peyote is the Americas node in a cross-continental pattern that the vault documents across four primary traditions:
- **Peyote** (*Lophophora williamsii* / mescaline) — North America (→ this node)
- Ayahuasca (*Banisteriopsis caapi* + DMT-plants) — Amazonia (→ ayahuasca node)
- [[haoma-zoroastrian]] (Vedic Soma / Avestan Haoma / *Ephedra* or *Peganum harmala*) — Indo-Iranian
- Eleusinian kykeon (barley + ergot alkaloids, hypothetically) — Greek (→ eleusinian-kykeon node)

No common origin is documented or claimed. The convergence is genuine — independent traditions on every inhabited continent discovering that a plant-substance can function as the medium of contact with divine reality. The consistency of the phenomenology (vision, death-and-rebirth, encounter with divine beings, loss of fear of death, moral transformation) across these chemically and culturally unrelated traditions is one of the most significant unresolved questions in comparative religion.

## Contested interpretations
- **"Is peyote a drug or a sacrament?"** is the political-legal dispute that shaped much of NAC history. In *Employment Division v. Smith* (1990), the US Supreme Court initially held that Oregon could criminalize peyote even in religious use; Congress reversed this in the American Indian Religious Freedom Act Amendments (1994), exempting NAC sacramental use. The legal-political debate does not affect the religious-historical analysis.
- **Psychedelic reduction**: some scholars and practitioners resist the category "entheogen" as imposing a Western psychopharmacological framing on what are religious-cultural practices. The Codex Atlas uses "entheogen" as a descriptive category of religious function (plant-substance mediating the divine) without implying that the experience is "merely pharmacological."
