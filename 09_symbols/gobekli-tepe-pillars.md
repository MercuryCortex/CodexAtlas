# Göbekli Tepe Pillar Carvings

## Identity

The **Göbekli Tepe pillar carvings** are the relief symbols and figures cut into the T-shaped limestone pillars and enclosure walls of **Göbekli Tepe** (*Güneydoğu Toros*, southeastern Turkey), the oldest known monumental religious/ceremonial site in the world. Excavated from 1994 by Klaus Schmidt of the German Archaeological Institute (DAI), the site dates to approximately **9600–8200 BCE** — making it approximately **6,000 years older than Stonehenge** and predating the development of agriculture by approximately 1,000–2,000 years.

The carvings are the **earliest monumental religious iconography** in the archaeological record. Their meaning is entirely unknown.

## Earliest attestation

- **Site construction begins c. 9600 BCE**: Enclosure D (Layer III) is among the earliest, with the largest pillars (up to 5.5 m tall, 15 tons). Carbon dating of organic material within the fill dates Layer III to c. 9600–8800 BCE.
- **Site use continues c. 8800–8200 BCE**: Enclosure A–C (Layer II) contains smaller pillars and more varied forms.
- **Deliberate burial c. 8000 BCE**: The site was intentionally backfilled — packed with debris, animal bones, and worked flint — burying all structures. The reasons for the burial are unknown.
- **Rediscovery**: Surveyed in 1963 by Peter Benedict (University of Chicago); recognized as a major prehistoric site in 1994 by Klaus Schmidt.

## What IS known

The following is archaeologically documented without serious dispute:

**Architecture:**
- The site consists of **at least 20 circular and oval enclosures**, of which only 5–6 have been excavated. Each enclosure has **two tall central T-pillars** and a ring of smaller T-pillars set into stone walls.
- Enclosure D (the largest excavated) has central pillars over **5.5 meters tall** weighing approximately **15–20 tons** each. The stone was quarried from the adjacent limestone plateau — unfinished pillars remain in the quarry.
- Construction required **organized communal labor of hundreds of individuals** across sustained periods — predating agriculture as a reliable surplus base by over 1,000 years. This is the most significant structural fact about Göbekli Tepe: **monumental religion preceded settled agriculture**, inverting the long-standing assumption that religion follows civilization.

**Carvings:**
- The T-pillars carry **low-relief carvings**: animals (foxes, snakes, vultures, cranes, aurochs, boar, scorpions, spiders, ducks, big cats); geometric signs (notably the recurring H-symbol); and — on some pillars — carved **human arms and hands** descending along the pillar sides, implying the T-shape represents a stylized human body.
- The **H-symbol** appears on multiple pillars across different enclosures and possibly across centuries of use. Its consistency suggests it is **meaningful** — a sign, not mere decoration. Its meaning is unknown.
- Pillar 43 (Enclosure D) carries a scene including a **vulture with a ball/disc on its wing, a scorpion, and a headless human figure**. This is the most debated single carving at the site.

**Subsistence evidence:**
- Large quantities of **animal bones** (gazelle, aurochs, wild boar, crane) indicate communal feasting — these are wild species, consistent with hunting-gathering. There is **no evidence of agriculture** in the earliest phases.
- **Large stone vessels** found at the site may have held fermented beverages (Dietrich et al. 2012).

**No habitation evidence:**
- There are **no hearths, no sleeping or storage areas, no permanent domestic structures** associated with the earliest layers. Göbekli Tepe appears to be a **ceremonial/pilgrimage site**, not a settlement.

## What is NOT known — the genuine mysteries

1. **Who built it.** The builders were Pre-Pottery Neolithic A (PPNA) hunter-gatherers. No written language; no named individuals; no surviving oral tradition. The cultural identity of the builders cannot be established.

2. **What the carvings mean.** The animal carvings may represent: totemic clan ancestors; dangerous cosmic forces requiring propitiation; calendar animals marking seasonal events; spirit-world entities; prey species honored in the hunt; deities or proto-deities. **None of these hypotheses is supported by evidence beyond inference from comparative anthropology.**

3. **What the H-symbol means.** The recurring H-form on multiple pillars is the most tantalizing mystery at the site. It appears with enough consistency to be a sign — but whether it marks an enclosure, a clan, a concept, a deity, or something else is entirely unknown.

4. **What the Pillar 43 scene depicts.** The vulture-scorpion-headless-human carving (sometimes called the "Vulture Stone") is the most-discussed single image. The sky-burial hypothesis is plausible but unverifiable. The astronomical-event hypothesis (Sweatman & Tsikritsis 2017, *Mediterranean Archaeology and Archaeometry* — Tier 3) is contested by most Göbekli Tepe specialists.

5. **What happened in the enclosures.** Feasting is documented archaeologically. But **what the ritual involved** — initiation? ancestor veneration? astronomical observation? shamanistic practice? seasonal ceremonial? — is not known.

6. **Why the site was buried.** The deliberate, large-scale burial of all enclosures c. 8000 BCE is one of the most discussed mysteries in Neolithic archaeology. Hypotheses include: ritual closure/decommissioning; protection from desecration; practical reuse of the site for agriculture; deliberate "sealing" of sacred power. None is confirmed.

## Cross-family appearance

Göbekli Tepe predates all other known religious traditions by millennia. It cannot be said to "appear" in other traditions. What can be documented is that specific **symbolic patterns** at Göbekli Tepe recur independently in later, geographically and temporally separate traditions:

- **Sky burial / excarnation by vultures**: The Pillar 43 vulture-headless-human scene, if correctly interpreted as sky burial, would be the **oldest documented sky-burial imagery in the world**. Sky burial as a funerary practice is independently attested in:
  - Pre-Pottery Neolithic Çatalhöyük (c. 7500 BCE — wall paintings of vultures with headless humans)
  - Zoroastrian *dakhma* (Tower of Silence) practice: the corpse exposed to vultures as the ritually correct form of disposal, to avoid polluting fire, earth, and water
  - Tibetan *jhator* (sky burial): the body offered to vultures as the highest form of generosity, releasing the soul
  - None of these are connected historically to Göbekli Tepe.

- **The T-pillar as axis mundi candidate**: The vertical T-pillar within a circular sacred enclosure is formally analogous to later axis-mundi symbolism (sacred center pole, world tree, cosmic pillar) found independently across most major civilizations. Göbekli Tepe predates all.

## Contested interpretations

- **The "religion caused civilization" thesis**: Schmidt's conclusion — that monumental religious construction preceded and may have stimulated the development of agriculture — remains the leading interpretation of Göbekli Tepe's significance. It is contested by scholars who argue agriculture and complex society may have been simultaneously co-developing in the region (Hodder 2006, Cauvin 2000).
- **The astronomical-event hypothesis**: Sweatman & Tsikritsis (2017) proposed that Pillar 43 encodes a specific astronomical date (~10,950 BCE, a proposed Younger Dryas Impact event). This is Tier 3; most Göbekli Tepe specialists regard it as premature given the state of evidence.
- **The "temple" designation**: Schmidt called Göbekli Tepe a "temple" — this is the most widely used term but is technically an interpretation, not an archaeological fact. It is a ceremonial/monumental site; whether it functioned as a temple in the sense of a deity-house is unknown.

## Mystery / unknown variants

Every carved symbol at Göbekli Tepe is, in the strict sense, undeciphered. The site is documented without claims about meaning. The H-symbol and the Pillar 43 scene are the two most discussed unsolved problems within the site's iconographic record.
