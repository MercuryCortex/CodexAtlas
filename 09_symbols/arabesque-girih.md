# Arabesque and Girih (Islamic Sacred Geometry)

## Identity

The **arabesque** (*tawrīq* — "leafing") and ***girih*** (Persian: گره — "knot") are the two interlocking pattern-systems of **Islamic sacred geometry** — together constituting the most sophisticated tradition of decorative mathematics in world history. The arabesque is the continuous vegetal scroll: interlaced stems, tendrils, and stylized leaf-and-flower forms that grow in every direction without terminus. The *girih* is the geometric star-polygon tile system: five canonical tiles (decagon, pentagon, elongated hexagon, bowtie, rhombus) that combine to cover a plane in complex non-repeating patterns. Both systems share the same theological logic: in Islamic doctrine, figural images of living beings are avoided in sacred spaces (*aniconism*), so **the infinite geometric pattern IS the visual language of divine infinity**. The pattern that has no beginning and no end, that can be extended in any direction without interruption, is a visual theology of *Allah's infinitude* — *al-Bāqī* (the Everlasting), *al-Wāsiʿ* (the All-Encompassing).

## Earliest attestation

- **c. 836 CE** — Earliest documented geometric tile patterns appear in **Abbasid-period Islamic architecture** (Samarra, Iraq). The geometric system as distinct from Hellenistic precursors is attested here.
- **c. 1000–1100 CE** — The **Seljuk period** in Persia and Anatolia sees the rapid elaboration of the girih tile system to cover mosque facades, mihrabs, and Quran illumination.
- **c. 1100–1200 CE** — The **al-Andalus tradition** (Spain): the Alhambra palace in Granada (Nasrid, 13th–14th c.) produces the most famous surviving arabesque + girih + muqarnas ensemble.
- **1453 CE** — The **Darb-i Imam shrine in Isfahan, Iran**: the prime example of quasi-periodic girih tiling, documented by Lu & Steinhardt 2007 as Penrose-equivalent.

## Meaning(s) attested

- **Visual theology of divine infinity** (documented in Islamic aesthetic theory, Nasr 1987, Burckhardt 1976): the pattern that extends without limit in every direction IS a visual statement about God's infinity. In sacred spaces where figural imagery is avoided, geometric infinity replaces the image as the vehicle of theophany.
- **The paradise garden** (*janna*): the arabesque vegetal scroll is explicitly associated with the Quranic paradise garden. The word *arabesque* ultimately derives from *Arabicus* (Latin) applied to the garden-scroll; the Islamic tradition self-consciously renders paradise as infinite vegetal geometry.
- **Sufi theology of rotation and return**: Rumi's Mevlevi *samāʿ* (the whirling ceremony) produces spiraling, rotating geometric forms in space — **the human body drawing the arabesque in air**. The bodily practice and the visual art are two expressions of the same Sufi theology: the soul's rotation around the divine center (*qutb*), the return to origin through geometric movement.
- **Mathematical cosmology**: in the tradition of the **Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ** (Brethren of Purity, c. 983 CE Basra), Islamic thinkers explicitly linked geometric forms to Neoplatonic emanation cosmology — the geometric pattern unfolds from the point (divine unity) through line, polygon, and tiling, encoding the same hierarchy as the Neoplatonic *henads*.

## Variants

### Arabesque / *tawrīq* (vegetal scroll)
The continuous interlaced stem-and-leaf form, derived from the Hellenistic acanthus scroll but progressively geometrized and infinitized. Found on Quran illuminations, woodwork, tilework, and textiles. The arabesque is the **garden of paradise made visible**: the word *janna* in Arabic means both "garden" and "paradise," and the arabesque scroll renders this verdant infinity on every surface.

### *Girih* tile system (geometric interlace)
The five canonical girih tiles — decagon, pentagon, elongated hexagon, bowtie, rhombus — tile the plane in complex, non-repeating star-polygon patterns. The mathematical structure, as demonstrated by Lu & Steinhardt (2007), is **quasi-periodic**: the pattern is self-similar at multiple scales (the same structural logic appears when you zoom in or out), has five-fold rotational symmetry, but is **not periodically repeating**. This is the mathematical structure of Penrose tiling (1974) and quasicrystals (Nobel Prize in Chemistry, 2011).

### *Muqarnas* (stalactite vaulting)
Three-dimensional projection of girih geometry: the honeycomb-cell stalactite vault that generates the visual experience of **infinite celestial depth** from a finite ceiling surface. The Alhambra's Hall of the Two Sisters is the defining example. The muqarnas ceiling IS the Islamic cosmos: a finite room opens into infinite mathematical sky.

## Cross-family appearance

**Cross-tradition finding: Penrose Tiling and Quasicrystals.** The discovery published by Peter Lu and Paul Steinhardt in *Science* (2007) — one of the most important papers in the history of mathematics and art history — demonstrated that the girih tiles at the Darb-i Imam shrine in Isfahan (1453 CE) are a **near-perfect quasi-periodic Penrose-equivalent tiling**. Roger Penrose "invented" Penrose tiling in 1974. **Medieval Islamic artists working 500 years earlier discovered the same mathematical structure through their sacred geometry practice.** Quasicrystals — materials whose atomic structure is quasi-periodic, non-repeating, and has five-fold symmetry — won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2011 for Dan Shechtman. The chain is: **medieval Islamic sacred geometry (1453 CE) → Penrose tiling (1974) → Nobel Prize physics (2011)** — one mathematical discovery, three historical contexts. The Islamic geometric artists were doing mathematics their contemporaries in Europe had not imagined.

**Islamic geometry and the Flower of Life**: The Islamic girih tile system independently generates hexagonal circle-flower patterns mathematically equivalent to the Flower of Life (see [[flower-of-life]]) as a natural consequence of 6-fold and 12-fold rotational grid logic. Islamic geometry and the global "sacred geometry" tradition (Critchlow 1976, Lawlor 1982) are different cultural framings of the same underlying mathematical structures.

**Celtic and Norse knotwork parallel**: The Islamic arabesque interlaced knotwork, Celtic knotwork ([[triquetra]], [[endless-knot]]), and the Buddhist endless knot are independent traditions generating continuous, non-terminating geometric interlace. The mathematical structure is shared across three cultural zones with no documented transmission. The convergence is a powerful case study in **convergent invention** driven by a shared aesthetic-theological motivation: the desire to represent infinity through pattern.

**Sufi geometry and Hindu yantra**: The Sri Yantra ([[sri-yantra]]) and the Islamic girih system are both sacred geometric systems where a complex geometric form is understood as a visual encoding of divine infinity and as a vehicle for contemplative practice. In both traditions, the geometric form IS the theology — not an illustration of it. No transmission; profound parallel.

## Contested interpretations

- **Intentionality of the quasi-periodic structure**: Lu & Steinhardt (2007) showed the quasi-periodic structure exists. It remains debated whether the Islamic tile-setters **consciously intended** the quasi-periodic property or arrived at it through a practical tiling algorithm that happened to generate quasi-periodicity without awareness of its mathematical significance. The more likely answer is the latter — but the mathematical achievement is no less remarkable.
- **Absolute aniconism**: The rule against figural imagery in sacred contexts is not absolute or universal across Islamic traditions. It applies most strictly to mosque interiors and Quran illumination; figural imagery is common in Persian manuscript painting (e.g., the Shahnama tradition), court contexts, and Shia iconography of Imams. The arabesque tradition is the product of the stricter interpretation; it is not universal Islam.
- **Arabesque derivation from Hellenistic acanthus**: The arabesque is clearly derived from Hellenistic and Byzantine vegetal scroll traditions via the early Islamic reception of Roman decorative vocabulary. The Islamic tradition transformed this inheritance, but did not generate it *ex nihilo*.
