# Medicine Wheel

## Identity

The **Medicine Wheel** (Lakota: *changleshka wakan* — Sacred Wheel; also called the **Sacred Hoop**) is **a circular arrangement of stones with spokes radiating from a central cairn**, used by many Plains and other North American peoples for ceremonial, astronomical, and cosmological purposes. As both a **physical monument** (more than 150 stone medicine wheels survive on the northern Great Plains of the United States and Canada) and a **symbolic teaching diagram** (the four-directional cosmic map used in ceremony and cosmological instruction), the medicine wheel is one of the most complex and cross-traditionally resonant indigenous symbols of North America.

The physical medicine wheels on the high plains of Wyoming, Montana, Alberta, and Saskatchewan align with astronomical phenomena — the heliacal risings of specific stars marking the seasons — making them functioning **horizon calendars in stone**, comparable in function (though not in scale) to Stonehenge. The symbolic medicine wheel divides existence into four quadrants (the Four Directions) with specific colors, elements, seasons, animal spirits, human life-stages, and cosmic principles assigned to each quadrant — making it a **total cosmological map** of existence encoded in the simplest possible geometric form: a circle with four spokes.

## Earliest attestation

- **c. -3000 BCE** — **The Majorville Cairn** (Alberta, Canada) is dated by radiocarbon analysis to approximately -3000 BCE, making it one of the oldest stone circle monuments in North America. It predates most of the European Bronze Age megalithic circles and is contemporaneous with the earliest phase of Stonehenge.
- **c. 1200–1700 CE** — **The Bighorn Medicine Wheel** (Wyoming, Bighorn Mountains, 2,800 m elevation) is the best-studied medicine wheel. Its spoke-and-cairn alignments document astronomical knowledge accumulating over centuries of highland use.
- **1932** — *Black Elk Speaks* (John Neihardt): the most important written record of the medicine wheel's theological meaning in English.

## Meaning(s) attested

### The Four Directions

The foundational symbolic act of the medicine wheel is the **quartering of the circle by the four cardinal directions**, with each direction assigned specific attributes:

In Lakota teaching (one formulation among many — assignments vary significantly by nation and teacher):
- **East (Yellow)** — Spring, birth, new beginnings, the rising sun, the eagle
- **South (Red)** — Summer, youth, growth, the coyote or mouse
- **West (Black)** — Autumn, adulthood, the harvest, thunder beings, the bear
- **North (White)** — Winter, elderhood, wisdom, the buffalo, the time of endings-and-return

The four directions are not merely compass points but **total ontological categories** — each quadrant contains a season, a stage of human life, an element, an animal spirit, a color, a time of day, and a cosmic principle. The medicine wheel encodes the totality of existence in a circle divided four ways.

### The Sacred Hoop

Black Elk's formulation in *Black Elk Speaks* (Neihardt 1932) is the most influential theological statement:

> "You have noticed that everything an Indian does is in a circle, and that is because the Power of the World always works in circles, and everything tries to be round. In the old days when we were a strong and happy people, all our power came to us from the sacred hoop of the nation, and so long as the hoop was unbroken, the people flourished."

The Sacred Hoop is the community conceived as a circle — unbroken = healthy; broken = suffering. The medicine wheel's circle *is* the community's life. The trauma of colonization is the *breaking* of the Sacred Hoop. The ceremonial work of the medicine wheel is the *restoration* of the circle.

### Astronomical function

The physical medicine wheels function as **horizon calendars** — observing the heliacal risings (first dawn-risings after a period of invisibility) of specific stars at specific points on the horizon:

- **Aldebaran** (Taurus) — its heliacal rising at the Bighorn latitude marks late spring (June); the astronomical beginning of the ceremonial year
- **Rigel** (Orion) — summer
- **Fomalhaut** (Piscis Austrinus) — autumn

Astronomer John Eddy (1974) demonstrated that the Bighorn wheel's spokes and outer cairns align specifically with these three stars' heliacal risings at the wheel's latitude and elevation, within the margin of error for precessional drift over the wheel's construction period. The medicine wheel is thus a **stone astronomical instrument** built at altitude for clear-horizon observation — analogous in function to Stonehenge (which also aligns with solstice phenomena) though built by a different technology and tradition.

## Cross-family appearance

### The medicine wheel as mandala — MASSIVE WIN

The medicine wheel and the Buddhist/Hindu **mandala** are structurally identical and functionally analogous:

| Medicine Wheel | Buddhist Mandala |
|---|---|
| Circle divided into four quadrants | Circle divided into four quadrants |
| Central cairn = sacred center | Central deity palace = sacred center |
| Four directions = cosmic principles | Four Dhyani Buddhas at four gates |
| Specific colors for each direction | Specific colors for each directional region |
| Used in ceremony and cosmological teaching | Used in meditation and ritual consecration |
| Encodes totality of existence | Encodes totality of existence |

**Independent origins**: Plains Indian traditions and Tibetan Buddhist traditions developed these forms without contact. This is one of the most striking **parallel-form convergences** in comparative religion — two independent traditions producing structurally identical cosmological diagrams performing identical functions. See [[mandala-circle]].

### The four-directions circle across traditions

The quartering of the circle by the four cardinal directions is one of the most universal symbolic gestures in world religion:

- **Native American Medicine Wheel** (North/South/East/West = Black/White/Yellow/Red in Lakota teaching)
- **Buddhist mandala** (four Dhyani Buddhas at the four directional gates)
- **Hindu mandala and temple plan** (four directional deities, *dikpalas*)
- **Christian cathedral orientation** (nave faces East, toward the rising sun and Jerusalem; four Evangelists at the four corners of the cosmic vision — Matthew/Mark/Luke/John = Tetramorph)
- **Aztec cosmogram** (five world-directions: four cardinal points plus Center = the five *solar ages*)
- **Egyptian temple orientation** (aligned to cardinal directions, with each direction carrying specific divine associations)
- **Mesoamerican sacred geography** (Mayan four *Bacabs* at four world-corners; four-color four-direction system in the Dresden Codex (Mayan))

The four-direction circle is likely **independently generated in every culture that observes the sun's movement** — East and West are given by sunrise and sunset; North and South by the midday sun's position in the northern hemisphere. The four-direction circle is the natural human response to astronomical observation.

### The stone circle as astronomical instrument

The Bighorn Medicine Wheel aligns with the stone circle tradition of astronomical function:
- **Stonehenge** (England, c. -3000 to -1500 BCE) — aligns with solstice sunrises and sunsets
- **Majorville Cairn** (Alberta, c. -3000 BCE) — contemporaneous with early Stonehenge; independent North American astronomical circle
- **Nabta Playa** (Egypt, c. -5000 BCE) — stone circle aligning with summer solstice sunrise in the Sahara
- **Caral** (Peru, c. -3000 BCE) — astronomical alignments in Andean ceremonial circles

Stone circles as astronomical instruments are a global phenomenon — independent convergent solutions to the problem of marking the seasons in a pre-literate astronomical tradition.

## Contested interpretations

- **Authenticity of specific meaning-assignments**: The four-direction color-season-animal assignments taught in popular "medicine wheel" contexts often blend multiple indigenous traditions, sometimes inaccurately. The Lakota, Ojibwe, Blackfoot, and Cheyenne each have distinct four-direction systems. Scholars including [[tradition-ojibwe]]-affiliated teachers have criticized the popularization of a generic "pan-Indian" medicine wheel that flattens real differences.
- **Colonial contamination of sources**: *Black Elk Speaks* was translated by John Neihardt and has been criticized by some scholars as reflecting Neihardt's own Catholic-influenced theological framing as much as Black Elk's original teaching. Raymond DeMallie's scholarly edition (1985) attempts to reconstruct the original transcripts.
- **Attribution of astronomical purpose**: Eddy's 1974 paper is widely accepted but some archaeoastronomers have questioned whether the Bighorn alignments are statistically robust given the small number of data points.
