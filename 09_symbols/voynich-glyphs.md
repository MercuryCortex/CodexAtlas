# Voynich Manuscript Script

## Identity

The **Voynich Manuscript** (Yale Beinecke Rare Book and Manuscript Library, MS 408) is an illustrated codex written in an entirely unknown script — commonly called "Voynichese" — on vellum carbon-dated to **1404–1438 CE** (early 15th century, probably Italian or Central European production). The manuscript is named after **Wilfrid Voynich**, a Polish book dealer who purchased it in 1912 from the Jesuit Villa Mondragone at Frascati, near Rome.

The script consists of approximately **25–30 distinct letter-forms** arranged into words, sentences, and paragraphs across approximately **240 surviving pages**. It has been subjected to over 100 years of attempted decipherment by cryptographers, linguists, medievalists, computational linguists, and amateurs. **No decipherment has been accepted by the scholarly community.**

The Voynich Manuscript is not a blank object; it has content — botanical illustrations, cosmological diagrams, human figures, pharmaceutical drawings, astronomical charts — which situate it clearly within the early 15th-century European manuscript tradition. But what that content *means*, what language or cipher underlies the script, and who produced it remain completely unknown.

## Earliest attestation

- **Vellum carbon-dating**: The vellum (animal-skin parchment) dates to **1404–1438 CE** with 95% confidence (Hodgins/McCrone 2011). The ink has not been independently dated. This establishes the *latest possible* date of the manuscript's material substrate; the text could have been written at any point after the vellum was prepared.
- **First documented owner**: Holy Roman Emperor **Rudolf II** (r. 1576–1612), who reportedly paid 600 gold ducats for the manuscript — equivalent to approximately 2 kg of gold, an extraordinary sum, suggesting it was believed to be of extraordinary significance. The purchase is recorded in correspondence associated with the court.
- **Athanasius Kircher**: The Jesuit polymath Athanasius Kircher (1601/2–1680), perhaps the most learned man of the 17th century, received the manuscript for study. He failed to decode it. The manuscript then passed through Jesuit hands until Voynich's 1912 purchase.
- **Current location**: Beinecke Rare Book and Manuscript Library, Yale University (acquired 1969).

## What IS known

**Physical description:**
- The manuscript comprises approximately **240 vellum pages** (some pages appear to have been removed; original pagination uncertain). Several pages are folded out, forming large multi-page spreads.
- The text is written **left to right**, with clear word separations and consistent word-length distribution.
- Approximately **25–30 distinct letter-forms** have been catalogued (the exact count depends on how variant forms are classified). The total word count is approximately **35,000–37,000 words** with a vocabulary of approximately **8,000 distinct word-forms**.
- The text has **consistent statistical properties**: letter frequency distribution, word-length distribution, and positional letter constraints are all consistent with a natural language — they are NOT the properties of random or pseudo-random text. This is one of the most important facts about the manuscript.

**Sectional structure** (see Variants):
- Botanical (largest section) / Astronomical-cosmological / Biological / Pharmaceutical / Stars.
- The sections are consistent with the structure of early 15th-century European encyclopedic or medical manuscripts (*compendium* or *herbarium*-type).

**Provenance chain:**
- [1404–1438 CE]: Vellum produced (carbon dating).
- [before 1586]: Owner unknown.
- [c. 1586–1612]: Owned by Rudolf II; believed to have been purchased for 600 gold ducats.
- [c. 1612–1666]: Owned by Jakub Hořčický of Tepenec (Rudolf II's physician/botanist), then Georg Baresch (Prague alchemist), then Joannes Marcus Marci (rector, Charles University Prague), who sent it to Athanasius Kircher in Rome in 1666 with a covering letter (the Marci letter survives and is a key primary source).
- [1666–1912]: In Jesuit possession, at the Jesuit Collegium Romanum (later Villa Mondragone, Frascati).
- [1912]: Purchased by Wilfrid Voynich.
- [1969]: Donated to Beinecke Library, Yale.

**Zodiac labels**: A zodiac section includes labels (in a different hand, possibly in abbreviated Latin or a related script) for the 12 zodiac signs — the only portion of the manuscript where any connection to a known language has been tentatively proposed.

## What is NOT known — the genuine mysteries

1. **Whether the script encodes a real language, a cipher, or is a hoax.** The three leading possibilities are:
   - **Natural language in cipher**: The most common scholarly hypothesis. The statistical properties suggest an underlying language, but no cipher has been identified.
   - **Constructed/artificial language**: Possible. Some 15th-century scholars (notably Johannes Trithemius, in his *Steganographia* and *Polygraphia*) were developing polyglot cipher systems. An artificial language with internal grammar could produce Voynichese's statistics.
   - **Elaborate hoax**: Rugg (2004) demonstrated that a Cardan grille (a grid with slots) used with a table of syllables could generate text with Voynichese's statistical properties in a very short time. If so, the manuscript could be a sophisticated forgery designed to deceive a wealthy buyer (such as Rudolf II). The hoax hypothesis does not require the text to have meaning.

2. **The identity of the author.** The Roger Bacon attribution (Voynich's original hypothesis, Newbold 1928) has been entirely discredited — Bacon died in c. 1292, 100+ years before the vellum was made. No other authorship identification has been accepted.

3. **The purpose of the manuscript.** Was it: a genuine medical or botanical treatise in an obscure language? An alchemical document using a private cipher to protect secret knowledge? A grimoire or magical text? An artificial-language experiment? A hoax designed for sale? All are consistent with the known facts.

4. **What the plant illustrations represent.** Dozens of professional botanists have examined the botanical section. **None has identified the illustrated plants with any known species.** They are either stylized beyond recognition, fictional, or represent plants not in the European botanical record — but which plants, if any, is unknown.

5. **Why the biological section contains the bathing figures.** This section has generated the widest range of speculation and the least scholarly consensus.

## Theories (tiered)

- **Natural language in cipher** — Tier 2 (mainstream scholarly hypothesis; statistically motivated)
- **Constructed/artificial language** — Tier 3 (possible; no consensus mechanism identified)
- **Elaborate 15th-century hoax** (Rugg 2004) — Tier 3 (methodologically serious; mechanically demonstrable; not conclusive)
- **Roger Bacon authorship** — Tier 4 (Voynich's original speculation; refuted by carbon dating)
- **Alien, supernatural, or "Atlantean" origin** — Tier 4 (fringe reception; documented as cultural phenomenon, not as possibility)

## Cross-family appearance

The Voynich Manuscript exists **within** the early modern European Hermetic-alchemical manuscript tradition. Its cosmological diagrams resemble alchemical mandalas and circular charts from texts like the *Rosarium Philosophorum* (c. 1550) and *Splendor Solis* (1531–1582). Its pharmaceutical section resembles the *Kräuterbuch* (herbal medicine) tradition. The coded knowledge-protection strategy is consistent with the widespread Renaissance-Hermetic practice of encoding secret philosophical or alchemical knowledge to protect it from inquisitorial scrutiny or commercial theft.

**This is hypothesis, not conclusion.** The manuscript's sectional structure and visual idiom are *consistent with* Hermetic-alchemical manuscript culture; whether it was produced within that tradition is not established.

The Rudolfine court context (Rudolf II was the greatest patron of alchemy, Hermeticism, and esoteric scholarship in 16th-century Europe, employing Tycho Brahe, Johannes Kepler, and John Dee) makes the manuscript's presence in that milieu entirely plausible — but does not establish its origin.

## Contested interpretations

The entire history of Voynich studies is effectively a history of contested and failed interpretations. The manuscript has attracted more sustained scholarly and amateur attention than any other undeciphered document, and **no interpretation has withstood scrutiny**. The most recent serious computational attempts (including natural language processing approaches using machine learning, 2019–2023) have generated speculative results that have not been accepted by the cryptographic and linguistic scholarly community.

The **hoax hypothesis** (Rugg 2004) remains the most methodologically rigorous alternative to the "encoded natural language" hypothesis. It is not conclusive.
