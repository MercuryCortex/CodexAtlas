# Latin cross

## Identity

The Latin cross (*crux ordinaria* or *crux immissa*) is the long-shaft cruciform symbol of mainstream Western Christianity: a vertical beam with a shorter horizontal crossbar set roughly two-thirds up its length. As the central emblem of the crucifixion of Jesus of Nazareth, it functions simultaneously as a Christological referent, a liturgical sign, a private devotional gesture (the sign of the cross), and a public marker of Christian presence (gravestones, churches, civic insignia). The form crystallizes during the 4th–6th c. CE; its prevalence is in part a consequence of Constantine's adoption of cross-symbolism (the *labarum*) after the Battle of the Milvian Bridge (312).

## Earliest attestation

- **1st–2nd c. CE textual**: The Pauline corpus (esp. 1 Cor 1:18, Gal 3:13) names *the cross* as a theological referent; the Gospel passion narratives ([[phase-3-016-gospel-of-mark]], [[phase-3-017-gospel-of-matthew]], [[phase-3-018-luke-acts]], [[phase-3-020-gospel-of-john]]) provide the crucifixion as historical event. Whether these texts visualize the *Latin* form (long-shaft) or a tau-form (T-shape) is a 20th-c. scholarly question; Jensen 2017 ch. 1 concludes the textual evidence is iconographically underdetermined.
- **Earliest iconographic** survivals are 2nd-c. graffiti including the famous Alexamenos graffito (c. 200 CE, Palatine Hill, Rome) — a hostile pagan caricature showing a crucified figure with an ass's head, captioned "Alexamenos worships [his] god." This is the earliest visual depiction of the crucifixion, and the cross-form depicted is closer to a tau than the developed Latin form.
- **Constantinian period (early 4th c.)**: the *labarum* (chi-rho on a military standard) becomes the imperial Christian emblem after [[event-edict-of-milan-313]] following [[constantine-i]]'s vision at the Milvian Bridge (Eusebius, *Vita Constantini* 1.28–32; Lactantius, *De Mortibus Persecutorum* 44). The developed long-shaft Latin cross-form becomes iconographically standard from the 5th–6th c. onward (Jensen 2017 ch. 2–3).

## Meaning(s) attested

- **Christological referent** — the cross is the instrument and emblem of the crucifixion; in Pauline theology the cross is *foolishness to the Greeks, scandal to the Jews* (1 Cor 1:23) and the locus of salvific reversal.
- **Sign of the cross** — gestural rite attested already in Tertullian (*De Corona* 3) c. 200 CE: "we trace the sign of the cross on our foreheads at every step we take." Andreopoulos 2006 traces the gesture's development.
- **Apotropaic mark** — protective use against demons in patristic and monastic literature ([[antony-the-great]], in Athanasius's *Life of Antony*).
- **Liturgical and architectural** — cross-plan churches, processional crosses, altar crosses, episcopal crosses, etc.

## Variants

The *labarum*, the *crucifix* (cross with corpus), and the *patriarchal cross* are body sub-sections here; the [[greek-cross]], [[celtic-cross]], [[coptic-cross]], and [[maltese-cross]] are separate nodes because each acquired an independent iconographic tradition.

## Cross-family appearance

- **Pre-Christian** — the τ-shape underlying the Latin cross precedes Christianity (Greek tau, Phoenician taw, Egyptian sandal-strap glyph). Tertullian (*Against Marcion* 3.22) already engages the resemblance as a Christian apologetic. See [[tau-cross]] and [[ankh]].
- **Mithraic and other Roman mystery cults** — competing 2nd–4th c. cults used cruciform and X-form iconography in the same urban environments. Older (Cumontian) accounts claimed direct Christian borrowing; Beck 2006 and Ulansey 1989 reject that as overreach. The relationship is best modeled as `parallel-form` — competing Roman-period iconographic traditions, not transmission. See [[mithraic-cross]].
- **Coptic Christianity** — the [[coptic-cross]] is a separate Egyptian-Christian line continuing the ankh tradition (Bagnall 1993, Frankfurter 1998), parallel to but distinct from the Latin cross's Greco-Roman line.

## Contested interpretations

- **Shape in the 1st c.**: scholars disagree on whether the historical crucifixion stake (*stauros*) was a Latin-form cross, a tau-cross, or simply an upright pole (Jensen 2017; Hengel 1977 *Crucifixion*).
- **Constantinian conversion**: the nature of Constantine's "vision" and the political vs. religious motivation for his adoption of the cross is contested (Bowersock essays in Cambridge UP Christianity volumes; Jensen 2017 surveys the debate).
- **Direct transmission from ankh / mithraic / pagan sources**: largely rejected by current scholarship for the Latin cross specifically (different from the well-documented Coptic case).

## Mystery / unknown variants

None at this level — the Latin cross is the best-documented Christian symbol. `mystery-status: documented`.
