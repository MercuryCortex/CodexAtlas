# Greek cross

## Identity

The Greek cross (*crux quadrata*) is the equal-armed cruciform of Byzantine and Eastern Orthodox Christianity: four arms of equal length meeting at a central intersection. Its dominance in the East parallels — but does not derive from — the Latin long-shaft cross's dominance in the West. Architecturally, the Greek cross is the defining ground-plan of mid- and late-Byzantine church architecture (the "cross-in-square" or *staurepistegos* church type, attested from c. 800 CE onward).

## Earliest attestation

- **Early Christian iconography (3rd–4th c. CE)**: equal-arms cruciform marks appear on Christian sarcophagi, lamps, and graffiti across the Mediterranean. Distinguishing them from generic crosses (or earlier sun-wheel and X-form non-Christian iconography) requires contextual evidence (Jensen 2017).
- **Byzantine architecture (6th c. onward)**: the cross-in-square floor plan emerges and consolidates as the canonical Eastern church type. Hagia Sophia of Constantinople (537) is technically a domed basilica, but smaller Byzantine churches of the 8th–11th c. exemplify the Greek-cross plan.
- **Liturgical and iconographic standardization** after the iconoclast crisis (8th–9th c., culminating in [[event-council-of-nicaea-787]] and the Triumph of Orthodoxy in 843): the Greek cross becomes the dominant form on processional crosses, altar covers, mosaics, and book covers (Belting 1994).

## Meaning(s) attested

- **Christological referent** — like the Latin form, the Greek cross is fundamentally a Christological sign; the equal-arms geometry is read in some Byzantine theological commentary as figuring the four directions / four corners of the cosmos under Christ's lordship.
- **Cosmological** — the four-quartered geometry is connected (in Byzantine iconographic exegesis) to the four evangelists, the four rivers of paradise, the four winds.
- **Liturgical and architectural** — the Greek-cross-in-square church plan organizes worship around a central altar with equal-arm transepts.

## Variants

The *cross pattée* and *cross fleury* are body sub-sections here. Both spread westward via medieval heraldic and Crusader iconography; the [[maltese-cross]] is one downstream variant within that lineage.

## Cross-family appearance

- **Byzantine ↔ Coptic exchange**: Eastern Christian monasticism connected Byzantine and Coptic Egyptian traditions; the equal-arms form appears in both, and material exchange between Constantinople and the Egyptian monasteries is well-documented through the 4th–7th c. ([[coptic-cross]], Bagnall 1993).
- **Western adoption**: equal-arms forms enter Western Christian heraldry by the early medieval period; the *cross pattée* becomes a heraldic and ecclesiastical standard. The Crusader orders adopt variants of this form (see [[maltese-cross]]).
- **Russian and Slavic Orthodox** — the Greek-cross form propagates through Slavic Christianization (10th c. onward); local variants (the Russian Orthodox cross with the slanted lower bar) develop but the equal-arms base remains.

## Contested interpretations

- The relationship between early equal-arms Christian iconography and earlier pagan equal-arms motifs (sun-wheels, certain X-forms) is iconographically suggestive but not transmissively documented; visual cognacy is the most defensible claim.
- Whether the Greek-cross church plan is iconographically motivated or primarily structural (the cross-in-square being a natural domed-bay solution) is debated — Belting 1994 treats it as both.
