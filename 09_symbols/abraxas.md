# Abraxas (Abrasax)

Abraxas — also spelled **Abrasax** in Greek manuscripts — is simultaneously a divine name, a supreme deity in the Basilidean Gnostic system, a word of power inscribed on hundreds of magical amulets, and a modern psychological symbol for the totality that transcends the good-evil duality. Its letters in Greek isopsephy (letter-number equivalence) sum to **365** — the number of days in the solar year and, in Basilides's cosmology, the number of heavens separating the supreme divine from the material world.

## Basilidean Gnostic Theology (c. 125–130 CE)

Basilides was a Christian Gnostic teacher active in Alexandria during the reign of Hadrian. Our primary sources are hostile: **Irenaeus** (*Adversus Haereses* I.24) and **Hippolytus** (*Refutatio Omnium Haeresium* VII.14) preserve accounts of his system, though they disagree on details and polemicize against it.

According to these accounts, Basilides taught a cosmology of **365 heavens**, each with its own ruling *archon* (cosmic power). The God of the Hebrew Bible — the creator of the material world — is merely the archon of the lowest of these 365 heavens. Above all 365 heavens stands the supreme divine principle: **Abraxas** (or the "non-existent Father," in other accounts). This supreme being is beyond all human conception, absolutely transcendent, unknowable by any name or attribute — a position parallel to the Neoplatonist's *hen* (the One).

### The Isopsephic Foundation

The Greek letters of ΑΒΡΑΣΑΞ sum as follows:
- **Α** (Alpha) = 1  
- **Β** (Beta) = 2  
- **Ρ** (Rho) = 100  
- **Α** (Alpha) = 1  
- **Σ** (Sigma) = 200  
- **Α** (Alpha) = 1  
- **Ξ** (Xi) = 60  
- **Total: 365**

This is not coincidence but theological architecture: the supreme god's name encodes the solar year, binding cosmology, number mysticism, and divine nomenclature into a single symbol. The same technique appears across ancient Mediterranean traditions — Hebrew gematria assigns numerical values to the Tetragrammaton ([[tetragrammaton]] = 26 in its simplest reduction); the divine creative word is always somehow quantifiable, bounded, and yet boundless.

## The Gemstone Amulets

More than **3,000 surviving magical gemstones** from the Greco-Roman period (c. 100–400 CE) bear the name Abraxas and/or its composite deity image. Campbell Bonner's 1950 study (*Studies in Magical Amulets, Chiefly Graeco-Egyptian*, University of Michigan Press) remains the definitive catalogue.

The standard image:
- **Rooster's head** — solar animal; the cock crows at dawn, heralding the sun; vigilance against darkness
- **Human body with armor** — the divine warrior aspect
- **Serpent legs** — chthonic power; the serpent as master of the underworld and regeneration (cf. [[ouroboros]])
- **Solar disk and/or whip** — solar authority and cosmic command

This composite image is a deliberate synthesis: Egyptian solar theology (the rooster-headed solar deity), Greco-Roman divine warrior iconography, Jewish divine name magic (the inscription of ABRASAX), and Greek Gnostic theology. The amulets were worn for protection against illness, evil spirits, and misfortune — a practical application of an abstract theology.

### Abracadabra — The Contested Etymology

One tradition derives "Abracadabra" from Abraxas, via a triangular diminution formula (the word written repeatedly with one letter removed each line, forming a triangle) used as a fever charm in Quintus Serenus Sammonicus (c. 200 CE). Another proposed etymology: Aramaic *avra kadavra* = "I create as I speak" (invoking the Logos doctrine). Both connections remain philologically contested; they should be treated as *possible* etymological pathways rather than established facts (Tier-3 etymology). What is established: the Abracadabra formula appears in Latin medical texts by the 2nd–3rd century CE, independent of whether it derives from Abraxas.

## MASSIVE WIN: C.G. Jung and the Seven Sermons to the Dead (1916)

In 1916, Carl Gustav Jung wrote a private document titled ***Septem Sermones ad Mortuos*** (Seven Sermons to the Dead), ascribed to the pseudonymous author **"Basilides of Alexandria"** — a conscious and deliberate invocation of the ancient Gnostic teacher. This text, privately distributed among friends, was later appended to *Memories, Dreams, Reflections* (1963).

In Jung's *Septem Sermones*, **Abraxas** is defined as:

> *"a god above whom nothing else is more powerful, the god of gods. ... He is hardness and softness, he is both terrible and kind, he is both visible and invisible. Abraxas is the god whom it is difficult to know. His power is the very greatest because man does not perceive it at all."*

For Jung, Abraxas is the symbol of the **totality of existence** prior to the ego's division of reality into the moral categories of good and evil. God (summum bonum) and the Devil (summa malum) are both *aspects* of Abraxas — partial, ego-bound conceptions of a reality that is neither good nor evil but simply total. This places Abraxas in direct psychological parallel to:
- The Taoist concept of the **Tao** as beyond all binary categories (cf. yin-yang [[yin-yang]])
- The Hindu **Brahman** as the ground of being before the projections of Vishnu and Shiva
- The Kabbalistic **Ein Sof** as the Infinite before the emanations of the [[sefirot-tree-of-life]]

Jung's *Collected Works*, Volume 6 (*Psychological Types*, 1921) develops the psychological infrastructure that the *Sermones* dramatizes. The *Sermones* themselves represent one of the most philosophically sophisticated deployments of ancient Gnostic theology in the 20th century — not as superstition but as a *symbolic grammar* for the structure of the psyche.

## MASSIVE WIN: The Divine-Name-as-Number Pattern

Abraxas = 365 (solar year) is one instance of a cross-tradition pattern: the divine creative principle is encoded in language and number simultaneously.

| Name | Tradition | Numerical Value | What It Encodes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Abraxas/Abrasax** | Basilidean Gnostic | 365 (Greek isopsephy) | Solar year; number of heavens |
| **Tetragrammaton (YHWH)** | Jewish | 26 (Hebrew gematria, simple) | The ineffable divine name |
| **AUM (OM)** | Hindu/Buddhist | 3 phonemes (A+U+M) | Creation/maintenance/dissolution |
| **INRI** | Christian | Iesus Nazarenus Rex Iudaeorum | The crucified king's cosmic title |

All four traditions encode the divine in specific linguistic/numerical form. The sound or number IS the god — not a pointer to it but an instantiation of it. This is the doctrine of the sacred name as ontological reality, not mere label: compare [[tetragrammaton]] and [[aum-om]].

## Sources and Tiering

**Tier 1 (primary and peer-reviewed scholarship):**
- Bonner, C. (1950). *Studies in Magical Amulets, Chiefly Graeco-Egyptian*. University of Michigan Press. — The definitive catalogue of Abraxas amulets; 3,000+ examples systematically analyzed.
- Irenaeus of Lyon. *Adversus Haereses* I.24 (ed. Harvey 1857; Eng. trans. ANF vol. 1). — Primary hostile source on Basilides; essential with critical awareness of polemical framing.
- Hippolytus of Rome. *Refutatio Omnium Haeresium* VII.14 (Eng. trans. ANF vol. 5). — Secondary hostile source; provides additional details diverging from Irenaeus.
- Fossum, J.E. (1985). *The Name of God and the Angel of the Lord*. Mohr Siebeck, Tübingen. — Rigorous analysis of divine name theology in Jewish, Samaritan, and Gnostic sources; places Abraxas in the divine-name tradition.
- Jung, C.G. (1916/1963). *Memories, Dreams, Reflections* (Appendix: Seven Sermons to the Dead). Pantheon. — Tier 1 for the modern psychological reception; Jung is a primary source for his own system.

**Tier 2:**
- Betz, H.D. (ed.) (1986). *The Greek Magical Papyri in Translation*. University of Chicago Press. — The PGM corpus; Abraxas appears throughout as an invocable divine name.

**Tier 4 (fringe — documented as such):**
- Modern chaos-magic invocations of Abraxas as a personal working deity. These represent contemporary magical practice drawing on the ancient name without connection to Basilidean theology; documented as fringe reception, not ancient tradition.
