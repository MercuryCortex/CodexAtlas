# Beauseant (Templar War Banner)

## Identity
The **Beauseant** (Old French *bauceant* / *baussant* — "piebald") is the **rectangular black-and-white war-banner** of the [[tradition-knights-templar|Knights Templar]] (~1147–1312). The banner is **per fess sable and argent** (divided horizontally, black-and-white; in some surviving witnesses with the black above, in others with the white above — both forms are attested), and was carried by the Templar field-army from the [[event-second-crusade-1147-1149|Second Crusade]] (1147) through the [[event-fall-of-acre-1291|Fall of Acre]] (1291) until the [[event-trial-of-templars-1307-1314|trial and suppression]] (1307–1312). The [[phase-5-036-rule-of-templars-latin|Latin Rule]] and the *Retraits* (the French operational additions to the Rule) explicitly regulate banner-discipline: **the Templar must rally to the beauseant on the field; the banner must not fall; surrender is forbidden while the banner stands**. The military-disciplinary centrality of the beauseant is one of the distinctive features of Templar field-warfare.

The name **beauseant** is from Old French *baucant* — "piebald, black-and-white-spotted" (originally of horses). The folk-etymology connecting it to *beau séant* ("fair seat" / "fair-sitting") is a later rationalization.

## Earliest attestation
- **c. 1147 onward** — the beauseant accompanies Templar field-forces from at least the Second Crusade.
- **c. 1170–1200** — the Latin Rule and the French Retraits codify banner-discipline.
- **1187** — at the Battle of [[saladin|Hattin]] (4 July 1187), the Templar beauseant is lost; subsequent Templar military reorganization on Cyprus and elsewhere re-establishes the banner.
- **1291** — at the [[event-fall-of-acre-1291|Fall of Acre]] (18 May 1291), the beauseant fights at the breached northern wall until Grand Master Guillaume de Beaujeu is killed.
- **1307–1312** — with the suppression of the Order, the beauseant ceases active use.

## Meaning(s) attested
- **Medieval Templar / Bernardine** — the black-and-white division is read theologically as the **dual nature of the Templar mission**: black for the world's evil that the Templar fights, white for the *miles Christi*'s pure consecration. Some medieval-Templar interpretations gloss the banner as *"black to enemies of Christ; white to friends of Christ"*.
- **Military-disciplinary function** — the banner is the **rallying-point** of the Templar field-army; its fall signals catastrophe. Templar bannerers (the *gonfanonier*) had specifically-regulated duties under the Rule's *Retraits*.
- **Modern reception** — the beauseant remains the principal Templar-visual-identity in 19th-21st-c. neo-Templarist literature and groups; the Holy-Blood-Holy-Grail / *Da Vinci Code* tradition uses the beauseant alongside the [[cross-pattee-templar|cross-pattée]] as definitive Templar emblem.

## Variants
The black/white orientation is contested in primary sources. The **black-above-white-below** (sable chief, argent base) form is attested in 13th-c. illuminated manuscripts (the *Chronica Majora* of Matthew Paris); the **white-above-black-below** form is attested in other witnesses. Some forms carry the red [[cross-pattee-templar|cross-pattée]] centered on the white field. The Order may have used **multiple variant forms** at different periods or in different theaters.

## Cross-family appearance
The beauseant is **specifically Templar** and does not migrate to other religious traditions or other military orders. The closest parallel is the [[tradition-hospitallers|Hospitallers']] own war-banner (red field with white cross — distinct from the Templar form). **Modern neo-Templarist appropriations** are reception-history phenomena rather than continuous transmission — see [[templar-gnostic-transmission-hypothesis]].

## Contested interpretations
- **Black/white orientation.** Primary sources are inconsistent; modern Templarist literature is unreliable on this point.
- **Whether the cross-pattée appears on the beauseant.** Some primary witnesses show the cross; some do not. The Order may have used multiple variants.
- **The folk-etymology.** *Beau séant* is medieval folk-etymology, not a serious philological derivation. The actual etymology is from *baucant* (piebald).
