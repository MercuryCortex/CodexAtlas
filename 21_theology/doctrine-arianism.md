# Arianism

Arianism is the subordinationist Christology named for the Alexandrian presbyter [[arius]] (c. 256–336): the teaching that the Son of God is a **creature** — the first and highest of created things, made by the Father out of nothing, of a **different essence** (not *homoousios*) from the Father, and therefore **not co-eternal** with him. Its slogan, preserved in hostile quotation, is *ēn hote ouk ēn* — "there was when he was not." It is the **losing side of the Nicene controversy**: the position against which the Council of Nicaea (325) coined *homoousios* (consubstantial), and which the Council of Constantinople (381) finally defeated. In the Atlas it sits as the defeated thesis paired with its resolution, [[doctrine-trinity-nicene]].

## The doctrine

The core Arian claims, as reconstructed from Arius's *Thalia* and letters (surviving only through opponents, chiefly [[athanasius-of-alexandria]]) and from the Nicene anathemas that target them:

1. **The Son is created** — "made of things that were not" (*ex ouk ontōn*). He is not eternally generated from the Father's own being but produced, like all creatures, by the Father's will.
2. **"There was when he was not"** (*ēn hote ouk ēn*) — there is a "before" to the Son. He is prior to time and to all other creatures, but not co-eternal with the unbegotten Father.
3. **Different essence** — the Son is *not* of one substance with the Father. In the developed **neo-Arian / Anomoean** theology of Aetius and Eunomius (350s–360s) this hardens into *heteroousios* / *anomoios*: the Son is of an *unlike* essence, and the unbegotten Father alone is truly God.
4. **A high mediator** — the Son is nonetheless the unique instrument through whom God made the world: a genuine intermediate between the utterly transcendent unbegotten God and the creation, worthy of worship yet ontologically a creature.

Gregg and Groh (1981) stress that this was not merely a metaphysical puzzle but a **soteriology**: the created Son is the perfectly obedient creature, the moral exemplar whose advancement in virtue is the pattern of salvation for other creatures — a reading whose substrate is as much Stoic-ethical as Platonic-metaphysical.

## In the controversy (318–381)

The dispute opened around 318 when Arius's preaching clashed with Bishop **Alexander of Alexandria**. Backed by the influential **Eusebius of Nicomedia** at the imperial court, the quarrel spread through the Eastern church until Constantine convened the **Council of Nicaea (325)** ([[event-council-of-nicaea-325]]). Nicaea condemned Arius, exiled him, and issued a creed engineered to exclude his teaching word by word: the Son is "true God of true God, **begotten not made**, of **one substance** (*homoousios*) with the Father," with explicit anathemas on "there was when he was not" and "made of things that were not."

Nicaea did not end the fight. Under Constantius II the Eastern church was largely anti-Nicene for a generation; the term *homoousios* was itself suspect to many bishops who were not Arians but feared it collapsed the Father and Son into one (Sabellianism). The controversy fractured into homoousian, homoiousian ("of *like* substance"), homoian ("of *like*" without essence-language), and the radical **Anomoean** neo-Arianism of Aetius and Eunomius. It was the labor of **[[athanasius-of-alexandria|Athanasius]]** (his *Orations against the Arians*) and then the **Cappadocians** ([[gregory-of-nazianzus]], [[gregory-of-nyssa]]) that rebuilt a durable pro-Nicene consensus, ratified at the **Council of Constantinople (381)**.

## Afterlife — the barbarian creed

Arianism's longest life was outside the Empire. The Gothic missionary-bishop **Ulfilas** (Wulfila, c. 311–383) evangelized the Goths in a homoian-Arian form and gave them a Gothic Bible. Arian Christianity thus became **the confession of the Germanic successor kingdoms** — Ostrogoths, Visigoths, Vandals, Burgundians, Lombards — setting a religious-political fault line against the Nicene Romano-Catholic populations they ruled. It persisted among the Visigoths until the conversion of Reccared (587) and among the Lombards into the 7th century (Berndt & Steinacher 2014).

## Cross-tradition significance — the created divine "second"

A striking cross-tradition parallel is to the **Islamic controversy over the createdness of the Qurʾān** (the Muʿtazilī *khalq al-Qurʾān* doctrine and the ʿAbbāsid *miḥna* of 833–848; see [[doctrine-mutazila-rationalist-kalam]]). Both controversies pose the *same structural question* in two different monotheisms: **is the divine "second" co-eternal with God, or is it created?** In Christianity the second is the Son / Logos; in Islam it is the Qurʾān as the eternal Speech of God. In both, the party that made the divine second a **creature** — Arius, and the Muʿtazila — ultimately **lost**, and "uncreated / co-eternal" became the orthodox confession (Nicene homoousios; the Ḥanbalī–Ashʿarī uncreated Qurʾān).

The honest framing matters. This is primarily a **structural / typological** convergence: two strict monotheisms, independently forced by their own logic of divine unity to ask whether a second thing spoken of as divine can be eternal without becoming a second God. **Harry Wolfson** (*The Philosophy of the Kalam*, 1976) pressed the resemblance into an explicit **transmission** thesis — that the Christian Logos-and-attributes debate historically shaped the Muslim discussion — but this stronger claim is **contested**: Josef van Ess and most current scholarship read the Muslim controversy as substantially an internal development of *kalām*, with the parallel to Arianism illuminating but not evidence of borrowing. Secure: the two debates are structurally the same problem. Not secure: that one caused the other.

A second, internal-Christian parallel is the **subordinationist substrate** the doctrine radicalized — the Logos-theology of Justin Martyr, Origen, and behind them the Hellenistic-Jewish Logos of Philo, in which the Word is a real but *derivative* divine mediator. Arius pushed the mediator across the line from "derived divinity" to "creature." Rowan Williams (2001) reads Arius as a rigorous Alexandrian Christian-Platonist rather than an innovator, which is why the label "Arianism" flattens a range of fourth-century positions that were never a single party.

## Disputes

- **What Arius actually taught.** Williams (2001), building on Stead and Lorenz, argues that the caricature "Arius said the Son was a creature, full stop" obscures a sophisticated Origenist position, and that the developed neo-Arianism of Aetius and Eunomius (350s–360s) departs significantly from Arius himself. "Arianism" is in part a heresiological construct — a point Ayres (2004) makes central.
- **Was there ever an "Arian party"?** Recent scholarship (Ayres, Hanson) dissolves the old picture of a unified Arian movement into multiple, shifting theological trajectories loosely tarred with Arius's name by their Nicene opponents.
- **The created-Qurʾān parallel.** As above: structural resemblance is agreed; Wolfson's historical-transmission claim is disputed.
