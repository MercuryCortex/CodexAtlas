# Divine Simplicity (Divina Simplicitas)

## Identity

Divine simplicity is the doctrine that God is **without composition of any kind** — no composition of matter and form, of essence and existence, of substance and accident, or of genus and differentia — and that, in consequence, God's attributes are not distinct realities added to a divine substrate but are **identical with God's essence and with one another**. God does not *have* goodness, wisdom, and power as a composite thing has parts or qualities; God *is* his goodness, which *is* his wisdom, which *is* his existence. Whatever is in God *is* God.

The doctrine is the shared metaphysical core of the three Abrahamic scholastic traditions. It is the one place where Christian, Jewish, and Islamic scholasticism converge — by documented transmission, not merely by typological resemblance — on the same Neoplatonic-Aristotelian claim about the God of scripture. In the Atlas it is homed in Latin patristic-scholastic Christianity (its earliest sustained articulation, Augustine → Aquinas, and the source of the standard term *divina simplicitas*), with its Islamic (*falsafa* / [[doctrine-tawhid|tawḥīd]]) and Jewish ([[maimonides|Maimonides]]) articulations carried as cross-tradition wires.

## In the scholastic doctrine of God

The doctrine has two roots that the scholastics fuse. The **Aristotelian** root is God as **pure actuality** (*actus purus*) with no admixture of potency (*Metaphysics* XII): what has no potency has no matter, and what has no matter has no composition of matter and form. The **Neoplatonic** root is Plotinus's **One** (*Enneads* VI.9), which is absolutely simple (*haploun*), beyond being and beyond predication, the partless source of all multiplicity. Augustine (*De Trinitate* VI–VII, *De Civitate Dei* XI.10) Christianizes the Plotinian simple One into a doctrine of the biblical God who "is what he has"; Boethius and [[anselm-of-canterbury|Anselm]] (*Monologion* 16–17) carry it into the Latin West.

The canonical statement is **Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* Ia q.3**, "On the Simplicity of God," which denies in eight successive articles that God is a body, that God is composed of matter and form, that God's nature differs from the divine *suppositum*, that essence and existence differ in God, that God belongs to a genus, that there is any accident in God, and that God is in any way composite. The keystone is article 4: in every creature essence and existence (*esse*) are really distinct — a thing's nature does not include its being — but **in God essence and existence are identical**; God's very nature is *to be*. This is the metaphysical form of the name revealed at the burning bush, "I AM WHO I AM" (Exod 3:14), as the scholastics read it.

Independently and earlier in systematic form, [[ibn-sina|Ibn Sīnā]] had defined the **Necessary Existent** (*wājib al-wujūd*) precisely by its lack of composition — above all, its lack of the essence/existence distinction that marks every contingent (*mumkin*) being. [[maimonides|Maimonides]] (*Guide* I.50–60) makes divine simplicity the engine of **negative theology**: since God is simple, any positive attribute would introduce multiplicity into him, so scriptural attributes must be construed as negations of privation or as descriptions of God's action, never as positive predications of his essence. When Aquinas writes *ST* Ia q.3 he cites both "Avicenna" and "Rabbi Moyses" — the moment the three lines visibly meet on one page.

## Cross-tradition significance

Divine simplicity is a **MASSIVE-WIN** for the Atlas because the cross-tradition convergence here is not speculative parallelism but **textually documented transmission**. The falāsifa (al-Kindī, al-Fārābī, and decisively Ibn Sīnā) reworked the Greek Neoplatonic-Aristotelian metaphysics of the simple first principle into a doctrine of the Necessary Existent. Maimonides, writing in Arabic and steeped in the falāsifa, made that simplicity the basis of his account of the divine attributes. Aquinas, reading Maimonides in Latin as "Rabbi Moyses" and Avicenna through the Toledo translations, absorbed both. David Burrell's *Knowing the Unknowable God* (1986) — the canonical study — treats Ibn Sīnā, Maimonides, and Aquinas as **participants in a single conversation** about the simple, unknowable God, and this node's principal claim rests on that documented chain.

The convergence is genuine but not total, and honesty requires marking the asymmetries. In **Islam**, the doctrine is entangled with the attribute controversy: the [[philosophy-mutazila|Muʿtazila]] denied that God has real attributes distinct from his essence (to protect the unity of [[doctrine-tawhid|tawḥīd]]), the falāsifa built simplicity into the Necessary Existent, and [[al-ghazali|al-Ghazālī]]'s *Tahāfut al-Falāsifa* attacked the philosophers for a simplicity so radical that it emptied God of real knowledge of particulars — one of his three charges of unbelief. In **Judaism**, simplicity is inseparable from Maimonidean negative theology and, later, from the Kabbalistic *[[ein-sof|Ein Sof]]* (the Limitless beyond all sefirotic attribute). In **Christianity**, it collides with the two hardest problems in the tradition (below). What all three share is the Neoplatonic conviction, transmitted and re-theologized, that the ultimate is not merely one-in-number but **partless** — that composition is a mark of the contingent and created, and God is neither.

## Disputes

**Simplicity vs. the Trinity.** The sharpest internal Christian problem: how can a God with *no* real internal distinctions be three *really distinct* persons? The scholastic solution distinguishes the divine essence (absolutely simple) from the relations of origin (paternity, filiation, spiration) that constitute the persons and are "really" distinct only from one another, not from the essence. Whether this is coherent — whether relations can be really distinct in a being with no composition — is debated from Aquinas's own day (the Latin–Greek quarrels behind the [[doctrine-filioque|Filioque]]) to the present.

**Simplicity vs. the essence/energies distinction.** The principal intra-Christian dissent is Eastern. [[gregory-palamas|Gregory Palamas]] and the Byzantine hesychasts reject **absolute** simplicity in the Western sense, distinguishing God's utterly unknowable essence (*ousia*) from his uncreated, participable **energies** (*energeiai*) — the distinction that makes deification (*theōsis*) possible (see [[essence-energies-distinction]]). For the East, Latin absolute simplicity dissolves the God we can actually participate; for the West and for Palamas's opponent Barlaam, the essence/energies distinction imports composition into God. Bradshaw (*Aristotle East and West*, 2004) traces this to divergent readings of Aristotle's *energeia*.

**The modern incoherence charge.** In contemporary analytic philosophy of religion the medieval attribute problem returns. [[philosophy-analytic-theism|Analytic theists]] led by **Alvin Plantinga** (*Does God Have a Nature?*, 1980) argue the doctrine is incoherent: if God is identical with his properties, then God *is* a property; but a property is an abstract object, not a person, and cannot know, will, or act. **Stump and Kretzmann** ("Absolute Simplicity," 1985) defend the doctrine by denying that identifying God with his nature makes God an abstract property; **Christopher Hughes** (*On a Complex Theory of a Simple God*, 1989) presses the objections specifically against Aquinas. The state of the question: the doctrine is the most metaphysically demanding of the classical divine attributes, its cross-tradition pedigree is secure and documented, and its coherence remains genuinely contested.
