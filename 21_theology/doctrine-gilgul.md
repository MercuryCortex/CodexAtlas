# Gilgul Neshamot (Kabbalistic Doctrine of Soul-Cycling)

## Identity

**Gilgul neshamot** (Hebrew גִּלְגּוּל נְשָׁמוֹת, "the cycling / rolling of souls") is the Kabbalistic Jewish doctrine of reincarnation: the belief that a human soul may return to embodied life one or more times in order to complete an unfulfilled commandment (*mitzvah*), to repair a moral failure, or to accomplish its portion of the cosmic restoration. The noun *gilgul* derives from the root *g-l-l*, "to roll," and the plural *neshamot* means "souls." Unlike the Indic model of a beginningless cycle to be escaped, gilgul is **bounded and purposive** — a soul is given a limited number of returns (the Zohar and later authorities variously specify up to three, after which further chances may be granted only in special circumstances) — and it terminates not in liberation *from* embodiment but in the eventual bodily resurrection affirmed by rabbinic eschatology.

The doctrine has **no basis in the Hebrew Bible or the Talmud**, and was unknown to (indeed rejected by) the classical rationalist theologians. It is an innovation of medieval Kabbalah, appearing first in the *Sefer ha-Bahir*, developed in the *Zohar*, and given its fullest metaphysical form by Isaac Luria in 16th-century Safed. A closely related concept is ***ibbur*** ("impregnation") — the temporary attachment of an additional, already-perfected soul to a living person to help them complete a task, as distinct from gilgul proper, in which a soul is reborn into a new body from birth. The negative counterpart is the ***dybbuk***, the intrusive attachment of a restless or malevolent soul.

## In the Kabbalistic corpus

The **Sefer ha-Bahir** (Provence, c. 1150-1200), the earliest Kabbalistic text, introduces gilgul as a solution to the problem of **theodicy** — why the righteous suffer and the wicked prosper. In a celebrated parable (Bahir §195) a vinedresser plants good and bad vines; when the vines fail he uproots and replants them, an image read as the soul's repeated return until it succeeds. Here reincarnation answers the question that the Book of Job leaves open: apparent injustice in one life is resolved across several.

The **Zohar** (Castile, c. 1280s) develops the doctrine far more richly. Its most sustained treatment is the *Saba de-Mishpatim* (the discourse of the "Old Man," Zohar II.94a-114a), which ties gilgul specifically to the biblical institution of **levirate marriage** (*yibbum*, Deut 25:5-10): a man who dies childless has failed the commandment to "be fruitful and multiply," and his soul returns — often, in the Zoharic reading, in the child born of the levirate union — to complete it. Gilgul thus becomes the mechanism of a specifically Jewish, commandment-centered soul-destiny.

The doctrine reaches its systematic climax with **Isaac Luria** (the Ari, 1534-1572), whose teachings survive chiefly through his disciple **Chaim Vital**, above all in *Sha'ar ha-Gilgulim* ("Gate of Reincarnations") and *Etz Chayyim*. In the Lurianic cosmology the primordial "vessels" that were to hold the divine light shattered (*shevirat ha-kelim*), scattering holy **sparks** (*nitzotzot*) throughout creation. Every soul possesses a "root" and a portion of sparks to gather; **gilgul is the process by which souls return, life after life, to reclaim their sparks and thereby accomplish *tikkun*** — the repair of the broken cosmos. Reincarnation is no longer merely personal theodicy but the engine of universal redemption. Lurianic Kabbalah develops an elaborate science of soul-roots, transmigration into animals and plants, and the diagnosis of which biblical figure a given soul is a gilgul of.

From the 18th century, **Hasidism** inherited this Lurianic framework wholesale, and gilgul — together with the redemption of sparks and the folk-belief in the dybbuk — passed into living Ashkenazi popular religion, where it remains a normative belief in Hasidic and much of Orthodox Kabbalistic culture to the present.

## Cross-tradition significance

Gilgul is **the Abrahamic tradition's internal reincarnation doctrine** — a striking case of a fully-developed transmigration belief arising *within* a monotheistic scriptural religion whose mainstream eschatology is otherwise linear (one life → death → bodily resurrection → judgment). This makes it the natural Jewish counterpart to the flagship Indic doctrine of saṃsāra ([[doctrine-reincarnation-samsara]]), and the comparison is instructive precisely because the two are **structurally parallel but framed on entirely different principles**:

- **Mechanism.** Indic saṃsāra is driven by impersonal *karma* — action mechanically conditions the circumstances of rebirth. Gilgul is driven by *tikkun / mitzvah-completion* — the soul returns to finish a specific commanded task or repair a specific fault, under personal divine providence rather than automatic law.
- **Shape.** Saṃsāra is a **beginningless cycle** from which liberation (*mokṣa* / *nirvāṇa*) is escape. Gilgul is **bounded and teleological** — a limited number of returns aimed at a definite completion, after which the soul rests until the general resurrection. Gilgul does not compete with resurrection; it precedes and serves it.
- **What returns.** Both affirm a persisting soul that re-enters embodied life (contrast the Buddhist *anātman* model), but Kabbalah adds a distinctive apparatus of soul-levels (*nefesh / ruach / neshamah*), soul-roots, and *ibbur*-impregnation that has no exact Indic analogue.

The relationship is **convergence / typological parallel, not transmission**. There is no documented historical channel carrying Indic saṃsāra into 12th-century Provençal Kabbalah, and Scholem treats the Jewish doctrine as internally generated — a response to theodicy and to the levirate texts — rather than borrowed. The one geographically plausible external proximity is the **Cathar** transmigration belief flourishing in Languedoc exactly where and when the Bahir emerged; a Cathar–Kabbalah contact has been speculated but never established, and the parallel to Cathar (and behind it Pythagorean-Platonic) metempsychosis is at most a shared regional atmosphere, not a demonstrated line of descent. Gilgul should therefore be wired to saṃsāra as a **contrast-and-convergence** parallel — same comparative slot (personal soul-rebirth), opposite governing principle (repair vs. karmic law) — in the same way the vault treats Pythagorean metempsychosis relative to the Indic cycle.

## Disputes

- **Normativity within Judaism.** Gilgul was fiercely contested. The rationalist tradition rejected it outright: **Saadia Gaon** (882-942), in *Emunot ve-Deot* (Book of Beliefs and Opinions) Treatise VI, catalogs transmigration among the erroneous opinions of certain sectarians and refutes it as contrary to reason and scripture — and Saadia wrote *before* the doctrine's Kabbalistic flowering, aiming at earlier (possibly Karaite or Islamic-sectarian) proponents. Maimonides and the philosophical mainstream ignored or opposed it. Kabbalists from the Bahir onward, and later Hasidism, embraced it as revealed truth. Judaism thus contains a genuine internal divide, with gilgul mainstream in the mystical stream and marginal or rejected in the rationalist and much of the modern non-Orthodox stream.
- **Origins of the medieval doctrine.** Whether gilgul's sudden 12th-century appearance reflects a purely internal Jewish development (Scholem's emphasis on theodicy and levirate exegesis), a revival of an older suppressed Jewish esoteric tradition, or contact with Cathar/Neoplatonic transmigration belief in Languedoc is unresolved; the evidence for external influence is circumstantial.
- **Scope of return.** The number of permitted gilgulim (three, per common Zoharic reading, versus unlimited under special conditions), and whether souls may transmigrate into animals, plants, or inanimate objects (affirmed in developed Lurianic Kabbalah), were debated among the Kabbalists themselves.
