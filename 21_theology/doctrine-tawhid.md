# Tawḥīd (Islamic Doctrine of Divine Unicity)

The foundational theological doctrine of Islam: the affirmation of the absolute *unicity* (not just numerical oneness) of God. *Tawḥīd* is the cornerstone of Islamic theology — every other doctrine derives from or presupposes it — and the single most-stated theological proposition in the Qur'an.

## The Qur'anic core

The most concise + canonical Qur'anic statement is Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ (Qur'an 112), four verses traditionally said to be equivalent in spiritual weight to one-third of the entire Qur'an:

> Say: He is God, One.
> God, the Eternal Refuge.
> He neither begets nor is begotten.
> Nor is there to Him any equivalent.

The four propositions function as a comprehensive set of *negations* + *affirmations*: (1) God's unity, (2) God's self-sufficiency, (3) rejection of generative-relational divine categories (with implicit polemic against Trinitarian-Christological theology), (4) rejection of any divine peer or comparable.

## The threefold classification (later kalām)

Later Islamic systematic theology, especially in the Salafi-Wahhabi tradition's articulation following Ibn Taymiyya (13th-14th c.), classifies tawḥīd into three aspects:

1. **Tawḥīd al-rubūbiyya** (unity of Lordship) — only God creates + sustains + governs the universe.
2. **Tawḥīd al-ulūhiyya** (unity of worship) — only God is the proper object of worship; *shirk* (associating partners with God) is the cardinal Islamic sin.
3. **Tawḥīd al-asmāʾ wa-l-ṣifāt** (unity of names + attributes) — God's names + attributes belong to God uniquely + are not analogically transferable.

Other schools (Ashʿarī, Māturīdī, Sufi traditions) develop the doctrine in different but related directions, including the Sufi articulation of tawḥīd as the existential-mystical realization that there is no reality but the Real (*lā mawjūda illā Llāh*) — see e.g. Ibn ʿArabī's *waḥdat al-wujūd* and the subsequent controversy.

## Cross-tradition positioning

Tawḥīd sits at the center of the Atlas's Abrahamic-monotheism cluster + offers two principal cross-tradition edges:

**Parallel to the Jewish Shema** (Deuteronomy 6:4): "Hear, O Israel: the LORD is our God, the LORD is one." The Shema is the daily-recitation credal center of Jewish liturgical life; the Shahāda (*lā ilāha illā Llāh*, "there is no god but God") plays a structurally cognate role in Islamic life. Both go beyond mere arithmetic monotheism toward affirming a deeper *unity* / *unicity*. The early Islamic community understood itself as continuous with broader Abrahamic monotheism on this axis — the Qur'anic *ahl al-kitāb* (People of the Book) doctrine specifically extends recognition to Jewish + Christian + Sabaean monotheism.

**Polemic-against Trinitarian Christology**: Qur'an 4:171 and 5:73-75 explicitly reject the formulation of "three" in the Christian Trinity. The Islamic position is that Trinitarian-Christological formulations crossed into *shirk* (association of partners with God) — making the Christian articulation, however well-intentioned, theologically defective. The Christian-Muslim theological dialogue on this question has been continuous from the 7th century to the present (Griffith 2008).
