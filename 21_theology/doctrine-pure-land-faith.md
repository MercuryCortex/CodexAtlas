# Pure Land Faith (Other-Power / Tariki)

## Identity

Pure Land faith is the Mahāyāna Buddhist doctrine that liberation is secured not by the practitioner's own accumulated merit and meditative discipline (*jiriki*, 自力, "self-power") but by entrusting faith in the already-accomplished compassion-vow of [[amitabha|Amida]] — the Buddha of Measureless Light — appropriated through the *nembutsu*, the calling of the Name (*Namu Amida Butsu*). This reliance on a saving power outside the self is *tariki* (他力, "other-power"). Its promised fruit is rebirth in Sukhāvatī, the "Pure Land of Bliss," where awakening becomes effectively assured. In the radical form given it by Shinran, the decisive moment is not the recitation itself but *shinjin* (信心), the entrusting faith that is itself Amida's mind awakened in the practitioner — so that even the faith is grace, not achievement.

This node is the **doctrine**, distinct from the [[tradition-pure-land-buddhism|Pure Land tradition]] node (the institutional/historical school) and from the [[amitabha|Amida]] node (the Buddha who is its object). In the Atlas it occupies the cross-tradition **grace-versus-works / salvation-by-faith** slot: the Buddhist member of a family that includes Christian [[justification-by-faith|justification by faith]] and Śrīvaiṣṇava *prapatti* within Hindu [[bhakti-devotion|bhakti]].

## In the Pure Land sūtras and the Kamakura reform

The doctrine's textual root is the **Primal Vow** (*hongan*) of the bodhisattva Dharmākara in the [[phase-4-104-sukhavativyuha-larger|Larger Sukhāvatī-vyūha Sūtra]] (redacted in northwest India c. 100–200 CE). Of his forty-eight vows, the **eighteenth** is the charter of other-power: that beings who "sincerely and joyfully entrust themselves" to him and call his Name even ten times shall be born in his land, or he will not accept Buddhahood. Because Dharmākara *did* attain Buddhahood as Amida, the vow is — on the Pure Land reading — already fulfilled; salvation is accomplished fact, not future reward.

The other-power interpretation is built in stages:
- **Tanluan** (曇鸞, 476–542) introduces the *tariki / jiriki* ("other-power / self-power") distinction and the "easy path" (*yi xing dao*) versus the "hard path."
- **Daochuo and Shandao** (7th c.) tie the easy path to the doctrine of *mappō* — the "Degenerate Age of the Dharma" in which self-power discipline is no longer efficacious, making Amida's power the only viable route.
- **[[honen|Hōnen]]** (1133–1212) breaks from Tendai to found Jōdo-shū (1175), teaching that the *nembutsu* alone suffices (*Senchakushū*, 1198).
- **[[shinran|Shinran]]** (1173–1263) radicalizes this into Jōdo-shinshū (*Kyōgyōshinshō*, 1224): no quantity of recitations *earns* rebirth, because the vow is already accomplished; *nembutsu* is gratitude, not effort, and *shinjin* is Amida's gift. His signature paradox is *akunin shōki* — "the evil person is the true object" of the vow: the more one grasps one's own incapacity, the more decisively one is the *intended* recipient of other-power. The *Tannishō* (c. 1290) preserves this in its most-quoted form: "Even a good person attains birth in the Pure Land, how much more so the evil person."

## Cross-tradition significance

Pure Land faith is the Buddhist occupant of the **grace-versus-works** slot, and the parallel to Protestant [[justification-by-faith|justification by faith]] is one of the most frequently drawn cross-tradition soteriological comparisons in the study of religion. The homology is precise on four points: (1) self-generated merit (*jiriki* / works of the Law) cannot save; (2) salvation is the free gift of a power outside the self (Amida's Vow / God's grace in Christ); (3) it is appropriated by entrusting faith (*shinjin* / *fides*), not by effort; (4) the saved being remains, in itself, unworthy — Shinran's *bombu* ("foolish being of blind passions") standing beside Luther's *simul iustus et peccator*. The comparison was opened by **Rudolf Otto** and made canonical by **Karl Barth**, who in *Church Dogmatics* I/2 (§17) named Jōdo-shinshū the one non-Christian faith whose grace-structure formally mirrors the Reformation — a providential "Japanese Protestantism" — precisely in order to argue that formal parallelism proves nothing, since the *material* difference is the Name of Christ.

The Indic analogue is **prapatti** (*śaraṇāgati*, "self-surrender") in Śrīvaiṣṇava [[bhakti-devotion|bhakti]]. The tradition's own **cat-hold / monkey-hold** debate crystallizes the same axis: the Teṅgalai "cat-hold" (*mārjāra-nyāya* — the kitten carried passively by its mother, pure grace) versus the Vaḍagalai "monkey-hold" (*markaṭa-nyāya* — the infant that must cling, cooperative effort). This maps directly onto Shinran's absolute *tariki* versus the residual self-power of easier Pure Land readings. Otto's *India's Religion of Grace* (1930) first gathered Amidism, Rāmānuja's grace-theology, and Christian *sola gratia* into a single "religion of grace" family.

**The honest frame is convergence, not transmission.** There is no historical contact linking 13th-century Kamakura Japan, 11th–12th-century Śrīvaiṣṇava South India, and 16th-century Wittenberg. The striking datum is exactly that a grace-versus-works polarity emerges *independently* in three unconnected traditions — a case of structural / typological parallel, and one of the strongest exhibits for the comparativist claim that the "religion of grace" is a recurrent human religious type rather than a single diffused idea.

## Disputes

- **Is the parallel real or a projection?** [[justification-by-faith|Justification]]-shaped readings of Pure Land are open to the charge of **Orientalism**. Galen Amstutz (*Interpreting Amida*, 1997) argues that Western scholarship pre-fitted Pure Land into a Protestant template — "the faith tradition of Buddhism" — distorting a tradition whose own categories (*mappō*, merit-transfer, ritual *nembutsu*, the buddha-*field* cosmology) are not those of Reformation soteriology. On this view the *tariki* / *sola gratia* likeness is partly an artefact of the comparativist's own frame.
- **Faith versus recitation.** Whether the operative cause of rebirth is the *act* of *nembutsu* (Hōnen's emphasis; the older Chinese tradition) or the inner *shinjin* that the recitation only expresses (Shinran's radicalization) is the central internal fault-line. Shinran's move — making even faith Amida's gift rather than the practitioner's response — is the point at which Pure Land becomes a doctrine of *absolute* grace, and the point most exactly parallel to Luther's *sola gratia*.
- **The Pure Land: real or expedient.** Shandao–Hōnen–Shinran treat Sukhāvatī as a real destination reached by rebirth; Chan/Zen-leaning syntheses (Yongming Yanshou, c. 960) read it as "mind-only," accessed by direct realization — collapsing other-power back toward self-power. The doctrine's identity depends on which reading holds.
