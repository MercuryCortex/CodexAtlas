# Advaita Vedānta

**Advaita Vedānta** is the non-dualist (*a-dvaita* = "not-two") systematic theology of Brahmanical Hinduism — the doctrinal position that Brahman (the ultimate) and Ātman (the self) are non-different, and that the apparent plurality of the world is *māyā* (the cosmic appearance) sustained by *avidyā* (ignorance). It is the dominant philosophical-theological articulation of Vedānta and the largest single school within Hindu *darśana* (philosophical-theological systems) from the 8th c. to the present, formalised by [[adi-shankara|Ādi Śaṅkara]] (c. 788–820 CE) on a base of Upaniṣadic exegesis (the *prasthāna-trayī* — Upaniṣads + Brahma-Sūtra + Bhagavad-Gītā — read through Gauḍapāda's *Māṇḍūkya-kārikā*).

## The doctrinal core

Five linked propositions constitute the canonical Advaita position as systematised by Śaṅkara:

1. **Brahman alone is real** (*brahma satyaṁ*). Brahman is *nirguṇa* (without qualities, beyond predication) and *nirviśeṣa* (without distinctions). All predications of Brahman are concessions to discursive ignorance — Brahman is in itself *neti neti* ("not this, not this"; Bṛhadāraṇyaka 2.3.6).
2. **The world is appearance** (*jagan mithyā*) — neither real (*sat*) nor unreal (*asat*), but *anirvacanīya* (indescribable), supported by *māyā* / *avidyā*. The standard analogy is the rope mistaken for a snake (Śaṅkara, *Brahma-Sūtra-Bhāṣya* 1.1.4 + 2.1.14).
3. **The individual self is identical with Brahman** (*jīvo brahmaiva nāparaḥ*). The apparent multiplicity of individual selves is a function of *upādhi* (limiting adjuncts — bodies, mind-complexes); the *jīva*'s essential nature is the universal Self.
4. **Liberation is the recognition of this non-difference** (*mokṣa* = the *jñāna* / direct knowledge that Brahman-Ātman is non-dual). Liberation is not a future state but the recognition of one's already-existent identity with Brahman — the famous distinction between *prāpti* (acquiring something new) and *prāptasya prāpti* (recognising what one already is).
5. **The Mahāvākyas** (the four "great sayings" of the Upaniṣads) are the scriptural ground: *tat tvam asi* ("That thou art" — Chāndogya 6.8.7), *ahaṁ brahmāsmi* ("I am Brahman" — Bṛhadāraṇyaka 1.4.10), *prajñānaṁ brahma* ("consciousness is Brahman" — Aitareya 3.3), *ayam ātmā brahma* ("this self is Brahman" — Māṇḍūkya 2).

## The two-truths hermeneutic

Śaṅkara distinguishes *pāramārthika satya* (ultimate truth — Brahman as nirguṇa) from *vyāvahārika satya* (conventional truth — the world of plurality, ritual, ethics, devotion to a personal *saguṇa* Brahman, i.e. Īśvara). The ritual-devotional content of the Veda is preserved at the conventional level; the ultimate Upaniṣadic teaching dissolves it. The parallel with Mādhyamika *saṁvṛti-paramārtha-satya* is structurally striking — and the Buddhist-Advaita historical relationship has been the central philological problem in Advaita studies since Bhattacharya 1943 and Nakamura 1983.

## The sub-schools

The Bhāmatī school (Vācaspati Miśra) and the Vivaraṇa school (Padmapāda → Prakāśātman) divide medieval Advaita on the locus and nature of *avidyā* — whether ignorance inheres in the *jīva* (Bhāmatī) or in Brahman-as-known (Vivaraṇa). The dispute looks scholastic from the outside but matters for the soteriological mechanism.

## Cross-tradition resonances

The non-dualist core of Advaita has been the most-cited Hindu doctrine in cross-tradition mystical-comparative work since the 19th c. — Schopenhauer, Deussen, Otto, Stace, Forman. Structural parallels have been drawn to:

- **Plotinian henōsis** (return to the One; *Enneads* VI.9) — the soul's recognition of its non-difference from the One.
- **Eckhart's Godhead-ground** — *Gott und Seelengrund* identity; explicitly condemned 1329 (In agro dominico).
- **Ibn ʿArabī's waḥdat al-wujūd** (oneness of being) — the doctrinal centerpiece of post-13th-c. Sufism, with explicit Advaitin parallels recognised in early-modern Mughal-era Hindu-Muslim comparative work (Dara Shikoh, *Sirr-i-Akbar*; *Majma-ul-Bahrayn*).
- **Mahāyāna-Madhyamaka śūnyatā** — Gauḍapāda's *ajātivāda* (book IV of the *Māṇḍūkya-kārikā*) is structurally Nāgārjunian.

Halbfass 1991 and Hacker 1995 caution against flattening these into a single "perennial philosophy" (the Schuon-Smith-Forman strand) — each tradition's structural-doctrinal commitments differ on key questions (the status of the world, the role of grace, the relation of the personal to the impersonal absolute). But the family resemblance is undeniable and historically generative.
