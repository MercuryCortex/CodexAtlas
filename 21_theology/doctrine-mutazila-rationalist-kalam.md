# Muʿtazila — Rationalist Islamic Kalām

The **Muʿtazila** (Arabic: *al-muʿtazila*, "those who withdraw") is the foundational rationalist school of Islamic systematic theology (*kalām*), originating in 8th-c. Basra with Wāṣil ibn ʿAṭāʾ (d. 748) and crystallised in the *uṣūl al-khamsa* (Five Principles) of ʿAbd al-Jabbār (d. 1024). It is the systematic-theological articulation of *ʿaql-priority* (reason-prioritising) Islam — the position that the rational obligations of moral knowledge, divine justice, and the moral knowability of the good are independent of revelation, and that revelation is intelligible because reason and revelation share the same ultimately rational ground.

## The Five Principles (*uṣūl al-khamsa*)

The canonical doctrinal core, systematised by ʿAbd al-Jabbār:

1. **al-Tawḥīd** (divine unity) — God is utterly one, simple, and incomposite. The divine attributes are not real entities superadded to the essence (which would compromise unity); they are predicable of the essence directly. The most controversial corollary is the doctrine of the *created Qurʾān* (*khalq al-Qurʾān*) — if the Qurʾān were eternal as Speech of God it would constitute a second eternal alongside God, violating strict tawḥīd. This was the doctrine the ʿAbbāsid caliph al-Maʾmūn imposed via the *miḥna* (833–848).
2. **al-ʿAdl** (divine justice) — God's justice is *objective*: good and evil are intrinsic to acts, knowable by unaided reason, and God necessarily wills only the good. God cannot do evil (not because God is constrained but because God's nature is just). This is the Muʿtazilī *ethical objectivism* / *rationalist ethics*, in direct opposition to Ashʿarī *theological voluntarism* (good = whatever God commands).
3. **al-Waʿd wa-l-Waʿīd** (the promise and the threat) — God's eschatological promises and threats are binding on God. A believer who dies with unrepented major sin must enter the Fire (else divine justice fails); a believer who repents must enter Paradise (else the promise fails).
4. **al-Manzila bayna al-manzilatayn** (the intermediate position) — the grave sinner (*fāsiq*) is neither believer nor unbeliever but in an intermediate category. This was Wāṣil's foundational doctrinal split from al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī's circle (per the doxographies, hence the school's name: "those who withdrew" from al-Ḥasan over this question).
5. **al-Amr bi-l-maʿrūf wa-l-nahy ʿan al-munkar** (commanding the right and forbidding the wrong) — the active social-political obligation to enforce justice. This is the principle that grounded Muʿtazilī involvement in ʿAbbāsid state-formation and the rationale for the *miḥna* inquisition.

## The miḥna (833–848)

Under al-Maʾmūn (and his successors al-Muʿtaṣim and al-Wāthiq), the ʿAbbāsid state imposed the Muʿtazilī doctrine of the *created Qurʾān* on the judiciary and the ʿulamāʾ by mandatory test. The Ḥanbalī jurist Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal (780–855) became the iconic resister — flogged and imprisoned but refusing to assent — and the *miḥna*'s eventual reversal under al-Mutawakkil (848) was a watershed: the rationalist-state-imposed creed lost, the traditionalist-popular-resisted creed won, and the long Sunni shift toward Ashʿarī-traditionalist synthesis was set in motion. The *miḥna* is one of the most-studied episodes in pre-modern Islamic political theology (Hinds, Madelung, Cooperson, Nawas).

## The school's historical trajectory

The 9th-10th-c. classical period (Basran + Baghdadi schools) gives way to the post-11th-c. Ashʿarī ascendance in Sunni territory; Muʿtazilism is largely absorbed or driven underground. But the school survives:
- **In Shīʿī Twelver kalām** — the rationalist legacy is preserved via the Buyid-era Imāmī engagement (al-Shaykh al-Mufīd, al-Sharīf al-Murtaḍā), and Twelver kalām remains structurally Muʿtazilī-rationalist in its theology of the divine attributes + the doctrine of divine justice to the present.
- **In Zaydī Yemen** — the Yemeni Zaydī tradition retained classical Muʿtazilī kalām more or less unchanged; the Yemeni manuscript corpus is the single largest preserved Muʿtazilī archive (the Schmidtke-led Yemen project of the 2000s-2020s).
- **In the modern Sunni Muʿtazilī revival** — Amīn al-Khūlī, Naṣr Ḥāmid Abū Zayd, Ḥasan Ḥanafī, and others self-consciously retrieve the rationalist heritage as the indigenous Islamic resource for modernist + reformist + post-colonial engagement with text and reason.

## Cross-tradition resonances

- **Neoplatonic + Maimonidean apophaticism.** The Muʿtazilī reduction of the divine attributes to the essence is structurally parallel to Maimonides's *Guide* I.50-60 doctrine of attributes-as-negations + the broader Neoplatonic apophatic tradition. The historical mediation runs through the 9th-c. Baghdad translation movement.
- **Stoic + Aristotelian rationalist ethics.** Muʿtazilī ethical objectivism (good and evil are intrinsic to acts, knowable by reason) is structurally parallel to Stoic natural law + the Aristotelian *to kalon* tradition, mediated through the same translation movement.
- **Calvinist anti-voluntarism.** The Reformed insistence on divine attributes as really inhering in God + the rejection of pure theological voluntarism shares structural moves with the Muʿtazilī position against Ashʿarī voluntarism — though the substantive theological commitments differ sharply elsewhere.
