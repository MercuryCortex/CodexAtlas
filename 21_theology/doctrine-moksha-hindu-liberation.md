# Mokṣa (Hindu Doctrine of Liberation from Saṃsāra)

The central soteric goal of all Hindu darśanas: **liberation from the beginningless cycle of birth-and-death** (*saṃsāra*) and from the karmic-conditioning that propels it. Mokṣa (Sanskrit *mukti*, "release") is the fourth and ultimate *puruṣārtha* — the human aim that transcends the other three (dharma, artha, kāma). What mokṣa *is* (identity with Brahman; isolation of puruṣa from prakṛti; eternal participation with Viṣṇu) divides the principal Vedānta schools; *that* it is the religious goal is shared across the tradition.

## The Upaniṣadic articulation

Pre-Upaniṣadic Vedic religion (~-1500 to -800) has no doctrine of liberation-from-rebirth. The Vedic soteric goal is *svarga* — heavenly afterlife secured through proper sacrifice. The shift to the saṃsāra-mokṣa framework is one of the **great religious hinge-events of Indian history**, occurring in the early Upaniṣads (~-800 to -500) in parallel with the contemporaneous Buddhist and Jain articulations of the same problem.

The locus classicus passages:

- **Bṛhadāraṇyaka 4.4**: "When all the desires that dwell in his heart are gone, then he, having been mortal, becomes immortal, and attains Brahman in this very life" — *jīvanmukti* (liberation-while-living).
- **Chāndogya 6**: Uddālaka Āruṇi's nine-fold "*tat tvam asi*" teaching: the ātman (innermost self) IS Brahman (ultimate reality); realization of this identity *is* mokṣa.
- **Kaṭha 2**: the chariot allegory — buddhi as charioteer, mind as reins, senses as horses; liberation through self-knowledge.

## The triple-path schematization (Bhagavad Gītā, ~-200)

The Gītā provides the threefold-path systematization that becomes canonical:

1. **Jñāna-yoga** — the path of knowledge (Upaniṣadic-Advaitic).
2. **Karma-yoga** — the path of action without attachment to fruits.
3. **Bhakti-yoga** — the path of devotion to Viṣṇu/Kṛṣṇa.

The Gītā argues all three lead to mokṣa; the medieval Vedānta schools elevate one or another as the principal path.

## The darśana variations on mokṣa

The six classical Hindu darśanas + the principal Vedānta sub-schools interpret mokṣa differently:

- **Sāṃkhya-Yoga** — *kaivalya* ("isolation"): puruṣa (pure-consciousness) released from prakṛti (matter-mind). Patañjali's *Yoga Sūtras* IV.34. Strictly dualistic — no theistic union.
- **Advaita Vedānta** ([[shankara]], ~800) — mokṣa = realization of ātman-Brahman non-dual identity. The empirical-individual-self is *māyā*-overlay; in liberation, only Brahman remains. See [[doctrine-advaita-vedanta]].
- **Viśiṣṭādvaita** ([[ramanuja]], ~1100) — mokṣa = eternal conscious-participation in Viṣṇu; soul's individuality preserved. See [[doctrine-vishishtadvaita-qualified-non-dualism]].
- **Dvaita** (Madhva, ~1250) — mokṣa = eternal hierarchical distinction from God; jīva remains ontologically subordinate. See [[doctrine-dvaita-dualism-madhva]].
- **Mīmāṃsā** (Kumārila, Prabhākara) — late tradition admits mokṣa as cessation of karmic-fruition + eternal-bliss.
- **Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika** — mokṣa = cessation of suffering through liberation; the liberated self is conscious but free of qualia.

## Cross-tradition positioning

**MASSIVE-WIN parallel — Buddhist nirvāṇa**: Hindu mokṣa and Buddhist nirvāṇa emerge in the **same Gangetic-plain ascetic-renunciate context** (~-500 to -200) addressing the same shared problem: release from saṃsāra. Bronkhorst 2007 (*Greater Magadha*) makes the structural argument that both develop from a common eastern-Gangetic non-Vedic substrate. The principal asymmetries:

- Mokṣa typically affirms a true-Self that is realized in liberation (ātman = Brahman; puruṣa-isolation; eternal participation).
- Nirvāṇa explicitly *denies* the metaphysical self (anātman) and frames the goal as **cessation of craving** (taṇhā-nirodha), not realization of any positive Self-identity.

The Hindu-Buddhist debate on this point is the central inter-tradition theological dispute in classical Indian philosophy (Śaṅkara vs. Buddhist Mādhyamika; Rāmānuja's critique of Advaita-Buddhist convergence).

**MASSIVE-WIN parallel — Eastern Orthodox theōsis**: Advaita-mokṣa (ātman-Brahman identity-realization) and Eastern Orthodox theōsis (participation in divine nature) are the **two most-developed religious doctrines of human-divine ontological-union** as the soteric goal. Both reject the Western juridical-atonement framework. The comparative-theology project (Otto 1932; Panikkar 1979; Coward 1985) treats this as one of the deepest cross-tradition convergences in world religion.

**vs. Western Christian satisfaction-atonement**: mokṣa has no concept of debt-to-be-paid, no honor-of-deity-offended, no vicarious-substitution. The Hindu soteric problem is *avidyā* (ignorance) and karmic-conditioning, not legal-guilt. The mokṣa-mechanism is *jñāna* (knowledge) + ethical purification + devotional practice — *self*-transformation rather than substitutionary-payment.

## Disputes within Hinduism

- **Jīvanmukti vs videhamukti** — is liberation possible while embodied (Advaita), or only at death (Mīmāṃsā, some Dvaita)?
- **Mokṣa for all or for some** — universalist (most schools) vs predestinarian (some Madhva-school readings positing eternal-damnation for some souls).
- **Mokṣa as bliss or as featureless** — Advaita's "Brahman is non-dual consciousness" vs Viśiṣṭādvaita's "mokṣa includes positive divine-experience."
