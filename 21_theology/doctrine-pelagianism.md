# Pelagianism (the doctrine of unaided free will and the denial of inherited guilt)

The 5th-century Latin doctrine — named for the ascetic teacher **Pelagius** (~360–~418) and systematised by his disciple **Caelestius** and, philosophically, by **Julian of Eclanum** — that human beings retain, after the Fall, an intact God-given capacity of free will (*posse*) by which they can take the first step toward the good and toward salvation *without a prior, interior gift of grace*, and that Adam's sin injured only Adam, transmitting to his descendants a bad **example** rather than an inherited **guilt**. It is the LOSING side of the controversy that defined the Western doctrine of grace by opposition — the exact structural counterpart to the way Arianism defined Nicene orthodoxy. Condemned at Carthage (418) and ecumenically at Ephesus (431); its mediating afterlife, **Semi-Pelagianism**, was condemned at the Second Council of Orange (529).

## The doctrine and its controversy (405–431)

Pelagius, a lay ascetic of British or Irish origin, taught in Rome from the 380s and won a reputation for rigorous moral seriousness. His theology grew from a pastoral conviction: that a genuine command implies a genuine ability to obey it. If God commands holiness, holiness must be *possible* by the powers God has given — otherwise the command, and moral responsibility itself, is a fiction. The tradition holds that Pelagius was scandalised by a line in Augustine's *Confessions* (X.29): *"Give what you command, and command what you will"* (*da quod iubes et iube quod vis*) — which seemed to him to dissolve human effort into divine determinism.

The system that his opponents labelled "Pelagianism" turns on a distinction Pelagius drew between three things: the **capacity** (*posse*) to do good, which is God's permanent gift to human nature; the **willing** (*velle*) of it; and the **doing** (*esse*) of it. The capacity is inamissible and grace-given at creation; the willing and doing are the human being's own. Grace, in this scheme, is real but largely **external and enabling from without**: the gift of a good nature, the illumination of the Law, the moral example of Christ, and the forgiveness of past sins in baptism — not an interior force that itself produces the good will.

From this follow the propositions condemned at the **Council of Carthage (411)** against Caelestius:

1. Adam was created **mortal** and would have died whether or not he sinned.
2. Adam's sin **harmed only himself**, not the human race.
3. Newborn infants are in the **same state as Adam before the Fall** — they are not born guilty.
4. The human race does not die through Adam's sin, nor rise through Christ's resurrection.
5. The **Law**, no less than the Gospel, admits to the Kingdom.
6. Even before Christ there were human beings **entirely without sin**.

Because infants inherit no guilt, Caelestius drew the inference that most alarmed the African bishops: infant baptism cannot be *for the remission of an inherited sin* — a conclusion that struck at settled sacramental practice and made the controversy explosive rather than academic.

## Condemnation and the Semi-Pelagian afterlife

The Sack of Rome (410) scattered Pelagius and Caelestius to Carthage, into Augustine's own province, and the clash became institutional. Caelestius was condemned at **Carthage (411)**. Pelagius, tried in the East, was **acquitted at the Synod of Diospolis (Lydda, 415)** — a result that infuriated the Africans, who reconvened at **Carthage and Milevis (416)** and secured Pope **Innocent I's** condemnation (417). Innocent's successor **Zosimus** at first reinstated Pelagius on the strength of his *Libellus fidei*, then reversed himself; the **Council of Carthage (418)** issued the decisive canons (affirming inherited sin and the necessity of infant baptism), backed by Emperor **Honorius's** civil rescript expelling the Pelagians, and Zosimus's *Epistula Tractoria* made the condemnation universal. The **Council of Ephesus (431)** condemned Pelagianism ecumenically, coupling it with Nestorianism.

The doctrine did not die but softened into **Semi-Pelagianism** among the monks of southern Gaul — the "Massilians." **John Cassian** (*Conferences* XIII, c. 426), later Vincent of Lérins and Faustus of Riez, accepted that grace is necessary for salvation but held that the *initium fidei* — the first movement toward faith — can begin in the human will, with grace then cooperating. Against them **Prosper of Aquitaine** defended Augustine's insistence that grace precedes even the desire to believe. The **Second Council of Orange (529)**, convened by Caesarius of Arles and ratified by Pope Boniface II (531), closed the controversy: it affirmed prevenient grace against the Massilians while pointedly declining to endorse Augustine's harshest double-predestination. The name "Semi-Pelagian" is anachronistic — a coinage of the late-16th/17th-century Molinist–Jansenist disputes read back onto the Gauls, who saw themselves as defenders of tradition.

## Cross-tradition significance (the libertarian pole of the grace-vs-free-will axis)

Pelagianism is the Atlas's Latin-Christian occupant of the **libertarian free-will slot** in the trans-traditional controversy over divine sovereignty and human agency ([[free-will-vs-determinism]]). Its structural counterpart in Islam is the **Qadarī–Mu'tazilī** doctrine of *qadar* — that human beings genuinely originate their own acts and are therefore justly rewarded or punished — set against the predestinarian **Jabriyya** and the Ash'arī compromise of *kasb* ("acquisition"), just as Pelagian free will stands against Augustinian prevenient grace (see [[doctrine-mutazila-rationalist-kalam]] and [[doctrine-asharism-traditionalist-kalam]]).

The comparison must be stated carefully. The resemblance is **structural convergence, not transmission**: the Basran *qadar* debate arises in the 8th century with no plausible line of contact with the 5th-century Latin controversy, and the two movements reason from wholly different scriptural bases — Romans 5 and Genesis 3 on one side, Qur'anic theodicy on the other. What recurs across both is the *problem-shape*, not any borrowing: a monotheism that affirms both an omnipotent, provident God and a genuinely responsible human agent is forced toward one of two poles, and both traditions independently produced a libertarian party defined against a determinist one (W. Montgomery Watt, *Free Will and Predestination in Early Islam*, 1948). At the opposite, deterministic pole sits the Qumran **Two-Spirits** doctrine, in which humans are sorted between the spirits of light and darkness from creation ([[two-spirits-doctrine]]).

Within Christianity the controversy is a taproot rather than a closed episode. The grace-vs-free-will problem it framed resurfaces at every major turn of Western theology: the **Reformation** (Luther's *De servo arbitrio* against Erasmus's *De libero arbitrio*, 1524–25), the Catholic **Molinist–Bañezian** *De Auxiliis* dispute, **Jansenism** (which accused the Jesuits of reviving Pelagianism), and **Arminianism** (accused in turn by the Reformed at Dort). "Pelagian" and "Semi-Pelagian" survive to the present as the standard theological epithets for any doctrine thought to concede too much to unaided human initiative.

## Disputes

- **What did Pelagius actually teach?** Gerald Bonner (1972) and B.R. Rees (1988) stress that much of "Pelagianism" is a *construct of its opponents* — especially of Augustine and Jerome — and that Pelagius's own surviving works (the Romans commentary, the *Letter to Demetrias*) are more moderate than the radicalised system of Caelestius or the polemics attributed to him. How much of the condemned doctrine Pelagius himself held is genuinely uncertain.
- **Romans 5:12.** The whole controversy turns on *eph' hō pantes hēmarton* — Pelagius reads sin spreading "by imitation," Augustine (via the Vulgate's *in quo*) "by descent." The Latin mistranslation is a contingency that hardened the Western inherited-guilt reading; see [[doctrine-original-sin-augustinian]].
- **Julian of Eclanum.** The most formidable Pelagian, Julian charged Augustine with **crypto-Manichaeism** — with smuggling a substantial evil (concupiscence, the fallen body) back into creation under a Christian name. Modern scholarship (Brown 1967) treats Julian's critique as the sharpest ancient reading of the Manichaean residue in Augustine's anthropology, even while judging the Church's verdict against him final.
- **"Semi-Pelagianism" as a label.** Rebecca Weaver (1996) and others argue the term is misleading: the Massilians were not "half-Pelagians" but a distinct traditionalist party, and applying a 16th-century polemical coinage to them distorts the 5th–6th-century debate.
- **The qadar parallel.** Whether the Christian and Islamic free-will debates should be compared at all, or only contrasted, is itself contested; the mainstream (Watt 1948; Ormsby 1984) treats them as independent convergences under shared monotheistic pressures rather than as a single transmitted problem.
