# Khalq al-Qurʾān — The Createdness of the Qurʾān

## Identity

**Khalq al-Qurʾān** (Arabic *خلق القرآن*, "the creation of the Qurʾān") is the doctrine — held by the [[doctrine-mutazila-rationalist-kalam|Muʿtazila]] and their forerunners the *Jahmiyya* — that the Qurʾān is **created** (*makhlūq*): an originated act of divine speech, not an entity co-eternal with God. Its denial — that the Qurʾān is **uncreated** (*ghayr makhlūq*), the eternal Speech of God (*kalām Allāh*) — became the normative Sunni position, defended by the Ḥanbalī traditionalists and later given systematic form by the [[doctrine-asharism-traditionalist-kalam|Ashʿariyya]] as the doctrine of *kalām nafsī* (eternal inner speech). The controversy is the doctrinal core of the *miḥna* (the ʿAbbāsid inquisition of 833–848), one of the most consequential episodes of state-enforced theology in pre-modern Islam.

The question is deceptively simple and structurally deep: the Qurʾān is the Speech of God; is the Speech of God, like God, eternal — or is it, like the world, created? To call it created seemed to the Muʿtazila the only way to preserve strict [[doctrine-tawhid|tawḥīd]] (divine unicity), since an eternal Qurʾān would be a second eternal existent beside God. To call it uncreated seemed to the traditionalists the only way to honour the perfection of God's own Word, which cannot be a mere creature. The Atlas places khalq al-Qurʾān beside the Christian [[theme-logos-theology|Logos]] and [[doctrine-trinity-nicene|Nicene/Arian]] controversies because it is the same question — the eternal-versus-created status of the divine Word — argued to the same crux in a different tradition.

## In the Islamic tradition

The doctrine of a created Qurʾān is attributed by the heresiographers first to **Jaʿd ibn Dirham** (d. c. 724) and **Jahm ibn Ṣafwān** (d. 746), from whom traditionalists derived the polemical label *Jahmiyya* for all who held it. It was absorbed and systematised by the Muʿtazila as a corollary of their first principle, *al-tawḥīd*: God's attributes are not real entities superadded to the essence, and God's Speech in particular cannot be an eternal hypostasis, on pain of admitting a second eternal. The Qurʾān is therefore *muḥdath* (originated) and *makhlūq* (created) — God's act, not God's co-eternal.

The doctrine's decisive moment is the **miḥna**. In 833 the caliph **al-Maʾmūn**, months before his death, issued a series of letters requiring judges, jurists, and traditionists to affirm on interrogation that the Qurʾān was created; those who refused were barred from office and, in some cases, imprisoned or flogged. His successors al-Muʿtaṣim and al-Wāthiq continued the test. The iconic resister was the traditionist **Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal** (780–855), who under interrogation and flogging refused to assent, insisting the Qurʾān is the uncreated Word of God, and became for later Sunnism the very model of the scholar who withstands the ruler. The miḥna was abandoned by **al-Mutawakkil** around 848–851; the reversal was a watershed. The rationalist creed backed by the caliphal state had lost; the traditionalist creed defended by the *ʿulamāʾ* had won; and religious authority in Sunni Islam was decisively confirmed as residing with the scholars of hadith rather than with the caliph.

The mature kalām resolution belongs to **al-Ashʿarī** (874–936), who defended the uncreated Qurʾān while defusing the Muʿtazilī reductio. He distinguished *kalām nafsī* — the eternal, uncreated Speech subsisting in the divine essence — from *kalām lafẓī*, the created and temporal ink, sound, and human recitation in which that eternal Speech is expressed. The eternal meaning is uncreated; the physical marks on the page and the reciter's voice are created. This became the mainstream Sunni settlement and remains normative; the question stays live at the seam between traditionalist and rationalist / modernist Islam, and was reopened in the 20th century by the Muʿtazilī-revival readings of the Qurʾān as a historically situated text.

## Cross-tradition significance

The createdness controversy is the Islamic member of a cross-tradition family of disputes over the **ontological status of the divine Word** — and its closest structural partner is the Christian **Logos / Arian controversy**. In fourth-century Christianity the question was whether the Word (the Logos, the Son) is co-eternal and consubstantial (*homoousios*) with the Father, as the Council of [[event-council-of-nicaea-325|Nicaea (325)]] and the [[doctrine-trinity-nicene|Nicene]] settlement held, or whether the Word is the first and highest of God's creatures — Arius's formula, "there was a time when he was not." In ninth-century Islam the question was whether the Speech of God is eternal or created. The doctrinal geometry is identical: the **uncreated / eternal Word** (Nicene Logos ≈ traditionalist Qurʾān) versus the **created Word** (Arian Logos ≈ Muʿtazilī Qurʾān). In both traditions the eternal-Word verdict became orthodoxy.

**Harry Austryn Wolfson** (*The Philosophy of the Kalam*, 1976, ch. 3) made this alignment the centrepiece of a **transmission thesis**: the Muslim debate over the eternity of the divine attributes, and of Speech in particular, was shaped by contact with Christian Trinitarian and Logos theology in the bilingual, inter-confessional milieu of Umayyad Damascus and ʿAbbāsid Baghdad — where John of Damascus already wrote on Muslim views and where Christian *mutakallimūn* argued in Arabic. On this reading the Islamic dispute inherits categories worked out earlier in the Christological controversies. The **convergence thesis**, associated with **Madelung** (1974) and **van Ess**, locates the doctrine's genesis inside Islam — in the radical transcendence-theology of Jahm ibn Ṣafwān and the internal logic of tawḥīd applied to the divine attributes — and treats the resemblance to the Logos debate as the natural result of two strict monotheisms being forced onto the same structurally narrow question (what is the status of a Word that is God's own?). The honest state of the question: the **structural parallel is exact and undisputed; the historical dependence is contested.** What is secure is that Muslim and Christian theologians in ʿAbbāsid Iraq were in direct, mutually aware contact (Griffith 2008), so the milieu for influence certainly existed — but the internal Islamic drivers are sufficient to explain the doctrine without it.

The doctrine also completes the cosmogonic [[creation-by-word|creation-by-word]] cluster. The Word that speaks the world into being — Ptah's creative utterance, the Hebrew *dābār*, the Johannine Logos, the Qurʾānic *kun fa-yakūn* — provokes, once posited, the further question of its own status. Khalq al-Qurʾān is the tradition that pressed that question hardest: not only *does* God create by speaking, but *what is that Speech* — creature or eternal?

## Disputes

Two disputes are worth distinguishing. The **internal** dispute is the original one — created versus uncreated — settled in favour of uncreatedness for mainstream Sunnism, with the Ashʿarī *kalām nafsī* distinction as the enabling move, though the strict-Ḥanbalī / later Salafī tradition regards even the kalām formulation as an over-philosophising of a plain traditionalist truth, and modern rationalist readers revive the created-text position for the purposes of historical-critical Qurʾānic study.

The **scholarly** dispute concerns the cross-tradition parallel itself. Wolfson's transmission thesis — that the attribute debates, and the Word-debate specifically, were formed under Christian theological influence — is the maximal claim; Madelung's and van Ess's careful reconstructions of the intra-Islamic pedigree (Jaʿd → Jahm → Muʿtazila) are the minimal, convergence-friendly counter. Most current scholarship treats the parallel as a genuine typological homology whose historical mechanism is under-determined by the evidence: the **shared question and the shared answer-space are real; who taught whom, or whether anyone did, remains open.**
