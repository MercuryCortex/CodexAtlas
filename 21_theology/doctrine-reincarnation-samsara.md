# Reincarnation / Saṃsāra (Indic Doctrine of Cyclic Rebirth)

The foundational eschatological doctrine of all classical Indic religious traditions (Hindu, Buddhist, Jain, Sikh): the **beginningless cycle of birth-death-rebirth** through which sentient beings transmigrate according to the karmic-residue of their actions. Sanskrit *saṃsāra* literally means "**wandering through**" — the cyclic existence from which liberation (mokṣa / nirvāṇa) is the sought escape. The doctrine emerges in the early Upaniṣads (~-800 to -500) and is shared across the four classical Indic traditions, with significant doctrinal variations on the nature of what reincarnates.

## The Upaniṣadic emergence

Pre-Upaniṣadic Vedic religion (~-1500 to -800) has no rebirth doctrine. The Vedic soteric goal is *svarga* — heavenly afterlife secured through proper sacrifice. The transition to the saṃsāra framework is one of the **great religious hinge-events of Indian history**, occurring in parallel with (and shared between) the emerging Brahmanical Upaniṣadic, Buddhist, and Jain traditions ~-800 to -500.

The earliest articulated rebirth-theory is the **pañcāgni-vidyā** ("doctrine of the five fires") found in two Upaniṣadic passages:

- **Bṛhadāraṇyaka 6.2** — Yājñavalkya teaches Pravāhaṇa Jaivali the doctrine.
- **Chāndogya 5.10** — Parallel formulation.

The doctrine traces the soul's post-mortem journey: through the funeral fire, into smoke, cloud, rain — re-entering the food-cycle and being reborn through procreative-fire. The cosmological mechanism is fully developed by the late-Vedic period.

## The Bhagavad Gītā's canonical articulation

The Gītā (~-200) provides the canonical popular-Hindu formulation. Bhagavad Gītā 2.22 is the locus classicus:

> "As a man, casting off worn-out garments, takes new ones, so the embodied [self], casting off worn-out bodies, enters into others that are new."

The Gītā's framing — death is body-change, the ātman is unchanged through transmigration — becomes the dominant Hindu understanding.

## The four-fold Indic variation

The four classical Indic traditions agree on *cyclic-rebirth-driven-by-karma* but differ on the metaphysics of what reincarnates:

1. **Hindu (Brahmanical)** — an *ātman* (true Self) transmigrates through embodied lives; in Vedānta, the ātman is identical with Brahman (Advaita) or eternally distinct (Dvaita) or qualified (Viśiṣṭādvaita).
2. **Buddhist** — *anātman* (no-self) doctrine denies a persisting self; what continues is a stream of karmically-conditioned consciousness-moments. The pratītya-samutpāda chain explains the mechanism without a substrate. See [[doctrine-nirvana-buddhist-cessation]].
3. **Jain** — the *jīva* (soul) is a separate substance bound by karmic-particles (sticky-matter); liberation = stripping off karmic-matter through ascetic-practice.
4. **Sikh** — saṃsāra is real but the goal is union with the formless One (Akāl Purakh) through devotion + gurmukh-living; reincarnation is the consequence of haumai (ego).

## The karma-rebirth mechanism

In all Indic traditions, **karma** (`doctrine-karma-action-fruit`) is the mechanism: actions produce karmic-residue (*saṃskāra*); the residue determines the conditions of future rebirth (realm — god/human/animal/hungry-ghost/hell; circumstances; lifespan; suffering or wellbeing). The Mānava Dharmaśāstra 12.40-103 provides a developed taxonomy of post-mortem destinations correlated with classes of karmic-acts.

## Pre-Indic and cross-cultural parallels

The doctrine is **not exclusively Indic**. Pre-modern parallels:

- **Greek Pythagorean-Platonic metempsychosis** — Pythagoras (~-550), Empedocles, Plato (*Phaedo*, *Phaedrus*, *Republic* X). Independent development or shared substrate? Long-debated; Obeyesekere 2002 treats comparatively.
- **Orphic mysteries** — soul-transmigration as the *prison-house of the body* (*sōma-sēma*) escape narrative.
- **Pre-modern Amerindian** — Obeyesekere 2002 documents rebirth-beliefs in pre-Columbian Inuit, Pueblo, and Andean traditions, independent of Old-World transmission.
- **Druidic** — Caesar (*Bellum Gallicum* VI) reports the Gallic druids taught soul-transmigration; the Pythagorean-Druidic connection is speculatively-traced in classical and modern sources.
- **Manichaean** — adopts Indian-derived rebirth schema integrated with Iranian-Gnostic dualism.

## Cross-tradition positioning

**Cross-tradition polemic — Christian bodily-resurrection**: the Christian eschatological doctrine **explicitly rejects** the cyclic-rebirth schema. Christian eschatology is **linear** (one embodied life → one death → one bodily resurrection → one judgment → eternal state). The Christian-Indic divide on this point is one of the deepest cross-tradition theological divides:

- **Linear bodily-resurrection** (Christian, also Pharisaic-Jewish, Islamic): one life, one resurrection, eternal-state.
- **Cyclic rebirth** (Indic): beginningless saṃsāra until liberation; no eschatological-bodily-resurrection of all-the-dead.

The 553 Constantinople II condemnation of Origenist pre-existence-of-souls forecloses Christian-internal metempsychosis. See `doctrine-resurrection-bodily-christian`. Tertullian, Augustine, Aquinas, the Reformers all polemicize against the cyclic-rebirth framework.

**Convergence — intermediate-state doctrines**: both traditions develop doctrines of the post-mortem interval — Christian intermediate-state / purgatory awaiting resurrection; Tibetan bardo awaiting next-rebirth. The structural-parallel is striking even within radically different eschatologies. See `doctrine-bardo-tibetan-intermediate-state`.

**Convergence with Pythagorean metempsychosis**: the Greek philosophical tradition contains a structurally-similar doctrine of soul-transmigration in Pythagorean-Orphic-Platonic literature ~-550 onwards. Whether this represents independent development, shared Indo-European substrate, or post-Achaemenid-period cultural-transmission is debated; Obeyesekere 2002 + West 2007 *Indo-European Poetry and Myth* treat the question.

## Disputes

- **What reincarnates** — ātman (Hindu) vs anātman (Buddhist) vs jīva (Jain); the principal classical Indian-philosophy dispute, central to the Hindu-Buddhist debate.
- **Pre-Upaniṣadic origin** — purely-internal Vedic development (older view) vs eastern-Gangetic non-Vedic substrate (Bronkhorst 2007).
- **Independent Pythagorean development vs Indic-borrowing** — comparative question of long debate.
- **Memory of past lives** — Buddhist + Hindu literature contains many past-life-memory narratives; modern psychiatric study (Ian Stevenson, *Twenty Cases Suggestive of Reincarnation* 1966) treats the empirical question.
