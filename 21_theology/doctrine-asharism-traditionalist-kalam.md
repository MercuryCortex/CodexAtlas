# Ashʿarism — Traditionalist-Synthetic Islamic Kalām

**Ashʿarism** (*al-Ashʿariyya*) is the dominant systematic-theological school of mainstream Sunni Islam from the 11th c. onward, founded by Abū al-Ḥasan al-Ashʿarī (874–936 CE) on a famous c. 912 defection from his Muʿtazilī teacher Abū ʿAlī al-Jubbāʾī, and systematised over the next three centuries by al-Bāqillānī, al-Juwaynī, [[al-ghazali|al-Ghazālī]], al-Shahrastānī, and Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī. It is the synthetic middle position between the rationalist Muʿtazila and the strict-traditionalist Ḥanbalī anti-kalām position — accepting kalām method (systematic-theological argument) while preserving traditionalist doctrinal substance (divine attributes as real, Qurʾān as uncreated, divine sovereignty as voluntarist).

## The doctrinal core

Six linked propositions constitute the canonical Ashʿarī position:

1. **Divine attributes are real, eternal, and inhere in the divine essence** — not identical with the essence (the Muʿtazilī position) and not other than it (the anthropomorphist position). The classical formula is *lā huwa wa-lā ghayruhu* ("neither it nor other than it"). The standard list is seven (life, knowledge, power, will, hearing, sight, speech) plus subsidiary attributes of action.
2. **The Qurʾān is uncreated** — as eternal divine speech (*kalām nafsī*, "self-speech") it is co-eternal with God's essence. The recited / written Qurʾān is the temporal expression of the eternal speech. This is the post-miḥna traditionalist position vindicated against the Muʿtazilī *khalq al-Qurʾān*.
3. **The difficult attributes** (the *mutashābihāt* — God's face, hand, eyes, throne-sitting) are affirmed *bi-lā kayfa* — "without [asking] how", "without modality". This is the synthetic middle between Muʿtazilī allegorisation (the attributes are figures of speech) and anthropomorphist literalism (God has a body).
4. **Divine acts are sovereign, not constrained by an antecedent justice** — God's will is the ground of moral predication, not vice versa. The good is what God commands; the evil is what God prohibits. This is *theological voluntarism* / *divine-command ethics*, in direct opposition to Muʿtazilī ethical objectivism. God can do anything not logically impossible — He could have damned the prophets and saved the worst sinners, and that would have been just because just is what God does.
5. **Human action is *kasb* (acquisition)** — God creates the act; the human "acquires" it (in some Ashʿarī articulations the human creates a non-efficacious *kasb*-act that maps onto the divine-created real act). The human is morally responsible for what is acquired. This is the compromise between Jabriya determinism and Muʿtazilī libertarianism, much-criticised as a verbal cover but doctrinally durable.
6. **Causation is occasionalist atomism** — the world consists of atoms + accidents, perishing and being recreated each moment by direct divine action. There is no natural causal nexus; what we perceive as causation is the regular concurrence (*ʿāda* — habit) by which God concomitantly creates fire and burning-of-cotton. This is the foundational Ashʿarī cosmology, articulated by al-Bāqillānī and given the classical defence by al-Ghazālī's *Tahāfut* 17th discussion.

## The post-Ghazālian synthesis

Al-Ghazālī's *Iḥyāʾ ʿulūm al-dīn* (12th c.) is the structural masterpiece of mature Ashʿarism — it integrates Ashʿarī systematic theology with Sufism (the inner devotional life) and with the Sharīʿa-juridical observance of the Shāfiʿī madhhab. The synthesis becomes the standard Sunni package: Ashʿarī (or Māturīdī) theology + Shāfiʿī (or Mālikī, Ḥanafī) jurisprudence + Junaydī Sufism. This is the dominant configuration of pre-modern Sunni intellectual life from the 12th c. through the 18th-c. Wahhābī reform.

## The Salafī-Wahhābī critique

From the 18th c. onward (Muḥammad ibn ʿAbd al-Wahhāb, then 19th-20th-c. Salafī movements: Rashīd Riḍā, the al-Albānī school), the Ashʿarī synthesis comes under sustained attack as theological innovation (*bidʿa*) that imported Greek philosophical method into Islamic doctrine. The Salafī alternative is a return to the pre-kalām traditionalism of Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal (the original *miḥna* resister) + Ibn Taymiyya (1263–1328, the medieval traditionalist-Ḥanbalī critic of both Muʿtazilī rationalism + Ashʿarī kalām-synthesis). The Ashʿarī-Salafī dispute has shaped 20th-21st-c. Sunni internal theology decisively.

## Cross-tradition resonances

- **Malebranchian occasionalism.** The 17th-c. Cartesian-Christian occasionalism of Malebranche is the structural parallel — God's direct causal monopoly + the denial of created secondary causation. Perler & Rudolph 2000 establishes the historical independence + doctrinal convergence: both positions are responses to the question of how divine sovereignty relates to natural causation in a created world.
- **Calvinist theological voluntarism.** The Ashʿarī divine-command ethics + the Reformed *potentia absoluta* / pure-sovereignty position are structurally parallel: God's will is the ground of moral predication, not vice versa. Both differ from Aquinas's modified Thomistic position that divine and creaturely goodness share an analogical structure.
- **Bilā kayfa as Christian apophaticism.** The *bilā kayfa* formula for difficult attributes (real but the manner is not specified) is structurally parallel to Christian apophatic Christology + Trinitarian doctrine (the *prosōpa* of the Trinity are distinct but the manner of distinction is not specified; the hypostatic union is real but the manner of union is not specified). The convergence is on the underlying logic of mystery + analogical predication.
