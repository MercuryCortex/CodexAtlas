# The Filioque (double procession of the Holy Spirit)

## Identity

The **Filioque** (Latin *fīlioque*, "and the Son") is a single word inserted by the Latin West into the third article of the Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed of 381. Where the original Greek confesses the Holy Spirit "who proceeds **from the Father**" (τὸ ἐκ τοῦ Πατρὸς ἐκπορευόμενον), the Latin recitation became "who proceeds **from the Father and the Son**" (*qui ex Patre Filioque procedit*). On that one word turns the central Trinitarian dispute between Catholic and Orthodox Christianity, and the doctrinal core of the East-West split symbolized by [[event-great-schism-1054|the Great Schism of 1054]].

The word encodes a theological claim about the *inner life of the Trinity*: whether the Holy Spirit takes its eternal origin from the **Father alone** (the Greek "single procession") or from the **Father and the Son** together (the Latin "double procession"). It is best understood not as a free-standing doctrine but as a contested amendment to the pneumatology of [[doctrine-trinity-nicene|the Nicene Trinitarian settlement]] — which is why that node lists the Filioque as its principal internal dispute.

## In the Latin and Greek traditions

**The Latin root.** The theological germ is **Augustine's** *De Trinitate* (~400-420), which describes the Spirit as the mutual love and bond (*vinculum amoris*) between Father and Son, proceeding *principaliter* ("principally") from the Father yet also *from the Son*. The earliest surviving credal recitation associated with the clause is the **Third Council of Toledo (589)** (its authenticity in the council acts is debated — Siecienski 2010) in Visigothic Spain — an anti-Arian setting, where affirming that the Spirit proceeds from the Son too was a way of underlining the Son's full, un-subordinated divinity. It became normative in the **Carolingian** church: Charlemagne's theologians promoted it, and the **Council of Aachen (809)** endorsed it. Crucially, **Pope Leo III** approved the underlying theology but refused to alter the creed itself, and (per the medieval account) had the creed engraved without the Filioque on silver shields displayed in St Peter's. Rome only adopted the interpolated creed around **1014**, under Pope Benedict VIII.

**The Greek objection.** The definitive Eastern rejection comes from **Photios of Constantinople**: his Encyclical to the Eastern patriarchs (867) attacked the clause as heresy, and his *Mystagogy of the Holy Spirit* (~885) is the classic refutation, insisting the Spirit proceeds **"from the Father alone"** (ἐκ μόνου τοῦ Πατρός). The Greek case rests on the **"monarchy of the Father"** — the Father as the single *cause* (αἰτία) and source within the Godhead — and on the specific force of the verb *ekporeusis*, which denotes origination from the ultimate cause. On that reading the Spirit *cannot* "ekporeuomenon" from the Son without giving the Godhead two sources. The East also objected on **procedural** grounds: an ecumenical creed may not be altered by one region of the church without a universal council.

**The mediating position.** Not every Greek father condemned the Latin usage. **Maximus the Confessor**, in his *Letter to Marinus* (~655), defended the Westerners: their *Filioque* did not make the Son a second *cause* but meant the Spirit proceeds from the Father **through the Son** (διὰ τοῦ Υἱοῦ) — a formula many later reunion efforts leaned on. The reunion councils of **Lyon II (1274)** and **Florence (1439)** both affirmed the Filioque "as from one principle," but the Florentine union collapsed when **Mark of Ephesus** refused to sign and the Byzantine populace rejected it under Ottoman pressure.

## Cross-tradition significance

The Filioque's comparative importance is unusual: its principal fault line runs **inside** Christianity, along the **Latin West / Byzantine East** axis, rather than between Christianity and another religion. It is the sharpest doctrinal expression of the divergence of two Christian traditions — [[tradition-latin-christianity]] and [[tradition-eastern-orthodoxy]] — that had shared the same creed for three centuries. In Atlas terms its cross-tradition reach is carried by wires: it **modifies** [[doctrine-trinity-nicene]], is **rejected by** the Orthodox East, is a **doctrinal cause** of the 1054 schism, and **bears on** [[doctrine-theosis-divinization-orthodox|theosis]] (if the Spirit — the agent of deification — is repositioned in the immanent Trinity, the economy of grace is touched).

An honest account resists two overstatements. First, this is **not** a case of cross-religious transmission or convergence like the primordial-deep or triadic-divinity clusters; there is no non-Christian counterpart being compared. Second, much modern scholarship (Siecienski 2010; the 1995 Vatican clarification) argues the dispute is partly **terminological**: Greek *ekporeusis* (origination from the sole cause) and Latin *processio* (the broader "coming-forth") are not exact equivalents, so the Latin *Filioque* was never meant to assert what the Greek *ek monou tou Patros* denies. On that reading the Father remains the sole *cause* in both traditions, and the Son's role is one of *mediation* (per Maximus). Whether this dissolves the disagreement or merely reframes it remains genuinely contested between Catholic and Orthodox theologians.

## Disputes

- **Single vs double procession** — the substantive question: is the Father the *sole* origin of the Spirit (Greek), or do Father and Son together spirate the Spirit (Latin)? Even the Latin position qualifies this — the Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son "*as from one principle*" (Florence 1439), not from two.
- **"From the Son" vs "through the Son"** — Maximus's *dia tou Huiou* is the bridge formula; the debate is whether it is compatible with the Latin *Filioque* or a genuinely different claim.
- **Cause vs terminology** — whether the whole controversy rests on the non-equivalence of *ekporeusis* and *processio* (Siecienski 2010; Vatican 1995) or on a real ontological disagreement (much of the Orthodox tradition, following Lossky 1944).
- **Legitimacy of the interpolation** — even Catholics who defend the doctrine concede the *procedural* problem that a Western region altered a conciliar creed; several modern Catholic recitations (and ecumenical proposals) omit the word while retaining the theology.
- **The date question** — as with [[event-great-schism-1054|1054]] itself, whether the Filioque "caused" the schism or was one crystallizing symptom of a longer estrangement (Dvornik, Meyendorff).
