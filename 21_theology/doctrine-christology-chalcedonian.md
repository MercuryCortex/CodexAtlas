# Chalcedonian Christology (Two-Natures-One-Person Definition)

The Christological doctrine defined at the **Council of Chalcedon (451 CE)**: Jesus Christ is **one person (hypostasis) in two natures (physeis)** — fully divine and fully human, the two natures united in the single person of the eternal Son **without confusion, without change, without division, without separation**. The Definition is the second great patristic doctrinal achievement (after the Nicene-Constantinopolitan Trinitarian settlement of 325/381) and the canonical Christology of the majority Christian tradition — Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, and magisterial Reformation Protestant.

## The Chalcedonian Definition (451)

The text of the Definition:

> "We confess one and the same Son, our Lord Jesus Christ, perfect in deity and perfect in humanity, **truly God and truly man** ... consubstantial (*homoousios*) with the Father as regards his deity, and the same consubstantial with us as regards his humanity ... acknowledged **in two natures (en dyo physesin), without confusion, without change, without division, without separation (asynchytōs, atreptōs, adiairetōs, achōristōs)** — the difference of the natures being in no way removed because of the union, but rather the property of each nature being preserved, and concurring in one person (*prosōpon*) and one hypostasis (*hypostasis*)."

The four adverbs ("without confusion, without change, without division, without separation") are the dogmatic-Christological key, bounding the doctrine against the four principal Christological heresies:

- **without confusion** — against Eutychian Miaphysite confusion of natures
- **without change** — against Apollinarian replacement of human nature with divine
- **without division** — against Nestorian division into two persons
- **without separation** — against the dyo-prosopa parallel reading

## The Christological controversies (430-451)

The Chalcedonian Definition emerges from a 20-year controversy:

**Nestorian crisis (428-431)**: Nestorius (Patriarch of Constantinople, 428) refuses *Theotokos* ("God-bearer") for the Virgin Mary, preferring *Christotokos* ("Christ-bearer") on the grounds that Mary cannot be the mother of God-as-such, only of the human Jesus. Cyril of Alexandria attacks: this implies two persons (one divine eternal Son, one human Jesus), reducing the incarnation to a *moral-union* of two persons. Council of Ephesus (431) condemns Nestorius; *Theotokos* is affirmed. **Formula of Reunion** (433): Cyril + John of Antioch agree on a compromise affirming *Theotokos* + two natures unconfused.

**Eutychian crisis (448-451)**: Eutyches (Constantinopolitan archimandrite) teaches that after the union there is **one nature** (*mia physis*) — the human nature is absorbed into the divine "like a drop of honey in the sea." Local synod 448 condemns; the **Robber Council of Ephesus 449** (organized by Dioscorus of Alexandria) rehabilitates Eutyches + deposes Flavian. Pope Leo I writes his *Tome to Flavian* (449) articulating the Latin two-natures position.

**Chalcedon (451)**: convened by emperor Marcian + empress Pulcheria. The Definition synthesizes Cyril's *Theotokos* + two-nature integrity + Leo's two-nature distinction + Antiochene anti-Nestorian-but-anti-Eutychian sensibilities. The reading of Leo's Tome at the council provokes the acclamation: "**Peter has spoken through Leo!**"

## Post-Chalcedonian dissent and the Oriental Orthodox / Church of the East divides

**Oriental Orthodox** (Coptic Egyptian, Syriac, Armenian, Ethiopian, Indian): reject Chalcedon as crypto-Nestorian (the *two-natures* language compromises Cyrilline-Alexandrine *one-incarnate-nature*). They preserve a **Miaphysite** formula — *mia physis tou Theou Logou sesarkōmenē* ("one incarnate nature of God the Word") — derived from Cyril (who actually used Apollinarian language he believed orthodox). Modern ecumenical dialogue (Anba Bishoy + Vatican; the 1990 *Christological Agreed Statement*) acknowledges the divergence is largely **terminological** — both sides affirm the same Christological reality.

**Church of the East** (East Syriac dyophysite, often labeled "Nestorian" though not historically Nestorius's exact position): rejects Chalcedon in the opposite direction — for using *hypostasis* in a way that compromises the distinctness of the two natures. They affirm *two qnōmē* (hypostases) in one *parsopa* (person). The Church of the East flourishes in Persian Empire + along the Silk Road through 14th-c.

## Post-Chalcedonian developments

- **Constantinople II (553)** — neo-Chalcedonian synthesis: condemns the *Three Chapters* (Theodore of Mopsuestia + Theodoret + Ibas) to placate Miaphysite sympathies; clarifies Chalcedon in Cyrilline direction.
- **Constantinople III (681)** — completes Chalcedonian Christology by affirming **dyothelitism** (two energies + two wills in Christ) against Monothelitism. Maximus the Confessor's articulation is canonical.
- **Nicaea II (787)** — defends iconography on Chalcedonian grounds: the incarnation makes images of the divine possible.

## Cross-tradition positioning

**Completes Nicene Trinitarian doctrine**: see [[doctrine-trinity-nicene]]. The two great patristic doctrinal achievements together specify: (a) God is Trinity (Nicaea 325 / Constantinople 381), (b) the eternal Son became incarnate as fully-divine-fully-human (Chalcedon 451). The dyad is the doctrinal-skeleton of canonical Christianity.

**Definitive articulation of incarnation**: see [[doctrine-incarnation]]. Pre-Chalcedonian incarnation-language is rich but unsystematic; Chalcedon defines the structure that subsequent theology operates within.

**Polemicized against by Monothelitism**: see [[doctrine-monothelitism]]. The Chalcedonian two-natures must logically entail two energies + two wills; the dyothelite completion is Constantinople III 681 (Maximus).

**Cross-tradition Christology-Buddhology**: see [[doctrine-buddha-nature-tathagatagarbha]] + [[doctrine-three-bodies-trikaya-buddhist]]. Williams 1989 + Lai 1980s treat the cross-tradition Christology-Buddhology comparison as a major comparative-theology topic. Both traditions articulate divine-human (buddha-sentient) ontological-structures.

**Presupposed by theōsis**: see [[doctrine-theosis-divinization-orthodox]]. Maximus the Confessor's integration of Chalcedonian Christology with theōsis-soteriology is the principal Byzantine synthesis.

## Disputes

- **Strict-Chalcedonian vs neo-Chalcedonian** — 6th c. debate on how much Cyrilline-Alexandrine language to integrate.
- **Communicatio idiomatum** — Lutheran-Reformed debate on how the properties of one nature can be predicated of the other (Lutheran: yes, in some sense; Reformed: only via the *person*).
- **Kenotic Christology** — 19th-c. Lutheran + Anglican theologians (Thomasius, Gore) argue the divine Son emptied (*kenōsis*, Phil 2:7) of certain divine attributes during incarnation. Controversial within Chalcedonian boundaries.
- **Modern critical-historical Jesus** — relationship between Chalcedonian-doctrinal-Christ + historical-Jesus reconstruction.
